Health Care for Women and Gender-Diverse Active-Duty and Reserve Uniformed Service Members and Veterans

Number 12 (Replaces Committee Opinion Number 547, December 2012)

This Committee Statement was developed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' Committee on Advancing Equity in Obstetric and Gynecologic Health Care in collaboration with Torie Comeaux Plowden, MD, MPH, and Alicia Christy, MD; with consultation from Jennifer L. Williams, PhD, MSN, MPH, FNP-BC.

ABSTRACT: An increasing percentage of the active-duty and reserve uniformed services force are women, and they are the fastest growing population in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA). Additionally, the VHA is one of the largest providers of gender-affirming care in the United States. Asking about a patient's military service and being aware of the unique health and reproductive health care needs of this population are critically important. Members of these populations may be at increased risk for a variety of health-related issues, including intimate partner violence, a history of military sexual trauma, and other health conditions. This Committee Statement highlights the unique reproductive and other health needs of and special considerations for women and gender-diverse current and former active-duty and reserve uniform service members.

Summary of Recommendations and Conclusions

Based on the principles outlined in this Committee Statement, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists makes the following recommendations. Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should facilitate health care among women and gender-diverse active-duty and reserve uniformed service members and veterans by doing the following:

  • Ask about a history of service, inquiring about active-duty, reserve, and veteran status, and assess for specific service-related health and reproductive health needs of the service member or veteran or partner of the service member or veteran.

  • Be knowledgeable about family planning, including prepregnancy counseling, available fertility services, abortion options, and contraceptive methods, for women and gender-diverse active-duty and reserve uniformed service members and veterans and the unique considerations deployment poses when providing these services.

  • Regularly screen and refer for interpersonal violence, including sexual trauma and posttraumatic stress disorder.

  • Identify gaps in care and availability of trauma-informed care and engage and coordinate care with the local TRICARE, Department of Defense or Veterans Health Administration facility, or both.

  • Educate community health care professionals on the unique needs of women and gender-diverse active-duty and reserve uniformed service members and veterans and how to access care for these patients.

  • Advocate for and promote research that studies the effect of military status on reproductive health.

  • Advocate for the expansion of health and reproductive health care services for active-duty service members and veterans and their spouses.

Background

An increasing percentage of the active-duty and reserve uniformed services force are women, and they are the fastest growing population in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA). Additionally, the VHA is one of the largest providers of gender-affirming care in the United States. It is estimated that there are more than 15,000 transgender people serving on active duty or in the Guard or Reserve forces 1. Members of these populations may be at increased risk for a variety of health-related issues, including intimate partner violence, a history of military sexual trauma, and other health conditions. Trauma-informed care is an important consideration for all women and gender-diverse individuals, and perhaps even more so for these vulnerable populations. This Committee Statement highlights the unique reproductive and other health needs and special considerations for women and gender-diverse current and former active-duty and reserve uniform service members.

Demographics

The number of active-duty service members, reserve service members, veterans, and members of the U.S. Public Health Service who are women is growing rapidly, increasing the need for access to health and reproductive health services for women. Approximately 18% of today's active-duty force and 22% of National Guard and Reserves are women 2. Among active-duty U.S. Public Health Service members, 55% of the force identifies as women 3. Women veterans also are enrolling in VHA health care at record levels, accounting for more than 30% of the increase in veterans enrolled over the past 5 years. Consequently, the number of women veterans using VHA health care has more than tripled since 2001 4 5 6. Services that are available through the VHA vary widely based on local resources. All obstetric and assisted reproductive technology (ART) services for eligible veterans are provided in the community 7.

Active-duty service members, reserve service members, and veterans who are women differ demographically from the general U.S. population. Black and Hispanic individuals are overrepresented in both the military and the VHA. Thirty-two percent of active-duty service members who are classified at women identify as Black, 20.5% identify as Hispanic, 8.5% identify as Asian or Pacific Islander, and 1% identify as American Indian or Alaskan Native 8. Among women veterans, 43% of those who used VHA health services in fiscal year 2020 belonged to a racial or ethnic minoritized group 4 5.

A 2014 study reported more than 15,000 transgender individuals were serving in the U.S. military, including nearly 9,000 serving as active duty 1. Additionally, a 2015 survey of active-duty service members reported that 0.6% identified as transgender 9. There is a notable number of transgender veterans, with data from the National Transgender Discrimination Survey estimating more than 130,000 transgender individuals hold veteran, retired National Guard, or Reserve status 10. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) data from 2013 demonstrated that the prevalence of gender identity disorder (gender dysphoria) was higher among veterans than in the general U.S. population 11. There are additional health and reproductive health considerations for gender-diverse individuals. A 2021 study examining health disparities in active-duty sexually minoritized and transgender service members found that transgender service members experienced higher rates of anxiety, suicidality, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) when compared with cisgender heterosexual and cisgender sexually minoritized service members 12. Therefore, identification of current and prior service among transgender individuals is an important consideration when delivering health care.

Although three out of four women veterans who used VHA health services in 2019 lived in urban areas, the number of women veterans living in rural areas is increasing 4. Additionally, a large number of women and gender-diverse active-duty service members and veterans have limited incomes and other psychosocial factors that put them at greater risk for poor outcomes 13 14 15. According to data from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, although total homelessness among veterans decreased by 4.5% (from 37,252 to 35,574) between 2020 and 2023, homelessness among women veterans actually increased by nearly 24% (from 3,126 to 3,980) 16. Of note, the vast majority of active-duty service members are enlisted, and many have incomes below the federal poverty level. Most enlisted families with one child qualify for WIC (the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children) 14 17 18.

Awareness of Current or Prior Service, Coverage, and Access to Care

Asking about a patient's military service and being aware of the unique health and reproductive health care needs of this population are critically important. Obstetrician–gynecologists should be aware that available medical services for active-duty service members and their spouses, reservists, and veterans receiving care through the VHA are determined by Congress and that some care, such as most abortion care, is restricted by law. Unlike public or private insurance programs in the community, medical services through the military (TRICARE) and the VHA are congressionally authorized entitlements; thus, any change in services requires legislative change. TRICARE is the uniformed services health care program for active-duty service members, active-duty family members, National Guard and Reserve members and their family members, retirees and retiree family members, survivors, and certain former spouses 19. Individuals may be eligible for VA health care if they served 24 months in the active military, naval, or air service and meet certain requirements and were not dishonorably discharged 20.

To effectively advocate for women and gender-diverse active-duty service members, reserve service members, and veterans, an understanding of the health care–delivery process is essential. Although military medical benefits (TRICARE) are similar to the VHA, there are some important differences. Legal spouses and dependent children of active-duty service members are eligible for medical services through TRICARE. Most spouses of veterans are not eligible for care through the VHA unless they qualify for CHAMPVA (the Civilian Health and Medical Program of the Department of Veterans Affairs) or under special circumstances, such as the fertility benefit for veterans with service-connected infertility 21. The newborns of women and gender-diverse veterans are eligible for care for the first 7 days of life 22.

Women and gender-diverse active-duty service members and veterans may seek primary and reproductive health care at military treatment facilities, through the U.S. Department of Defense's TRICARE program, in the community (through Medicaid, Medicare, or private insurance), through the VA, or some combination thereof. Most gynecologic care is available to active-duty service members and veterans through military treatment facilities or VHA medical centers. Gynecologic subspecialty care, such as gynecologic oncology, reproductive endocrinology and infertility, minimally invasive gynecologic surgery, and urogynecology and reconstructive pelvic surgery, is available to active-duty service members in most military treatment facilities that have residency training programs. When needed services are not available through a military treatment facility, referral for off-post care is arranged through TRICARE. For veterans, availability of gynecologic subspecialty care services is limited to just a few VHA medical centers, though these services can be obtained in the community as a covered benefit.

Abortion Care

The 2022 Dobbs v. Jackson Women' s Health Organization decision made it imperative for both the military and the VHA to preserve access to abortion care and contraception. The Hyde Amendment restricts the use of federal funding to provide abortions 23. As such, TRICARE covers abortions only in the following circumstances: 1) the life of the birthing person is at risk or 2) the pregnancy is the result of an act of rape or incest. Meanwhile, the VHA allows for the following provision of abortion services: 1) the life or health of the pregnant veteran or VHA beneficiary would be endangered if the fetus were carried to term or 2) the pregnancy is the result of an act of rape or incest. Both the military and the VHA consider restrictions to abortion services a patient-safety issue, and it is policy to offer abortion care to eligible individuals even when abortion is prohibited in that state. In those instances, active-duty individuals and veterans are allowed to travel across state lines to the nearest facility to receive necessary care 24. Additional legislation, the Shaheen Amendment, has been proposed to ensure that service members who need abortion services do not incur financial hardship secondary to traveling to access care and also are given the appropriate convalescent leave 25.

Pregnancy Care

Pregnant service members and the spouses of active-duty service members receive their pregnancy care through either the Department of Defense health care system or civilian health care professionals within the community 26. However, active-duty service members are expected to receive maternity services at their military treatment facility when available. If the local military treatment facility does not provide obstetric services, the patient's primary care physician provides a referral to an accessible civilian health care professional. The VHA does not provide routine prenatal or pregnancy care onsite; therefore, pregnancy care always is referred out to community obstetric care professionals. To bridge gaps between civilian and veteran reproductive health care needs, the VHA has implemented innovations to address coordination of care. These include VHA-based maternity care coordinators, who are assigned to pregnant veterans to coordinate care and provide support and referral as needed, and educational programs for community health care professionals about the unique needs and characteristics of veterans 27.

Infertility Care

Infertility services, including in vitro fertilization (IVF), are limited, and active-duty service members and veterans often experience a lack of access to ART 28. Assisted reproductive technology services are available only to seriously ill or injured active-duty service members if the impairment causes the infertility and is so severe that the active-duty service member's illness or injury makes it unlikely for them to be able to stay on active duty 29. Because TRICARE does not cover ART services, active-duty service members who seek ART services in the community must pay out-of-pocket. In general, active-duty servicewomen are not covered for ART services such as artificial or intrauterine insemination, costs related to donors or semen banks, reversal of tubal ligation, or noncoital reproductive procedures, including IVF, gamete, or zygote intrafallopian transfers, or tubal embryo transfers 30. A small number of military treatment facilities (those with obstetrics and gynecology residency programs) do have ART services available for active-duty service members and their spouses; however, there are still substantial out-of-pocket costs. The VHA made IVF services available in 2017, and VHA policy, by law, mirrors military policy. Within the VHA, only veterans who have a service-connected condition causing their infertility and their legal spouses are eligible to receive fertility services, including IVF, under a VHA authorization. Like the military, the VHA also requires that individuals be able to produce their own gametes.

Gender-Affirming Care

Care is available for gender-diverse individuals within the military and the VHA. TRICARE covers gender-affirming hormone therapy and psychological counseling. In general, TRICARE does not cover surgical treatment for gender dysphoria, but active-duty service members may request a waiver for medically necessary gender-affirming surgery 31. Within the VHA, all medically necessary gender-affirming care is available to veterans. However, gender-affirming surgical care is not a covered benefit at time of this publication 32.

Use of Language

ACOG recognizes and supports the gender diversity of all patients who seek obstetric and gynecologic care. In original portions of this document, authors seek to use gender-inclusive language or gender-neutral language. When describing research findings, this document uses gender terminology reported by investigators. To review ACOG's policy on inclusive language, see https://www.acog.org/clinical-information/policy-and-position-statements/statements-of-policy/2022/inclusive-language .

Recommendations and Conclusions

Ask about a history of service, inquiring about active-duty, reserve, and veteran status, and assess for specific service-related health and reproductive health needs of the servicemember or veteran or partner of the service member or veteran.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists supports screening women and gender-diverse individuals for military service (current or past, reservist or active duty) and inquiring about veteran status at the initial health care visit. Additional information regarding obtaining service history can be found at https://www.va.gov/oaa/docs/mhpcmobile.pdf. Obstetrician–gynecologists and other reproductive health care professionals should ensure that all patients who are veterans are aware of VHA-related resources for their physical and mental health care. This information is available at http://www.va.gov/healthbenefits/access/medical_benefits_package.asp.

Be knowledgeable about family planning, including prepregnancy counseling, available fertility services, abortion options, and contraceptive methods, for women and gender-diverse active-duty and reserve uniformed service members and veterans and the unique considerations deployment poses when providing these services.

Although service members and veterans experience some of the same challenges as the general population, they also may have additional considerations, especially regarding reproductive health care needs 33 34 35 36 37 38 39. Service can create unique situational challenges when trying to address a service member's reproductive and other health needs. Deployment to austere environments (eg, a war zone) can result in limited access to medical services and menstrual and sanitary supplies 40. This often increases the inconvenience and logistic difficulty of management of menstruation and can predispose deployed individuals to a greater risk of gynecologic conditions. For some individuals, such deployment might interrupt preventive care or ongoing treatment or evaluation for gynecologic conditions. When caring for women or gender-diverse individuals on active duty, civilian gynecologists should inquire about any potential deployment plans and adjust evaluations and follow-up plans of care based on the patient’s deployment schedule. Assuring, at a minimum, that all test results are available to military medical personnel during deployment may avoid delay in gynecologic services or unnecessary repeat examinations or tests or both. Timely access to appropriate cervical cancer screening is critical for all people who have a cervix. In response to this issue, the military now includes cervical cancer screening as part of the annual periodic health assessment (a health and administrative assessment) checklist and the predeployment checklist. Although deployment screening is performed at the military mobilization sites, situations that require follow-up, such as abnormal cervical cancer screening results, should be performed early enough to allow for appropriate follow-up. It is critical for health care professionals to ask about deployment status as well as the proposed deployment timeline to ensure that all issues are addressed.

Contraception, management of adverse effects, and renewal access are available to all active-duty service women who request these services. Some women service members report discontinuation of contraceptive use during deployment because long work shifts and rapid travel across multiple time zones can affect adherence to a regular contraceptive schedule. In addition, depending on the location of deployment, contraceptive methods may need to be altered because some combat areas are not conducive to storage of certain contraceptives, such as depot medroxyprogesterone acetate and vaginal rings, and harsh climates have been reported to diminish the adhesive integrity of the contraceptive patch. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists encourages the education of obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals, women and gender-diverse active-duty service members, reserve service members, and veterans regarding the use of all contraceptive methods. This includes long-acting reversible contraceptives such as intrauterine devices and the contraceptive implant, particularly for menstruating individuals facing long deployments. Many active-duty service members are interested in menstrual suppression, particularly during deployment, and may welcome the oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea associated with levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine devices 40.

TRICARE and the VHA both offer a wide range of prescription contraceptive methods that are available at little or no cost. Increased efforts to train VHA primary care health professionals regarding a wide range of basic reproductive health and women's health issues, including contraception, are ongoing. Obstetrician–gynecologists and other reproductive health care professionals should consistently discuss contraceptive options with women and gender-diverse active-duty service members, reserve service members, and veterans.

Regularly screen and refer for interpersonal violence, including sexual trauma and posttraumatic stress disorder.

Obstetrician–gynecologists providing care to women and gender-diverse active-duty service members, reserve service members, and veterans should be aware of health problems, such as PTSD, military sexual trauma, and intimate partner violence, that are more prevalent in these populations. It is important to consider how their service might affect these patients' health care needs 41 42 43 44. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends screening and counseling for intimate partner violence during preventive health visits and provides guidance on screening questions 45 and recommends routine screening of all patients for a history of sexual assault, paying particular attention to patients who report pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, or sexual dysfunction 46. Of note, a meta-analysis revealed that 15.7% of active-duty personnel and veterans experienced military sexual trauma (3.9% of men and 38.4% of women) when the measure included both harassment and assault 43.

Identify gaps in care and availability of trauma-informed care and engage and coordinate care with the local TRICARE, Department of Defense or Veterans Health Administration facility, or both.

Trauma-informed care is an approach that recognizes and anticipates that many individuals experience trauma. With this approach, the care environment and patient–health care professional interactions are constructed to address an individual's physical, emotional, and behavioral needs resulting from trauma while being aware of and avoiding situations and practices that might trigger a trauma response, or retraumatization, of the survivor. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists supports an approach of universal implementation of a trauma-informed approach across all levels of practice, with close attention to avoiding stigmatization and prioritizing resilience 47. The VHA's National Center for PTSD has several resources and toolkits for health care professionals on trauma-informed care 48. Additional research is necessary to identify potential gaps in care and highlight solutions that make trauma-informed care more widely available.

Educate community health care professionals on the unique needs of women and gender-diverse active-duty and reserve uniformed service members and veterans and how to access care for these patients.

Both the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists 49 and the American Society for Reproductive Medicine 50 have collaborated with the VHA to provide educational webinars on reproductive health care needs of women and gender-diverse service members and veterans. Additional training resources have been created by the VHA for health care for women and gender-diverse veterans receiving care in the community. Both the Department of Defense and the VA provide detailed information on how to become a TRICARE or VA health care professional 51. Additionally, the VA has a variety of resources available for community health care professionals to equip them with the necessary knowledge to successfully provide care to veterans 52.

Advocate for and promote research that studies the effect of military status on reproductive health.

More women and gender-diverse individuals are serving in uniform than ever, and their roles have changed substantially from historically predominantly supportive roles to more the active roles traditionally held by male service members. Increasing deployments in austere or combat zones, the broadening and diversifying of available occupations, and the complexity of today's military creates unique health risks for the women and gender-diverse individuals who serve 53. Despite advances in available literature, persistent gaps in knowledge regarding infertility, sexually transmitted infections, and menopause still exist among women and gender-diverse active-duty service members, reserve service members, and veterans. These gaps are particularly salient in the context of continued expansion of infertility care, known trends of increasing sexually transmitted infections in the United States, growing numbers of women veterans entering their perimenopausal or postmenopausal years, and occupational and environmental hazards unique to service. Lastly, further studies are needed on the role that sexual trauma and mental health have in influencing reproductive health care needs and experiences across the life course 54.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists encourages continued synthesis of current literature and supports a comprehensive research and surveillance agenda for women and gender-diverse individuals examining the effects of service in uniform to address their specific health-related risks and outcomes and to develop tailored clinical guidelines, women-centered programs, and gender-specific policies to improve the quality and availability of services to women, gender-diverse active-duty service members, and veterans.

Advocate for the expansion of health and reproductive health care services for active-duty service members and veterans and their spouses.

Available medical services for active-duty service members, reservists, and veterans receiving care through the VHA are determined by Congress. As such, some care (including most abortion care) is restricted by law. Unlike civilian health care in the community, medical services obtained through the military (TRICARE) and through the VHA are congressionally authorized entitlements. Any change in services requires a congressional legislative change. As per the Hatch Act and in the Department of Defense Directive 1344.10, federal providers and active-duty service members cannot self-advocate, making advocacy by the community even more of an imperative 55.

Conclusion

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists supports the health and well-being of women and gender-diverse active-duty and reserve service members and women veterans. By understanding the unique needs of women and gender-diverse individuals who serve or who have served in uniform and being aware of and utilizing the full range of available resources, health care professionals can better support those who serve across their lifespans.
