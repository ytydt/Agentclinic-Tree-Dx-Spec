Number 7

Committee on Clinical Consensus–Gynecology. This Clinical Consensus was developed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' Committee on Clinical Consensus—Gynecology in collaboration with Anne-Marie E. Amies Oelschlager, MD, Kimberly Gecsi, MD, and Melissa Rodriguez, DMSc, PA-C.

Summary: Patients with gynecologic pain may use cannabis to manage pain, especially when it is not effectively managed by traditional methods. There are insufficient data to make a recommendation regarding the use of cannabis products for management of pain associated with gynecologic conditions. Clinicians should be aware of the possibility of patients' use of cannabis products for pain management and be prepared to counsel them about the theoretical benefits based on the endocannabinoid pathway, potential adverse effects, and the limitations of the data on the use of cannabis products for the management of gynecologic pain.

This document excludes the use of cannabis for the management of cancer-related pain, including gynecologic malignancy, or symptoms of chemotherapy; that topic is addressed elsewhere 1.

Background

Cannabis has undergone a significant shift in public perception and legal status over the past several years. The increasing acceptance of cannabis for both medical and nonmedical purposes has led to a substantial rise in interest, and cannabis use continues to increase. Although the federal Controlled Substances Act of 1971 designated cannabis as a Schedule I substance—reserved for substances without accepted medical use and with high potential for abuse, making it illegal federally—state-level legalization, which began in 1996, has increased the accessibility of cannabis for medical purposes and generated more permissive public attitudes throughout the United States 2 ( seeBox 1 for a list of terms). An analysis of the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (N=282,768) found that prevalence of past-month use of cannabis increased 38.2% from 2015 to 2019 (8.3–11.5%, P<.001) 3. As of 2024, there is discussion at the federal level to potentially reclassify cannabis to a Schedule III drug, defined by the United States Drug Enforcement Administration as drugs with a moderate to low potential for physical and psychological dependence. Examples of Schedule III drugs include Tylenol with codeine, ketamine, anabolic steroids, and testosterone 4.

Important Terms

Cannabidiol (CBD): A cannabinoid with contrasting mechanisms of action and therapeutic indications to delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC); as an isolated substance, CBD does not cause intoxication.

Cannabinoid: Any of a group of closely related compounds that include cannabinol and the active constituents of cannabis.

Cannabis: A broad term that can be used to describe the various products and chemical compounds derived from the Cannabis sativa or Cannabis indica species. Typically, Cannabis sativa has a higher THC-to-CBD ratio compared with Cannabis indica.

Cannabis use disorder: A problem-causing pattern of cannabis use leading to clinically significant impairment or distress, as manifested by at least two distinguishing symptoms (eg, cannabis is taken in larger amounts or for longer periods than intended; experience of craving; continued cannabis use despite the experience of physical, social, or interpersonal problems caused by cannabis use) occurring within a 12-month period.

Cannabis-based products for medicinal use: Medicinal products containing cannabis or cannabinoids derived from the cannabis plant (eg, THC or CBD, or both).

Delta-8-tetrahydrocannabinol: A cannabinoid derived from hemp used as an herbal remedy; it has moderately intoxicating effects.

Delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC): A cannabinoid used for medicinal purposes and nonmedicinally for its intoxicating effects.

Dronabinol: A synthetic delta-9-THC; this medication is approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of anorexia for those with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and for nausea and vomiting for those receiving chemotherapy for cancer.

Hash or Hashish: Cannabis resin, compressed trichomes (resin) from buds.

Hash oil: Resin extracted in purest form (oil-like), 50% THC or greater.

Hemp: Cannabis plants with very low levels of THC (0.3% or less) are not considered marijuana, but instead hemp.

Marijuana: The use of this “slang” term is controversial because it is associated with U.S. campaigns in the 1930s to demonize the plant by associating it with immigrants from Mexico.

Nabilone: Has a chemical structure similar to THC and is synthetically derived. It is approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.

Nonmedicinal cannabis: Material from the cannabis plant that is not regulated as a medicinal product; widely used for its intoxicating effects.

Nonmedicinal CBD products: Products containing CBD that are widely sold as herbal remedies but are not regulated as medicinal products.

Nonmedicinal synthetic cannabinoids: Synthetic cannabinoids that are not structurally related to naturally occurring cannabinoids and are not currently recognized for medicinal use (eg, synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists, found in products such as “Spice”).

Palmitoylethanolamide (PEA): Endocannabinoid-like lipid mediator with documented anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and neuroprotective effects.

Synthetic cannabinoids for medicinal use: Medicinal products containing synthetically produced cannabinoids that typically mimic the effects of THC (eg, rabinol and nabilone).

Of the more than 100 cannabinoids that have been identified in cannabis, the most recognized are cannabidiol (CBD) and delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) ( seeTable 1 for methods of cannabis consumption). Delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol is associated with intoxication (“feeling high”); although CBD is considered psychoactive as an isolated substance (without the addition of THC), it does not cause intoxication. Notably, there is a lack of consistency in dosage, so the amount of THC in cannabis products may be unknown. Although CBD has been marketed as a treatment for a wide variety of medical conditions, data are lacking on its use for conditions other than Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome, or tuberous sclerosis complex.

In 2018, the Agriculture Improvement Act (also known as the Farm Bill) removed hemp and hemp plant–derived constituents from Schedule 1 of the Controlled Substances Act, classifying cannabis plants with THC concentrations of 0.3% or less as hemp. This effectively legalized hemp on the federal level, but not cannabis-derived CBD products 5 ( seeFigure 1).

With the introduction of the Farm Bill and the loosening of restrictions on hemp-based products, major retailers introduced topical and edible CBD products, leading to increased availability in several states. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has stated that non–FDA-approved CBD products are not regulated for quality and have not been approved for the diagnosis, treatment, cure, mitigation, or prevention of disease. Although the FDA has recommended that the public beware of using such products, particularly because they may not contain the amount of CBD claimed 6, survey data show that the use of CBD products is not uncommon. For instance, data from the 2019 International Cannabis Policy Study, a web-based random sampling survey of respondents aged 16–65 years conducted in Canada and the United States, showed that 26.1% of U.S. respondents reported using CDB products 7. Of those respondents who had used CBD products, more than 50% reported that CBD oil was beneficial for health; the most reported reason for use was management of pain, anxiety, and depression. Additionally, between 14% and 33% of cannabis consumers in Canada and the United States reported using cannabis to manage headaches or pain 8. A study that used data from the 2013–2020 National Survey on Drug Use and Health to determine how often cannabis use was recommended by a physician reported the prevalence of U.S. residents using cannabis for medical purposes increased from 1.2% in 2013–2014 to 2.5% in 2019–2020, with an average annual percentage change of 12.9% (95% CI, 10.4–15.5) 9. In the multivariable-adjusted model, people who lived in states that legalized medical cannabis had higher rates of use (adjusted odds ratio 4.10; 95% CI, 3.68–4.56).

Selective cannabinoids (synthetic cannabinoids that contain only THC and cannabis-based extracts that contain a combination of THC and CBD) for chronic neuropathic pain have been the focus of several systematic reviews that reported either a statistically significant but clinically small benefit or inconsistent improvements in pain across trials 10 11 12. Participants in the trials had heterogeneous diagnoses, including multiple sclerosis, diabetic neuropathy, brachial plexus injury, and chemotherapy-induced pain. None of the studies focused on women with chronic pelvic pain. A 2022 systematic review of 18 randomized placebo-controlled trials (n=1,740) and seven cohort studies (n=13,095) that assessed the use of cannabinoids for any chronic pain (not specifically gynecologic pain) found that synthetic products with high THC-to-CBD ratios (more than 98% THC) may be associated with moderate improvement in pain severity and response (30% or more improvement) but are associated with dizziness and sedation 13. A meta-analysis of 32 unique trials (n=5,174), 29 of which compared medical cannabis or cannabinoids with placebo, found with moderate-to-high certainty of evidence that noninhaled medical cannabis or cannabinoids result in a small to very small improvement in pain relief, physical functioning, and sleep quality among patients with chronic pain, along with transient cognitive impairment, vomiting, drowsiness, impaired attention, and nausea compared with placebo 14. There were no subgroup differences in pain relief based on neuropathic compared with nonneuropathic pain ( P=.21) or chronic noncancer-related pain compared with chronic cancer-related pain ( P=.16). A 2017 report from the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine stated that there is conclusive or substantial evidence that cannabis or cannabinoids are effective for the treatment of chronic pain in adults 15. However, data on their use for gynecologic pain are lacking. Despite the lack of efficacy data, there is interest in using cannabinoids to self-manage pelvic pain. An online survey of adult women (n=995) found that 61.2% of those who reported never using cannabis and 90.0% of ever users ( P<.001) were willing to use cannabis to treat gynecologic pain 16. After cannabis legalization in Canada, the prevalence of current cannabis use increased from 13.3% to 21.5% ( P<.001) in Canadian women with self-reported moderate-to-severe pelvic pain 17.

Receptor System

There are two main types of cannabinoid receptors: 1) CB1 receptors and 2) CB2 receptors Table 2. The CB1 receptors are most closely linked with THC, whereas CBD has an alternate pathway and does not bind to receptors in a similar way. Cannabinoid receptors, along with the endocannabinoid system, are essential for maintaining homeostasis within the body. They respond to endogenous cannabinoids (naturally produced by the body) as well as exogenous cannabinoids from cannabis plants and those that are synthetically produced. The complex interactions between cannabinoids and these receptors have implications for the use of cannabis, as well as for ongoing research into the development of new medications targeting the endocannabinoid system.

Legalization and Criminalization

Due to the evolving nature of cannabis laws, clinicians should be aware of the cannabis regulations in their own localities, as well as potential conflicts with federal law. Additional potential legal implications exist for federal employees, including military service members, civilian employees, and government contractors. Frequently updated online repositories of state cannabis laws, such as the National Conference of State Legislatures' website 18, may be useful resources. To date, the FDA has approved one synthetic cannabis-derived and three synthetic cannabis-related prescription-only drug products. One CBD oral solution is FDA-approved for the treatment of three specific seizure disorders—Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome, and tuberous sclerosis complex 19. Additionally, two dronabinol products that include a synthetic THC (for treatment of anorexia associated with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome [AIDS] and nausea and vomiting associated with chemotherapy) and one synthetic cannabinoid (nabilone, for nausea and vomiting associated with chemotherapy) have been approved by the FDA for the aforementioned indications 20.

Clinicians should strive to foster a practice where patients can feel safe sharing their use of or interest in cannabis for pain management. The history of the surveillance and criminalization of cannabis use in the United States is inextricably linked with racism. Although White and Black individuals use cannabis at similar rates, Black individuals are 3.73 times more likely to be arrested for cannabis possession 21. Seeking obstetric–gynecologic care should not expose a patient to criminal or civil penalties such as incarceration, involuntary commitment, loss of custody of children, or loss of housing. Additionally, screening for substance use, using objective criteria, should be applied equally to all people, regardless of age, sex, race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status. Clinicians are encouraged to reflect these nonpunitive and nonbiased principles as they care for patients.

Methods

This Clinical Consensus document was developed using an a priori protocol in conjunction with the authors listed above. The a priori protocol was modeled after the Clinical Consensus methodology; a full description of the Clinical Consensus methodology is published separately 22. The description below is specific to this Clinical Consensus document.

Literature Search

American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) medical librarians completed a comprehensive literature search for primary literature within Ovid MEDLINE and PubMed for human-only studies written in English. A systematic review published in 2022 served as an evidence base for this Clinical Consensus 23, and ACOG's literature search was limited to the end date of this review (2021 to March 2023). MeSH terms and keywords can be found in Appendix 1 (available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/D678). Search terms for racial, ethnic, socio-economic, and vulnerable population disparities in the use of cannabis products for gynecologic pain were incorporated into the literature review, and recommendations were drafted with the intent of promoting health equity and reducing these disparities. A bridge literature search was completed in January 2024. Any updated literature was incorporated into the text and recommendations, as appropriate.

Study Selection

The scope of this document excludes the use of cannabis to manage symptoms associated with cancer treatment or obstetric care or for the management of pain due to gynecologic malignancy. Qualifying studies passed both title and abstract screen and full-text screen and met the following inclusion criteria: conducted in countries ranked very high on the United Nations Human Development Index 24, included female participants, and included all study designs. Studies that passed full-text screen by the authors were included in a summary evidence map (Appendix 2, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/D679).

Consensus Voting and Recommendation Development

At a meeting of the Committee on Clinical Consensus–Gynecology, a quorum of two-thirds of eligible voting members was met, and the committee held a formal vote for each proposed recommendation. All recommendation statements met or exceeded the 75% approval threshold required for consensus.

Use of Language

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recognizes and supports the gender diversity of all patients who seek obstetric and gynecologic care. In original portions of this document, authors seek to use gender-inclusive language or gender-neutral language. When describing research findings, this document uses gender terminology reported by investigators. To review ACOG's policy on inclusive language, see https://www.acog.org/clinical-information/policy-and-position-statements/statements-of-policy/2022/inclusive-language.

Summary of Consensus Recommendations

Cannabis and Gynecologic Pain: General Principles

• Better-quality data on the use of cannabis products are needed. There are insufficient data to make a recommendation regarding the use of these products for management of pain associated with gynecologic conditions.

• Clinicians should be aware of the possibility of patients' use of cannabis products for pain management and be prepared to counsel them about the theoretical benefits based on the endocannabinoid pathway, potential adverse effects, and the limitations of the data on the use of the cannabis products for the management of gynecologic pain.

Cannabis and Gynecologic Pain: General Principles

Better-quality data on the use of cannabis products are needed. There are insufficient data to make a recommendation regarding the use of these products for management of pain associated with gynecologic conditions.

Clinicians should be aware of the possibility of patients' use of cannabis products for pain management and be prepared to counsel them about the theoretical benefits based on the endocannabinoid pathway, potential adverse effects, and the limitations of the data on the use of cannabis products for the management of gynecologic pain.

Survey data indicate that patients with endometriosis and chronic pelvic pain may use cannabis to manage pain, especially when their pain is not effectively managed by traditional methods (eg, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, hormonal medications, surgical treatment). Registry data from a tertiary care referral clinic for pelvic pain and endometriosis revealed that 15% of the patients with endometriosis (402/2,663 patients) and 14% of the patients with other types of chronic pelvic pain (106/757 patients) reported using cannabis 17. Those who used cannabis had higher rates of anxiety, depression, and catastrophizing and worse functional quality of life compared with those who were not using cannabis at the time of the survey. In a cross-sectional survey of 113 respondents from a single outpatient gynecology office, 23% of patients with chronic pelvic pain (n=26) reported using cannabis products; CBD was used by 24% of those respondents, THC was used by 12%, and a combination of CBD and THC was used by 60% 25. Data showed no significant demographic difference between cannabis users and nonusers, and individuals in both groups used a variety of medications and therapies for the management of chronic pelvic pain. Cannabis users reported improvement in pain, cramping, muscle spasms, anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances, libido, and irritability. Those who used CBD combined with THC reported the most improvement. Nearly one-third of users reported a reduction in the use of opioids due to cannabis use. Of the 26 patients who reported using cannabis, 84% reported experiencing adverse effects, including dry mouth, sleepiness, and feeling “high” 25.

Online surveys that recruited participants through patient support groups reported substantial rates of cannabis use among respondents, with most participants reporting using it for pain relief and as a sleep aid. Additionally, a majority of respondents reported that cannabis use led to a decrease in use of other medications, such as opioids and other analgesics 26. In one anonymous, cross-sectional online international survey (1,634 responses) developed and promoted by endometriosis advocacy and support organizations, the most common methods of reported cannabis use were inhalation followed by ingestion 27. Although data are lacking on the efficacy of cannabis products for gynecologic pain, limited data demonstrate that some individuals self-report a reduction in pain. It is important for clinicians to validate patient experiences, particularly those regarding pain, and present a balanced and unbiased approach to medical cannabis use.

Limited, low-quality survey data show that cannabis use may lead to decreased use of opioids. However, more data are needed before cannabis products can be substituted for other pain medications.

Opioids are not recommended for the treatment of chronic pelvic pain. Patients already using opioids should be slowly weaned 12. Due in part to the potential risk of some patients developing opioid use disorder, alternative options for pain management are needed. Although the endocannabinoid system may offer a potential alternative option to reduce pain associated with conditions such as endometriosis 28, current data do not support that clinicians should recommend cannabis or opiates for the management of chronic gynecologic pain. Small, low-quality studies have found that, after the legalization of cannabis, patients who used cannabis for pain relief report a reduction in the use of other analgesics, including opioids 17 23 25. A Canadian retrospective analysis of women with pelvic pain found that, after legalization, cannabis users (n=143) were less likely to use opioids than nonusers (n=523), including daily use (11% vs 19%, P<.026) 17. Data from a Canadian and U.S. cross-sectional survey of individuals aged 16–65 years who had ever tried cannabis (n=44,119) found that approximately 78–85% reported using cannabis instead of opioids or prescription pain medication to manage headaches or pain 8. More data are needed before cannabis products can be substituted for other pain medications.

Palmitoylethanolamide (PEA)-Transpolydatin

There are limited randomized controlled trial data demonstrating the efficacy of PEA-transpolydatin for pain relief for primary dysmenorrhea, endometriosis, and chronic pelvic pain. However, more and stronger data are needed before making a definitive recommendation.

Palmitoylethanolamide (PEA) is an endocannabinoid-like lipid mediator with anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and neuroprotective effects. Marketed as an over-the-counter dietary supplement, it is an endogenous fatty acid that contains neither THC nor CBD 29. The available data on the use of PEA for gynecologic pain are derived from studies that combined PEA with an antioxidant (transpolydatin, polydatin, or alpha-lipoic acid), not the use of PEA alone. In a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 220 patients (aged 16–24 years) with primary dysmenorrhea, 110 patients received oral combined PEA-transpolydatin (400 mg+40 mg, 1 tablet a day for 10 days from the 24 th day of cycle) and 110 received a placebo 30. Participants in the study were not using any other hormonal or analgesic medication. Of those using PEA-transpolydatin, 98.18% reported improvement in pain, compared with 56.36% of the placebo group. The mean improvement was 4 points in the PEA group compared with 1 point in the placebo group using a visual analog scale. Several prospective cohort studies of patients with endometriosis or chronic pelvic pain have evaluated the effects of twice-daily dosing of PEA (doses ranged from 300 mg to 600 mg) combined with alpha-lipoic acid or polydatin 23 31 32 33 34. The studies demonstrated improvement in dysmenorrhea and dyspareunia, with few reported adverse effects. Based on the initial available evidence, more than 70% of patients with endometriosis or chronic pelvic pain may demonstrate some improvement with PEA therapy, with most reporting improvement after 3 months of use 35. It is important to note that most of these studies are limited by the lack of a control group and use of varying dosages. Several studies excluded patients using first-line therapies for endometriosis, such as hormonal suppression or nonsteroidal analgesics. Additionally, the one randomized controlled trial with placebo demonstrated a large placebo effect 30. Palmitoylethanolamide shows some promise for pain relief for individuals with primary dysmenorrhea, endometriosis, or chronic pelvic pain. However, more robust data—particularly double-blinded, randomized, and placebo-controlled studies—are needed before PEA can be recommended as a treatment for these gynecologic conditions.

Adolescents and Use of Cannabis

Adolescents using cannabis products are at increased risk of detrimental cognitive effects and psychotic disorders when compared with adults. Data on short-term and long-term effects on adolescent brain development are needed before medical cannabis products can be recommended for adolescents for the management of gynecologic pain.

Although data are lacking on adolescents' use of cannabis specifically for the management of gynecologic pain, data demonstrate the use of cannabis in this age group. The 2019 Youth Risk Behavior Survey found that 36.8% of high school students reported that they used cannabis one or more times during their lives, and 20.7% reported that they had used it at least once within the previous 30 days 36. Although the survey was not designed to capture why a respondent may have used cannabis, it indicates that a meaningful number of adolescents are using cannabis. The use of cannabis through adolescence and into young adulthood is associated with negative effects on neurodevelopment. The prefrontal cortex, which is involved in judgement and decision making, is not fully developed in adolescents; it does not complete development until individuals are in their mid-twenties. Some neuroanatomic studies have suggested that, for adolescent cannabis users, there may be structural alterations to the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, areas of the brain that are involved in memory and executive functioning 37 38. Cannabis use in adolescence is associated with a decrease in both verbal memory 39 40 and IQ 41. Additionally, cannabis use is associated with increased symptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder 42 and may affect academic function, including resulting in lower rates of high school completion 43. There is compelling evidence that using cannabis in adolescence, especially before age 17 years, is associated with higher rates of cannabis use disorder, most commonly developed a few years after first use of cannabis 44. Adolescent use of cannabis also is associated with higher rates of adult mental health disorders, particularly depression and schizophrenia 45 46 47 48. Although there are many confounders with observational and longitudinal studies assessing outcomes from adolescent substance use, the data are concerning and do not demonstrate that the use of cannabis is safe for adolescents. Based on the known pulmonary and cognitive effects, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that clinicians counsel adolescents against the use of cannabis 48.

Studies on the adolescent use of cannabis products including THC and CBD contain minimal information about THC potency or the THC-to-CBD ratio. Additionally, due to a lack of consistency in dosages, the amount of THC in cannabis products may be unknown. Although it appears that the primary effect of cognitive dysfunction may be related to THC, there is insufficient evidence to determine what the effect of CBD is, independent of THC, on adolescent neurocognitive development. Of note, the one FDA-approved CBD oral solution used for management of specific seizures disorders is associated with potential increased suicidal behavior and ideation, elevated liver enzymes, and somnolence 49. Given the lack of evidence to support CBD use outside of treatment for these seizure disorders, combined with concerning adverse effects in adolescents, CBD is not recommended for adolescent use outside of the current FDA-approved indications.

Associated Effects and Drug Interactions

Cognitive effects associated with cannabis, particularly substances with higher concentrations of THC, include intoxication, decreased short-term memory, impaired perception, impaired motor skills, somnolence, insomnia, and, for adolescents, an increase in suicidality. Additional adverse effects include dry mouth, respiratory effects from inhaled products, transaminase elevation, diarrhea, nausea and vomiting, and rashes from ingested or topical routes. Clinicians should be aware that, contrary to popular belief, some users may develop a cannabis use disorder. For further information on cannabis dependence and cannabis use disorder, see the National Institute on Drug Abuse 50.

For interactions with FDA-approved CBD- or THC-containing medications, clinicians and patients should refer to the product labeling. Cannabidiol- or THC-containing products that are not approved for use by the FDA may contain varying concentrations, so it is unknown what dosages would cause the greatest risk for drug interactions. However, based on what is known about pharmacokinetics, many commonly prescribed medications potentially could result in drug interactions with the use of CBD- or THC-containing products 51 52. Additionally, patients should be advised that alcohol and other sedatives may increase impairment and somnolence with the use of CBD. Patients should be advised to avoid operating heavy machinery and driving when using CBD or THC products.

Further Research

Future research is needed on the clinical pharmacology of cannabis and cannabinoids for pain relief to better understand the biology of the endocannabinoid system, particularly regarding weight, age, and gender. More and better-quality data on the efficacy and safety of the use of cannabis products for the management of gynecologic pain, including menstrual phase–specific pain, are needed. Data on newer products, such as those containing CBD, plus those with potential to treat pain, such as PEA, including as an additive medication for primary therapies (eg, hormonal suppression and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories), would be beneficial. Further research on potential drug interactions with the use of THC- and CBD-containing products is necessary. Well-designed randomized controlled trials are needed to quantify the difference between intervention and placebo. Future research is needed on the clinical pharmacology of cannabis and cannabinoids for pain relief and, moreover, to inform the understanding of the biology of the endocannabinoid system 53.

Barriers to research are substantial and include a patchwork of varying state laws, plus limited access to federally funded research on this topic 1. In addition to a lack of standardization of products and dosages, researchers often are unable to access the quantity, quality, and type of cannabis products needed to understand the health effects of cannabis products 15. A rescheduling of cannabis (from Schedule 1) would allow for federal funding and improve the ability to perform research on this topic.

In addition to investigating the effects of cannabis use in adolescents and young adults and in individuals aged 65 years and older, studies should include historically excluded and marginalized populations. Black and Hispanic communities continue to be disproportionately criminalized by drug policies and underrepresented in biomedical research. In response, researchers have issued a call to action for enhanced equity and inclusion in the rapidly growing field of cannabis research, recommending 1) inclusive recruitment, 2) improved demographic reporting in publications, and 3) strengthening publication requirements and commitments to anti-racism and equity 54.
