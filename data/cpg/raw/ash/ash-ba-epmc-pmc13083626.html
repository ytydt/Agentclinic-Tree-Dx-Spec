Key Points

  • •

    Ruxolitinib protects the epithelium in intestinal organoids from inflammatory GVHD damage, whereas steroids do not.

  • •

    The combination of ruxolitinib and steroids prevents GVHD damage in both human and murine organoids from the colon and ileum.

Visual Abstract

Abstract

Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), an often-fatal complication of allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation, is driven by inflammatory injury that damages target organs of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, skin, and liver. Effective therapies must not only suppress GVHD reactivity of donor lymphocytes but also permit regeneration of the damaged epithelium, particularly in the GI tract. Systemic corticosteroids are the standard first-line treatment for GVHD because of their powerful immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory properties but may retard epithelial repair. Ruxolitinib, a selective JAK1/2 inhibitor, is an approved therapy for steroid-refractory GVHD, although its direct effects on epithelial repair are unknown. Intestinal stem cells (ISCs), which are critical for maintaining gut integrity and barrier function, are key cellular targets of GVHD. We used both ileal and colonic organoid cultures to study the direct effects of methylprednisolone and ruxolitinib under conditions that controlled the strength of the GVH reaction. Ruxolitinib prevented inflammatory apoptosis in both human and murine organoids and preserved ISC function and proliferation, whereas corticosteroids offered no protection and in fact suppressed proliferation. This study highlights the importance of GVHD therapies that facilitate epithelial repair and regeneration, protect target tissues, and suppress alloreactivity of donor T cells.

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) is a curative therapy for a wide range of hematologic diseases.1, 2, 3 The benefit of allogeneic HCT is frequently compromised, however, by its principal toxicity, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), affects the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal (GI) tract in 30% to 50% of transplantation recipients.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 The intestinal epithelium is an especially important target, and GI GVHD is associated with mortality rates of 20% to 37%.9, 10, 11, 12 The key target cells are the intestinal stem cells (ISCs), which are located at the base of the intestinal crypt and are defined by lgr5 and olmf4 expression.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 ISCs maintain barrier integrity that is critical to epithelial repair, and the loss of ISCs in GVHD is associated with poor prognosis.15,19, 20, 21

High-dose systemic corticosteroids are the standard first-line treatment for acute GVHD because of their broad immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory properties, but 35% to 50% of patients are steroid refractory.22, 23, 24, 25, 26 Recently, the JAK/STAT pathway inhibitor ruxolitinib has been approved to treat patients who do not respond to systemic steroids.27 Acute GVHD develops through a multiphase process in which host antigen-presenting cells activate donor T cells (phases 1 and 2), leading to an inflammatory cascade and tissue injury (phase 3).7,28, 29, 30 The effects of both steroids and ruxolitinib in suppressing the first 2 phases of GVHD are well documented.31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36 However, few studies have focused on the direct effects of these immunosuppressive agents on the target cells and tissues.11,37,38

Some studies suggest that steroids may actually retard GVHD tissue repair in both the skin and the intestine.37,39 Inflammatory soluble mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) are responsible for a significant proportion of GVHD damage.40 In this study, we compare the direct effects of steroids and ruxolitinib and their combination on GVHD damage to the GI target tissues.

Material and methods

Mice

We obtained C57BL/6 (B6; H-2b) and B6.Lgr5-eGFP-CreERT2 (Lgr5-eGFP; H-2b) from The Jackson Laboratory and conducted all animal experiments in accordance with the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol: Molecular and Cellular Mechanism of GVHD and GVL-IACUC-2014-0202).

Organoid establishment and passage

Murine organoids were established and passaged as previously described.14 Briefly, mice were euthanized, and 20 cm of the duodenum and 7 cm of the colon were dissected, flushed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), opened longitudinally, and then thoroughly washed to remove fecal content. Intestinal segments (5 mm) were vigorously pipetted in cold PBS to remove connective tissue and debris until the supernatant appeared clear. The fragments were then incubated with Gentle Dissociation Reagent (Stemcell Technologies) for 20 minutes at room temperature on a rocking platform. Following incubation, tissue fragments were resuspended in cold PBS containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and pipetted vigorously for 30 seconds. The suspension was filtered 3 times through a 70 μm cell strainer to enrich the crypt fraction, centrifuged at 300g for 5 minutes at 4°C, washed with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/F-12 (STEMCELL Technologies), and centrifuged again. Murine crypts were then resuspended in Mouse IntestiCult Organoid Growth Medium (STEMCELL Technologies) at an approximate concentration of 2000 crypts per milliliter.

Murine organoids were suspended in a 1:1 volume-to-volume ratio (v/v) mixture with growth factor reduced Matrigel (Corning) and were plated as 50 μL domes in prewarmed 24-well plates (Corning). Human ileal and colonic organoids were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (ileum SCC368 and colon SCC311). Human organoids were suspended in human organoid growth medium (STEMCELL Technologies) and mixed with Matrigel in a 1:10 (v/v) and then plated as 30 μL domes in prewarmed 24-well plates. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 1 hour to allow Matrigel polymerization before adding Mouse IntestiCult Organoid Growth Medium and Human Organoid Growth Medium, respectively. Media were changed every 2 to 3 days, and organoids were passaged every 5 to 7 days. Before passaging, organoids were disrupted by pipetting in cold PBS, washed to remove residual Matrigel, and incubated with Gentle Dissociation Reagent for 20 minutes. Dissociated crypts were resuspended at 2000 crypts per milliliter, mixed with Matrigel, and replated as described.

Culture conditions

Three days after passaging, recombinant IFN-γ (10 ng/mL) and recombinant TNF-α (10 ng/mL) were used to model cytokine-induced damage as found in GVHD.14,41 Ruxolitinib (1μM; MedChemExpress) and/or methylprednisolone (1μM; MedChemExpress) were added to some organoid cultures. Concentrations were selected to approximate therapeutic plasma concentrations as previously published.37,42

Serum samples

Murine serum was obtained from mice that underwent syngeneic or allogeneic transplantation on day 14 after HCT.Human HCT recipient serum samples were obtained from the MAGIC Biorepository, which contains clinical information and serum samples collected from 23 HCT centers using a prospective-specimen-collection, retrospective-blinded-evaluation study design.43,44 Patients were prospectively monitored weekly for acute GVHD symptoms according to institutional guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki under an institutional review board–approved protocol. Serum samples were obtained from patients with lower GI GVHD diagnosed between days 28 and 63 after HCT and before treatment. For the non-GVHD control samples, day 28 posttransplantation samples were used from individual patients not diagnosed with GVHD.

Serum samples were added at a 1:4 (v/v) ratio.

Viability measurement

Organoids were grown under optimal conditions and treated with the experimental variables described previously on day 3 after passage. Organoid viability was assessed after 48 hours using the CellTiter-Glo assay reagent (Promega). Luminescence was measured using a plate reader in white opaque 96-well plates (Corning). The number of organoids per well correlated with viability (supplemental Figure 1). We chose viability as the principal metric because of its widespread use in the literature and minimal interoperator variation.45, 46, 47

Flow cytometry

Organoids were collected after 24 hours and washed in cold PBS to remove Matrigel and then incubated with TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 minutes at 37°C to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were stained with live/dead violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 minutes followed by anti-mouse epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) (CD326) staining for 30 minutes. Samples were fixed and permeabilized using Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience) and stained with anti-mouse Ki67 (Invitrogen) for 30 minutes at room temperature. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting data were acquired using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo V10 (Tree Star). Lgr5+ ISCs were identified as live single EpCAM+Lgr5-GFP+ cells.

Western blot analysis

Snap-frozen organoids were lysed in Triton buffer: 20mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 135mM NaCl, 1.5mM MgCl2, 1mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, and Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes on ice and centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C; total protein was resolved on sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and immunoblotted with antibodies for caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology) and cleaved caspase-3 (D175; Cell Signaling Technology). Levels of both full-length and cleaved caspase-3 were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to actin (Cell Signaling Technology).

RNA isolation and qPCR

Organoids were resuspended in TRIzol (Invitrogen) after 24 hours and washed with cold PBS. RNA was extracted following chloroform (Thermo Fisher Scientific) phase separation and purified using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) following manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA synthesis was performed using the Applied Biosystems Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with primers listed in supplemental Table 1. Incubation was performed on a CFX384 instrument (Bio-Rad), and target gene expression was normalized to the GAPDH housekeeping gene using the 2−ΔΔCt method.48

Statistical analysis

Data visualization and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Parametric data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni correction as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. Representative data are shown from a minimum of 2 experiments.

Results

Ruxolitinib but not methylprednisolone prevents damage from GVHD inflammation in both human and murine organoids

We evaluated the effects of methylprednisolone, ruxolitinib, and their combination on human ileal (Figures 1A and 2) and colonic (Figures 1B and 2) organoids treated with serum from HCT recipients without clinical evidence of GVHD (GVHD−) or with clinical evidence of GVHD (GVHD+). Serum samples that were obtained from patients with GVHD before they received systemic treatment contained elevated concentrations of IFN-γ and TNF-α. The damage observed when serum was cultured with human organoids was mediated primarily by IFN-γ (Figure 3). Under optimal noninflammatory culture conditions with serum from patients without GVHD, methylprednisolone reduced viability in organoids derived from both ileum and colon (Figure 1, open bars, left panels), consistent with published results in murine organoids.37 The presence of ruxolitinib had no effect. When organoids were cultured with serum from patients with significant GI GVHD, viability decreased by ∼50% (Figure 1, solid bars, left panels). Methylprednisolone failed to prevent this injury, whereas ruxolitinib significantly protected both ileal and colonic organoids. When organoids were exposed to GVHD serum and the combination of methylprednisolone and ruxolitinib, damage was largely reversed, similar to cultures with ruxolitinib alone.

We have previously shown that both TNF-α and IFN-γ are elevated during acute GVHD and that the combination of these 2 cytokines at levels similar to those observed in GVHD causes damage to organoids.14 When organoids were cultured in media without additives, methylprednisolone again impaired organoid viability. A combination of TNF-α and IFN-γ in cultures again reduced organoid viability by 50%, and the addition of methylprednisolone had no effect, whereas ruxolitinib restored viability similar to control levels even when methylprednisolone was also added to cultures (Figure 1, solid bars, right panels).

We used the same experimental conditions to interrogate murine organoids derived from either ileum or colon. We observed a pattern of response identical to that in human organoids: methylprednisolone reduced viability under otherwise optimal conditions and was unable to rescue organoids from inflammatory injury, whereas ruxolitinib afforded significant protection (Figure 4; supplemental Figure 2).

We further evaluated whether improved viability was due to reduced apoptosis or increased proliferation. Ruxolitinib both prevented apoptosis as measured by cleavage of caspase-3 and restored proliferation as measured by Ki67 staining (Figure 5). Methylprednisolone actually reduced proliferation under optimal conditions and did not prevent apoptosis caused by inflammatory cytokines.

Ruxolitinib protects Lgr5+ ISCs from inflammatory damage

ISCs are key cellular targets of GVHD whose preservation is critical for the maintenance and regeneration of the intestinal epithelium.13,18,19 We used murine organoids whose Lgr5+ ISCs contain green fluorescent protein to investigate the potential protection of ISCs by these immunosuppressive agents. We cultured organoids as previously described and quantified Lgr5+ cells by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (Figure 6). Under optimal conditions, methylprednisolone decreased ISC numbers by 50% and failed to protect them from the damage caused by IFN-γ and TNF-α, whereas ruxolitinib offered significant protection (Figure 6, left panels). We confirmed these observations by analyzing ISC-associated genes Olfm4 and Lgr5 by qPCR (Figure 6; supplemental Figure 3). Once again, methylprednisolone either damaged ISCs or offered no protection from inflammation, whereas ruxolitinib significantly prevented the loss of ISCs and restored olmf4 expression to normal levels even when combined with methylprednisolone.

We observed an identical pattern of protection when we analyzed human organoids for ISC function by expression of Olfm4 and Lgr5 genes (Figure 7). Once again, ruxolitinib prevented ISC damage whereas methylprednisolone did not, whether damage was mediated by GVHD sera (Figure 7, left panels) or the combination of IFN-γ and TNF-α (Figure 7, right panels).

Discussion

In this study, we used intestinal organoid models of inflamed target tissue to compare the direct effects of 2 immunosuppressive agents used to treat GVHD, methylprednisolone, and ruxolitinib. Methylprednisolone reduced organoid viability under optimal conditions and provided no protection to the intestinal epithelium, whereas ruxolitinib prevented inflammatory injury and preserved ISCs, which are critical for regeneration of the GI mucosal epithelium. The protective effect of ruxolitinib was dominant when used in combination with steroids, and findings were similar in ileal and colonic organoids derived from both humans and mice.

The lower GI tract is the GVHD target organ most resistant to treatment, and even when complete response is achieved, flares of acute GVHD often involve the lower GI tract and are associated with significant risk of nonrelapse mortality.49 Repair mechanisms that are intrinsic to target tissue, such as autophagy or anti-apoptotic pathways, are known to influence the severity of GVHD.50, 51, 52 These clinical observations have led to the concept of tissue tolerance50 as a critical determinant of GVHD pathophysiology.

Systemic corticosteroids have been the primary GVHD therapy for half a century. They possess powerful immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory properties, but their long-term use entails numerous toxicities, including increased infection risk, hyperglycemia, mood swings, hypertension, bone disease, and poor wound healing.53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59 Until recently, there have been no approved therapies for GVHD, and thus there was little to be gained in emphasizing the downside of steroid use because of the lack of alternatives. With the approval of ruxolitinib for steroid-resistant GVHD, several preclinical studies have begun to explore the effects of steroid treatment on target organ tissue. One murine study revealed that corticosteroids impair small intestinal epithelial growth under noninflammatory conditions and may exacerbate inflammatory damage.37

In another murine study, topical corticosteroids failed to protect skin stem cells from GVHD in contrast to topical ruxolitinib.60 The JAK inhibitor also reduced infiltration of donor T cells into the skin, so its direct effect on target cells could not be determined in that mouse model. Murine studies have also shown that IFN-γ damages ileal ISCs and that ruxolitinib prevents such damage.11,42

Our results confirm and extend these findings in several ways. First, we show that serum from both humans and mice causes damage to ISCs that is mediated primarily by IFN-γ. Second, we perform parallel experiments in organoids derived from both colon and ileum, whereas a previous study evaluated organoids derived only from ileum.37 Third, we show that inflammatory damage causes both apoptosis of ISCs and reduced their proliferation; during inflammatory damage, ruxolitinib prevents both ISC apoptosis and preserves ISC proliferation, whereas steroids have no effect on ISC apoptosis. Fourth, we clearly show no benefit of corticosteroids on GI tissue damage; in contrast to an earlier study,37 we demonstrate that ruxolitinib protects against such damage, even in combination with corticosteroids. These findings are consistent with the inhibition of the STAT pathway, which mediates apoptosis in epithelial tissue.40

Strengths of this study include analysis of both human and mouse organoids as targets and the comparison of GVHD serum and recombinant cytokines to inflict tissue damage. Limitations of this approach include the relatively rapid (48 hours) target damage and the use of soluble inflammatory mediators; the effects of these interventions on other mechanisms of cellular toxicity remain unexplored. An additional limitation is the incomplete modeling of the complex multistep pathophysiology of GVHD in vivo including effects on vascular and stromal tissue components.

Therapies that protect and promote regeneration in target tissue without further impairing immune reconstitution are likely to be critical in improving GVHD outcomes. Such strategies include amplification of cellular populations such as innate lymphoid cells that support tissue repair and homeostasis.61, 62, 63, 64 An important step in this direction will be to minimize or even replace steroids as a first-line treatment of acute GVHD. One clinical trial using JAK inhibition as monotherapy for acute GVHD has already revealed proof of principle of this concept.65 Additional trials are warranted to confirm this approach that has the potential to reduce the burden of this HCT complication and improve the quality of life of transplantation patients.
