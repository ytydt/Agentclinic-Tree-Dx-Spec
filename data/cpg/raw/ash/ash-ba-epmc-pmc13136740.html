Key Points

  • •

    Bridging was often used for perioperative anticoagulation interruption in thrombotic APS, and perioperative complications were rare.

  • •

    Guideline-directed perioperative anticoagulation tailored to thrombotic and procedure bleed risk is feasible and safe in thrombotic APS.

Visual Abstract

Abstract

Patients with thrombotic antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) frequently require interruption of anticoagulation for invasive procedures; however, data to guide the perioperative management remain limited. We conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study of adults with thrombotic APS who underwent planned periprocedural interruption of anticoagulation between 2016 and 2025. The primary outcomes were the 30-day risks for arterial thromboembolism (ATE), venous thromboembolism, and major bleeding; secondary outcomes included clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (CRNMB), microvascular APS manifestations, and catastrophic APS (CAPS). Among 172 patients who underwent 282 interruptions, most (84.9%) received warfarin and 25% were triple-positive (anticardiolipin, anti–β2-glycoprotein I, and lupus anticoagulant). Bridging with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was used in 84.7% of warfarin interruptions. Therapeutic-dose bridging was more frequently used in patients with triple-positive APS (odds ratio [OR], 7.6; 95% confidence interval [CI], 3.2-18.2) and in those with a previous ATE (OR, 3.4; 95% CI, 1.5-7.8). The 30-day risk for ATE was 0.7% (95% CI, 0.2-2.6). Major bleeding occurred in 0.7% (95% CI, 0.2-2.6) of patients with both events following high bleed–risk procedures. One bleeding event was fatal, corresponding to a mortality risk of 0.4% (95% CI, 0.1-2.0). CRNMB occurred in 3.2% (95% CI, 1.7-6.0) of patients. A single case of probable CAPS occurred in a triple-positive patient who received postoperative prophylactic LMWH. Guideline-directed perioperative anticoagulation management tailored to individual thrombotic and procedural bleeding risks seems feasible and safe. Our findings support individualized, risk-adapted perioperative anticoagulation management in APS.

Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder that is defined by arterial, venous, or microvascular thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, and nonthrombotic manifestations, such as thrombocytopenia and valvular heart disease.1,2 APS is characterized by persistent positivity for antiphospholipid (aPL) antibodies, including anticardiolipin antibodies, anti–β2-glycoprotein I (aβ2GPI) antibodies, and the lupus anticoagulant (LA). Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is the most frequent initial clinical manifestation. Arterial thromboembolism (ATE) is less common, and catastrophic APS (CAPS) is rare.2, 3, 4 Both are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality.3

Long-term anticoagulation is the cornerstone of therapy for thrombotic APS. Most patients are treated with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), particularly warfarin, because of their established efficacy in preventing recurrent thrombosis. However, VKAs present unique challenges in the perioperative setting given their long half-life (∼40 hours) and slow re-establishment of therapeutic anticoagulation, which can lead to 1 to 2 weeks of subtherapeutic anticoagulation during planned interruptions.5 Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are increasingly prescribed, but randomized trials and meta-analyses show that DOACs are inferior to VKAs in high-risk APS, particularly in triple-positive patients.6, 7, 8, 9

Perioperative management of anticoagulation in APS is challenging.10 APS is recognized as a high-risk thrombotic condition, and minimizing perioperative recurrent VTE and ATE during interruption of antithrombotic therapy is a clinical priority.10,11 Bridging anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) can be used to mitigate the perioperative risk for thrombosis; however, data from both randomized controlled trials and observational studies of patients without APS suggest that bridging increases postoperative bleeding without clearly reducing perioperative thromboembolism.12, 13, 14 Additional perioperative challenges in patients with APS include the use of concomitant antiplatelet therapy, higher international normalized ratio (INR) targets in recurrent thrombosis, coexisting immune thrombocytopenia, and the perceived risk for CAPS with periprocedural anticoagulation withdrawal and associated surgical stress, all of which further complicate decision-making.2,10,15, 16, 17

Despite these complexities, the current guidelines, including those of the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology, recognize APS as a high-risk condition that warrants individualized perioperative management.11,18 However, these recommendations are based mainly on expert opinion and extrapolation from non-APS populations. There are limited data that describe the perioperative management of antithrombotic therapy for patients with thrombotic APS. The efficacy and safety of the current perioperative anticoagulation management practices in patients with APS are not known.

To address these important knowledge gaps, we conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study to evaluate the risks for postoperative thromboembolism and bleeding after perioperative interruption of anticoagulation in patients with thrombotic APS who underwent planned surgical procedures.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study of anticoagulated patients with thrombotic APS who underwent periprocedural anticoagulation management for planned surgery between 1 January 2016 and 14 April 2025. Patients were identified using a screening roster of all consecutive patients who underwent perioperative assessment for anticoagulation interruption at The Ottawa Hospital (TOH)’s Thrombosis Clinic and a list of filter functions that limited the screening list to patients with any previous documented APS test results conducted at TOH. Filtered outpatient perioperative visits at the TOH Thrombosis Clinic were screened for eligibility.

Patients were included if they were ≥18 years of age, had a diagnosis of thrombotic APS (primary or secondary), and were prescribed anticoagulation with a VKA, DOAC, or LMWH. The diagnosis of APS was made by the managing thrombosis physician and was confirmed during record review based on the revised 2006 Sapporo classification criteria,1 requiring (1) documentation of arterial, venous, or microvascular thrombotic events, and (2) a clinically relevant aPL profile defined as persistently positive LA, anticardiolipin (aCL immunoglobulin G [IgG]/IgM), or aβ2GPI IgG/IgM antibodies, confirmed on ≥2 occasions at least 12 weeks apart.1,19 Eligibility was determined using the 2006 Revised Sapporo Criteria because of the anticipated difficulties in retrospectively classifying patients using the 2023 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology criteria.20 Patients with microvascular thrombotic manifestations were included if the treating clinician documented the event as likely attributable to thrombotic APS pathophysiology. Patients who concurrently received antiplatelet therapy or those with advanced chronic kidney disease (creatinine clearance <30 mL/min or on dialysis) were also included. A planned procedure was defined as a surgery/procedure that occurred ≥72 hours from the decision that a surgical/procedural intervention was needed.

Patients were excluded if (1) they had isolated obstetric APS without vascular thrombosis, (2) they underwent a minor procedure that was deemed safe to perform without interrupting anticoagulation therapy (eg, minimal bleeding risk procedures21), (3) they had inadequate documentation regarding the perioperative anticoagulation management, (4) the planned procedure was cancelled, (5) the procedure was emergent/unplanned (<72 hours from decision to intervene), or (6) the patient was pregnant at the time of perioperative interruption.

aPL antibody assays

aPL testing at the Eastern Ontario Regional Laboratory Association was performed using the Bio-Rad enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) platform (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) before 2018. From 2018 onward, Eastern Ontario Regional Laboratory Association transitioned to the BioFlash chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) platform (Inova Diagnostics, San Diego, CA). Both systems were used to detect aCL IgG/IgM and aβ2GPI IgG/IgM antibodies. The antibody titers were considered positive for both ELISA and CLIA when the values exceeded a nonparametric 99th percentile threshold that was established using a reference population of healthy individuals in accordance with international consensus criteria for APS classification and testing for aPL with solid-phase assays.1,19 This 99th percentile threshold corresponded to a titer of ≥20 GPL/MPL units for ELISA and ≥20 chemiluminescent units for CLIA. LA screening was performed using a lupus-sensitive activated partial thromboplastin time (LA-sensitive aPTT) assay (PTT-SP; Instrumentation Laboratory, Bedford, MA) and a dilute Russell’s viper venom time assay (LaCHECK; Precision BioLogic, Dartmouth, NS, Canada) using a Siemens CS-2100 analyzer. LA confirmation was performed using a dilute Russell’s viper venom time-based confirmatory assay with excess phospholipid (LaSure; Precision BioLogic, Dartmouth, NS, Canada). Conversion thresholds that were established by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) Scientific and Standardization Committee on Lupus Anticoagulant/Antiphospholipid Antibodies were used to harmonize the semiquantitative interpretation of titer values across the ELISA and CLIA platforms.22 The aPL titers were categorized as low, moderate, or high using these thresholds (supplemental Table 1).

Perioperative anticoagulation management

Perioperative anticoagulation management was carried out according to local practice at the TOH Thrombosis Clinic in accordance with contemporary clinical practice guidelines.11,23 Decisions on periprocedural anticoagulation management were made by the treating physician. Procedural bleed risk classification was determined post hoc as minimal bleed risk, low/moderate bleed risk, or high bleed risk based on physician assessment and consistent with the ISTH procedural bleed risk stratification.21

VKAs were discontinued 5 days before the planned procedure and resumed on the evening of the procedure (postoperative day 0). VKAs were usually resumed using a postoperative loading dose (typically double the maintenance VKA dose for 2 days) at the discretion of the treating physician.14,24 Preoperative INR testing was routinely performed on the day before surgery, and oral vitamin K was administered to patients with an INR >1.5. The decision to use bridging anticoagulation with LMWH was at the treating physician's discretion; LMWH bridging practices typically aligned with clinical practice guideline recommendations.11 DOACs were interrupted according to the ACCP clinical practice guideline recommendations and Thrombosis Canada guidance,11,25 which are consistent with the approach used in the PAUSE study.26 DOACs were typically interrupted 1 day before low/moderate bleed–risk procedures and 2 days before high bleed–risk procedures with no dose given on the day of surgery. DOACs were resumed ∼24 hours after low/moderate bleed–risk procedures and 48 to 72 hours after high bleed–risk procedures.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were the 30-day postoperative risks of (1) ATE, (2) VTE, and (3) major bleeding. ATE included ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), and systemic embolism; VTE included pulmonary embolism and DVT (definitions are provided in supplemental Table 2). Major bleeding was defined according to the ISTH surgical major bleeding criteria.27

Secondary outcomes included the 30-day postoperative risks for clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (CRNMB), microvascular thrombotic events, CAPS, and all-cause mortality. CRNMB was defined according to the ISTH criteria.28 Microvascular thrombotic events were defined as new or recurrent small-vessel manifestations that were attributable to thrombotic APS pathophysiology (eg, digital ischemia and renal thrombotic microangiopathy), as determined by the treating physician and verified by chart review. CAPS was defined according to international consensus criteria.29 Outcomes were adjudicated in duplicate (M.P., J.R.S.) and were assessed for 30 days following the date of procedure, up to the study end date of April 14th, 2025.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and aPL characteristics were summarized on a per-patient basis, whereas procedural and clinical outcome data were analyzed on a per-interruption basis. For patients who underwent >1 perioperative interruption within 30 days, outcomes were measured leading up to the second procedure, and outcomes were then ascertained for 30 days following the second procedure. If multiple procedures occurred consecutively during the same perioperative interruption, the outcomes were ascertained for 30 days following the last procedure in that interruption period. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and proportions. Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (SDs) for normally distributed data or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for nonnormally distributed data.

Exploratory analyses were performed to evaluate procedural and clinical predictors of perioperative bridging use patterns and postoperative bleeding. Each perioperative interruption was analyzed as a distinct clinical episode, and generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a binomial family, a logit link, a robust sandwich variance estimator, and an independence working correlation structure were used to account for within-patient clustering. Given the limited number of postoperative bleeding events, associations between ISTH procedural bleed risk stratification (minimal/low/moderate vs high), urologic or gynecologic procedures (vs all other procedures), and postoperative bleeding were assessed using separate univariable GEE models. In contrast, associations between triple-positive APS status, previous ATE, and receipt of any therapeutic-dose LMWH bridging among VKA-treated patients were examined using a multivariable GEE model (because of an adequate number of bridging exposures). For paired comparisons of preoperative and postoperative bridging intensity, the analyses were limited to VKA-treated patients who received preoperative bridging, and McNemar’s test was used to evaluate changes in preoperative bridging dosing to postoperative dosing. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs); statistical significance was defined as P < .05. All analyses were exploratory and not adjusted for multiplicity. The 30-day risks for clinical outcomes were reported as proportions with corresponding 95% CIs, calculated using exact methods (Clopper-Pearson).30 All statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (version 2511) and R Statistical Software (R Core Team 2025 v4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ottawa Health Sciences Research Ethics Board. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Study population and procedure types

A total of 1792 perioperative encounters were screened for eligibility between January 2016 and April 2025 (supplemental Figure 1), and of those, 172 patients who underwent 282 perioperative interruptions met the criteria for inclusion in the analysis. The mean ± SD age of the cohort was 59.7 ± 13.9 years (Table 1), and a majority of patients were female (n = 98; 57.0%). Most patients were anticoagulated with VKAs (n = 146; 84.9%); a small proportion were prescribed a DOAC (n = 25; 14.5%). Most patients had a target INR of 2.0 to 3.0 (93.9%), whereas a minority were maintained at a higher therapeutic range of 2.5 to 3.5 (4.9%). The mean ± SD CHA2DS2-Va score (congestive heart failure, hypertension, age, diabetes, prior stroke/TIA or systemic arterial embolism, vascular disease) in patients with atrial fibrillation was 4.4 ± 1.4. Advanced chronic kidney disease (creatinine clearance <30 mL/min or dialysis) was present in 5.2% of patients, and 4 (2.3%) were on dialysis.

More than half of the patients had a history of VTE (n = 118; 68.6%) and/or ATE (n = 87; 50.6%). Perioperative anticoagulation management

VKAs were discontinued for 5 days preoperatively (last dose on postoperative day [POD] ˗6) in nearly all interruptions (95.4%; Table 4). VKAs were resumed in the evening on the day of surgery in most patients (median, POD 0; IQR, POD 0-1). Most (81.2%) restarted on POD 0, and 16.1% resumed on POD 1. A total of 21 patients (7.4%) had a preoperative INR of ≥1.5, and preoperative vitamin K was required in 3.2% of interruptions because of INR ≥1.5 on POD ˗1. Warfarin was resumed using a postoperative loading dose in 102 (43.2%) interruptions. For DOAC-treated patients (Table 4), the median duration of preoperative interruption was 72 hours (IQR, 60-72) for high bleed–risk procedures and 48 hours (IQR, 42-66) for minimal/low/moderate bleed–risk procedures. Postoperative DOAC resumption occurred at a median of 60 hours (IQR, 36-72) after high bleed–risk procedures and 36 hours (IQR, 36-36) after minimal/low/moderate bleed–risk procedures. Concurrent antiplatelet therapy was documented in 12.7% of interruptions; antiplatelet agents were continued in more than half of the interruptions that involved antiplatelet-treated patients (58.3%).

Bridging practices among VKA-treated patients are summarized in Figure 1. In terms of preoperative bridging, 90 interruptions (42.9%) involved any therapeutic-dose LMWH bridging, whereas 77 (36.7%) involved prophylactic-dose bridging and 43 (20.5%) involved no bridging. Among VKA-treated patients who received any preoperative bridging (n = 188), 35.1% received therapeutic-dose bridging preoperatively and were transitioned to prophylactic-dose postoperatively (i.e., de-escalation). Otherwise, 43.6% maintained prophylactic dosing from before to after the procedure. Only 0.5% escalated from prophylactic preoperative bridging to therapeutic intensity postoperatively, and 20.7% remained on therapeutic bridging throughout. A pairwise analysis demonstrated significantly higher odds of de-escalating bridging postoperatively (McNemar paired OR, 66.0; 95% CI, 9.1-500.0; P < .0001), reflecting substantially more transitions from preoperative therapeutic-dose bridging to postoperative prophylactic bridging (or no bridging) than the reverse.

Triple-positive patients were more likely to have received any therapeutic-dose bridging therapy than those who did not have triple positivity (72.2% vs 30.0%; OR, 7.6; 95% CI, 3.2-18.2; P < .001). Similarly, therapeutic bridging was more commonly administered for patients with a history of ATE than for those without a previous ATE (53.1% vs 27.4%; OR, 3.4; 95% CI, 1.5-7.8; P = .003).

Clinical outcomes

There were 2 postoperative ATEs, yielding a 30-day postoperative ATE risk of 0.7% (95% CI, 0.2-2.6). The first event was a TIA on POD 13 after an umbilical hernia repair (54-year-old male on warfarin, INR target of 2.0 to 3.0, no concurrent ASA, aCL positive, history of recurrent stroke/TIA) that occurred despite resuming warfarin using a loading dose and receiving postoperative LMWH prophylaxis (dalteparin 5000 IU subcutaneous [SQ] once daily until INR > 2.0; INR was 3.4 at presentation with TIA). The second event was a systemic embolism (right superficial femoral artery) on POD 2 after a facet injection (59-year-old male on warfarin, INR target of 2.0 to 3.0, taking 325 mg ASA daily, aCL positive, history of TIA) requiring catheter-directed thrombolysis on POD 5. This occurred despite resuming warfarin with a loading dose, receiving postoperative LMWH prophylaxis (dalteparin 5000 IU SQ once daily until INR > 2.0), and aspirin 325 mg daily starting on POD 1. There was 1 central line–associated upper extremity DVT (0.4%; 95% CI, 0.1-2.0) that occurred on POD 10. There were 2 major bleeding events during the 30-day follow-up period, yielding a 30-day major bleeding risk of 0.7% (95% CI, 0.2-2.6; Table 5). Both occurred in patients who underwent high bleed–risk procedures. The first event occurred following a kidney biopsy in a patient on warfarin (preoperative INR = 1.1, no concurrent antiplatelet therapy), which led to a perinephric hematoma with interval expansion on repeat ultrasound and an associated drop in hemoglobin of >20 g/L. The second event was fatal and occurred in a 46-year-old female (aCL positive, previous recurrent VTE, thrombocytopenia) following a right hemipelvectomy (high bleed–risk procedure; estimated blood loss 3.5 L) owing to nonmetastatic myxofibrosarcoma. The patient (warfarin at baseline, bridging with prophylactic fondaparinux owing to a history of injection reaction to LMWH) developed iliac artery rupture with massive bleeding on POD 8, requiring activation of a massive transfusion protocol and leading to immediate pulseless electrical activity (PEA) arrest. Despite urgent vessel ligation, right lower-limb amputation, and massive transfusion, the patient developed multiorgan failure and died on POD 17. There were 9 CRNMB events (Table 5), corresponding to a 30-day risk of 3.2% (95% CI, 1.7-6.0). Eight events occurred in patients who were anticoagulated with warfarin, and 1 occurred in a patient who was treated with rivaroxaban. High bleed–risk surgery (vs minimal/low/moderate) was associated with significantly greater odds of postoperative bleeding (composite of major bleeding + CRNMB: OR, 7.3; 95% CI, 1.5-35.4; P = .01). Similarly, undergoing a urologic or gynecologic procedure (vs all others) was also associated with substantially increased odds of postoperative bleeding (OR, 6.6; 95% CI, 1.8-24.5; P = .005).

Discussion

Data to guide the perioperative management of antithrombotic therapy for patients with thrombotic APS are limited. Herein, we report results from a single-center, retrospective cohort of anticoagulated adults with thrombotic APS who underwent a planned invasive procedure or surgery. Perioperative anticoagulation management was generally consistent with contemporary guideline-directed practice. The 30-day risks for ATE, VTE, and ISTH-defined major bleeding were low and comparable with contemporary non-APS cohorts. CAPS, a recognized concern in the setting of anticoagulation withdrawal and surgical stress, was rare. Our findings suggest that contemporary perioperative anticoagulation strategies, when applied with individualized attention to thrombotic and bleeding risk, can be safely implemented in patients with APS.

Perioperative management in our cohort followed a consistent framework that reflected both guideline-concordant practices and local practice patterns. Most VKA-treated patients received some form of parenteral bridging anticoagulation, reflecting the ACCP guideline designation of APS as a high thrombotic–risk condition for which bridging may be appropriate. Concurrent antiplatelet therapy was uncommon (∼13%), and complicating perioperative thrombocytopenia was rare (4.3%). Few patients had a higher INR target (2.5-3.5; ∼5%). Holding warfarin for 5 days before surgery led to INR normalization (INR < 1.5) in most (∼92.6%) VKA-treated patients. In a previous single-center APS surgical cohort, guideline-concordant periprocedural anticoagulation management was associated with fewer thrombotic and hemorrhagic complications (7% vs 33%; OR, 0.14; 95% CI, 0.02-0.81; P = .04), and an INR of ≤1.5 at surgery was associated with fewer major bleeding events (6% vs 29%; OR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.02-0.98; P = .05).31

The 30-day risks for major bleeding (0.7%; 95% CI, 0.2-2.6) and CRNMB (3.2%; 95% CI, 1.7-6.0) compared favorably with those of contemporary non-APS cohorts. The 30-day risks for major bleeding in the BRIDGE trial were 1.3% and 3.2% in the nonbridging and bridging arms, respectively, whereas the risks ranged from 0.9% to 1.9% in the PAUSE study depending on the DOAC in question.13,26 Importantly, in our study, major bleeding events were attributed to anatomic and procedural factors rather than anticoagulation status at bleeding onset. Most bleeding events occurred after a high bleed–risk procedure, including both of the recorded major bleeding events, and more than half of the bleeding events involved urologic or gynecologic procedures (renal biopsy, hysterectomy, transurethral resection of bladder tumor, uterine ablation). Few early bleeding events occurred during therapeutic-dose bridging; most occurred after appropriate perioperative interruption with INR values within the expected range at surgery. In contrast, delayed CRNMB events typically occurred after discontinuation of bridging during warfarin therapy with INRs within the therapeutic range (ie, not supratherapeutic), suggesting that these delayed events were not directly attributable to bridging. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of carefully considering the type of procedure (eg, urologic procedures) and postoperative bleeding risk when devising a perioperative anticoagulation management plan.

Data from non-APS populations indicate that bridging increases postoperative bleeding without clearly reducing the thromboembolism risk, but these studies enrolled patients with lower-risk indications (eg, atrial fibrillation) and may not be generalizable to APS.12,13 APS is both clinically and biologically heterogeneous.32 More than half of our cohort met Miyakis category I criteria, one-quarter were triple-positive, and moderate to high aPL titers were observed across multiple isotypes. These features typically indicate a higher thrombotic risk. Our cohort also demonstrated a substantial burden of clinical thrombotic risk factors. More than half had a previous ATE, the majority had a history of VTE, and nearly one-third had a concurrent autoimmune disease. Clinicians seemed to incorporate APS-related thrombotic risk into their perioperative decision-making. Patients with triple-positive APS and those with a previous ATE were more likely to receive therapeutic-dose LMWH bridging. Our findings suggest that a one-size-fits-all approach is unlikely to be appropriate in APS. Tailoring perioperative anticoagulation management to both APS-related thrombotic risk and procedural bleed risk seems to have been both feasible and safe in this heterogeneous, higher-risk cohort. Postoperative bridging in our cohort frequently comprised prophylactic-dose LMWH after high bleed–risk procedures; this may, in part, represent local extrapolation of postoperative dose modulation concepts that have been evaluated in other high thrombotic–risk populations (eg, patients with a mechanical heart valve),14 recognizing that therapeutic-dose anticoagulation in the immediate postoperative period is a shared driver of major bleeding risk. Given the observational design and low event counts, this practice pattern should be interpreted cautiously. Both ATE events occurred while patients were receiving postoperative LMWH prophylaxis. Microvascular events were uncommon overall (0.7%) but occurred exclusively in triple-positive patients; 1 event met the criteria for probable CAPS, which happened while the patient was receiving postoperative prophylactic LMWH. Although definitive causal inferences cannot be made, these observations, together with published case reports describing rapid, severe thrombotic events in triple-positive secondary APS (often with concomitant systemic autoimmune disease) after brief warfarin interruption despite LMWH bridging, supports considering therapeutic-dose bridging and tightly timed periprocedural anticoagulation strategies that minimize time off anticoagulation in the highest-risk phenotypes (ie, triple-positive APS and/or previous ATE).33 Conversely, for lower-risk phenotypes and/or higher bleed–risk procedures, initiating prophylactic-intensity anticoagulation early in the postoperative period with escalation as surgical hemostasis permits may offer a pragmatic balance. Prospective studies are needed to confirm optimal strategies across APS phenotypes.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it represents the largest, single-center cohort that evaluated perioperative anticoagulation management in patients with thrombotic APS. Perioperative antithrombotic strategies at our center followed relatively standardized workflows, enabling detailed characterization of warfarin interruption intervals, preoperative INR assessment, vitamin K use, LMWH bridging dose and timing, and postoperative VKA resumption practices. The outcome definitions adhered to widely recognized international standards, and the cohort encompassed a broad mix of invasive procedures with more than one-third classified as high bleed–risk procedures. TOH is the regional referral center for thrombosis, APS, and stroke, and patients with APS undergo close, frequent monitoring, including regular pharmacist-specialist contacts for VKA management. Consequently, although retrospective analyses are inherently susceptible to variable documentation and information bias, the likelihood of missing major thrombotic or bleeding outcomes is low. Important limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the measurement of aPL antibodies is itself a known challenge; our cohort spanned a period during which laboratory methodology transitioned from ELISA to CLIA platforms. Although we harmonized titers using published ISTH conversion thresholds and applied a 99th percentile positivity cutoff, some misclassification remains possible.22 Second, several patients underwent multiple perioperative interruptions, which led to more interruptions than there were patients. Although GEE models were used to account for within-patient clustering when evaluating bridging patterns and overall bleeding risk, events were too few to support multivariable modelling of clinical outcomes. Thus, the determined estimates and their CIs should be interpreted cautiously. Third, serial postoperative INR trajectories were not uniformly available across interruption episodes, precluding estimation of time spent below therapeutic range and time to reattain a therapeutic INR after warfarin resumption. Fourth, concomitant hydroxychloroquine use was not systematically captured, limiting assessment of its potential influence on perioperative thrombotic and bleeding outcomes. Finally, as a retrospective, single-center analysis, perioperative decisions, including bridging intensity, were not protocolized and event counts were low, leading to wide confidence intervals for the event estimates.

In summary, patients with thrombotic APS who underwent planned interruption of anticoagulation at our center experienced low 30-day risks for ATE, VTE, and major bleeding when managed using contemporary perioperative strategies. Clinical practice patterns reflected a risk-adapted approach. Clinicians used therapeutic-dose LMWH bridging more often in patients with triple-positive APS or a history of ATE. They used prophylactic-dose bridging more frequently after high bleed–risk procedures. Individualized management that integrates APS-related thrombotic risk, procedural bleed risk, and clinical context seems safe. The occurrence of microvascular APS manifestations, including 1 case of probable CAPS, underscores that high-risk phenotypes remain vulnerable during anticoagulation withdrawal and merit heightened vigilance. Future research should focus on multicenter prospective studies to refine risk stratification, define optimal bridging strategies across APS phenotypes, and better characterize clinical outcomes in the perioperative setting.
