Accurate identification of sickle cell disease cases in a large genotyped cohort using electronic health record data

Key Points

  • •

    Our algorithm identifies SCD with 97.6% sensitivity and 99.9% specificity using electronic health record and β-globin gene sequencing.

  • •

    The algorithm differentiates HbSS/HbSβ0 and HbSC with >93% sensitivity, supporting accurate, real-time SCD surveillance.

Visual Abstract

Abstract

The US Food and Drug Administration recently approved sickle cell disease (SCD) gene therapies that require a contemporaneous cohort to compare their outcomes with those of individuals who did not receive gene therapy. Previously, we developed an automated algorithm to create a contemporaneous cohort of children and adults with SCD using electronic health record data. We tested the hypothesis that the updated Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC) algorithm can identify individuals with SCD with sensitivity and specificity of >95%. In the VUMC health care system, we identified a cohort of 33 141 adults and children of primarily African ancestry who underwent β-globin gene sequencing, with a 1.4% prevalence of SCD (473/33 141). For SCD, the performance of the VUMC algorithm using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) and laboratory data showed a sensitivity of 97.6% (462/473), specificity of 99.9% (32 663/32 668), positive predictive value of 98.9% (462/467), and negative predictive value of 99.9% (32 663/32 674). Three SCD phenotypes account for 98.5% of the individuals in the cohort. The cohort had sensitivities of 96.8% (272/281), 93.7% (136/145), and 85% (34/40) for hemoglobin SS (HbSS)/Hb Sβ0-thalassemia (HbSβ0), HbSC, and HbSβ+, respectively. The cohort had a specificity of 99.9% across all 3 phenotypes: HbSS/HbSβ0, HbSC, and HbSβ+, respectively. We have developed an updated algorithm using ICD and laboratory data that accurately identifies individuals with SCD within a large health care system, and distinguishes HbSS/HbSβ0, HbSC, and HbSβ+.

Introduction

The surveillance of individuals with sickle cell disease (SCD) has been a goal of multiple public health initiatives, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Sickle Cell Data Collection (SCDC) program, which began in 2015.1 In 2023, the US Food and Drug Administration approved 2 gene therapies for individuals with SCD.2 However, with the approval, 2 requirements must be met: (1) all gene therapy participants receive at least 15 years of ongoing surveillance, and (2) the patients that receive gene therapy must have their clinical outcomes compared with a contemporaneous cohort that did not receive gene therapy. To address the latter requirement, we built on previous studies to develop algorithms for SCD surveillance using clinical records and claims data to create an automated, contemporaneous cohort of children and adults with SCD derived from electronic health record (EHR) data within a single health care system.3, 4, 5, 6 Such cohorts could complement the more labor-intensive work involved in developing registries, such as the Sickle Cell Disease Implementation Consortium patient registry, which has enrolled 2400 individuals with SCD.7 Previous studies of International Classification of Diseases (ICD) algorithms, including ours, were carried out in cohorts with nearly 90% SCD prevalence, an SCD prevalence not representative of the general population. For comparison, SCD prevalence is ∼0.03% nationwide in the United States, and 0.057% in the state of Tennessee.1,8 Our previous study reported a sensitivity of 93.9% and a positive predictive value (PPV) of 98.9%. Despite its strength, our study had 3 significant limitations: (1) a high SCD prevalence (99.0%), which is also why specificity and negative predictive value (NPV) were not reported; (2) the absence of β-globin gene (HBB) sequencing in individuals with and without SCD to distinguish individuals with SCD from those with sickle cell trait (SCT) and from those without either SCD or SCT; and (3) the absence of HBB genotype data in individuals with SCD to distinguish the specific types of SCD including hemoglobin SS (HbSS), Hb S-β0-thalassemia (HbSβ0), other compound heterozygous genotypes such as HbSC and HbSβ+.

To address limitations in most algorithms used to detect SCD, we extended our cohort to include adults and children receiving medical care at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC) who had samples included in whole-genome sequencing (WGS) projects. In our unique cohort of individuals with HBB genotyping, we tested the primary hypothesis that we could accurately identify an SCD cohort using EHR data with sensitivity and specificity of ≥95% (higher than in previous reports due to WGS). We tested 2 secondary hypotheses: (1) that our algorithm can accurately discriminate between HbSS/HbSβ0, HbSC, and HbSβ+ genotypes; and (2) that using ICD codes or laboratory values independently reduces the algorithm's performance when compared with the entire EHR. ICD codes increase sensitivity (capture breadth of diagnosed SCD) whereas laboratory definitions increase specificity (confirm phenotype). The combined rule maximizes balanced accuracy for surveillance and quality reporting.

Methods

Genotyped data set

We conducted an observational study using VUMC’s EHR data and genotyping of the HBB sequence as an ancillary analysis of the R01HL168179 grant-funded study of clonal hematopoiesis in SCD. VUMC’s biorepository, BioVU, contains DNA samples linked to deidentified medical records in the synthetic derivative data warehouse. Recently, VUMC participated in an initiative to perform short-read WGS on 35 024 BioVU samples from predominantly African American individuals. Before that initiative, 347 BioVU samples from individuals purported to have SCD were sequenced as part of the Genetic Variation of Heart, Lung, and Kidney Disease in Sickle Cell Disease: Pre- and Post-Curative Therapies Trans-Omics in Precision Medicine (TOPMed) project phs002617.9 This resulted in an aggregated set of 35 092 individuals with genotype and clinical data (279 with WGS data from both projects).

Using short-read WGS, we assessed the aggregated cohort for β-globin genotypes, including HbSS, HbSβ0, HbSβ+, HbSC, HbSD, HbSE, HbSO, HbSKorleBu, SCT, or no SCD, based on known variants cataloged in the Hb variant database, also included in the database of single-nucleotide polymorphism (supplemental Table 1).10,11 However, short-read WGS sequencing does not identify all β0 variants, because a substantial proportion arises from structural or regulatory variants within the HBB cluster.12 Furthermore, because hereditary persistence of fetal Hb (HbF) is defined by a functional phenotype characterized by HbF levels and HbF cellular distribution, short-read WGS alone cannot accurately identify all individuals with this phenotype. We did not include targeted structural variant analysis associated with persistent HbF.

There was no discordance in HBB genotyping results across 279 individuals from both WGS projects, supporting internal consistency across the projects, while acknowledging the known limitations of short-read sequencing for structural and regulatory variants. Individuals were excluded from phenotyping analysis if they lacked a Hb fractionation result and had <3 visits.

VUMC SCD identification algorithm

The VUMC SCD identification algorithm was applied to clinical data for this cohort, available in the synthetic derivative data warehouse, from 1 January 2004, through 31 August 2024. In brief, the algorithm predicts whether an individual is expected to have SCD by analyzing laboratory data and ICD code history. The genotype data are not included in the algorithm. The results of both the laboratory and ICD definitions are used to make the final prediction of whether a person has SCD if they either (1) have a positive laboratory case definition for SCD; or (2) have a positive ICD case definition and have no Hb fractionation results that would exclude a diagnosis of SCD, for instance, evidence of a non-SCD hemoglobinopathy (Figure 1).

VUMC SCD classification

The laboratory case definition not only identifies SCD cases, but also classifies the type of SCD, if possible (HbSS/HbSβ0, HbSS/HbSβ0 with hereditary persistence of HbF, HbSβ+, HbSC, HbSD, HbSE, SCD other, or SCD indeterminate). For SCD classification analyses, the WGS-derived genotypes for HbSS and HbSβ0 were grouped into a single category, HbSS/HbSβ0, because laboratory results cannot readily distinguish between them.13

Hereditary persistence of HbF is defined by a functional phenotype, characterized by HbF level and its pancellular vs heterocellular distribution. Although WGS can detect some sequence variants associated with hereditary persistence of fetal Hb (HbF), short-read sequencing does not reliably detect all structural or regulatory variants within the HBB cluster and does not assess functional globin expression. Consequently, WGS alone cannot accurately identify all individuals with hereditary persistence of HbF.14

The ICD case definition includes a classification method using ICD tenth revision, clinical modification (ICD-10-CM) codes that compares the frequency of ICD-10-CM codes for HbSS, HbSβ0, HbSβ+, HbSC, SCD other, or SCD nonspecific in a hierarchical schema to determine a classification of HbSS/HbSβ0, HbSβ+, or HbSC if possible (supplemental Figure 1; supplemental Tables 2 and 3). ICD-9-CM codes are not used because they are not specific enough for these classifications.

The algorithms use logic analogous to a clinician’s assessment of a complete EHR of a patient’s medical history to determine the patient's disease phenotypes. The details of the hierarchical decision-making logic describing the laboratory’s case definition, the ICD’s case definition, and full algorithms for SCD identification and classification are described in supplemental Tables 4-9.

Algorithm availability without HBB genotype and implementation environment

We collected and processed the EHR data in a Databricks notebook within a Microsoft Azure environment, using an Apache Spark 3.5.0 cluster running on Databricks Runtime 15.4 LTS with Scala 2.12. The cluster comprised a Standard_E16ds_v4 driver node with 128 gigabytes (GB) of random access memory and 16 virtual central processing units, along with 4 Standard_E8ds_v4 worker nodes, each equipped with 256 GB of random access memory and 32 virtual central processing units. This distributed computing configuration enabled efficient parallel processing and in-memory computation, optimizing performance for large-scale data analysis tasks. The notebook performs the entire process of identifying the potential SCD cohort (individuals with variant Hb laboratory results or ICD codes for hemoglobinopathies), collecting the data required to execute the complete algorithm, and then executing the algorithm.

To demonstrate the algorithm's generalizability without the HBB genotype sequence, we also downloaded the data to an encrypted 2019 MacBook Pro with a 2.6 GHz 6-core Intel Core i7 processor and 16 GB of 2667 MHz DDR4 memory. We ran the algorithm without any specialized parallel processing beyond what is inherently parallelized in the required Python packages. The Python code for implementation is freely available in a GitHub repository (https://github.com/kwuichet/VUMC_SCD_Phenotyping/).

Performance metrics (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy) were reported to 1 decimal place without rounding.

Secondary use of the TOPMed data was approved by the VUMC institutional review board. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

High sensitivity and specificity for detecting those with SCD using ICD and laboratory data

We reviewed EHR data from 1 January 2004 through 31 August 2024 for our cohort of 35 092 individuals who underwent WGS sequencing. Analyses included 33 141 individuals after excluding 1951 individuals lacking sufficient clinical data; only 6 of the excluded individuals had an SCD genotype. All denominators and event counts below pertain to the final cohort. The demographic features of the included and excluded cohorts are described in Table 1, along with the clinical history, indicating a mixed population of pediatric and adult individuals with SCD. The performance of the full VUMC algorithm using ICD and laboratory data (Table 2) showed a sensitivity of 97.6% (462/473), specificity of 99.9% (32 663/32 668), PPV of 98.9% (462/467), and NPV of 99.9% (32 663/32 674).

Among individuals predicted to have SCD by the algorithm, 5 of 467 (1.1%) were false positives, meaning the clinically assigned phenotype was not confirmed to have SCD after HBB genotyping. A comprehensive analysis of those 5 individuals showed that 3 of those individuals were identified as SCD in our laboratory-only case definition. Each individual had multiple laboratory values definitive for SCD (high HbS levels of >60%, absent or negligible HbA levels, elevated reticulocytes, and low mean corpuscular volume), suggesting that there may have been a genotyping misclassification or other error, given the absence of the HbS allele in these 3 cases. Based on genotyping and chart review, the other 2 false positives had SCT. In the SCT subset, 2063 of 2065 (99.9%) were correctly predicted not to have SCD.

Among individuals predicted not to have SCD based on the algorithm, 11 of 32 674 (0.03%) were false negatives. Namely, individuals in this group were predicted not to have SCD but were confirmed to have SCD by genotyping. These individuals were missed by the algorithm primarily due to limited clinical data availability (supplemental Table 10) that manifested in 3 ways: (1) 7 individuals with moderate visit history (≥12 visits) but limited or inconsistent ICD coding (ie, codes for SCT or missing codes for SCD in most visits) and absent or indeterminate laboratory results; (2) 3 individuals with the minimal visit history (only 3 visits) to be included and absent or indeterminate laboratory results; and (3) 1 individual with insufficient visit history (<3 visits) and laboratory results more supportive of SCT. False negatives had fewer visits, whereas false-positive results had more.

Overall, only 16 of 33 141 (0.05%) individuals exhibited genotype/phenotype discordance that may reflect incorrect data-sample linkage, sequencing-related factors, both, or neither, when using the full VUMC algorithm with laboratory and ICD data. We recognize that not all the data used in the full algorithm may be readily obtainable at other sites (eg, blood transfusion and hematopoietic stem cell transplant history), so we examined the performance of the full algorithm when less laboratory data, defined as no reticulocyte, blood transfusion, or hematopoietic stem cell transplant data were used in addition to evaluating the independent performances of the laboratory and ICD case definitions. Using limited laboratory data in the full algorithm resulted in a difference of <1% in the algorithm's performance metrics for identifying SCD cases (supplemental Table 11), with only 22 of 33 141 (0.07%) instances of genotype/phenotype discordance. The ICD case definition maintains high performance (>94%) across all metrics despite a higher number of false positives and false negatives, with 47 of 33 141 (0.14%) of individuals showing genotype/phenotype discordance. The laboratory case definition exhibits reduced sensitivity (413/473 [87.3%]) because not all individuals have confirmatory laboratory data in the VUMC EHR. This results in more false negatives, with 63 of 33 141 (0.19%) instances of genotype/phenotype discordance total. The full performance results for the WGS cohort, with and without exclusions, are presented in supplemental Tables 11 and 12, respectively.

High sensitivity and specificity for classifying HbSS/HbSβ0 and HbSC

For the SCD classification results, we compared the HBB genotype with the SCD classification results from the complete VUMC SCD algorithm using both laboratory and ICD data (Table 2). We found the best performance in predicting the HbSS/HbSβ0 group with a sensitivity of 96.8% (272/281), specificity of 99.9% (32 848/32 860), PPV of 95.7% (272/284), and NPV of 99.9% (32 848/32 857; Table 2). The performance of HbSC cohort identification was also robust, with a sensitivity of 93.7% (136/145), a specificity of 99.9% (32 995/32 996), a PPV of 99.2% (136/137), and an NPV of 99.9% (32 995/33 004). Identification of the HbS+ cohort showed reduced sensitivity (85.0%) compared with HbSS/HbSβ0 and HbSC, but specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy were all >97% (Table 2).

The genotyped cohort included 7 SCD individuals with rare compound heterozygous genotypes: 3 HbSKB, 2 HbSE, and 2 HbSO. Analyses of rare genotypes (eg, HbSE, HbSO, HbSKB) are descriptive only. The 2 HbSE individuals were correctly classified as SCD other (eg, SCD other than HbSS/HbSβ0, HbSC, or HbSβ+). Of 3 HbSKB individuals, 2 could not be classified with a specific type, and the other was not identified as having SCD by the identification portion of the algorithm and was therefore not classified. One HbSO individual could not be classified with a specific type, and the other was misclassified as HbSC due to a likely laboratory misclassification because HbO migrates similarly to HbC under certain electrophoresis conditions.

When the laboratory and ICD case definitions are used independently rather than together in the full algorithm, there is typically an increase in false negatives (Table 2). When only limited laboratory data are used, the sensitivity of the classification of HbSS/HbSβ0 drops to 90.3% (254/281), and the HbSC and HbSβ+ sensitivities change by <3% (supplemental Table 11). Additional effects of using limited laboratory or ICD data sets on the performance of SCD classification methods are described in supplemental Table 4.

Limited clinical data availability reduces algorithm performance

Given that some EHR data sets may be limited by changes in EHR software, such as the switch at VUMC to the Epic EHR system in 2017, we examined how well the algorithm performed when only the last 5 years of data were available. In this analysis, we found a large drop in sensitivity (62.7%), whereas the other performance metrics were >98% (supplemental Table 11). However, in the switch in EHR systems at VUMC, the legacy laboratory data were imported into the new EHR system, and we repeated the analysis using laboratory results from 2004 through 2024, and limited all other data (eg, diagnoses codes, encounters, transfusions) to the last 5 years. After the legacy laboratory results were implemented, the sensitivity increased to 94.0%, and all remaining metrics exceeded 99% (supplemental Table 11).

Improvement in the sensitivity of detecting SCD levels off after ∼7 years of EHR data

Given the effects on performance when using the recent data set from the last 5 years, we further examined how the duration of longitudinal data availability affected the sensitivity and specificity of the full laboratory and ICD algorithm, the laboratory case definition alone, and the ICD case definition alone. We incrementally censored the data, allowing only 1, 2, 3, etc, years of data after the first encounter date to be used in the algorithm. An increase in sensitivity began to level off after 7 years of available data, whereas the specificity remained relatively unchanged with increasing years of available data (Figure 2).

The VUMC ICD case definition shows improved performance compared with the SCDC ICD definition for the identification of SCD cases

The SCDC program of the Centers for Disease for the surveillance of SCD uses an ICD-based administrative claims definition in which a person is predicted to have SCD if they have ≥3 SCD-coded encounters in 5 years.1,5 When we applied this definition to our data set and compared it with our ICD case definition, we found that our method outperformed all metrics, with the SCDC method achieving 89.8% PPV compared with 95.5% for the VUMC ICD case definition (supplemental Table 11). Additionally, the PPV performance of the SCDC definition shows a greater decrease with increasing years of available data than that of the VUMC ICD case definition (supplemental Figure 1).

Efficient identification of a large SCD cohort

The genotyped cohort included 35 092 individuals, but only 2866 met the minimal data requirement for hemoglobinopathy analysis, defined as having at least 1 variant Hb type in laboratory results or at least 1 ICD code for hemoglobinopathies. Without laboratory values or ICD indicators for hemoglobinopathies, an individual could not be predicted to have SCD.

Our entire set of analyses took a total time of <20 minutes to (1) identify the 2866 cases with the minimal data needed for hemoglobinopathy analysis, (2) collect the data for that set, (3) run the Python implementation of the algorithm, and (4) save the results. Most of the time was spent collecting data and saving results, because the algorithm ran for less than a minute.

Our genotyped data set represents only a small portion of individuals seen at VUMC. We also assessed the efficiency of our algorithm on the entire synthetic derivative warehouse, which contains clinical data for 3 965 634 individuals in the VUMC health care system. In this expanded cohort, the analyses took a total of 15 minutes to run all processes to (1) identify the 24 366 with the minimal data needed for hemoglobinopathy analysis, (2) collect the data for that set, (3) run the Python implementation of the algorithm, and (4) save the results. Most of the time was spent identifying the analysis cohort and collecting the data, because the final step of running the algorithm took only 1.5 minutes despite evaluating a nearly 10-times larger cohort. From this set, we identified 1280 predicted SCD cases within the VUMC health care system, and all 468 previously identified predicted SCD cases in our genotyped data set were also found in this expanded SCD set.

We downloaded the data from the 24 366 cohort to an encrypted laptop, and the algorithm took only 2.2 minutes to run, supporting that the algorithm itself is not prohibitively computationally intensive and does not require a high-performance local computer or cloud environment.

Discussion

Administrative data sets that combine ICD-9 and ICD-10 coding with laboratory values are increasingly used to reconstruct the clinical history of SCD.1,3,5,8,15,16 However, accurate identification of patients with SCD is a critical first step toward enabling longitudinal outcome studies, and these analyses are constrained by the absence of the gold-standard HBB sequence to identify individuals with and without SCD, definitively. To our knowledge, this is the first study to have validated the results of an SCD identification algorithm using HBB sequencing data. Among individuals with SCD, our algorithm accurately identifies individuals with HbSS/HbSβ0 and, to a lesser degree, other heterozygous SCD phenotypes using laboratory and ICD coding data.

Previous efforts to use clinical data in conjunction with ICD data to identify individuals with HbSS/HbSβ0 have been limited primarily to SCD, rather than to other compound SCD heterozygotes.4 However, to our knowledge, the VUMC algorithm is the first to identify multiple SCD genotypes, including HbSC and HbSβ+, in addition to HbSS/HbSβ0, in an automated fashion rather than relying heavily on manual review. Furthermore, we show that ICD codes do not achieve the same performance as laboratory data in predicting SCD genotype, but that combining laboratory and ICD data yields the highest accuracy. Our study establishes a framework for quality improvement projects and distinguishes individuals with HbSS/HbSβ0 from those with HbSC, which may inform evidence-based treatment guidelines.17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Our automated approaches will help improve local, regional, and national efforts to follow this population and monitor adherence to treatment guidelines.

A distinguishing feature of our algorithm is its computational efficiency and scalability, enabling informatics and SCD teams to assess its applicability across health care systems, including multihospital networks spanning states for quality-control assessments. Once implemented, the algorithm can identify individuals with SCD from large data sets in <3 minutes, once the data are collected. Here, we identified a SCD cohort at VUMC that was half the size of the Sickle Cell Disease Implementation Consortium registry's enrolled cohort (1280 vs 2400), with high accuracy and minimal manual effort. For optimal performance, defined by maximizing sensitivity and specificity, at least 7 years of EHR data should be available, and data quality is the most critical performance factor for identifying individuals with SCD.

At VUMC, ICD codes and laboratory data for patient encounters are readily available. Still, we provide alternative implementation strategies that maintain high performance even when laboratory data are unavailable. Although the ICD-only or laboratory-only case definitions performed less accurately than the combined ICD and laboratory approach, their performance remains sufficient for specific-use cases. For example, in health care settings in which laboratory data are unavailable, the ICD-based definition provides a viable alternative. Conversely, when it is more important that the identified cohort comprises only confirmed individuals with SCD rather than a larger cohort that may include some misclassified SCD predictions, the laboratory-based definition is preferable. Our ICD-based definition outperforms the SCDC claims definition used for surveillance in multiple states and maintains consistent performance as more years of data are included, supporting its usefulness for ongoing longitudinal outcomes studies.

As expected, our algorithm has limitations. First, its development benefited from the extensive, structured longitudinal patient data available at our SCD Center of Excellence, in which pediatric and adult hematologists have collaborated for >14 years. As a result, many individuals at VUMC have multiple recorded encounters, enhancing phenotype differentiation within the EHR. Additionally, our WGS cohort of individuals predominantly with African American ancestry shows a 1.4% prevalence of SCD, which is ∼5 times higher than the 1 in 365 prevalence of SCD among African American newborns in the United States.22 This high prevalence reflects the referral pattern of our VUMC SCD Center of Excellence. Given that both PPV and NPV depend on prevalence, predictive values may differ across health care systems with lower SCD prevalence, even when sensitivity and specificity are similar to those reported here.

Our algorithm has not been validated across diverse settings, particularly in smaller SCD populations and other health care systems. However, our model incorporated an extensive tertiary-care and community-based health care system, because the VUMC EHR includes data from community clinics in addition to the main hospital system. Future work will be required to assess the algorithm’s test characteristics in settings with limited knowledge of SCD management, limited knowledge of managing individuals with SCD, a small number of individuals with SCD, or all of these characteristics. We have made the Python code for implementing the algorithms freely available via a GitHub repository (https://github.com/kwuichet/VUMC_SCD_Phenotyping/) to support future validation studies. GitHub is a publicly accessible, web-based platform for version-controlled software repositories that enables transparent sharing and reuse of research code.

Our enhanced VUMC algorithm demonstrates high accuracy (sensitivity and specificity of >95%) and efficiency when identifying individuals with SCD within large health care system EHRs. Importantly, our algorithm can distinguish between HbSS/HbSβ0 and HbSC and HbSβ+genotypes, facilitating improved cohort surveillance and clinical decision-making for SCD phenotype-specific care.
