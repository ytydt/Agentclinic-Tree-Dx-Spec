Abstract

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited red blood cell disorder with significant complications, including vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC), the most common cause of emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalizations. Pain in children with SCD is often underestimated. We evaluated the impact of a standardized institutional nurse–initiated ED SCD pain protocol, using intranasal (IN) fentanyl (INF) as the initial agent, on hospital admission rates. We conducted a preintervention/postintervention study of patients with SCD (aged 0-21 years) presenting to a quaternary pediatric ED for uncomplicated VOC pain from June 2015 to December 2019. Overall, 162 patients accounted for 471 ED visits in the preintervention period and 80 patients accounted for 162 visits in the postintervention period. After intervention, hospitalization rates (P = .0017) and inpatient length of stay (P = .0019) decreased, but median pain scores at presentation (P = .0047) and discharge (P = .1451) remained comparable. The time to initial analgesia dose significantly improved in the postintervention (P < .0001), along with the time from physician order to initial analgesia administration (P = .0005). IV and IN analgesia use rose significantly in the postintervention (odds ratio, 4.7; P < .0001), with INF being the preferred analgesic agent (61%) and a corresponding reduction in oral analgesic use (P < .0001). In an ad hoc sustainability analysis, INF use remained high and time to first dose was sustained. Implementing a standardized ED SCD pain protocol improved timeliness and consistency in analgesia administration and decreased overall hospitalization rates. This study also highlights the role of INF as an effective initial analgesic within standardized ED protocols for pediatric VOC.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited red blood cell disorder affecting >100 000 individuals in the United States and millions globally.1, 2, 3 SCD is linked to many potentially devastating complications that occur secondary to vaso-occlusion. The underlying pathophysiology of the disease involves rigid polymers of deoxygenated hemoglobin which damage the red blood cell membrane,4 subsequently activating various abnormal cell-signaling pathways that lead to tissue hypoxia and ischemia.5, 6, 7 This is thought to occur primarily in postcapillary venules, resulting in acute, episodic pain, which is the most common complication found in SCD.8,9 These severe acute pain episodes or vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC) can evolve with distinct phases characterized by an initial prodromal crescendo and a final resolution phase lasting from a few days to several weeks.8,10

Acute VOC accounts for >75% of emergency department (ED) visits and a significant number of annual hospitalizations for individuals with SCD, making the ED a key setting for SCD management.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 The time to initial dose of opioid analgesia is an important quality-of-care metric for the effective management of acute VOC pain.18 The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI)19 and the American Society of Hematology20 published recommendations in 2014 and 2020, respectively, for the management of VOC pain which focus on an all-encompassing approach to patient analgesia. This includes recommendations for triage levels, initiation of analgesia within 60 minutes of patient arrival to the ED, and preferred initial opioid dosing. These guidelines also recommend focusing on the patient's pain perception and emphasize repeat pain assessments in determining disposition.19,20 Vital signs and laboratory evaluation are not reliable measurements of the individual pain experience in SCD,17,21 and thus, diagnosis and management should rely heavily on patient-reported pain and symptoms.22 Despite the availability of effective analgesic options and evidence supporting timely pain management, significant gaps remain in the quality of care delivered to patients with SCD during acute VOC pain episodes,20,23, 24, 25, 26, 27 which contribute to negative patient experiences in the ED.8,28, 29, 30

These gaps are compounded by systemic challenges, including health care provider biases and inconsistencies in adherence to clinical guidelines. Studies of children with SCD demonstrate 2 to 3 times the expected wait time for the first dose of analgesia.31,32 Furthermore, analgesia dosing for the treatment of acute pain is often inadequate.33, 34, 35 Any perceived incongruence by providers of objective findings, such as vital sign changes or laboratory markers with patient-reported pain scores, can ignite suspicion regarding the authenticity of the pain,10 with providers often overestimating the risk of opioid-related substance use disorder despite low prevalence in SCD.36,37 This is exacerbated by limited ED provider education regarding SCD and care recommendations.38 A prior study evaluated our institution's ED pain management practice for sickle cell VOC by assessing provider awareness of these 2014 NHLBI guidelines for pain management and found discordance between provider comfort level and knowledge of management guidelines.26

Furthermore, interventions that combined standardized analgesia protocols with provider training have demonstrated improvements in both clinical outcomes and provider adherence to national guidelines for pain management in SCD. Treadwell et al39 were among the first to advocate for nurse-initiated analgesia protocols setting the stage for subsequent studies. Opioids, such as morphine, fentanyl, and hydromorphone, are frequently used agents in the ED for acute pain management in SCD.33,40,41 Prior studies, primarily in adults, have demonstrated that implementing pain management protocols using these agents can reduce time to initial analgesia, improve compliance with pain assessments, optimize patient disposition, and, in some cases, decrease hospitalization rates.42, 43, 44, 45, 46 The impact of similar protocols in children and adolescents using intranasal (IN) fentanyl has been found to decrease the time to initial analgesia in pediatric patients with SCD.31,32,47 However, these benefits remain underused in pediatric settings, and implementation remains inconsistent across institutions. Persistent barriers are evidenced by high health care utilization and provider-driven variability in care patterns that continue to influence clinical outcomes.48,49 Given these challenges, we hypothesized that the multidisciplinary development and implementation of a standardized pain protocol that incorporates the use of IN fentanyl (INF) would improve pain management in children and adolescents with SCD in the ED.

Methods

We conducted a preintervention/postintervention study to evaluate the impact of a standardized pain protocol in pediatric patients with SCD between ages 0 and 21 years old presenting to the Riley Hospital for Children ED for evaluation of VOC pain. A retrospective electronic medical record (EMR) chart review was performed between June 2015 and May 2018, preceding protocol implementation in June 2018. Clinical data were collected prospectively from January to December 2019 following protocol implementation. In addition, to assess long-term intervention sustainability, we collected a 1-year post hoc sample from January to December 2024.

We included patients with a confirmed diagnosis of SCD evaluated in the ED for uncomplicated VOC in our analysis. SCD diagnosis was verified through a combination of EMR problem list documentation using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, codes and review of hematology clinic notes with genotype documentation. Uncomplicated VOC was defined as pain attributable to SCD without an alternative diagnosis for pain or acute complication. We excluded patients with SCD and VOC complicated by other acute SCD-related complications where additional interventions may be prioritized or overlapped, such as for fever, acute chest syndrome, or stroke.

The primary outcome of the study was the time from ED check-in to the first dose of analgesia. Secondary outcomes included hospital admission rates, length of stay (LOS), and a further analysis of the appropriateness of analgesia selection, number of analgesia doses, and pain scores. Data collected included age, sex, sickle cell genotype, frequency of pain score reassessment, patient-reported pain scores, analgesia dose, and timing of analgesia dosing. Pain scores were collected from the pain assessment section of the EMR using a 0 to 10 numerical pain score assessment. This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board.

The intervention

The institutional standardized SCD ED pain protocol was developed by a multidisciplinary, collaborative team consisting of hematologists, ED physicians, pharmacists, and nursing managers. The protocol was written based on the 2014 NHLBI recommendations and used INF as the initial analgesic agent due to emerging evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of INF in pediatric SCD pain management. An assessment of ED provider knowledge performed during this time helped inform implementation.26 This team met quarterly in the year preceding implementation to review current ED workflows and potential barriers to timely analgesia administration. These iterative discussions informed the protocol development, which emphasized rapid triage alongside early and timely initial analgesia dosing. This nurse-initiated algorithm triggered the rapid triaging of patients with SCD and a direct conversation with an ED provider to place an order for the initial analgesia dose. In addition, it provided guidance on the timing between check-in, triage, pain assessments, analgesia administration, and recommendations for when to contact the hematology team. The final protocol was approved by ED and Hematology leadership and integrated into an institutional algorithm (supplemental Figure 1) and a corresponding EMR order set.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were summarized as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations for continuous variables. A generalized estimating equation (GEE) model was used to model the outcomes accounting for repeated measures per subject. For pain scores, a GEE model was fit with intervention time point, admit/discharge status, and administration route. Various interactions were tested, but only the interaction between the time point and administration route was kept. Continuous variables were modeled as normal distribution with identity link, binary variables were modeled as a binomial distribution with logit link, and count variables were modeled as a Poisson distribution with log link. Least-square means were used to test for significant differences in time between all 3 time periods. SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC) was used for all analyses. All tests were considered significant at P < .05.

Results

Demographics

In the preintervention period, 162 unique patients accounted for a total of 471 ED encounters. After intervention, there were 80 unique patients with 162 ED encounters for uncomplicated VOC. The mean age at presentation was higher in the postintervention cohort (pre vs post, 9.2 ± 5.9 years vs 11.1 ± 5.5 years; P < .0001) (Table 1). Sex was similar among the groups (P = .2703), and the genotypes of both cohorts were representative of typical epidemiological data, with most having HbSS disease (pre vs post, 66% vs 65%; P = .8271) (Table 1).

LOS

The average ED LOS (minutes) was similar both preintervention and postintervention (mean difference, 2.715 minutes; P = .7711) (Table 2). The odds of hospital admission significantly decreased from 67% preintervention to 52% postintervention (odds ratio [OR], 0.5412; P = .0017). There was no difference in the probability of admission between patients receiving IV vs IN analgesia as the initial agent in the preintervention (P = .6757) vs postintervention period (P = .6944). Inpatient LOS (days) significantly decreased from 4 to 3 days in the postintervention cohort (mean difference, −1.02 days; P = .0019) (Table 2). The percentage of 24- and 72-hour return rates across both cohorts was low (24 hours, pre 1.3% vs post 1.5%; 72 hours, pre 3.5% vs post 4.4%).

Pain scores

The initial pain score at presentation was significantly higher in the post group (mean difference, 0.8692; P = .0047), but the final pain score documented before ED discharge was not significantly different between pre and post (mean difference, 0.7880; P = .1451) (Table 2). The average time from ED check-in to first documented pain score was not significantly different between pre and post (mean difference, −1.1432; P = .4485) (Table 2). Preintervention, 17% of patients had no initial pain score documented, compared with 11% in the postintervention period. In the preintervention period, every instance of pain scores not being documented was for patients who received oral (PO) analgesia. The odds of a pain score being documented in the postintervention group were 39.5 times higher for the IV/IN route patients (OR, 39.5; 95% confidence interval [CI], 21.0-74.3; P < .0001) compared with the PO group.

Analgesia

Time from ED check-in to first analgesia dose (mean difference, −23.1822 minutes; P < .0001) (Table 2; Figure 1) and time from physician order to first analgesia dose (mean difference, −6.2326; P = .0005) were significantly lower in the postintervention cohort (Table 2). Although there was no significant difference in time between analgesia doses 1 and 2 from pre to post (mean difference, −1.9125; P = .7632), there was a significant difference in time between analgesia doses 2 and 3 from pre to post (mean difference, −22.6404; P = .0036) (Table 2; Figure 1). The number of doses to disposition decision significantly increased from pre to post by a factor of 1.2816 (P < .0001), aligning with the protocol standard of 3 doses postintervention (Table 2).

IV/IN analgesia was used initially in 69% of preintervention encounters, with morphine being the preferred analgesic (31%), followed by hydromorphone (16%). The OR of IV/IN use vs no IV/IN was significantly higher postintervention (OR, 4.7; 95% CI, 2.3-9.6; P < .0001). Postintervention, IV/IN analgesia use increased to 91%, with INF being the preferred initial analgesic agent (61%), followed by IV morphine (14%). The odds of receiving INF vs no INF increased significantly postintervention (OR, 11.1; 95% CI, 7.1-17.5; P < .0001). The time to first analgesia dose was significantly shorter for IN compared with IV opioids (mean difference, 17.7 minutes; P < .0001) (Figure 2). In addition, the odds of receiving any parenteral opioids within 60 minutes of ED check-in nearly doubled in the postintervention vs preintervention period (OR, 1.97; 95% CI, 1.21-3.20; P = .0061). Consequently, PO analgesic use decreased (pre vs post, 29% vs 9%; OR, 0.2; 95% CI, 0.1-0.5; P < .0001).

Paired analysis

A total of 54 subjects were reflected in both the preintervention and postintervention cohorts and compared in a subanalysis of the last ED visit before intervention to the first ED visit postintervention (Table 3). Admission rates significantly decreased in the postintervention cohort (pre vs post: 70.4% vs 50%; P = .0116), and the median number of analgesia doses increased (pre vs post: 2 [1-3] doses vs 3 [2-4] doses; P = .0028) (Table 4). The average time between ED check-in and the first analgesia dose was significantly lower postintervention (pre vs post: 82.8 ± 65.6 vs 49.9 ± 29.8 minutes; P = .0007). There was no difference in the average ED LOS (pre vs post, 314.89 ± 133.46 minutes vs 309.78 ± 140.64 minutes; P = .8389) or inpatient average LOS (pre vs post, 3.7 ± 3 days vs 3.7 ± 2.7 days; P = .5217) (Table 4).

Sustainability period

Recognizing that our postintervention cohort preceded the COVID-19 pandemic, we conducted a retrospective post hoc analysis of 2024 data to evaluate the sustainability of our intervention. Between January and December 2024, 82 unique patients represented 141 encounters, with similar demographic characteristics to the postintervention cohort (Table 1). The median time from ED check-in to first dose of analgesia was sustained at 57 minutes (P < .0001) with continued use of INF as the initial analgesic agent (57%; P < .0001) (Figure 2). The interval between doses 1 and 2 was significantly shorter in the sustainability period compared with both preintervention (mean difference, 17.6 minutes; P = .0076) and postintervention (mean difference, 16.4 minutes; P = .0096).

Admission rates rose compared with the postintervention period (63% vs 51%) but remained lower than preintervention (66%; P = .0116). ED LOS was significantly longer than both the preintervention (P < .0001) and postintervention periods (P < .0001). Frequency of revisits was low (24 hours: 4% [n = 4] vs 72 hours: 9.2% [n = 9]).

Across all periods, patients receiving parenteral opioids were more likely to receive their first dose within 60 minutes of ED check-in compared with those started on PO analgesics (P = .0352). Admission rates did not differ significantly between IV and IN opioids as the initial analgesic agent (pre, P = .6757; post, P = .6944; sustainability, P = .7166).

Discussion

The acute nature of many SCD complications, including VOC, positions the ED as an everyday setting where acute pain management of this patient population occurs. Acute pain,50 distinct from chronic pain,51 necessitates the prompt and appropriate use of opioids as outlined by national clinical pain guidelines for SCD.19,20 The time between ED presentation and the initial dose of analgesia is a key metric for effective VOC management.18 However, provider understanding of SCD VOC management is often not congruent with current guidelines, irrespective of self-reported comfort level in managing VOC.26 This highlights the importance of the results of this study, which demonstrated the impact a standardized institutional ED pain management protocol can have on inconsistent initial analgesia choice, prolonged time to medication administration, and high admission rates in children and adolescents with SCD.

Previous studies have revealed that the utilization of INF can be advantageous in acute VOC management to minimize time to the initial analgesia dose and increase the likelihood of ED discharge.31,32,47 Randomized controlled trials in adult52 and pediatric53 patients with SCD have demonstrated that INF is a safe and effective alternative to IV morphine and placebo, respectively, for acute VOC pain management. A quality improvement study by Kavanagh et al31 reported delays from ED check-in to initial analgesia dose similar to those in our study. The implementation of a standardized protocol using INF as the initial analgesic agent at their center increased adherence to the NHLBI guideline standards from 41% to 75%, correlating with an increase in INF selection as the initial analgesia agent from 39% to 75%.31 In addition, in a multicenter cross-sectional study, Rees et al47 found a ninefold greater adjusted odds of ED discharge for 75 children with SCD in whom INF was used as the initial agent. Similarly, in our postintervention cohort, increased use of INF as the preferred initial analgesic agent resulted in an overall increase in IV/IN use from 69% to 91%, which likely contributed to improvement in time to first analgesia dose and hospitalization rates. A subanalysis of 54 patients captured in both the preintervention vs postintervention analysis reaffirmed these findings. The reduction in time from physician order to initial analgesia administration further highlights improved efficiency in the pain management process, ensuring faster symptom relief, which likely contributed to lower hospital admission rates and improved timing between subsequent analgesia doses.

The impact of standardized pain protocols on pain scores is variable in the literature, with some studies not seeing an effect on patient-reported pain scores54 or hospitalization rates55,56 despite overall improvements in management. Tanabe et al,57 however, found that nurse-initiated standardized protocols did improve pain scores in adult patients despite no change in time to initial analgesia. Although our study did not find a difference in overall pain scores postintervention, we did find a statistically significant improvement in pain scores when comparing encounters where IV/IN therapy was used vs PO therapy. This was an important finding, as there was a 20% reduction in patient encounters receiving initial analgesia orally in the postintervention period, despite a similar severity in initial pain scores observed in the preintervention and postintervention cohorts. In addition, the reduction in PO analgesia use postintervention in our study was associated with improved compliance with initial pain score documentation from 83% to 89%.

The use of individualized pain management plans in the ED has also been found to enhance the quality of care for patients with SCD, leading to increased patient satisfaction and reduced hospital admissions.54,58, 59, 60, 61, 62, 63 The ESCAPED study, the first prospective study to demonstrate differences in outcomes between acute care settings (ED vs infusion centers) in managing uncomplicated VOC in adults with SCD, provides additional context for care optimization.64 The authors found that infusion centers, compared with the ED, were even more impactful in aligning with the 2014 NHLBI guidelines regarding time to initial analgesia dose and frequency of pain reassessments. However, this may not be a consistent or reliable option for centers without adequate infusion space or pediatric centers with shared infusion centers. Furthermore, maintaining up-to-date individualized plans and protocols can be challenging due to the dynamic and evolving nature of patient needs and the additional workload imposed on medical teams, emphasizing the important role of standardized ED pain protocols.

Despite the improvements found in the postintervention period, the overall average ED LOS in our study was not significantly different, regardless of the number of doses administered before disposition. This suggests that there was a considerable amount of time spent in the ED that is not accounted for by analgesia dosing and may reflect potential delays in repeat assessments and the discharge process that still need to be addressed. ED overcrowding and staffing limitations have long been recognized as a barrier to timely care delivery in adult populations and, more recently, in pediatric settings.65, 66, 67 These systemic challenges compounded by increasing patient complexity can disrupt patient flow and discharge processes. As a result, even with the implementation of standardized pain management protocols, the overall effectiveness of such interventions may be diminished due to extended ED LOS.65

Our sustainability analysis provides further context. The time to initial analgesia consistently remained under 60 minutes with INF continuing to serve as the predominant initial analgesic agent. The absence of differences between IV and IN routes when evaluating time between doses 1 and 2 and admission rates (regardless of whether the initial analgesic was given IN or IV) indicates that reductions in hospitalizations were more likely driven by protocol adherence and standardization of care rather than the specific choice of initial analgesic agent. INF functioned effectively as a bridge while IV access was being established, providing rapid relief without subsequent delays to IV opioid administration. Notably, 24- and 72-hour revisit rates remained low in the postintervention and sustainability periods, suggesting decreased admission rates compared with preintervention did not result in higher early return visits. However, absolute numbers were small, limiting interpretation. Despite initial improvements in the immediate postintervention period, hospitalization rates and ED LOS increased in the sustainability phase. Protocol adherence remained high, with timely analgesia, consistent INF use, and dosing consistency maintained. This pattern points to other system- or patient-level factors not captured in our study as potential contributors to these later trends (eg, staffing turnover, disease severity) rather than loss of protocol adherence.

We acknowledge that a limitation of our study is the inability to discern the individual contributions of the implementation of a standardized protocol, INF as the initial analgesic agent, and recurrent provider education in achieving the primary outcomes. Although all these factors likely played substantial roles, the implementation of the protocol itself was an instrumental driver in achieving improvements in the primary outcomes of reduced time to initial analgesia and lower admission rates. Our sustainability data are also likely affected by programmatic changes in the 5-year period following intervention, including the establishment of a daytime infusion room, which likely influenced patient flow and care decisions between infusion room and ED management.

Acute care settings, such as the ED, play an important role in the management of individuals living with SCD. Our study highlights the inconsistencies in care processes, including prolonged times between analgesia dosing, for acute VOC management in children and adolescents with SCD. These issues can potentially be addressed through the use of standardized, evidence-based protocols with INF in the ED alongside provider education on pain management guidelines and the importance of timely analgesia administration. Moreover, integrating patient-centered metrics such as satisfaction, perceived discrimination, and trust in providers could help guide protocol refinement and ensure more equitable care. Future studies will assess the impact of collaborative, standardized pain protocols on cost, patient-reported outcomes, satisfaction, and continued evaluation of protocol adherence.
