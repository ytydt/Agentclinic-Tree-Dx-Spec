Abstract

The incidence of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) increases with older age. Thus, subgroup reporting for older adults in clinical trials is necessary to determine the relevance of novel therapy outcomes to this vulnerable population. This study assessed subgroup reporting of older adults (aged ≥65 years) enrolled in US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency registration trials for DLBCL between 2017 and 2023. Clinical efficacy end points for each trial were extracted from ClinicalTrials.gov registry data and study protocols. Primary and secondary publications reporting on included studies were identified through a systematic review process within Covidence. Ten therapies with 12 indications were approved between 2017 and 2023. Ninety-two publications were included for data extraction. The availability and completeness of older adult subgroup data relating to protocol-defined efficacy end points, as well as data relating to baseline patient characteristics, health-related quality-of-life outcomes, and toxicity outcomes, were assessed according to predefined criteria. Of 12 primary end points, 10 (83.4%) were reported completely, 1 (8.3%) was reported partially, and 1 (8.3%) was not reported among older subgroups. Of 66 secondary end points, 25 (37.9%) were completely reported, 6 (9.1%) were partially reported, and 35 (53.1%) were unreported. Baseline characteristics, health-related quality-of-life outcomes, and toxicities outcomes among older adults were mostly unreported. This analysis highlights a need for improvement in older adult subgroup reporting in DLBCL trials, particularly among secondary end points.

Introduction

The incidence of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is rising with increasing age.1 The median age at diagnosis is in the mid-sixties, and 55% of patients are aged ≥65 years at that time.2 Although the incidence rates in the United States were stable over the last 2 decades, an increase can be expected with an aging population demographically.1 Although ∼60% of patients are cured by first-line treatment, 10% to 15% are refractory to first-line treatment, and up to 25% experience disease relapse.2 Due to comorbidities and frailty, not all older adults are eligible to receive standard-of-care approaches. This particularly pertains to high-dose chemotherapy with sequential autologous stem cell transplantation for relapsed disease, which is more toxic with older age.3 Therefore, there is a strong unmet need for well-tolerated and efficacious treatment approaches, particularly in the setting of relapsed and refractory disease.

The treatment landscape of DLBCL has changed substantially over the last decade due to the approval of chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapies, the T-cell–engaging bispecific antibodies glofitamab and epcoritamab, and the drug-coupled antibodies polatuzumab and loncastuximab. The unique toxicity patterns associated with these therapies raise important considerations regarding their risk–benefit profile in older patients who may profit disproportionally compared with the younger population.4 Additionally, because several of these treatment approaches depend on host T-cell proficiency, features of T-cell aging, including T-cell exhaustion or senescence, may compromise their efficacy,5 underlying the need for comprehensive reporting in this subgroup.

Older adults are underrepresented in oncology trials6 compared with the general population of older adults with cancer.7 A systematic review reported barriers identified from 12 studies and identified system barriers (eligibility criteria including comorbidities, availability), provider barriers (concern for age, concern for toxicity), patient barriers (patients believing they are too old), and caregiver barriers (caregiver burden).8 In addition to increasing participation of older adults in clinical trials, detailed subgroup reporting of older adults enrolled in registration trials is critical to contextualize efficacy and toxicities in this subgroup. Hitherto, the risk-to-benefit ratio of many cancer therapies remains largely unknown in older adults, which can hamper informed treatment decisions.7 From a regulatory point of view, regulation 21 Code of Federal Regulations §314.50 states that “safety data must be presented by gender, age, and racial subgroups. When appropriate, safety data from other subgroups of the population of patients treated also must be presented, such as for patients with renal failure or patients with different levels of severity of the disease.”9(p104-105) In addition, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently published recommendations for clinical trial design with regard to geriatric patients.10 In those recommendations, inclusion of older adults in early clinical trials is encouraged in order to evaluate the safety and efficacy of novel therapies among patients with common comorbidities, organ dysfunction, polypharmacy, or frailty. Approaches including age-based stratification or parallel open-label safety studies have also been proposed. These guidelines also note that subgroups of older adults should undergo subpopulation assessment and reporting per the FDA’s guidance for industry Integrated Summary of Effectiveness (October 2015).11 Of note, the International Council for Harmonization Efficacy guideline (ICH E9), section 5.7, from 1998 required sponsors to prespecify the respective subgroups that they intended to analyze for investigation of the potential heterogeneity of treatment effects across patient subgroups.12 To underline the importance of subgroup reporting, a recent position paper by several editors of the Journal of Clinical Oncology provides guidance for authors on that topic and states the priority of that journal for adequate representation of minorities, including also racial and/or social minorities, in clinical trials.13

In this systematic review, we analyze subgroup reporting of baseline patient characteristics, efficacy, toxicities, and quality of life (QoL) of older adults in FDA and European Medicines Agency (EMA) registration trials from 2014 to 2023, leading to approval between 2017 and 2023, in order to provide a status quo on current subgroup reporting of older adults with DLBCL.

Methods

Trial sample

FDA and EMA approvals for DLBCL (n = 12) between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2023 were obtained from publicly available FDA and EMA resources.14,15 Approvals for biosimilars were not considered. In total, 12 trials were included.

Literature searches, according to trial name, National Clinical Trial registration number, and drug name, were conducted on 15 March 2024 and updated on 15 May 2025 in Medline ALL (Ovid), Embase, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, Cochrane CENTRAL, and CINAHL, for all publications relating to included trials. An English language filter was applied. In addition, study results and publications listed on the respective trial’s ClinicalTrials.gov record were also retrieved.

The search results were imported into the Covidence software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). After removal of duplicates, search results were assessed for inclusion or exclusion by title and abstract screening, with subsequent full-text review by pairs of reviewers (R.J., P.T., K.P., and N.R.N.). Conflicts, if any, were resolved by the corresponding author.

Study efficacy end points were extracted from ClinicalTrials.gov registry data, published end points, and the most recent study protocol/statistical data analysis plan (supplemental Table 1). Primary and secondary clinical efficacy end points were included. Assessments of toxicity and QoL were evaluated separately; therefore, end points related to pharmacokinetics, predictive biomarkers, and other basic science, toxicity, safety, and QoL were excluded from primary/secondary end points even if defined as such in the study protocol and analyzed within the respective categories (toxicity, QoL). If the coprimary end point included a toxicity end point, this was excluded from primary end point analysis and, instead, included in the toxicity assessment. Studies that did not include QoL assessment in their study procedures were excluded from QoL analysis. End points that were not published for the entire study population were also excluded from analysis and listed in supplemental Table 6. If interim overall survival (OS) data were reported for the full study cohort but not for older adults, these data were considered unavailable.

Evaluation of data completeness

For rating of data completeness, we used the approach as established by Chan et al and further refined by Mac Eochagain and Battisti.16, 17, 18 According to those methods, 4 categories of reporting were determined: complete, partial, qualitative, and unreported. These categories were defined based on the availability of data relating to effect size and measure of precision, sample size, and includability in meta-analysis. Depending on the data type (paired, unpaired, continuous, etc), the criteria for grading differed in accordance with the statistical characteristics as summarized in Table 1. Multidimensional domains, including baseline characteristics, toxicity, and QoL data, were rated according to minimal aggregate data thresholds as summarized in Table 2. Data for which only 1 component (for instance, a single defined toxicity, eg, neutropenia, or a single baseline characteristic, eg, prognostic biomarker status) was reported completely did not reach the threshold for full reporting. Primary and secondary efficacy end points as unidimensional domains were not rated with respect to a minimum threshold.

Data for older adults, defined as aged ≥65 years, were extracted for each trial by paired reviewers. Conflicts, if any, were resolved by the corresponding author. The following data were collected: trial characteristics and enrollment, baseline patient characteristics, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, primary and secondary efficacy outcomes, QoL assessments, toxicities, and conduct and reporting of baseline geriatric assessments (GAs) or geriatric-specific patient-reported outcomes. QoL assessment was only included if a validated instrument was used (supplemental Table 3).19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 For toxicities, cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and/or immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were rated as “single organ site” within the CAR-T trials. For first-line treatment, reporting of the International Prognostic Index and/or double-expressors/double-hit lymphoma was included as prognostic factors. For second-line or later treatments, lactate dehydrogenase levels, primary refractory disease, or number of previous lines were considered for assessment of prognostic factors.

In cases in which an individual end point was assessed both as a primary and secondary end points (eg, overall response rate assessed by central review as the primary end point, and overall response rate assessed by local investigator as the secondary end point), this end point was only included once, because, often, detailed comparisons or specifications were not available. Likewise, in cases in which individual end points were assessed according to multiple definitions as primary and secondary end points, these were summarized as a single end point and rated as available when any component of the aggregate definition was available (eg, OS at 24 months and after 5 years).

The protocol was registered at https://www.protocols.io25 and the results were reported following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines.

Statistical analysis

In an exploratory analysis, we applied the Pearson correlation coefficient to determine whether any associations existed among the level of reporting completeness, availability of dedicated reports, participant’s median age, approval year, and the proportion of individuals aged ≥65 years.

All statistical analyses were performed in SPSS, version 28.0.0.0 (Chicago, IL). Figures were created with BioRender (Toronto, ON, Canada).

Results

Trial characteristics

Between 2017 and 2023, the FDA and EMA approved 10 therapies with 12 indications for the treatment of DLBCL, corresponding to 12 registration trials. An overview of included trials and the approval timeline is provided in Figure 1A. A total of 7369 results were retrieved through a systematic literature search of registration trials, including relevant conference abstracts. After removal of duplicates, a total of 3877 abstracts and titles were assessed in duplicate by paired reviewers. After full-text assessment of 490 publications, 92 were included for data extraction. Across studies, a median of 6.5 publications were included, with data of interest extracted from 38 publications (supplemental Tables 4 and 5). In addition, 12 ClinicalTrials.gov registration data sets were included. A PRISMA diagram indicating reasons for exclusion of full-texts is depicted in Figure 2.

Characteristics of clinical trials are summarized in Table 3. Eleven trials were in the setting of relapsed and/or refractory disease. One trial, POLARIX, was in the first-line setting. Five trials assessed CAR-T therapies, 3 assessed antibody-drug conjugates, 2 assessed bispecific antibodies, 1 assessed a monoclonal antibody, and another trial assessed a small molecule.

Two trials imposed an upper age inclusion criterion of 75 years (TRANSFORM) and 80 years (POLARIX), respectively. The 12 trials included a total of 2655 patients, of whom 45.8% were aged ≥65 years. Inclusion of patients aged ≥65 years is further depicted in Figure 1B.

With regard to organ dysfunction, 6 (50%) trials excluded patients with renal impairment (defined by 5 trials as an estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] of <60 mL/min and by 1 trial as an eGFR of <50 mL/min). Just 2 trials included patients with an eGFR of 30 to 40 mL/min. Four trials specified a minimal left ventricular ejection fraction for inclusion, of which 2 allowed inclusion of patients with a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of 40% - 50%, and 45-50% respectively. An overview of inclusion and exclusion criteria is depicted in Figure 1C.

A dedicated secondary report on older adults was available for 5 trials (ZUMA-1,26 SADAL,27,28 LOTIS-2,29 ZUMA-7,30 and POLARIX31,32), either as a full-text or conference abstract.

Reporting on efficacy end points

Twelve primary end points and 66 secondary end points were assessed. Of 12 primary end points, 10 (83.4%) were reported completely, 1 (8.3%) partially, and 1 (8.3%) was not reported among older subgroups (Figure 3A). Of 66 secondary end points, 25 of 66 (37.9%) end points were completely reported, 6 of 66 (9.1%) end points were partially reported, and 35 of 66 (53.0%) end points were unreported (Figure 3A). All 12 trials included OS as a secondary end point. Of these, 7 (58.3%) end points reported OS completely for older populations, and 5 (41.7%) did not report OS subgroup data. Among other secondary end points that were addressed in >75% of trials were progression-free survival (PFS) and duration of response (DOR). PFS was completely reported in 7 of 11 (63.6%) trials and unreported in 4 of 11 (36.4%) trials. DOR was completely reported in 4 of 12 (33.3%) trials, partially reported in 1 of 12 (8.3%) trials, and remained unreported in 7 of 12 (58.4%) trials.

Reporting of baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics were completely reported in 4 (33.3%) trials and partially reported in 1 (8.4%) trial. Of 12 trials, 7 (58.3%) did not report basic baseline characteristics in older subgroups (Figure 3C). One trial explicitly reported renal function in patients aged ≥70 years;28 and further comorbidities were not reported by any trial.

None of the trials included a GA. Of note, the mini-mental status examination, which is frequently used to assess cognition during GA, was included as part of the clinical monitoring for ICANS at baseline and at several follow-up examinations in the ZUMA-1 trial.33 None of these assessments were reported.

Reporting of health-related QoL and toxicity

QoL using a validated instrument was assessed in 8 of 12 (66.7%) trials (supplemental Table 3). GO29365 did not use a general QoL assessment but assessed polyneuropathy-related symptoms by the Treatment-induced Neuropathy Assessment Scale (TINAS) questionnaire.24 Because this is only partially related to QoL, this was not included in QoL assessments. None of the trials used an instrument that is specifically validated for older adults. QoL was completely reported in 1 of 8 (12.6%) trials, partially in 2 of 8 (25.0%) trials, and was unreported in 5 of 8 (62.5%) trials (Figure 3C).

None of the trials completely reported toxicities in older adults, but 6 (50%) trials partially reported toxicities in older adults, whereas the remaining 6 (50%) trials left toxicities unreported in older adults. Of note, 3 of 5 (60%) trials on CAR-T therapy at least partially reported the rate of CRS and ICANS in older adults. Of those, 2 trials (ZUMA-1 and TRANSCEND) included CRS/ICANS rates, and ZUMA-7 reported adverse events as coprimary end points because of the specific spectrum of adverse events during CAR-T therapies.

Correlating factors with completeness of reporting

Correlation analysis was performed between the completeness of reporting, the existence of dedicated reports, the median age of inclusion, the year of approval, and the percentage of participants aged ≥65 years. The existence of dedicated reports correlated significantly with the completeness of reporting (for primary and secondary end points; P = .03). None of the other factors showed a statistically significant correlation (data not shown).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the end point reporting for older subgroups in FDA and EMA registration trials for DLBCL. Older subgroups were at least partially reported in >90% of trials for primary end points (mostly complete response rate), in >50% trials for OS end points, and >60% trials for PFS end points. Subgroup reporting for other secondary end points, baseline characteristics, QoL, and toxicities was poor.

Other studies evaluating older adult subgroup reporting in trials leading to FDA approvals in solid malignancies similarly found that secondary end points were less likely to be reported. One study on breast cancer found that reporting was poor for secondary efficacy end points (81.8% unreported), baseline characteristics (70.4% unreported), toxicity (55.6% unreported), and health-related QoL outcomes (87.5% unreported).18 The same investigators also analyzed pivotal immunotherapy trials across various solid malignancies, and found that 87.2% of primary efficacy end points and only 17.9% of secondary efficacy end points were reported in older adults.17 This suggests that the underreporting of secondary end points is a recurrent finding across various malignancies both in oncology and malignant hematology.

It might be argued that explicit reporting on older adults is not necessary in trials in which the participation rate of older adults in a trial is sufficiently high. In our analysis, half (6/12) of the trials had a median age of enrollment of ≥65 years, thus reflecting the median age at diagnosis, and half (6/12) of the trials included at least 50% of participants aged >65 years. This high participation rate of older adults is very encouraging and should be acknowledged. Nonetheless, we argue that older adults are not just a unique group but also a very heterogeneous group of patients whose efficacy and toxicity profiles are affected by a myriad of age-related factors.34 Once a sufficiently high threshold of older adult enrollment has been reached, we regard it as important to report not only data on patients aged ≥65 years of age but also by additional age groups beyond 70 and 80 years. Although a defined toxicity rate may be described as low for an entire study population, it may occur selectively in older patients, a signal that might be missed due to underreporting. For example, in the ECHELON-1 study for Hodgkin lymphoma, although brentuximab plus doxorubicin hydrochloride (Adriamycin), vinblastine, and dacarbazine (AVD) showed improved PFS and OS compared with ABVD (doxorubicin hydrochloride [Adriamycin], bleomycin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine) in the entire study cohort, the 5-year PFS in patients aged ≥60 years was similar between the 2 groups.35 In fact, concomitant brentuximab-AVD was associated with significantly higher rates of peripheral neuropathy and febrile neutropenia in older adults. This poor tolerance led to development of the sequential brentuximab-AVD regimen,36 which remained the standard of care until it was recently supplanted by nivolumab-AVD.37,38

Older adults may have differences in drug response and toxicity relative to the general population. For example, a decline in GFR is frequently seen in the general geriatric population; an eGFR of <60 mL/min is prevalent in up to 46% of the population in those aged >70 years.39,40 Of the 12 trials, 5 excluded patients with an eGFR of <60 mL/min, only 2 trials allowed an eGFR of <40 mL/min, and none allowed an eGFR of <30 mL/min. Although several substances included in the registration trials, such as fludarabine or lenalidomide, carry a potentially increased risk for toxicities in patients with impaired kidney function,41 not all restrictions for impaired organ function are justified. Fludarabine was part of the lymphodepletion in all 5 trials for CAR-T products. Despite the requirement for a thorough dose-adjustment according to impaired kidney function, an eGFR of <60 mL/min is not an absolute contraindication, because several reports demonstrate feasibility in chronic kidney disease, including in a patient dependent on hemodialysis.42, 43, 44 The TRANSCEND trial included patients with an eGFR of >30 mL/min and provided a subgroup analysis for an eGFR of <60 mL/min vs ≥60 mL/min (partial reporting), independent of age.45 This demonstrates nicely that the inclusion of patients with comorbidities is feasible in many cases, as requested by regulation 21 Code of Federal Regulations §314.50.9 Even if a specific comorbidity is related to increased toxicities, it might be acceptable with a favorable benefit-to-risk ratio in severe and life-threatening situations, such as relapsed lymphoma. It would be desirable for more studies to follow such an approach and would subsequently include more older adults with physiological decline in organ function.

Dedicated publications relating to outcomes for older adults enrolled in the included studies were available for 5 trials: ZUMA-1,26 SADAL,27,28 LOTIS-2,29 ZUMA-7,30 and POLARIX.31,32 ZUMA-1 led to approval of axicabtagene ciloleucel in October 2017, and a dedicated analysis of outcomes in older adults was published in 2020. Subgroup reporting for LOTIS-2 and ZUMA-7 similarly occurred a year or more after approval. Subgroup reporting for SADAL occurred within months of approval.27 More recently, POLARIX led to an approval in April 2023, and a subgroup analysis of older patients was published in May 2023, at least as a conference abstract.31 POLARIX demonstrates how an analysis of older adults can occur near the time of final trial publication and drug approval. This timeliness is clinically useful when recommending a novel therapy for older adult patients in a real-world setting. Of note, the trials that had dedicated publications showed significantly increased reporting of primary and secondary end points. These dedicated reports were the only factor analyzed that was found to be associated with reporting completeness of primary and secondary end points for older adults. Therefore, we encourage more trials to include a publication dedicated to older adult subgroup analysis. In addition, we further encourage efficacy and outcome data to be reported separately for patients aged ≥65 years and ≥75 years.

Although baseline oncogeriatric assessment is supported by several international societies,46, 47, 48 the analyzed trials did not include such assessments. GA can provide a more nuanced description of the older adults who have been enrolled. A common criticism when extrapolating clinical trial data to the real world is that patients enrolled in trials are fitter, and their efficacy and toxicity may not appropriately represent those seen in clinical practice. This is especially important in DLBCL, in which baseline characteristics such as fitness and comorbidities captured by the simplified GA49 or geriatric prognostic index50 have been demonstrated to be highly predictive of outcomes. Having detailed data on baseline characteristics of older adults enrolled in trials can help mitigate this knowledge gap and inform clinical decision-making. We additionally encourage clinical trials to develop end points that are pertinent to older adult patients. Examples of such end points include DOR, patient-reported toxicity (eg, Patient-reported Outcomes Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [PRO-CTCAE]), QoL-related measures (eg, Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System [PROMIS], European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer [EORTC], Quality-adjusted time without symptoms or toxicity [Q-TWiST]), and aging-related measures (eg, measures that capture function or cognition).8 Of note, the ZUMA-1 trial included serial measurements of the mini-mental status examination as part of the assessment for ICANS, but the analysis for this measurement is not included in any report.33

Limitations

This analysis focused on publications of full manuscripts and also included analyses that were contained within a conference abstract to capture data available before full-text publications. Because our analysis focused only on registration trials, other data sets that may have supported approval of these therapies may have been excluded. For example, the ALYCANTE trial evaluated axicabtagene as a second-line therapy in transplant-ineligible patients and included 55 of 62 (88.7%) older adults.51 The PILOT study evaluated lisocabtagene in a similar setting and included older patients with poorer performance status, notably involving 28 of 61 (46%) patients aged >75 years and a sizable proportion of patients with a performance status of 2 (26%).52

Conclusions

Older adults may have differences in drug response and toxicity due to age-related physiologic changes, a greater number of comorbidities, and use of concomitant medications. Standardization of subpopulation analysis in older adults (aged >65 years and >75 years) will better enable benefit-risk profiling of new agents for older adults with DLBCL. Ultimately, this will provide guidance on safe use and efficacy of new agents in the older adult population. As more efforts are concentrated on increasing older adult enrollment to ensure adequate representation, it is equally important to report end points within this older adult subgroup. We encourage dedicated articles and abstracts of older adult subgroup analyses and uniformity in reporting to best inform treatment decisions for older adults in clinical practice.
