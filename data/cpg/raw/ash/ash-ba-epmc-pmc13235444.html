Abstract

Peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCL) are a rare, heterogeneous subset of non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL) that are biologically and clinically distinct from B-cell lymphomas. They are typically resistant to chemotherapy and associated with poor outcomes compared to most B-cell malignancies. Despite this, CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) or CHOP-like regimens remain the standard frontline therapy, even though pivotal studies establishing CHOP in NHL included only B-cell patients, with no evidence for PTCL. For decades, efforts to improve outcomes have focused on “CHOP-plus” strategies, adding novel agents to the backbone. Outside of anaplastic large cell lymphoma, in which brentuximab vedotin has improved survival, this approach has generally failed; in most PTCL subtypes, 5-drug regimens add toxicity without clear benefit. After 2 decades of disappointing results, this review critically examines the evidence, the limitations of current strategies, and opportunities to rethink future approaches.

Introduction

Peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCL) are now considered the most challenging hematologic malignancy, surpassing even acute myeloid leukemia in terms of poor prognosis. Multiple registries report a median overall survival (OS) of <2 years for newly diagnosed PTCL, with a 5-year survival rate of ∼30%.1, 2, 3, 4 Even with autologous stem cell transplantation, 5-year outcomes remain dismal, with progression-free survival (PFS) and OS rates of only 24% and 33%, respectively.5, 6, 7, 8 Although several trials are now investigating the role of frontline autologous stem cell transplantation, none have yet been randomized. In the relapsed/refractory (R/R) setting, patients face even greater challenges, with median PFS and OS of only 3.7 and 6.5 months, respectively.9 As relapses accumulate, outcomes typically worsen, with each subsequent relapse associated with increasingly smaller PFS and OS.

Treatment is further limited by the absence of randomized data supporting 1 chemotherapy regimen over another, aside from the ECHELON-2 trial. ECHELON-2 compared CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) to CHP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone) plus brentuximab vedotin (Bv; vincristine omitted due to overlapping neurotoxicity) in CD30+ PTCL.10,11 Bv, an anti-CD30 antibody-drug conjugate, was initially approved for R/R anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL), in which CD30 expression is universal. By contrast, CD30 expression in most non-ALCL PTCL subtypes is variable, typically ranging from 10% to 30%.12 Accordingly, ECHELON-2 showed clear benefit only in ALCL, whereas angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma and PTCL–not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS) derived no advantage over CHOP. The trial was statistically positive largely because ∼70% of participants had ALCL, the most Bv-sensitive subtype.9,10 Within ALCL, CHP-Bv especially improved outcomes in ALK-negative disease, historically less favorable than anaplastic lymphoma kinase-positive ALCL.1,2

Attempts to improve on CHOP with other agents have largely failed. The randomized phase 4 Ro-CHOP trial found romidepsin-CHOP (Ro-CHOP) inferior to CHOP, with added toxicity in PTCL-NOS and angioimmunoblastic T-Cell lymphoma.13 Romidepsin, a histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi) once approved for R/R PTCL,13, 14, 15, 16, 17 was subsequently withdrawn despite clear single-agent activity, sparking debate.18 Similarly, the randomized LUMIERE trial showed alisertib inferior to romidepsin or pralatrexate in R/R PTCL.19

These failures highlight a systemic problem: accelerated approvals in the R/R setting rarely translate into benefit when combined with CHOP in front line. Many required confirmatory trials are never completed due to disease rarity, creating “dangling approvals.” The field has been driven toward poorly conceived CHOP-based combinations or empiric pairings with little biological rationale.

The idea of a “standard of care” (SOC) in PTCL is often misapplied, based on usage rather than strong clinical evidence. CHOP is the most widely used regimen but not the most effective. Non-ALCL subtypes achieve overall response rates of only 60% to 70% and 5-year OS of 20% to 30%.10,13,20,21 CHOP’s high relapse rates underscore its inadequacy in PTCL and the urgent need for rational, subtype-specific approaches. Below, we describe the evolution of care over the decades that led to this current landscape.

The problem with T-cell malignancies: too many types, too few patients

T-cell neoplasms are characterized by remarkable heterogeneity, reflecting the intrinsic heterogeneity of normal T cells. This diversity arises from the specialized functions of T-cell subsets, primarily CD4+ helper T cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. CD4+ T cells are further classified into T-helper 1 (TH1) and T-helper 2 (TH2) cells, each with distinct roles. TH1 cells stimulate cellular immunity, macrophage inhibition, and B-cell activation to produce immunoglobulins such as immunoglobulin M (IgM) and IgG.22,23 TH2 cells induce cytokines such as interleukin-3 (IL-3) and IL-4, promoting humoral immunity and eosinophil differentiation. This classification underpins the identification of PTCL subtypes. CD4+ T cells give rise to PTCL-NOS, ALCL, and T-follicular helper cell (TFH) lymphomas such as PTCL-TFH and angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma. PTCL-NOS is now subdivided into TH1-polarized (TBX21 subgroup) and TH2-polarized (GATA3 subgroup) lymphomas, with the former showing better outcomes.22,23 Similarly, PTCL-TFH, derived from TFH cells, exhibits a characteristic immunophenotype, expressing Bcl-6 and PD-1. In contrast, ALCL derives from TH17 T-lymphocytes, which produce IL-17, crucial in adaptive immunity against bacteria and fungi. The CD8+ T-cell neoplasms, or cytotoxic T-cell lymphomas, are rare and aggressive, often presenting as cutaneous malignancies resistant to conventional therapy. The molecular and cellular heterogeneity of T-cell neoplasms is unparalleled among malignancies.

Current classifications22,23 recognize ∼36 T-cell neoplasm subtypes. Identifying the cell of origin provides critical insights into biology and may inform precision therapies. Despite this, CHOP and CHOP-like regimens remain the most common frontline treatments, although efficacy varies by subtype and supporting evidence is limited.4,24 These regimens are mainly used for aggressive subtypes such as ALCL, PTCL-NOS, nodal TFH lymphomas, hepatosplenic, and intestinal lymphomas, still rare overall within non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL).

The second defining feature of PTCL is its rarity. In 2024, there were 80 620 newly diagnosed patients with NHL (4% of cancers) and 20 140 deaths (3.3% of cancer-related deaths), with a 5-year survival of 74.3%.4 PTCL accounts for ∼10% of NHL or 8000 to 10 000 annual US cases.25,26 The most common subtypes, PTCL-TFH and PTCL-NOS, each account for only 1000 to 2000 cases annually. With ∼15 000 board-certified oncologists in the United States, the data suggest that the average one sees only 3 to 4 cases over 5 years. This rarity, combined with heterogeneity and lack of consensus, continues to hinder progress in PTCL treatment.

The origins of our SOC: an absence of evidence

CHOP became the de facto standard for aggressive lymphomas, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and PTCL, after the pivotal 1993 Southwest Oncology Group (SWOG) study (Fisher et al).27 That trial compared CHOP, a first-generation regimen, with more intensive multiagent protocols (methotrexate, bleomycin, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, dexamethasone, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, etoposide cytozar, bleomycin, vincristine, methotrexate and prednisone, and methotrexate, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, prednisone, bleomycin) and found that none of the intensified regimens improved survival compared with CHOP, despite greater toxicity. CHOP produced complete responses in 40% to 45% of patients and cured ∼30% to 35%, setting a new benchmark for tolerability and effectiveness.

At the time, lymphoma classification relied on the Working Formulation, which grouped diseases by clinical behavior rather than lineage. The SWOG trial included intermediate- and high-grade lymphomas, such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and Burkitt lymphoma, but did not report outcomes for any patients with T-cell lymphoma. Nonetheless, CHOP was adopted for PTCL by default, under the assumption that all aggressive lymphomas shared similar biology.

It was not until the Revised American European Lymphoma classification in 1994 that lineage distinctions were formally recognized. Today, the World Health Organization classification identifies ∼80 lymphoma subtypes, including 36 T-cell types. Despite advances in B-cell lymphoma treatment (eg, rituximab and any drug conjugate), attempts to improve CHOP or R-CHOP with small molecules have largely failed.28

CHOP’s dominance in PTCL is thus based more on historical precedent than tailored evidence. Although CHOP demonstrates activity in T-cell lymphomas, outcomes remain suboptimal, highlighting the need for regimens specifically designed for PTCL. Given PTCL’s distinct biology, it is worth asking the following: are poor outcomes due to the disease itself or the continued use of regimens never designed for it? If the foundational treatment model is flawed, every trial built on it may be too.

Are CHOP-plus really a CHOP-minus propositions in PTCL: what do the data say?

The emergence of novel targeted drugs in oncology has raised questions about how to best establish clinical benefit. Traditionally, this requires demonstrating superiority over an SOC. Yet in PTCL, no definitive SOC exists in either the frontline or R/R setting. Data from the COMPLETE Registry highlight wide variation in real-world practice,25 with approaches differing across countries. Clinical guideline panels further underscore this point by recommending clinical trial enrollment for all eligible patients.4,24 If trial participation is the preferred option, the notion of a universally accepted SOC becomes difficult to defend.

With all the caveats as presented, there is little question that the predominant approach to establish “clinical benefit” of a new drug in PTCL has been to conduct “CHOP-plus” studies; that is, to add the promising novel drug to the existing backbone of CHOP and then compare it to CHOP to see whether the novel drug adds benefit. This strategy has dominated the field for 2 decades, with countless studies relying on this design, as detailed in Tables 1 and 2.

Table 1 compares the dose intensity (DI) of novel drugs as single agents at their recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) or maximum tolerated dose (MTD; ie, the dose recommended in the package insert or based on vetted phase 2 studies) to the identified MTD when the new drug is used in combination in a CHOP-plus regimen. A calculation of the difference in DI between these 2 doses provides a measure of the toxicity caused by the addition of the fifth drug. Table 1 shows that the DI reduction goes from no change (belinostat and Bv) to as high as 73% (tucidinostat). The mean dose reduction across these studies is ∼38%. Table 2 shares the details of many of these studies, including toxicity, discontinuation rate, percent dose reductions and dose interruptions, and efficacy. For many studies, there are inconsistencies in reporting, because details on discontinuations, dose reductions, and interruptions are not reported. Discontinuations range from 0% to as high as 20%, whereas dose reductions range from single digits to as high as 50%, and interruptions range from single digits to as high as 60%. The mean rates of reported discontinuations, dose reductions, and interruptions were 12%, 23%, and 27% respectively. These data suggest a few important conclusions about how we define optimal doses of drugs in combination. Although the usual goal is to identify the dose that results in grade 3 or 4 toxicity in one-third or fewer patients, it is clear that an exceptionally large number of patients still experience significant compromise in DI due to the toxicity of the combination, leading to large numbers of patients unable to receive the full optimal dose of the targeted drug.

For example, let us take a selective look at one drug, romidepsin. Romidepsin is a potent HDACi originally approved in 2011 for patients with R/R PTCL and in 2009 for patients with cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, the former based on an accelerated approval and the latter a full approval.15 A postmarketing requirement to satisfy the accelerated approval required a randomized study to establish unequivocal benefit. The study design compared CHOP against Ro-CHOP. The primary end point of the study was PFS, and the secondary end points were PFS assessed by the investigator, OS, objective response rate (ORR), complete remission (CR) plus unconfirmed CR, duration of response, and safety.13 The dose and schedule of romidepsin as a single agent is 14 mg/m2 on days 1, 8, and 15 every 28 days, which gives a daily DI of 1.2 mg/m2 per day over a 28-day cycle.38 The dose of romidepsin in combination with CHOP was determined to be 12 mg/kg on days 1 and 8 every 21 days, leading to a DI of 1.14 mg/m2 per day over the 21-day cycle. This difference alone, without consideration of other dose or schedule modifications, represents a 24% reduction in the DI from the single-agent phase 2 dose. In addition, although the fraction of patients who were discontinued was not reported in the publication, ∼40% of patients required a dose reduction or interruption of romidepsin in the Ro-CHOP arm compared to the CHOP arm.13 In fact, grade 3 or 4 hematologic toxicity was nearly 2-times higher in the Ro-CHOP arm than CHOP, at 81% and 47%, respectively. Another stark difference was the reported dose discontinuation and reduction/interruption rates, which were 0% and 40% in the CHOP arm and 8% and 37% to 63% in the Ro-CHOP arms, respectively.13 These data suggest that even with 37% to 63% of patients requiring either a dose reduction or interruption, further compromising the DI, the 12 mg/m2 dose of romidepsin administered on days 1 and 8 determined from the phase 1 was likely still too high.13 The base proposition for the 5-drug combination started with a 30% reduction in romidepsin DI.

One interpretation of these data might suggest that a ∼50% reduction in DI may compromise the efficacy by an approximately equal amount, assuming some linear relationship in the concentration-response curve. Romidepsin is a drug that, although effective in a subset of patients, carries a single-agent ORR and PFS of only 25% and ∼3 to 4 months, respectively.15 A 50% reduction in a drug with marginal activity might well be estimated to produce ORR and PFS of 10% to 20% and 2 to 3 months, respectively, at best. Couple these findings with the fact that there are no data to suggest that romidepsin, or maybe any drug for that matter, synergizes with CHOP, it is highly unlikely one could expect better outcomes than the worst-case scenario. Synergistic pairings of drugs are the only way to achieve superior activity with lower dose of the component drugs. With these concepts in hand, it becomes extremely difficult to foresee the merits of adding these drugs, without any compelling preclinical evidence, together in combination in this fashion. The paradigm detailed here for romidepsin is in no way unique to romidepsin and likely reflects a set of principles and interpretations that could be germane to most of the CHOP-plus studies described in Tables 1 and 2. Falchi et al published on a small series of patients with treatment-naïve PTCL (n = 11), who were treated with 5-azacytidine and romidepsin.39 In this subset, the ORR and CR rate were 70% and 50%, respectively, which was at least comparable to the CHOP control arm from the Ro-CHOP study (ORR 60% and CR 30%).39 This suggests that certain doublets may well achieve similar efficacy as SOC combinations, with markedly less toxicity. Of note, this pairing of drugs was selected based on strong preclinical data affirming drug-drug synergy.

Of course, the alternative to adding drugs to CHOP would be to explore the merits of novel-novel combinations. Tables 3 and 4 share a number of trials and similar data regarding DI, safety, and efficacy profiles. At the designated RP2D, novel drug dose modifications appeared to require less dose reductions overall. These results can be summarized as follows: 2 of the doublet combinations did not require any dose reduction (romidepsin plus bortezomib and romidepsin plus the PI3K inhibitor [PI3Ki] tenalisib); 1 combination allowed a 300% increase in one of the drugs, with the companion drug requiring a 28% reduction in DI (romidepsin plus the PI3Ki duvelisib); 2 studies required a 20% reduction in DI for each of the 2 drugs (azacytidine and romidepsin); and 1 study required a 50% reduction for each of the 2 drugs (romidepsin plus pralatrexate). For the doublets, the discontinuation across all studies ranged from 0% to 17% (romidepsin plus duvelisib), with dose reductions in 17% to 45% of cases, with dose interruptions generally not being reported. In addition, the doublet studies, which were predominantly conducted in patients in the relapsed setting, appeared to produce ORR, CR, and PFS results that were not dissimilar to that seen with the CHOP-plus combinations, although the PFS was typically shorter in the relapsed setting. Mak et al reported that for patients in their first relapse, the observed PFS was ∼3 to 4 months, with an OS of ∼6 months.9 Although most of the patients reported in these phase 2 studies had multiply relapsed disease, with a median number of prior therapies in the range of 2 to 3, it appears that select doublets appear to improve PFS beyond the findings of Mak et al, despite being conducted in more heavily treated patients.

A second observation relates to efficacy. In some cases, the regimens appeared to be selectively more active in patients with T-cell malignancies (5-azacytidine plus romidepsin and romidepsin plus pralatrexate), with data in B-cell malignancies being substantially inferior. The data also suggest that some doublets appear more favorable than others, exhibiting ORR, CR, and PFS greater than the sum of the parts. The question is why do select combinations exhibit outcomes greater than the sum of the parts? In these 2 former examples, the answer may reside in how the doublet combinations were selected. In each of those cases, Jain et al and Scotto et al had clearly established the potent synergy of the doublet in a host of preclinical models of T-cell malignancy.39 The finding of synergy suggests that lower concentrations or dose in patients could have the effect of lowering toxicity burden while increasing the efficacy end points of ORR and CR rate. In fact, in the case of the 5-azacytidne and romidepsin doublet, the best ORR and CR for these drugs as single agents is modest: romidepsin ORR and CR of 25% and 8%; 5-azacytidine ORR and CR of 25% and 10%, respectively. In the simple additive proposition, one might expect ORR and CR of 50% and 18%, respectively, for the combination, although the published clinical data reported ORR and CR of 73% and 55%, respectively. These data, at the very least, suggest a better-than-additive effect and likely recapitulate the synergy appreciated in the preclinical setting.39,41, 48, 49, 50, 51 A similar argument could be made for the romidepsin plus pralatrexate combination.17,43

Another interesting observation from these data is that, in the case of duvelisib and romidepsin, there was a 300% increase in the MTD of the PI3Ki in combination with romidepsin. PI3Ki are well-known for class toxicity that largely revolves around autoimmune toxicities such as hepatitis, diarrhea and colitis, rash, and pneumonitis.52 In the case of duvelisib, the package insert for the drug approval in chronic lymphocytic leukemia recommends a dose of 25 mg administered orally daily for 28 days.53 On this single-agent regimen, one of the most common adverse events was hepatotoxicity, with transaminase elevations reported in 40% of patients. Remarkably, in the doublet study of romidepsin plus duvelisib, the MTD was defined as 75 mg orally daily for 28 days.44 Even with this increase in dosage, the noted incidence of transaminase elevation was significantly lower, reported to be ∼13.6%.44 Although this was a small single-arm study, these data suggest that, perhaps in this case, romidepsin may have contributed to the attenuation of the major toxicities associated with the PI3Ki, allowing for a dose intensification of the PI3Ki. However, despite this substantial dose intensification of the PI3Ki, the ORR and CR rate do not reflect an additive or better-than-additive effect. In the additive proposition, the expected ORR and CR would be 25% and 8% for romidepsin and 50% and 19% for duvelisib, respectively. In combination, the expectation might be an ORR and CR of 75% and 30%; however, the published clinical data reported an ORR and CR of 58% and 42%, respectively. Hence, despite the ability to administer these drugs at an increased DI, the lack of any preclinical synergy or even additive benefit produced an underwhelming efficacy for the doublet.44

Again, although the data reported in Table 4 are all in patients with R/R disease after 2 to 3 lines of prior therapy on average, there is a sense that the overall toxicity burden appears less compared to the 5-drug regimens shown in Table 2. The best way to appreciate the net toxicity burden in any of these trials is to understand the rates of discontinuation, dose reduction, and dose interruption. Although the reporting of these specific variables is inconsistent across the studies shown, a fact that should be rectified in future combination studies, the important variable of discontinuation rates suggests the novel-novel regimens are well tolerated.18

Are we doing it all wrong? Rethinking chemotherapy-plus approaches

Although debates continue about the best approach, the CHOP-plus strategy has largely been unproductive. The traditional model for CHOP-plus trials uses the classic Fibonacci 3+3 dose-escalation design, fixing CHOP at standard doses while escalating the targeted drug (Figure 1A). Alternatively, Figure 1B illustrates a different strategy: holding the targeted drug at its RP2D, MTD, or optimal biological dose, while escalating CHOP components. This alternative approach prioritizes maintaining the DI of the targeted drug, which often has the strongest evidence for efficacy in PTCL. Escalating CHOP, which lacks the same robust data, may better complement the targeted agent’s effects. Without clear synergy with CHOP, outcomes are generally additive at best, emphasizing the need to optimize the targeted agent's impact.

There is often resistance to this study design, because many clinicians and institutional review boards believe patients must receive standard doses of the SOC. This view stems from concerns that lowering doses might be seen as “unethical.” Although this concern is common in cancer studies, it is less clear when applied to PTCL. Although CHOP is widely used in the community, it is harder to argue that it rightfully holds the status of SOC. Therefore, when CHOP-plus regimens are tested in clinical trials, they align with international guidelines,4,24 which advocate for enrolling patients in clinical trials as the preferred option for frontline treatment. If we accept these international guidelines, the trial design in Table 2 should be considered acceptable and explored in future CHOP-plus trials (Figure 1A-B).

In an ideal world, it would be valuable to conduct a randomized trial comparing the 2 approaches. In such an endeavor, it would be possible to learn whether one design is superior to another, and correlative studies would shed light on the advantages and disadvantages of lowering the DI of the targeted drug on the underlying biology. Given the many barriers to conducting trials in orphan diseases, let alone randomized trials, it is unlikely such a trial will ever be conducted in PTCL. Nonetheless, it represents a reasonable alternative to the present approach, which for the past 20 years has not succeeded in improving the care of patients with PTCL.

If not CHOP, then what?

The ideal way to treat any malignant disease is to target the biology driving malignant transformation and persistence, thus requiring deep understanding of molecular pathogenesis. In T-cell neoplasms, many biologically driven therapies have been tested with limited success. Still, growing insight into cellular origins and molecular drivers is beginning to reveal potential vulnerabilities.

A common theme in many novel doublet studies is the use of the HDACi romidepsin in combination with other agents. The HDACi vulnerability observed in PTCL is likely serving as a surrogate marker of a wider epigenetic dysregulation state.26 For now, combinations such as HDAC inhibitors that target epigenetic vulnerability seem promising and worth further exploration.54

The key question in the field is how to determine which drug combinations should be advanced to clinical trials. In most cases, this decision depends on the willingness of drug companies to collaborate. However, a more systematic approach—such as preclinical evaluation of drug combinations informed by concepts of synergy and toxicity in in vivo models—could offer a promising strategy. In the case of 2 clinical studies discussed in Table 3, the combinations of 5-azacytidine plus romidepsin and romidepsin plus pralatrexate were translated from preclinical studies that confirmed strong synergy in vitro and in vivo. This may explain why these combinations show greater-than-expected clinical efficacy with manageable tolerability.

With the field continues to identify critical targets in T-cell lymphoma, every patient should be treated within a clinical trial, both in the frontline setting and beyond. Based on the analysis presented here, it is reasonable to suggest that the field shift its focus toward exploring novel drug combinations rather than relying on CHOP-plus strategies. The idea of developing a new backbone for the disease is frequently discussed, though it will clearly take time to fully develop. One potential avenue for novel treatments could involve drugs or regimens with disease selectivity, those that are more effective in T-cell lymphoma than in B-cell lymphoma or other cancers. Although this approach may be complex, it indicates that these drugs are targeting biology that is both unique and critical to the disease’s behavior. The desire to explore novel doublet combinations suggests that sponsors of these agents afford the field as much latitude as possible to allow the innovation of investigators interested in the disease to explore the possibilities. Streamlining these combinations based on strong preclinical evidence is a logical way to increase the probability of success in the clinic (Figure 1C).
