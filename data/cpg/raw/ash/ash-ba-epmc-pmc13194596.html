Key Points

  • •

    Reduced-dose 40 Gy CCRT with weekly cisplatin provides durable long-term control with minimal sinonasal toxicity in localized ENKTL.

  • •

    Most relapses occur at distant sites, and Epstein-Barr virus DNA dynamics combined with response status strongly stratify prognosis.

Visual Abstract

Abstract

Radiotherapy (RT) is central in the management of limited-stage extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL), nasal type. Although international guidelines recommend 50 to 56 Gy, emerging data suggest that reduced-dose RT with a radiosensitizer may provide comparable control with less toxicity. We evaluated the long-term outcomes of 40 Gy concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) followed by systemic chemotherapy. We retrospectively analyzed 155 patients newly diagnosed with limited-stage ENKTL treated at Samsung Medical Center between 2000 and 2023. All patients received 40 Gy in RT in 20 fractions with weekly cisplatin (30 mg/m2), followed by systemic chemotherapy. Clinical characteristics, treatment response, survival outcomes, and relapse patterns were assessed. The median age was 50 years, and 64.5% were male. Most patients (73.5%) had nasal-type disease, whereas 20.0% had combined nasal and nonnasal upper aerodigestive tract involvement. At a median follow-up of 73 months, the 5-year progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival rates were 68.0% and 82.2%, respectively. Pretreatment Epstein-Barr virus DNA positivity correlated with inferior PFS (P = .043). Patients receiving CCRT plus chemotherapy had better PFS than those receiving CCRT alone (P = .004). Relapses occurred in 54 (34.8%) patients, predominantly at extranodal distant sites (21.3%), whereas the local control rate remained high (86.5%). Secondary malignancies developed in 3 patients. Median 22-item sinonasal outcome test score was 8, indicating minimal sinonasal symptom burden. A 40 Gy CCRT regimen followed by systemic chemotherapy achieved durable local control and favorable long-term survival in limited-stage ENKTL. The predominance of distant, rather than regional, relapse supports the feasibility of this reduced-dose CCRT strategy combined with systemic therapy.

Introduction

Extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL), nasal type, is a distinct subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphomas and is highly prevalent in East Asia and Latin America.1,2 It is characterized by its strong association with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, aggressive clinical behavior, and poor prognosis in the advanced disease.3,4 By contrast, most patients present with localized diseases, typically involving the nasal cavity and upper aerodigestive tract (UADT; nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, oral cavity, larynx, and trachea).5 For patients with limited-stage ENKTL, involved-site radiotherapy (RT) may be combined with nonanthracycline-based chemotherapy through concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), sequential chemotherapy followed by RT, or “sandwich” regimens. Among these approaches, cisplatin-based CCRT followed by systemic chemotherapy has become a widely used treatment strategy in many East Asian centers.6

RT plays a central role in the management of limited-stage ENKTL, because local control is essential for long-term disease outcomes. International guidelines, including those from the Asian consensus panels, generally recommend higher RT doses, typically in the range of 50 to 56 Gy.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 These recommendations are primarily based on early retrospective studies demonstrating a dose-response relationship between RT dose and local control.13,14 However, subsequent institutional analyses suggest that local control rates (LCR) with 40 Gy and concurrent weekly cisplatin as a radiosensitizer are comparable with those achieved with higher RT doses, while potentially reducing treatment-related toxicities.15, 16, 17, 18

Moreover, the optimal RT field design remains an area of ongoing debate. Earlier approaches frequently used extended-field RT encompassing the nasal cavity and bilateral cervical lymph nodes to minimize regional recurrence. However, extended fields increase the risk of late toxicities, including xerostomia, dysphagia, and cranial neuropathy. More recent evidence favors field-reduction strategies, which limit irradiation to the primary tumor and adjacent structures while omitting elective nodal coverage. This approach aims to maintain disease control while minimizing long-term morbidity.4

In addition to RT dose and field design, concurrent administration of cisplatin has emerged as a critical component of treatment for limited-stage ENKTL. Cisplatin functions as a radiosensitizer, augmenting DNA damage and apoptosis induced by radiation, and has been shown to improve locoregional control in ENKTL. According to the previous studies, this radiosensitizing effect provides a rationale for exploring whether 40 Gy RT doses may still yield durable disease control when combined with concurrent cisplatin.15,19

Therefore, we hypothesized that a reduced-dose, limited-field RT (40 Gy in 20 fractions) with concurrent weekly cisplatin, followed by systemic chemotherapy, would maintain long-term disease control while reducing late toxicities in patients with limited-stage ENKTL. Specifically, we assessed survival outcomes, relapse patterns, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) using the 22-item sinonasal outcome test (SNOT-22) questionnaire19 to capture the prevalence and severity of treatment-related sequelae over a median follow-up period of 5 years.

Methods

Study design and data collection

We conducted a retrospective study to evaluate the long-term outcomes of 155 patients newly diagnosed with limited-stage ENKTL, nasal type, who were treated at Samsung Medical Center between 2000 and 2023. All patients received definitive aim RT with a prescribed dose of 40 Gy in 20 fractions, delivered concurrently with weekly cisplatin (30 mg/m2), followed by subsequent chemotherapy according to institutional protocol. Eligible patients included those with nasal-type ENKTL and nonnasal upper aerodigestive tract type (nonnasal UADT; nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, oral cavity, larynx, and trachea) ENKTL. Patients with extra-UADT ENKTL (skin and soft tissues, gastrointestinal tract, lung, testis, and other rare extra-UADT sites) were excluded from the analysis.5 Cisplatin was administered once weekly at 30 mg/m2 during the 4-week CCRT course, for a total of 4 cycles. This schedule was uniformly applied to all patients receiving CCRT.

Clinical data at diagnosis were collected, including demographics (age, sex), disease characteristics (primary site, Ann Arbor stage, and prognostic index of natural killer lymphoma [PINK] and prognostic index of natural killer cell lymphoma with EBV DNA [PINK-E] scores), and laboratory findings such as complete blood count, lactate dehydrogenase, and circulating EBV DNA titer (copies per milliliter). EBV DNA negativity was defined as less than the detection limit (<1.02 copies per uL whole blood) according to institutional laboratory cutoffs. Baseline EBV DNA (pretreatment) was measured before CCRT, and posttreatment EBV DNA was measured after completion of all planned consolidation chemotherapy. Treatment details, including RT field design, concurrent chemotherapy administration, subsequent systemic regimens, and l-asparaginase exposure, were reviewed. This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board of Samsung Medical Center (2016-11-040-025). The requirement for informed consent was waived. The final update of the survival data was performed in June 2025.

RT

The gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated based on findings from nasal endoscopy, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging, and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT. For stage I disease, the clinical target volume (CTV) was generated by adding appropriate margins to the GTV to encompass the bilateral nasal cavities and the ipsilateral medial maxillary sinus, while excluding the contralateral maxillary sinus. When the GTV involved the ipsilateral maxillary sinus, the entire ipsilateral maxillary sinus was included in the CTV. In cases in which the tumor extended across the midline structures, the CTV was expanded to include the medial portion or the whole of the contralateral maxillary sinus, depending on the extent of involvement. Depending on the superior and posterior extent of the GTV, the CTV was further expanded to cover part or all of the ethmoid sinus and nasopharynx on the involved side. For stage II disease with cervical lymph node involvement, the CTV additionally encompassed the involved cervical lymphatic regions with adequate margins from the nodal GTV.

The treatment era primarily influenced the selection of RT techniques (3-dimensional [3D]–CRT vs intensity-modulated radiation therapy [IMRT]). At our institution, IMRT for head and neck lymphoma was introduced around 2012-2013, and its use increased markedly after 2015 with the expansion of National Health Insurance coverage. Consequently, patients treated before 2012 predominantly received 3D-CRT, whereas IMRT became the standard technique in subsequent years. Reflecting this temporal pattern, 48 patients (31.0%) in our cohort were treated with 3D-CRT, and 107 patients (69.0%) received IMRT.

Subsequent chemotherapy and response evaluation

After CCRT, 141 (91.0%) patients proceeded to subsequent chemotherapy. The regimens administered were as follows: VIDL (etoposide, ifosfamide, dexamethasone, and l-asparaginase), MIDLE (methotrexate, etoposide, ifosfamide, dexamethasone, and l-asparaginase), VIPD (etoposide, ifosfamide, cisplatin, and dexamethasone), and GDP (gemcitabine, cisplatin, and dexamethasone). The choice of subsequent chemotherapy regimen (l-asparaginase or non–l-asparaginase containing) was determined by the physician's discretion, patient comorbidities, and treatment era. Radiologic assessment was performed using neck, chest, and abdomen-pelvis CT as well as fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT scans at three predefined time points: at diagnosis, after CCRT, and at the end of subsequent chemotherapy. Thereafter, follow-up imaging was conducted every 3 to 6 months during the first year and subsequently every 6 to 12 months, or as clinically indicated. Circulating EBV DNA monitoring was performed in parallel with imaging assessments, at diagnosis, after CCRT, and at the end of subsequent chemotherapy. During follow-up, EBV DNA levels were measured at 3- to 6-month intervals in the first year and every 6 to 12 months thereafter. Treatment responses were evaluated according to the International Working Group response criteria.20, 21, 22

PROs

For long-term survivors, PROs were collected to evaluate health-related quality of life and treatment-related sequelae. Surveys were distributed via mobile text message, and responses were obtained using the SNOT-22 questionnaire.23 Patients were asked to report the type and severity of late toxicities after treatment, including nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, loss of smell/taste, postnasal drip, sleep disturbance, fatigue, and emotional impacts. Symptom severity was graded according to the SNOT-22 scoring system, and data were analyzed to assess the prevalence and clinical effects of long-term treatment-related sequelae. PROs were obtained from long-term survivors without disease, but response rates and patient selection may limit generalizability.

Statistical analysis

The primary objective was to assess progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) after this reduced-dose CCRT strategy. Secondary objectives included evaluating LCR, relapse patterns (local vs distant), the impact of additional consolidation chemotherapy, and PROs for long-term survivors. PFS was defined as the time from the initiation of RT to either disease progression or death from any cause. OS was measured from the initiation of RT to death or the last follow-up. In addition, PFS2 was defined as the time from the first documented relapse to progression on salvage therapy or death from any cause. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify prognostic factors associated with PFS and OS. Variables with P value <.10 in univariate analysis or considered clinically relevant were entered into the multivariate model. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and a 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using time-dependent covariates. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Missing data were handled with listwise deletion.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 155 patients with newly diagnosed limited-stage ENKTL were included in this study. The median age was 50 years (range, 15-82), and 100 patients (64.5%) were male. Baseline EBV DNA was detectable in 59 patients (38.1%). Most had nasal-type disease (n = 114 [73.5%]), whereas 10 patients (6.5%) presented with nonnasal UADT involvement. Combined nasal and nonnasal UADT disease was observed in 31 patients (20.0%). At diagnosis, 101 patients (65.2%) had stage IE disease and 54 (34.8%) had stage IIE disease. Low-risk groups were predominant, with 107 patients (69.0%) classified as PINK low (0) and 133 patients (85.8%) as PINK-E low (0-1). After completion of CCRT, 141 patients (91.0%) proceeded to subsequent chemotherapy, whereas 14 patients (9.0%) did not, due to advanced age, disease progression, or intentional omission in cases with limited disease burden. Among those who received chemotherapy (CCRT-CTX), 96 patients (61.9%) were treated with l-asparaginase–containing regimens (VIDL or MIDLE). Comparison of baseline characteristics between patients who did and did not receive subsequent chemotherapy revealed no significant differences in age, stage, EBV status, or RT technique (3D-CRT vs IMRT; supplemental Table 1). There were no significant differences in clinical baseline characteristics between patients who received l-asparaginase–containing consolidation and those who did not. The imbalance in RT techniques (higher IMRT use in the l-asparaginase group) reflects a treatment era effect rather than baseline differences. At our institution, 3D-RT was predominantly used between 2004 and 2012, whereas IMRT became standard thereafter, coinciding with the increasing adoption of l-asparaginase–based regimens (supplemental Table 2). Response evaluation and survival outcomes

At a median follow-up of 73 months (range, 62.3-83.7), treatment responses were evaluable in all patients. All patients treated with CCRT received 4 planned weekly cycles of cisplatin concurrently with RT. After completion of CCRT, the overall response rate in the CCRT-CTX group was 95.7% (n = 135), with 101 patients (71.6%) achieving complete response (CR) and 34 patients (24.1%) achieving partial response (PR). Five patients (3.5%) had stable disease or progressive disease, and 1 patient (0.7%) was not evaluable. Among 34 patients who achieved PR after CCRT, 21 (61.8%) converted to CR after subsequent chemotherapy. Thus, after chemotherapy, 121 (85.8%) achieved CR, 12 (8.5%) achieved PR, and 8 (5.7%) had disease progression.

Among 14 patients treated with CCRT alone, 9 (64.3%) achieved CR, 1 (7.1%) achieved PR, and 3 (21.4%) had stable disease or progressive disease. At the 3-month evaluation, 8 patients (57.1%) maintained CR, whereas 4 (28.6%) experienced disease progression. Due to the small number of patients in the CCRT-alone group, no formal statistical comparison was performed with the CCRT-CTX group (Figure 1A).

During follow-up, the median PFS was 142.0 months (95% CI, 88.9-195.1), with a 5-year PFS rate of 68%. The median OS was not reached, and the 5-year OS rate was 82.2% (Figure 1B).

Subgroup analysis for PFS

In the subgroup analysis of PFS, patients with positive pretreatment EBV DNA showed significantly worse outcomes compared with those with EBV DNA negativity (P = .043; Figure 2A). Similarly, posttreatment EBV DNA positivity indicated a trend toward poorer PFS without reaching statistical significance (P = .092; Figure 2B). Neither the PINK (P = .717; Figure 2C) nor the PINK-E (P = .200; Figure 2D) scores demonstrated significant prognostic value for PFS. Treatment modality had a notable effect, with patients who received CCRT followed by chemotherapy having significantly better PFS than those treated with CCRT alone (P = .004; Figure 2E). The use of l-asparaginase (P = .721; Figure 2F) and the choice of chemotherapy regimen (P = .781; Figure 2G) did not yield significant differences in PFS. Among patients who achieved CR, those with pretreatment EBV DNA negativity had significantly superior PFS; conversely, non-CR status portended poor PFS regardless of EBV DNA (P < .001; Figure 2H). When response status and posttreatment EBV DNA stratified patients, those achieving CR with EBV negativity showed the most favorable PFS. In contrast, patients without CR with EBV DNA positivity had the worst outcomes (P < .001; Figure 2I). In the univariate analysis for PFS, pretreatment EBV DNA positivity (HR, 1.679; 95% CI, 1.010-2.790; P = .046) was significantly associated with inferior PFS. However, in multivariate analysis, none of these variables retained statistical significance for PFS (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis for OS

In the subgroup analysis of OS, pretreatment EBV DNA positivity (P = .176; Figure 3A) and posttreatment EBV DNA (P = .389; Figure 3B) did not present statistical significance. Neither the PINK score (P = .650; Figure 3C) nor the PINK-E score (P = .315; Figure 3D) demonstrated prognostic discrimination. Treatment modality did not affect OS, although patients treated with CCRT-CTX showed a favorable tendency compared with those treated with CCRT alone (P = .115; Figure 3E). The use of l-asparaginase (P = .435, Figure 3F) and the choice of chemotherapy regimen (P = .761; Figure 3G) were not associated with OS differences. The patients who achieved CR with pretreatment EBV DNA negativity had the most favorable OS. In contrast, patients who did not achieve non-CR with EBV positivity experienced the worst outcomes (P < .001; Figure 3H). When stratified by both response status and posttreatment EBV DNA, patients achieving CR with EBV negativity tended to have the best OS. Still, patients who did not achieve CR with persistent EBV positivity showed the poorest survival. However, the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .061; Figure 3I). In the univariate analysis for OS, poor performance status (HR, 2.064; 95% CI, 1.018-4.187; P = .045) was significantly associated with inferior OS. In multivariate analysis, variables did not show statistical differences for OS (Table 2).

Relapse patterns

Disease progression occurred in 54 patients, including 49 in the CCRT-CTX group and 5 in the CCRT-alone group. Among the CCRT-alone group, 3 patients experienced disease progression immediately after completion of CCRT. After progression, subsequent treatments consisted of chemotherapy (n = 38), chemoradiotherapy (n = 14), or no further therapy (n = 2; supplemental Figure 1). Of these 54 patients, the median PFS2 after first relapse was 6.1 months (95% CI, 1.0-11.2; Figure 4A). At the time of analysis, 65.2% of patients remained free of disease progression, whereas 34.8% (n = 54) developed disease progression (Figure 4B). Of these 54 patients, extranodal distant sites were the most common pattern (n = 33 [21.3%]) followed by primary sites and local lymph nodes involvement (n = 10 [6.5%]), primary sites recurrence (n = 6 [3.9%]), and combined primary sites with extranodal distant sites (n = 5 [3.2%]; Figure 4C). Accordingly, the LCR was 86.5%, indicating that most treatment failures occurred outside the primary sites.

PFS2 did not differ significantly between patients with recurrence at primary vs nonprimary sites (P = .512; Figure 4D) or among subgroups classified as primary sites, primary sites with local lymph nodes, primary sites with extranodal distant sites, and extranodal distant sites (P = .511; Figure 4E). A Sankey diagram further illustrated the treatment flow after relapse, showing that patients most frequently received systemic chemotherapy (n = 40) or RT, including definitive RT (n = 11), reirradiation (n = 14), and palliative RT (n = 3; Figure 4F).

Among 14 patients treated with CCRT alone, 5 patients (35.7%) experienced relapse: 1 with local recurrence at the primary site and 4 with extranodal distant site recurrence involving the bone, adrenal gland, testis, skin, and breast, respectively (data not presented). In addition, 3 patients developed secondary malignancies during follow-up. Two patients were in the CCRT-CTX group: 1 developed breast cancer and died 8 months after the diagnosis of ENKTL, whereas another developed melanoma 130 months later and subsequently died. One patient in the CCRT-alone group developed bladder cancer 26 months after ENKTL diagnosis and died thereafter.

PRO

Among long-term survivors, 32 disease-free survivors were eligible to receive the PRO questionnaire; the remaining survivors could not be contacted due to relapses, completion of follow-up, or follow-up in other hospitals or departments. The median total SNOT-22 score among 18 long-term survivors was 8 (range, 0-76), consistent with the level observed in healthy controls (<10; supplemental Table 3). Among nasal symptoms, most patients had minimal complaints, with scores of >2 points observed in <28% across all categories. Postnasal drip (n = 5 [27.8%]) and nasal obstruction (n = 4 [22.2%]) were the most frequently reported, whereas other nasal symptoms such as need to blow nose (n = 3 [16.7%]), nasal obstruction (n = 4 [22.2%]), loss of smell or taste (n = 4 [22.2%]), sneezing (n = 2 [11.1%]), running nose (n = 3 [16.7%]), and thick nasal discharge (n = 3 [16.7%]) were uncommon (supplemental Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the long-term outcomes of 155 patients with limited-stage ENKTL treated with a 40 Gy RT combined with concurrent cisplatin, followed by systemic chemotherapy in most patients. At a median follow-up of 73 months, the 5-year PFS, OS, and LCR were 68.0%, 82.2%, and 86.5%, respectively. These survival outcomes and local control rates are comparable to those reported in previous studies using higher RT doses of 50 Gy or more (Table 3). Systemic chemotherapy after CCRT significantly improved PFS, and more than half of patients with PR after CCRT achieved CR with subsequent systemic chemotherapy. Thus, these findings demonstrate that a 40 Gy CCRT followed by a systemic chemotherapy strategy provides durable local control and favorable long-term survival, supporting the feasibility of a 40 Gy RT dose in patients with limited-stage ENKTL.

Our results suggest that cisplatin may play a crucial role as an effective radiosensitizer, enabling a reduction in RT dose without compromising disease control. Historically, international guidelines have recommended higher RT doses (50-56 Gy), because earlier studies demonstrated a dose-response relationship between RT dose and local control in limited-stage ENKTL.11,29,30 However, our study, together with the report by Oh et al, indicates that cisplatin-based 40 Gy CCRT achieves survival outcomes comparable with those obtained with higher RT doses (50-56 Gy), while potentially reducing long-term toxicities. The radiosensitizer effect of cisplatin, which augments radiation-induced cytotoxicity and DNA damage,19 likely accounts for the durable local control observed without the need for higher radiation doses. These findings support the feasibility of 40 Gy RT in selected patients with stage I/II disease when combined with radiosensitizing cisplatin.15

The addition of systemic chemotherapy after CCRT provided a significant survival advantage. These findings are consistent with previous reports. Oh et al demonstrated favorable outcomes with 40 Gy CCRT followed by consolidation chemotherapy.18 At the same time, Zhu et al reported 5-year PFS and OS rates of 89.4% and 93.3%, respectively, with upfront CCRT plus pegaspargase, gemcitabine, dexamethasone, and cisplatin.31 Moreover, the risk-adapted therapy study by Yang et al showed that CCRT with subsequent chemotherapy led to encouraging survival in early-stage ENKTL.32 They suggested that RT alone might be sufficient in selected low-risk patients, as assessed by 5 categories, including age of >60 years, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 2 to 4, stage II, and site of primary tumor invasion. However, this risk stratification did not incorporate more recently validated prognostic markers such as PINK, PINK-E, or EBV DNA status. Given that these biomarkers have proven prognostic significance, relying solely on earlier risk models to select patients for RT alone may be insufficient, and systemic therapy should still be considered even in apparently lower-risk disease. Therefore, these data reinforce the treatment strategy that CCRT or RT alone is likely insufficient for optimal long-term disease control in limited-stage ENKTL, and that consolidation systemic therapy should be considered a standard component of multimodal strategies. However, because the CCRT-alone group included only 14 patients, the observed survival benefit associated with the addition of systemic chemotherapy should be interpreted cautiously and warrants confirmation in larger cohorts.

Moreover, according to the analysis of relapse patterns, most treatment failures occurred at extranodal distant sites (21.3%), whereas primary site recurrence was relatively uncommon (13.5%). Consequently, the LCR was 86.5%, underscoring the effectiveness of 40 Gy CCRT in achieving durable local control. However, the predominance of systemic relapse emphasizes the need for systemic therapy to complement CCRT. The observation that consolidation chemotherapy not only converts PR to CR but also mitigates the risk of systemic relapse further highlights its importance in achieving durable disease control. Thus, the predominance of distant extranodal relapse highlights the need for systemic control; however, we were unable to identify strong predictors of systemic relapse, which should be addressed in further studies.

The role of l-asparaginase in limited-stage ENKTL has been highlighted in several studies, which report favorable outcomes when asparaginase-based chemotherapy is incorporated alongside RT. Multiple phase 2 trials have demonstrated high CR rates and encouraging survival in limited-stage patients treated with upfront CCRT followed by l-asparaginase–containing regimens.2,3,16,17,33,34 These results suggest that asparaginase-containing regimens may contribute to improved systemic control even in limited-stage ENKTL. However, in our study, l-asparaginase–containing consolidation did not provide a statistically significant advantage in PFS or OS. Several factors may explain this observation.

First, all patients received highly effective 40 Gy CCRT with weekly cisplatin, achieving excellent local control and favorable long-term survival, which may have reduced the marginal impact attributable to the l-asparaginase–containing regimen. Second, treatment selection bias inherent to retrospective studies cannot be excluded. Chemotherapy containing l-asparaginase was more often chosen in contemporary practice. It may have been preferentially used in patients perceived to have higher systemic relapse risk, thereby attenuating any survival difference despite similar recorded baseline characteristics. Third, temporal evolution in treatment practice contributed to differences between groups. From 2004 to 2012, 3D-RT was predominantly used, whereas IMRT became routine in later years. During the same period, L-asparaginase–containing consolidation regimens were increasingly adopted in contemporary practice. Consequently, the higher frequency of IMRT in the l-asparaginase group reflects era-dependent treatment patterns rather than underlying clinical differences, and this temporal effect may have attenuated any relative advantage attributable to l-asparaginase in our cohort. Finally, our retrospective design and the number of events limit the statistical power to detect modest differences in survival between chemotherapy subgroups.

Taken together, our findings do not challenge the well-established role of l-asparaginase in advanced or high-risk ENKTL. Instead, they suggest that in the specific context of 40 Gy CCRT followed by nonanthracycline chemotherapy, the incremental survival benefit attributable to l-asparaginase may be less pronounced. This effect likely varies according to individual patients’ risk profiles and the sequencing of therapy. Prospective, risk-adapted studies will be required to determine which subsets of limited-stage ENKTL derive the most significant marginal benefit from l-asparaginase–containing chemotherapy.

Cho et al built on recent immune subtyping studies, which revealed favorable responses to programmed death-ligand 1 blockades in limited-stage ENKTL with an immune-tolerant microenvironment.35 These observations have prompted interest in whether immunotherapy could eventually be integrated into RT-based combined-modality strategies. Thus, we initiated a phase 2 study of concurrent tislelizumab (a monoclonal antibody targeting the programmed cell death protein 1 receptor) and RT for limited-stage ENKTL.24 As our research underscores the central role of a 40 Gy RT in achieving durable locoregional control, an emerging question is whether immune checkpoint inhibition might function as an alternative radiosensitizing approach within this framework. We expect that this strategy may represent a new therapeutic paradigm by integrating immunotherapy into the backbone of chemoradiation and addressing both locoregional and systemic disease control.

In the long-term follow-up, the incidence of secondary malignancies was low, and no treatment-related deaths were reported. Moreover, PROs reported minimal sinonasal symptoms, with a median total SNOT-22 score of 8 (range, 0-76), comparable with that in healthy controls. Less than 30% of patients reported clinically significant nasal symptoms (>2 points), and moderate-to-severe symptoms were rare. These findings collectively suggest that deescalating RT to 40 Gy not only maintained favorable disease control but also minimized late toxicities, supporting its potential as a less toxic yet effective frontline strategy for limited-stage ENKTL. However, our PRO findings should be interpreted with caution. Among long-term survivors, only 32 patients who were disease-free remained under active follow-up within our department and were eligible for assessment, of whom 18 responded (56.3%). Most survivors could not be contacted because they had completed follow-up or were receiving follow-up care in other hospitals or departments. Consequently, the PRO sample represents a selected subset of survivors, introducing potential nonresponse and selection bias. Respondents may be healthier or less symptomatic than nonrespondents, which limits the generalizability of these quality-of-life results.

The strengths and innovations of this study include its large real-world cohort with long-term follow-up, the systematic comparison of consolidation regimens including l-asparaginase–based therapy, the evaluation of outcomes across 2 RT eras (3D-RT and IMRT), and the integration of EBV DNA dynamics as a prospective factor. These features distinguish our findings from previous studies evaluating 40 Gy CCRT. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that 40 Gy of RT with concurrent cisplatin, followed by systemic chemotherapy, achieves durable local control and favorable long-term survival in limited-stage ENKTL, with outcomes comparable with those reported with higher RT doses. The achievement of CR with posttreatment EBV negativity emerged as a practical prognostic marker. Although CCRT alone provides effective local disease control, consolidation chemotherapy is essential to minimize systemic relapse. Significantly, a reduced-dose regimen was associated with minimal long-term sinonasal toxicity and a low incidence of secondary malignancies, supporting 40 Gy CCRT as a safe and effective frontline approach. Further strategies that incorporate immunotherapy as a radiosensitizer or a consolidation agent may establish a new paradigm for treating limited-stage ENKTL.
