Abstract

Patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) are at an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and bleeding. We aimed to compare the risk of recurrent VTE and bleeding between direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and warfarin in patients with IBD and VTE. The study included adults with IBD and VTE who were newly prescribed DOACs or warfarin. The primary study outcomes were recurrent VTE and a composite of serious bleeding. We used Cox proportional hazard model after propensity score matching to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In a secondary analysis, we compared the risk of bleeding in patients on DOACs with IBD vs patients without IBD. Our matched IBD cohort of patients initiating oral anticoagulants comprised 2174 patients (1087 DOAC users and 1087 warfarin users). After propensity score matching, DOAC use (compared with warfarin) was associated with a lower risk of bleeding (HR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.41-0.85) with no difference in the risk of recurrent VTE (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.59-1.15). In the secondary analysis of patients with VTE who received DOACs, there was a twofold increase in the risk of bleeding in patients with IBD compared with those without IBD (HR, 1.99; 95% CI, 1.64-2.41). In patients with IBD and VTE, DOAC use was associated with a lower risk of bleeding compared with warfarin, without a significant difference in the risk of recurrent VTE. However, even with the use of DOACs, patients with IBD were twice as likely to experience serious bleeding as those without IBD, highlighting the importance of close monitoring.

Introduction

Nearly 0.7% of Americans have been diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD),1 which corresponds to 2.4 to 2.7 million adults. IBD, comprising ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD), is a chronic condition characterized by inflammation of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. Beyond the GI manifestations, patients with IBD face an elevated risk of extraintestinal complications, including thrombosis.2 Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), presents a significant burden in the IBD population.3 Compared with individuals without IBD, patients with IBD have a twofold to threefold increased risk of developing VTE.4,5 The burden of VTE in patients with IBD extends beyond the initial event, resulting in a high risk of recurrence,6 mortality,7, 8, 9 and substantial cost.10 Previous research shows that 33% of patients with IBD with VTE develop recurrent events.6 VTE-related hospitalizations and the average length of hospital stay in patients with IBD are rising.3 Hospitalized patients with IBD who develop VTE incur an additional $14 939 per admission compared with those without VTE.10 Given the rising prevalence of known risk factors of VTE, including age and obesity, the burden of VTE in patients with IBD is likely to increase.

VTE events often present in the outpatient setting,11, 12, 13 with most occurring within the first few months after hospital discharge.14 Anticoagulants can reduce the risk of VTE but are associated with increased bleeding risk. This is particularly relevant in IBD, given that GI bleeding is a cardinal manifestation of the disease, particularly UC.15 Yet, there is limited evidence regarding the anticoagulant-associated bleeding risk in IBD and the relative safety of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) compared with warfarin in patients with IBD. This is particularly relevant to patients with IBD because, for those experiencing a VTE in the absence of a clear inciting incident, indefinite anticoagulation may be considered.16 Also, IBD alters the gut microbiome, potentially affecting the absorption and bioavailability of orally administered drugs and the sensitivity to vitamin K antagonists.17 These alterations may have implications for the pharmacokinetics of oral anticoagulants, potentially increasing the risk of bleeding complications and/or breakthrough thrombosis. Therefore, we aimed to compare the risk of recurrent VTE and serious bleeding between DOACs and warfarin use in patients with IBD and VTE. In a secondary analysis, we evaluated bleeding risk attributable to DOAC use, comparing patients with IBD with those without IBD.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study analyzed deidentified, patient-level healthcare claims data from MarketScan IBM (1 January 2015 to 31 December 2022). The data include >200 million unique patients in the United States who are employees or recent retirees and their dependents insured by employer-sponsored plans. The IBM MarketScan Commercial and Medicare Supplemental Databases capture administrative claims data from a large, diverse population across the United States and have been shown to broadly reflect the demographic distribution of the insured US population.18 The data provide deidentified information on patient demographic characteristics, hospital admission, dates of enrollment and disenrollment in healthcare plans, outpatient visits, healthcare use cost, and out-of-hospital prescription drug claims. MarketScan IBM has been extensively used to examine healthcare costs, delivery, use, and health outcomes.

Study population

We emulated a hypothetical target trial using a retrospective active comparator cohort design to examine the risk of recurrent VTE and serious bleeding events among new users of DOACs vs warfarin in patients with IBD and VTE (supplemental Figure 1; supplemental Table 1). We selected warfarin as the referent control group because it (1) has been widely used as the standard-of-care anticoagulant in previous approval trials and is used routinely for prevention of recurrent VTE in real-world settings; and (2) serves as an active comparator, eliminating reliance on nonusers as a control because of concerns of confounding by indication and immortal time bias. The cohorts consisted of patients who were aged ≥18 years, were naïve to anticoagulant therapy, initiated treatment with DOACs or warfarin between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2022, had continuous enrollment in medical and pharmacy benefits for 12 months before treatment initiation (ie, lookback period), had a diagnosis of VTE before anticoagulant initiation (supplemental Table 1), and had a diagnosis of IBD including UC and CD (international classification of disease clinical-modification [ICD-CM] codes of UC, 556.XX, K51.XX, and CD, 555.XX, K50.XX). Diagnosis codes for IBD were previously validated against medical chart records and were found to have a high sensitivity (92% for CD and 84% for UC) and specificity (99% for both CD and UC).19 Similarly, diagnosis codes of VTE were found to have a positive predictive value of 95% (95% confidence interval [CI], 93-97).20

A secondary analysis was designed to assess the increased risk of DOAC-associated bleeding in patients with VTE, comparing patients with IBD vs patients without IBD (supplemental Table 2). This analysis included patients aged ≥18 years who had at least 12 months of continuous enrollment in medical and pharmacy benefits, along with a new prescription for DOACs and a diagnosis of VTE before the initiation of treatment. The primary comparison focused on patients with VTE and IBD vs those with VTE without IBD.

Exposure ascertainment

We used information obtained from outpatient pharmacy prescription claims to define exposure groups. Outpatient pharmacy prescription claims include the National Drug Code, which serves as a universal identifier for medications in the United States. DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, and edoxaban) were the primary exposure of interest, and warfarin represented the reference group. Patients were classified into oral anticoagulant exposure categories based on the index oral anticoagulant prescription. The follow-up period started on the initiation date and ended upon treatment discontinuation, which was defined as the presence of a gap of >7 days between contiguous refills, a switch to the comparator medication, development of study outcomes, loss of eligibility in the health care plan, or the study end date of 31 December 2022.

Outcome ascertainment

The primary effectiveness outcome was recurrent VTE, defined as a composite of DVT and PE identified using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes listed in the primary position in inpatient discharge claims. The primary study outcome was serious bleeding-related hospitalization.21,22 Bleeding-related hospitalizations were identified from hospital principal discharge diagnosis codes, suggesting the reason for admission using previously validated algorithms21,22 and included a composite of intracranial, gastrointestinal (eg, gastroduodenal, esophageal, uppergastrointestinal bleeding, and lower gastrointestinal bleeding), genitourinary, and other bleeding events (supplemental Table 1). We examined the effectiveness outcome by individual components, including DVT and PE for VTE. Similarly, we examined the components of the bleeding outcome, including gastrointestinal, intracranial, hematuria, and other bleeding events.

Covariates

We identified 97 covariates, selected through a combination of literature review and expert input, which may be associated with the study outcomes and oral anticoagulant therapy, as detailed in Table 1 and supplemental Table 3. These covariates included demographics (eg, age and sex), insurance type, baseline comorbidities (eg, angina, anemia, hip or knee replacement, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, liver disease, peptic ulcer disease, pulmonary heart disease, stroke, and trauma), baseline medications (eg, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, beta-blockers, biologics, direct vasodilators, Janus kinase inhibitors, mesalamine, proton pump inhibitors, statins, corticosteroids, and sulfasalazine), measures of frailty (eg, wheelchair use/ limitations, and use of cane), and measures of health care use (mean hospitalizations, outpatient visits, outpatient prescriptions, and procedures). The selection of these variables was informed by previous research on oral anticoagulants and used validated case definition algorithms when available.23

Statistical analyses

We controlled for all 97 baseline covariates in our propensity score model. The propensity score was defined as the probability of initiating DOACs vs warfarin given baseline covariates. We estimated the propensity score using logistic regression models. After constructing propensity score models, we randomly sorted the data to select possible matches defined as observations that are within a caliper width of 0.1 deviations of the logit of the propensity score. We used 1:1 matching in our primary analyses. We examined the success of the matching process by calculating standardized differences with differences of ≤0.10, suggesting an acceptable match. We created love plots, a dotted plot that shows variable names on the y-axis and standardized mean differences on the x-axis. Each dot represents the standardized mean difference of a specific variable before and after matching. We reported the incidence of the effectiveness and safety outcomes per 100 person-years. The marginal hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated in the propensity score matched cohorts using Cox proportional hazards regression. Dependencies in the matched samples were corrected using robust variance estimation.

Sensitivity analyses examined the impact of alternative study design decisions and statistical analyses on study results. Cohort entry criteria were modified to exclude individuals with extreme propensity score observations by trimming the 95th and 5th percentiles and the 85th and 15th percentiles. We challenged our choice of a caliper width of 0.1 by using 0.02 and 0.01 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. We used alternative estimands such as average treatment effects, and average treatment effects on the treated. We conducted an intention-to-treat analysis, removing censoring due to treatment discontinuation. We assessed the potential for effect modification within selected subgroups by including an interaction term in the primary models. We also conducted sensitivity analyses adjusting for IBD-related surgeries, use of oral contraceptives, the calendar year of cohort entry, and evidence of flare of IBD, as described hereafter. Clinically relevant subgroups of interest included IBD flares and IBD subtypes (UC vs CD). Because VTE occurs more commonly during IBD flares, we identified patients more likely to be experiencing an IBD flare based on meeting any of the following criteria: (1) new use of biologic therapy; (2) systemic corticosteroids; (3) colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy; (4) magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis or pelvis alone; or (5) testing for Clostridium difficile. We used Bonferroni adjustment to account for multiple testing within subgroup analyses. We considered results statistically significant if the P value for interaction was ≤.05/n, in which “n” equals the total number of subgroup analyses. Lastly, to assess the potential for unmeasured confounding, we calculated and reported the E-value. The E-value provides the minimum needed strength of association between an unmeasured confounder and both the exposure and the study outcomes to move the observed effect estimates toward the null value of 1, conditional on measured confounders (www.evalue-calculator.com).24 All analyses were done using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

The study was approved by the Vanderbilt University School of Medicine institutional review board (230922).

Results

We identified a total of 4007 users of DOACs and 1383 users of warfarin (Table 1). The distribution of propensity scores before and after matching is presented in supplemental Figure 2. After propensity score matching, we included 1087 users of DOACs and 1087 users of warfarin. Baseline characteristics of the 2 groups were well balanced after matching, with similar age (49 years for DOACs vs 49 years for warfarin) and comparable prevalence of comorbid conditions: angina (2% vs 2%), heart failure (7% vs 7%), hip or knee replacement (6% vs 5%), iron-deficiency anemia (20% vs 19%), peripheral vascular disease (5% vs 5%), pulmonary valve disease (1% vs 1%), stroke (6% vs 5%), and trauma (1% vs 2%; Table 1; supplemental Table 3; supplemental Figure 3). The use of other medications was also similar, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (12% vs 12%), beta-blockers (19% vs 18%), biologics (2% vs 2%), direct vasodilators (1% vs 1%), mesalamine (19% vs 21%), statins (20% vs 18%), corticosteroids (2% vs 2%), and sulfasalazine (4% vs 4%). Measures of health care use during the 12-month lookback period were comparable between DOAC and warfarin users, such as mean hospitalizations (2 vs 2), outpatient visits (23 vs 23), outpatient prescription drug claims (26 vs 26), and procedures (0.1 vs 0.1). All covariates remained well balanced after matching, as indicated by standardized differences of <0.1.

The median follow-up duration was 107 days (with 25th and 75th percentiles at 46 and 225 days, respectively) for DOAC users, compared with 110 days (25th and 75th percentiles: 42 and 250 days) for warfarin users. Reasons for censoring participants included treatment discontinuation (72% for DOACs, and 67% for warfarin); initiation of, or switching to, the study comparator (1% for DOAC, and 8% for warfarin); and end of enrollment (21% for DOAC, and 17% for warfarin).

Rate of recurrent VTE comparing patients with IBD using DOACs vs warfarin

In the matched sample, 65 patients had recurrent VTE events among 1087 DOAC users (11.0 events per 100 person-years) compared with 80 among 1087 warfarin users (12.7 per 100 person-years; HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.59-1.15; Table 2; Figure 1A). Results were consistent for DOACs vs warfarin for PE (HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.42-1.21) and DVT (HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.59-1.40).

Rate of serious bleeding events comparing patients with IBD using DOACs vs warfarin

In the matched sample, 49 patients had serious bleeding events among 1087 DOAC users (8.2 events per 100 person-years) compared with 84 among 1087 warfarin users (13.6 events per 100 person-years; HR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.41-0.85; Table 2; Figure 1B). Results were similar for DOACs vs warfarin for gastrointestinal bleeding (HR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.35-0.92). Results were consistent for other types of bleeding, although the results crossed the null value of 1 for intracranial hemorrhage (HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.11-4.21), hematuria (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.33-1.46), and other bleeding events (HR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.26-1.51).

Rate of serious bleeding events comparing DOAC users with IBD vs patients without IBD

Before matching, we identified a total of 119 100 patients with VTE who were new users of DOACs (4007 with IBD and 115 093 without IBD; supplemental Table 4). The matched cohort included 7894 DOAC users (3947 with IBD vs 3947 without IBD). Most baseline characteristics were relatively comparable between patients with IBD and those without IBD before matching, including age (48 years vs 48 years) and presence of comorbid conditions, namely, angina (2% vs 2%), heart failure (6% vs 5%), hip or knee replacement (7% vs 7%), peripheral vascular disease (5% vs 4%), and pulmonary valve disease (1% vs 1%). All covariates remained well balanced after matching, as indicated by standardized differences of <0.1. The incidence rates of bleeding in patients with IBD were higher than in patients without IBD (11.2 vs 5.8 per 100 person-years, respectively; supplemental Table 5). In the matched cohort, the risk of bleeding was higher among patients with IBD than patients without IBD (HR, 1.99; 95% CI, 1.64-2.41).

Results from sensitivity and subgroup analyses

The sensitivity and subgroup analyses produced consistent results with the primary findings (supplemental Table 6). This included trimming the tail of the propensity score distribution, adjusting the caliper width, and modifying the estimand to average treatment effects and average treatment effects on the treated. Our results remained consistent using an intention-to-treat approach, for which we did not censor patients when they discontinued treatment. Results remained consistent after adjusting for calendar year of cohort entry for both effectiveness (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.53-1.09) and safety (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.38-0.84). Also, results remained consistent when adjusting for IBD flares in the outcome model for effectiveness (HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.60-1.17) and safety (HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.43-0.87).

Additionally, an examination of effect modification by the presence of IBD flares and IBD subtypes showed nonsignificant P values for interaction, with results indicating P values of .08 for VTE and .17 for bleeding. Similarly, the P values for interaction by IBD subtypes were .28 for VTE and .15 for bleeding. The E-value corresponding to the lower bound for the serious bleeding outcome was 1.63, and for the point estimate was 2.78 (supplemental Figure 4). The observed CI for serious bleeding events could be moved to include the null value of 1 by an unmeasured confounder that was associated with both exposure and the effectiveness outcome by a risk ratio of 1.67-fold each, above and beyond the measured confounders included in the propensity score model, but weaker confounding could not do so. These values suggest that an unmeasured confounder would have to have a moderate association with the choice of anticoagulant and bleeding to explain the results.19 Results for E-value calculations are presented in supplemental Figure 4.

Discussion

In this target trial emulation using an active comparator design of patients with IBD and VTE, the use of DOACs compared with warfarin was associated with a lower incidence of serious bleeding complications, including GI bleeding, with no difference in the risk of recurrent VTE. These findings remained robust across several sensitivity and subgroup analyses, including evaluations of effect modification by IBD subtype (CD vs UC) and by the presence of active disease flares. Although there are some concerns that IBD may affect oral drug absorption due to impairment of the mucosal barrier, potentially affecting the effectiveness of DOACs, our findings offer reassuring evidence. Our analytical approach of patients with VTE using DOACs revealed a twofold increase in bleeding in patients with IBD compared with patients without IBD. Thus, although our findings support the use of DOACs over warfarin, patients with IBD receiving DOAC therapy should be carefully monitored for signs of GI bleeding.

Our findings support the use of DOACs as a safe therapeutic option in patients with IBD for the prevention of recurrent events. Unlike warfarin, DOACs have relatively short half-lives, fewer drug-food interactions, fewer drug-drug interactions, fixed dosing, and do not require routine laboratory monitoring. These advantages are particularly relevant to patients with IBD who have an increased prevalence of immune-mediated disease, pulmonary disease, ischemic heart disease, and cancer, leading to polypharmacy and potentially increasing the risk of interactions between drugs. Our findings underscore the distinct clinical characteristics of patients with IBD with VTE relative to the broader VTE population. Compared with previous analyses of general VTE cohorts,25 patients with IBD and VTE were notably younger (mean age, 49 vs 68 years) and had a lower prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities, including heart failure (7% vs 19%), hyperlipidemia (29% vs 47%), hypertension (44% vs 69%), and previous stroke (5% vs 18%). Despite this lower burden of comorbidity, the risk of bleeding events remained substantial, reflecting the inherent hemorrhagic risk associated with IBD.

This target trial emulation examined the comparative effectiveness and safety of DOACs and warfarin among patients with IBD and VTE. Although VTE is a relatively common complication of IBD, it remains sufficiently rare that an adequately powered prospective clinical trial comparing DOACs with warfarin in this population is unlikely to be feasible. Thus, our target trial emulation provides the best available evidence for selecting an anticoagulant in this clinical setting. We used advanced pharmacoepidemiologic methodologies to estimate the burden of serious bleeding associated with oral anticoagulant use in patients with IBD. The propensity score matching method was used, incorporating a wide range of confounders, including demographics, comorbidities, and medications, to minimize the potential for measured confounding. We have used similar methods in populations without IBD and observed similar results with DOACs having a better safety and effectiveness profile. Despite these strengths, there are potential limitations to observational studies. First, although we adjusted for many covariates in our model, key clinical variables related to IBD severity and activity were incompletely captured or absent in the MarketScan IBM data. For instance, laboratory values such as C-reactive protein and fecal calprotectin, critical indicators of disease severity, were available for <20 patients. Although we relied on surrogate indicators such as the use of biologics, systemic corticosteroids, endoscopic procedures, cross-sectional imaging, and testing for C difficile infection, these proxies may have missed or misclassified disease activity in a subset of the cohort. Furthermore, variables such as disease duration, endoscopic findings, over-the-counter medications, lifestyle factors, and international normalized ratio (for patients on warfarin) were not captured in the current data. Although we used the E-value to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding in our study, this method has limitations, including the lack of clear guidelines for interpreting its magnitude and the fact that it does not account for other potential biases beyond unmeasured confounding.26 Second, there is potential for outcome misclassification. We focused on bleeding events that resulted in hospitalization, with previous studies having shown a lower positive predictive value for events captured in outpatient settings. Bleeding that requires hospitalization is more clinically relevant. Moreover, the use of insurance claims data for this study has advantages over electronic health records because patients with VTE or serious bleeding may be hospitalized at their nearest hospital rather than where they get their usual care. As such, insurance claims would be expected to have greater sensitivity to capture the outcomes of interest. Third, our study focused on adults aged ≥18 years with IBD and VTE. Because ∼10% of patients with IBD are aged <18 years, the relative effectiveness and safety of anticoagulants and the incremental risk of bleeding in pediatric patients warrant investigation. Similarly, there are relatively few older patients in the data, and the generalizability of the results to that population is unknown. Fourth, our study aimed to compare DOACs vs warfarin, and, therefore, the relative effectiveness and safety of individual DOACs, such as apixaban and rivaroxaban, warrant further investigation. Fifth, because the median follow-up was only 107 days, our findings primarily reflect short-term outcomes and do not capture potential long-term effects. Our findings are generalizable to individuals with employer-sponsored commercial or Medicare Supplemental insurance and may not extend to populations with other types of health coverage or to uninsured individuals.

Conclusion

In patients with IBD and VTE, DOAC use was associated with a lower risk of bleeding compared with warfarin, without a significant difference in the risk of recurrent VTE. However, even with the use of DOACs, patients with IBD were twice as likely to experience serious bleeding as those without IBD, highlighting the importance of close monitoring.
