Key Points

  • •

    Compared with 7+3, CPX-351 improves OS in patients with AML-MR gene mutations who meet the enrollment criteria of the pivotal trial.

  • •

    There is no OS benefit of CPX-351 among enrolled patients with TP53 mutations, irrespective of allelic state.

Visual Abstract

Abstract

CPX-351 was approved for the treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) using now-outdated definitions of AML with myelodysplasia-related changes (AML-MRC) and therapy-related AML. We evaluated whether the overall survival (OS) benefit of CPX-351 over standard cytarabine plus anthracycline (7+3) therapy is confined to molecularly defined AML subgroups by performing DNA sequencing in 184 patients enrolled in the pivotal phase 3 randomized trial. Patients were categorized hierarchically based on gene mutations: (1) TP53-AML, (2) DDX41-AML, (3) myelodysplasia-related AML (AML-MR) defined by the World Health Organization fifth edition criteria, or (4) other-AML. TP53-AML was subclassified as single (TP53single) or multihit (TP53multi) based on the number of alleles altered via mutation, deletion, or copy neutral loss of heterozygosity. Two-year OS differed significantly across molecular subgroups: TP53-AML (7%), AML-MR (19%), other-AML (37%), and DDX41-AML (70%) (P < .001). CPX-351 improved survival in patients with AML-MR compared with 7+3 (median, 9.7 vs 6.8 months; P = .037), with no benefit in TP53-AML or other-AML. For patients undergoing transplantation, CPX-351 improved 2-year survival (76% vs 27%; P < .01), an effect primarily observed in AML-MR. Multivariable analysis confirmed the independent association with survival of both CPX-351 and hematopoietic cell transplantation in AML-MR. TP53multi demonstrated significantly worse survival than TP53single (median, 3.8 vs 7.0 months; P = .004). The OS benefit of CPX-351 observed in the trial was driven by AML-MR with no benefit of CPX-351 in TP53-AML, in which the primary prognostic factor was allelic state. This trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as #NCT01696084.

Introduction

In a pivotal phase 3 trial, CPX-351 (liposomal cytarabine and daunorubicin) demonstrated improved overall survival (OS) over standard cytarabine plus anthracycline (7+3) therapy in patients aged 60 to 75 years with high-risk and/or secondary acute myeloid leukemia (AML).1,2 The regulatory approvals of CPX-351 were based on the clinical and cytogenetic enrollment criteria from this trial,3 which targeted (1) AML with myelodysplasia-related changes (AML-MRC) as defined either by a clinical history of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) or by the presence of myelodysplasia-related cytogenetic changes and (2) postcytotoxic therapy AML (t-AML). The recognition that AML genetic alterations drive disease biology and responsiveness to treatment has prompted revision of diagnostic categories.4, 5, 6 Therefore, the disease subtypes that underlie the original trial enrollment criteria have been supplanted by classification models that prioritize genetic features over clinical history.

The design of the phase 3 trial was informed by a planned subgroup analysis of a prior randomized phase 2 study, in which the CPX-351 benefit compared to standard induction was primarily observed in patients with an antecedent MDS/myeloproliferative neoplasm or cytotoxic therapy exposure.7 Although the benefit of CPX-351 in the phase 3 trial was observed across clinical subgroups, the trial could not account for defining genetic alterations now recognized in contemporary classification systems, namely myelodysplasia-related (AML-MR) mutations in SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2, ASXL1, EZH2, BCOR, or STAG2, which are highly specific for secondary AML,8 germ line DDX41 mutations associated with favorable outcomes,9, 10, 11, 12, 13 and TP53 mutations, which mediate treatment resistance and adverse prognosis among patients with AML after prior cytotoxic exposure.4,14, 15, 16 Among the previously heterogeneous clinical category of secondary AML, AML-MR, DDX41-AML, and TP53-AML now define distinct populations characterized by differential chemosensitivity and response to emerging therapies such as venetoclax-based regimens.17, 18, 19, 20

Although CPX-351 is approved for the treatment of newly diagnosed t-AML and AML-MRC, its efficacy across molecularly defined subgroups has not been established. We therefore performed a comprehensive genetic analysis of samples from the randomized phase 3 CPX-351 vs 7+3 trial,1 reclassifying patients according to contemporary molecular criteria to determine whether the observed survival benefit is restricted to specific genetically defined AML subgroups. Our findings demonstrate that the benefit of CPX-351 over 7+3 is primarily confined to patients with AML-MR mutations who proceeded to hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT), whereas no significant benefit was observed for patients with TP53 mutations regardless of allelic state.

Methods

Clinical cohort

We obtained baseline samples from the CLTR0310-301 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01696084),1 a multicenter, randomized phase 3 study comparing CPX-351 vs 7+3 in patients aged 60 to 75 years with newly diagnosed high-risk AML defined as follows: (1) documented prior cytotoxic therapy/radiation for an unrelated malignancy, (2) AML with a history of MDS/CMML, or (3) de novo AML with MDS-associated cytogenetic abnormalities.21,22 Diagnostic samples were available from 184 of 309 patients (CPX-351 arm: 93 of 153 [61%]; 7+3 arm: 91 of 156 [58%]; P = .7) (supplemental Figure 1). Baseline clinical characteristics, best response, and survival outcomes were comparable between patients with and without an available sample (supplemental Figure 2; supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Baseline characteristics among sequenced patients are shown in supplemental Table 3, and outcomes between treatment arms by age subgroup are shown in supplemental Figure 3. Clinical outcomes were determined based on the final 5-year follow-up analysis.2 Median follow-up in survivors was 60.2 months.

Genetic analysis

We performed targeted DNA sequencing of 113 genes recurrently mutated in myeloid malignancies (supplemental Table 4) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), using a variant allele fraction (VAF) cutoff ≥0.02 to call mutations that were then classified as pathogenic based on gene-specific criteria.14,23,24 Detailed sequencing methods are provided in the supplemental Methods. AML with ≥1 TP53 mutation was categorized by allelic state based on mutation number and the presence of TP53 deletion or copy neutral loss of heterozygosity (CN-LOH). Those with ≥2 TP53 mutations or ≥1 point mutation plus TP53 CN-LOH/deletion/chromosome 17/17p deletion were classified as TP53multi, whereas those with a single TP53 point mutation without LOH were classified as TP53single.24,25 DDX41 variants were curated according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines,26 with the application of strong pathogenic criteria for missense and in-frame indels (insertions and deletions) associated in the literature with a second somatic DDX41 variant similar to prior studies.27 KMT2A partial tandem duplications and FLT3 internal tandem duplications (ITD) were identified as previously described.28,29 The annotation of mutations was blinded to clinical characteristics, and genetic analysis was locked before merging with clinical data.

Statistical analysis

We performed between-group comparisons by the Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables and by the Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Complete remission (CR) and CR with incomplete count recovery (CRi) were defined according to the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2017 criteria.30 We calculated time to count recovery only in responders, measured from the first day of treatment through nadir until platelet count ≥50 × 109/L (PLT50) or absolute neutrophil count ≥500 × 109/L (ANC500). OS was measured from the randomization date until death from any cause or until censoring at last contact.2 We estimated OS curves using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared them using the log-rank test. We fitted univariable and multivariable Cox proportional regression models for OS to calculate hazard ratios (HR). We followed the events-per-degree-of-freedom principle to avoid overfitting. Our prespecified Cox model included treatment (primary focus of the study), age (major baseline prognostic factor and trial stratum), and allogeneic HCT as a time-dependent covariate (dominant postbaseline determinant of hazard). The impact of HCT on OS was assessed using a time-dependent analysis with Simon-Makuch plots and by integrating transplant as a time-varying covariate in Cox regression analyses.31 We conducted a sensitivity analysis with a unified Cox regression model, stratified by age group and AML clinical subtype, similar to the parent trial. We evaluated molecular group vs treatment interactions in the unified model and estimated differences with global heterogeneity testing.

This study was conducted with the approval of the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Institutional Review Board. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Overall genetic characteristics

The distribution of somatic gene mutations was similar between CPX-351 and 7+3 (supplemental Figure 4; supplemental Table 5). AML-MR gene mutations (60% vs 54%; P = .5) and TP53 mutations (27% vs 41%; P = .061) were common, and NPM1 mutations were uncommon (7% vs 6%; P > .9), consistent with the clinical enrollment criteria focusing on clinically or cytogenetically defined high-risk AML. As expected, most RUNX1 mutations co-occurred with ontogeny-defining AML-MR mutations 88% (43 of 49).8,18,32 RAS/MAPK signaling mutations (NRAS, KRAS, PTPN11, FLT3, CBL, RIT1, and NF1) did not differ between the arms (42% vs 44%; P = .9) and frequently co-occurred with AML-MR mutations (57/79 [72%]) or TP53 mutations (17/79 [22%]). FLT3 alterations included ITD (12% vs 9%; P = .6) and non-ITD mutations (3% vs 7%; P = .3). We identified pathogenic putative germ line DDX41 variants in 6% of patients (n = 11), with 6 having concurrent somatic DDX41 mutations and 1 having a concurrent TP53 mutation.

Molecular reclassification and association with clinical outcomes

We reclassified patients hierarchically into 4 groups: (1) TP53-AML (n = 62 [33.7%]), defined by the presence of ≥1 TP53 mutations; (2) DDX41-AML (n = 10 [5.4%]), defined by pathogenic or likely pathogenic DDX41 mutations among patients without TP53 mutations; (3) AML-MR (n = 88 [47.8%]), defined by ≥1 mutations in SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, ZRSR2, ASXL1, BCOR, EZH2, or STAG2,5,8 among patients without TP53 or DDX41 mutations; and (4) other-AML (n = 24 [13.0%]) (Figure 1A; supplemental Figure 5; supplemental Table 6).

Clinical and cytogenetic characteristics varied among the molecular groups (supplemental Table 7). TP53-AML exhibited predominantly poor cytogenetic risk (90%), with frequent complex (72%) and monosomal (65%) karyotypes. Patients with DDX41-AML were predominantly male (80%), younger (90%, aged 60-69 years), and had uniformly nonadverse cytogenetics. AML-MR was largely male (75%), with mostly intermediate cytogenetic risk (60%) but nearly universally adverse ELN 2022 risk (95%), frequently arising after antecedent MDS or CMML (69%) and prior hypomethylating agent treatment (52%). The other-AML group was predominantly female (75%), with a relatively high frequency of favorable ELN 2022 risk (35%).

Molecular characteristics varied across the clinically defined AML subgroups. In AML-MRC with antecedent hematological malignancy, the most prevalent group was AML-MR (60%), whereas TP53-AML was less common (18%); most cases with DDX41-AML were in this group (80%). De novo AML with MDS cytogenetics was composed predominantly of TP53-AML (53%) and AML-MR (40%), whereas t-AML showed a high prevalence of TP53 mutations (54%), with the remaining cases distributed across the AML-MR (23%) and other-AML (20%) groups (Figure 1A). Although clinical groups showed no survival differences (Figure 1B; P = .6), molecular subgroups demonstrated markedly different CR rates and 2-year OS: TP53-AML (7%), DDX41-AML (70%), AML-MR (19%), and other-AML (37%) (supplemental Table 8; Figure 1C).

OS by genetics and treatment

A global test of interaction demonstrated that treatment effects differed significantly across molecular groups (χ2[3] = 18.32; P = .0004), confirming substantial heterogeneity in the impact of CPX-351 by genetic subgroups (supplemental Figure 6). We therefore analyzed outcomes by treatment arm within each molecular category.

CR/CRi rates varied significantly across molecular groups (TP53-AML, 35%; DDX41-AML, 100%; AML-MR, 38%; and other-AML, 63%; P < .001) but did not differ between treatment arms within each group (supplemental Tables 8 and 9). In AML-MR, OS was significantly longer with CPX-351 (median, 9.7 months; 95% CI, 6.2-13.7) than with 7+3 (6.8 months; 95% CI, 3.6-9.6; HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.39-0.98; P = .037; Figure 2A). No survival differences between treatment arms were observed in TP53-AML (CPX-351: median OS, 4.5 months; 95% CI, 2.9-7.6; 7+3: median OS, 5.1 months; 95% CI, 2.9-7.3; HR, 1.10; 95% CI, 0.66-1.85; P = .70; Figure 2B) or in other-AML (CPX-351: median OS, 11.3 months; 95% CI, 5.6 to not reached; 7+3: median OS, 16.9 months; 95% CI, 4.0 to not reached; HR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.44-3.00; P = .77; Figure 2C). In DDX41-AML, median OS was 56.4 months (95% CI, 36.6 to not reached) with CPX-351 vs 17.1 months (95% CI, 4.6-not reached) for patients treated with 7+3 (HR, 0.32; 95% CI, 0.07-1.39; P = .11; Figure 2D). In a sensitivity analysis, we performed regression analysis within each molecular group, stratified by age and clinical AML subtype, which showed stable HR, consistent with a benefit of CPX-351 for enrolled patients with AML-MR gene mutations (supplemental Table 10).

In an analysis of survival by treatment arm within the ELN 2022 risk groups, we observed a benefit of CPX-351 over 7+3 solely in the adverse risk group, consistent with a benefit restricted to AML-MR, which is considered adverse risk by ELN 2022 (supplemental Figure 7). Because the World Health Organization and International Consensus Classification frameworks classify AML first by NPM1 before hierarchical assignment to TP53, AML-MR, or other categories, we performed an exploratory analysis treating NPM1-mutated AML as a distinct molecular subgroup. In this analysis, patients with NPM1-AML (n = 11) were mostly from the other-AML group (9/11). NPM1-AML as a separate group demonstrated similar survival to the remaining other-AML group and did not meaningfully alter the overall heterogeneity inference for OS with respect to the AML-MR, TP53, and DDX41 molecular groups (supplemental Figure 8).

Survival benefit restricted to patients with AML-MR who underwent transplantation

We next examined whether the CPX-351 benefit in AML-MR was influenced by the receipt of allogeneic HCT. HCT rates were comparable between CPX-351 and 7+3 overall (30% vs 32%; P = .9) and within each molecular group (supplemental Table 9). To evaluate the impact of the treatment arm on OS before HCT, we conducted a sensitivity analyses censored at transplant, with no differences observed between treatments across the entire cohort or within molecular groups (supplemental Figure 9A-D). However, among patients who underwent transplantation, CPX-351 induction was associated with significantly longer survival (2-year OS, 76% vs 27%), with this benefit observed only in the AML-MR group (supplemental Figure 9B). Time-dependent analysis within the AML-MR group showed that the CPX-351 benefit in AML-MR was apparent only among patients who underwent transplantation (2-year OS: 69% with CPX-351 vs 26% with 7+3), with no difference among those who did not undergo transplantation (2-year OS: 4% vs 0%; P = .72) (Figure 3A). In the multivariable analysis, both HCT (HR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.11-0.48; P < .001) and CPX-351 treatment (HR, 0.6; 95% CI, 0.38-0.97) independently improved survival (Figure 3B).

Genetic characteristics and clinical outcome of TP53-AML

We identified 74 TP53 mutations in 62 patients, which were predominantly missense substitutions (n = 63 [80%]) affecting the DNA-binding domain (Figure 4A). Most cases were classified as TP53multi (n = 44 [71%]), with 7 patients having ≥2 TP53 mutations and 37 having LOH due to deletion (n = 21), CN-LOH (n = 12), or chromosome 17/17p karyotype alterations (n = 4). TP53multi had significantly higher VAF than TP53single, (median, 0.45 vs 0.13; P < .001) (Figure 4B). Given that TP53 VAF ≥0.5 is often used as a surrogate marker for multihit allelic state when empirical assessment of LOH is infeasible,4 we analyzed the proportion of cases with TP53single vs TP53multi having VAF above and below this threshold. We found that VAF ≥0.5 had 100% specificity (18/18) but only 47% sensitivity (21/44) for multihit status, indicating that many cases with TP53multi are at risk for misclassification based on VAF criteria alone.

Although clinical characteristics such as age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, and disease subtype (AML-MRC vs t-AML) were similar between groups, we observed several key differences in genetic characteristics and clinical outcomes. Adverse-risk cytogenetics were markedly more frequent in TP53multi (98%) than in TP53single (67%, P = .003; Figure 4C), and TP53single was significantly more commonly associated with comutations in AML-MR genes (50% vs 14%; P = .004). OS was significantly longer in TP53single patients than in TP53multi patients (7.0 vs 3.8 months, P = .0041; Figure 4D), despite similar transplant rates (22% vs 27%; P = .760). No survival differences between treatments were observed in either TP53 group, suggesting limited efficacy of intensive induction regardless of regimen (supplemental Figure 10). The absence of treatment benefit remained consistent when stratified by TP53 allelic state, indicating that both single-hit and multihit TP53-AML derive no advantage from CPX-351 compared with 7+3.

Count recovery and early mortality

In the overall trial, CPX-351 was associated with prolonged count recovery among patients achieving CR + CRi after initial induction, although early mortality rates were similar between CPX-351 and 7+3.1 To explore whether AML genetic groups influenced these key safety outcomes, we evaluated count recovery times and early mortality across molecular subgroups.

No significant differences in neutrophil (P = .8) or platelet (P = .3) recovery times were observed across molecular subgroups (supplemental Figure 11). However, within each subgroup, count recovery was consistently slower with CPX-351 than with 7+3. Median neutrophil recovery was longer with CPX-351 than with 7+3 in TP53-AML (34.5 vs 28 days; P = .013), DDX41-AML (35 vs 28 days; P = .2), AML-MR (40 vs 28 days; P < .001), and other-AML (36 vs 28.6 days; P = .091) (Figure 5A). Similarly, platelet recovery was delayed with CPX-351 across all molecular groups (Figure 5B). Despite prolonged myelosuppression with CPX-351, early mortality was similar between treatment arms within molecular groups. Mortality at 30 and 60 days was similar among AML-MR (8% and 18%), other-AML (4% and 17%), and TP53single (5.9% and 11.8%) groups, whereas TP53multi had the highest early mortality (13.3% and 26.6%, respectively). No early deaths occurred in DDX41-AML.

Discussion

In the pivotal phase 3 CLTR0310-301 trial, compared with 7+3, CPX-351 improved OS among older adults with high-risk and/or secondary AML. Our post hoc genetic reclassification shows that this benefit was confined to enrolled patients with AML-MR mutations, particularly those who underwent allogeneic transplantation, whereas no advantage was observed in TP53-mutated AML, irrespective of allelic state.

Molecular reclassification clarified prognostic heterogeneity that was obscured by the trial’s original clinical enrollment groups. The hierarchical model (TP53-AML, DDX41-AML, AML-MR, and other-AML) identified distinct clinical and cytogenetic features and wide variation in 2-year OS (7%, 70%, 19%, and 37%, respectively). These findings reinforce that genetic rather than clinical definitions better capture disease biology and therapeutic responsiveness, even in high-risk and secondary AML.

The AML-MR subgroup, currently recognized as a distinct entity in diagnostic and prognostic classification models,4, 5, 6 demonstrated significantly improved outcomes with CPX-351 compared to 7+3. These mutations are highly specific for prior MDS and identify patients with typically poor outcomes following standard intensive induction approaches.8 Notably, in our analysis, this benefit was exclusively observed among patients who subsequently received allogeneic HCT, with no survival advantage evident in those who did not undergo transplantation. Similar results in the National Council Research Institute (NCRI) AML19 trial (median OS: 38.4 vs 16.3 months with CPX-351 vs FLAG-Ida in MDS-related AML) suggest that this benefit may extend to younger patients.33 However, whether the benefit of CPX-351 extends beyond the original trial inclusion criteria, in particular to patients with AML-MR mutations without clinical secondary AML, therapy-related AML, or AML with MDS-related cytogenetics, necessitates prospective validation.

The mechanism of this transplant-specific benefit is uncertain. Across disease contexts, pretransplant disease burden strongly correlates with posttransplant survival outcomes,23,34, 35, 36 and higher measurable residual disease (MRD)-negativity rates have been proposed as the mechanism underlying the benefit of CPX-351.37 However, the supporting evidence is limited by the lack of MRD assessment or posttreatment sample banking in this trial. This model is not supported by the results of the NCRI AML19 trial, in which MRD-negativity rates in patients with MDS-associated mutations were similar between the CPX-351 and FLAG-Ida arms (36% vs 33%).33 An analysis of patients on the phase 3 trial who underwent transplantation indicated that lower nonrelapse mortality explained OS differences between treatment arms.38

In contrast, TP53-mutated AML derived no benefit from CPX-351, and outcomes were dictated primarily by allelic state. The poor prognosis of TP53multi-AML highlights a major unmet need39 because neither standard intensive chemotherapy, including CPX-351, nor current novel therapies including venetoclax-based combinations have demonstrated meaningful efficacy in this high-risk population.18 Our analysis also clarifies that although a TP53 VAF ≥0.5 reliably identifies multihit cases, many multihit patients fall below this threshold, emphasizing the need for the direct assessment of allelic state rather than reliance on VAF alone and underscoring the importance of implementing suitable approaches for TP53 allelic assessment in clinical practice.

We identified germ line DDX41 mutations in ∼5% of patients in this high-risk cohort, predominantly in patients with AML-MRC defined by antecedent MDS. These mutations were associated with high CR rates, no early mortality, and remarkably prolonged survival, supporting prior reports of favorable outcomes for intensively treated patients with DDX41-AML.12,13,23,40 Although our analysis showed a trend toward improved survival with CPX-351 in this subgroup (median OS, 56.4 vs 17.1 months; HR, 0.32), the limited sample size precludes definitive conclusions regarding the optimal induction regimen for these patients. The identification of germ line DDX41 mutations has important clinical implications because these patients can present in older age without syndromic features yet demonstrate favorable prognosis even within high-risk cohorts. Our findings underscore the importance of incorporating germ line DDX41 testing into AML diagnostic workflows, particularly for older patients with antecedent myeloid disorders.11,27,41 Future studies specifically designed to determine the optimal treatment approach for DDX41-AML are warranted, given the distinct biology and clinical behavior of this molecular subgroup.

The phase 2 trial that preceded CLTR0310-301 suggested greater CPX-351 efficacy in secondary AML defined by prior therapy or antecedent MDS. Our genetic reclassification indicates that this apparent sensitivity was likely driven by patients harboring AML-MR mutations under the current World Health Organization fifth edition criteria, rather than by all patients meeting historical clinical definitions of AML-MRC and t-AML. Although AML-MR mutations were not yet defined at the time of the phase 2 trial interpretation or phase 3 design, this study offers a template for how incorporation of correlative genetic studies into early phase trials might inform rational phase 3 trial design. Specifically, the molecular stratification of the phase 2 study may have prompted more specific targeting of patients with a favorable genetic profile while excluding those with TP53-AML, potentially yielding a more striking treatment effect and clearer clinical guidance for postapproval treatment selection.

The treatment landscape for older individuals with AML has evolved considerably, with the approval of venetoclax-hypomethylating agent (HMA) combinations now widely used beyond the VIALE-A criteria.42 A prospective randomized trial demonstrated similar outcomes between 10-day decitabine and 7+3 in patients aged >60 years,43 and multiple retrospective analyses show comparable results between intensive chemotherapy (including both 7+3 and CPX-351) and HMA-venetoclax regimens.18,44, 45, 46 The clinical focus has therefore shifted from eligibility for intensive therapy to selecting those most likely to benefit. Patients with AML-MR mutations appear to benefit from both approaches,18 and ongoing trials comparing HMA-venetoclax with intensive induction (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04801797)47 will clarify their relative roles.

This study was a post hoc analysis of a randomized trial not designed to test efficacy across molecular subgroups. Without systematic biobanking in the trial protocol, diagnostic samples were available for only 60% of enrolled patients, but sequenced and unsequenced cohorts were comparable by baseline characteristics, minimizing selection bias. Limited sample size within DDX41 and other-AML groups in particular restricts definitive conclusions. The results of the AMLSG 30-18 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03897127) and ALFA2101 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05260528) trials may clarify the role of CPX-351 when compared against other therapeutic strategies such as 7+3 or HMA plus venetoclax.

In summary, our findings support CPX-351 as the preferred intensive treatment for older transplant-eligible patients with AML-MR who otherwise meet the inclusion criteria of the original phase 3 study. However, for patients with TP53-AML, the role of CPX-351 is limited given its demonstrated excess toxicity and lack of efficacy in this population.
