The interplay of von Willebrand disease and women’s reproductive health

Abstract

Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder and is often diagnosed in the setting of heavy menstrual bleeding. Because estrogen upregulates von Willebrand factor synthesis, pregnancy and management of menses may influence VWD testing and diagnosis. Throughout the life span, the management of women’s reproductive health is frequently affected by VWD. We seek to describe the interplay between VWD and women’s reproductive health at 3 distinct phases in life: menarche and menstruation, maternal health and fertility, and menopause and aging. Planning for care requires screening for VWD in the setting of reproductive bleeding, counseling patients about expectations, and monitoring for iron deficiency across all 3 phases. Hemostatic plans should be tailored to both factor levels and patient preference. Emphasis is placed on areas lacking vital data.

Introduction

Worldwide, von Willebrand disease (VWD) is the most common bleeding disorder affecting ∼1 in 1000 individuals, though estimates vary.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Despite an autosomal inheritance pattern, which is not affected by biological sex, women are more likely to come to medical attention because of the impact of VWD on menses and reproductive health.7 However, VWD remains underappreciated as a contributor to women’s reproductive health, which faces its own diagnostic and management challenges. Here, we seek to describe the impact of VWD in 3 distinct settings: menarche and menstruation, maternal health and fertility, and menopause and aging (Figure 1). We take a case-based approach to evaluate common clinical challenges, available evidence, and areas in need of additional research.

Menarche and menstrual management

Case 1

A 12-year-old girl with type 1 VWD and history of epistaxis presents for her annual visit. Since her nasal cauterization several years ago, she has not had concerns regarding bleeding. She has developed physical signs of puberty since her last annual visit. She is premenarchal, but has friends who are menstruating, and is anxious about what she should expect with a bleeding disorder. Her mother has type 1 VWD and is eager for her daughter to not “suffer like I did for years.”

Premenarche planning

Taking time to discuss “what to expect when you are menstruating” is an invaluable part of care for young women with bleeding disorders and their parents, who often face this new phase of life for their daughter with some anxiety. First, it is worth noting that this patient is in the position of knowing that she has VWD before menarche, which allows time for such counseling and proactive measures. Diagnostic delay unfortunately remains common among patients with VWD, and these delays disproportionately affect women.8, 9, 10

The “7-2-1” rule is a simple explanation for teens and families of the warning signs of heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB). We advise patients to contact our clinic if menses are lasting for seven days or more, if they need to change their pad or tampon more frequently than every 2 hours, or if they are passing clots >1 inch in diameter (or 1 Euro coin). We also discuss that anovulatory cycles due to immaturity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis (or “brain hormones learning how to talk to pelvis hormones”) is very common in the first few years after menarche. Anovulatory cycles can cause irregular and/or HMB, which may be exacerbated in the setting of underlying VWD.

It is often overwhelming to review the many treatment strategies for HMB with families at this time,11 but it is important for them to understand that a wide variety of options will be available including hormonal and nonhormonal options, options that can regulate and lighten menses, and options that more aggressively suppress menses if that is their desire. Tranexamic acid (TXA) holds high appeal as an initial management strategy for early adolescents, as it does not require daily adherence, has a low side effect profile, and does not interfere with cycle regulation in the early months of menses, when ongoing maturation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis may naturally lead to reduced bleeding. This author’s practice is to prescribe TXA after these menarche counseling visits, so that the family has the medication at home and can institute immediately over the phone with our guidance should HMB develop. TXA has been found to be moderately more effective for HMB than desmopressin12 and comparably effective to VWF concentrate,13 so we favor this for most patients. However, desmopressin did reduce HMB and preexisting patient and family comfort with intranasal desmopressin can make this agent more accessible. For those patients who already have intranasal desmopressin in the home, we review how this agent may be used for HMB: once daily, for no >3 days in a row, and in conjunction with fluid restriction guidelines.

If HMB persists in a patient with VWD, the American Society of Hematology, International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis, National Hemophilia Foundation, and World Federation of Hemophilia 2021 guidelines on the management of VWD suggest using either hormonal therapy (combined hormonal contraception or levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine device [IUD]) or TXA than desmopressin for women who do not wish to conceive.14 With the increasing availability of procedural sedation for placement of IUDs, barriers to their use in young adolescents, those with developmental disabilities, or anxiety surrounding pelvic examinations have decreased substantially, and an increasing amount of literature is available on their use with bleeding disorders.15, 16, 17, 18 Consideration of IUDs for first-line management is also in keeping International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis guidance for HMB in adolescents.19 Von Willebrand factor (VWF) replacement is an option to consider in the event that first-line therapies have failed, but data remain limited for both adults and adolescents.13,20

Maternal health and fertility

Case 2

A 29-year-old woman with VWD type 1 presents for preconception counseling. She is gravida 0. Her VWD was diagnosed in adolescence when she presented with HMB. HMB has been manageable with oral contraceptive pills (OCPs), though she notes that at peak flow, she must change a super plus tampon approximately every 3 hours to avoid bleeding through onto her clothes. She recollects difficulty with bleeding through menstrual products during classes in high school and is anxious about coming off OCPs and having HMB interfere with her job performance. Her pediatric laboratory testing result is not available for review.

Preconception planning

As noted, OCPs are recommended as first-line therapy for the management of HMB due to VWD,14 but are incompatible with pursuing pregnancy. In clinical practice, anticipation of resurgence of HMB is often anxiety provoking. Menstruating patients of reproductive age should be reminded of noncontraceptive methods of bleeding management while on OCPs to avoid the false, but common, assumption that stopping OCPs requires enduring a return to HMB. Antifibrinolytics, desmopressin, and factor concentrates may be used to control HMB and other bleeding symptoms while pursuing conception with antifibrinolytics being preferred for most patients.14

Patients should be counseled that desmopressin and antifibrinolytics are usually avoided during gestation though not contraindicated. Desmopressin has been safely used during gestation predominantly for diabetes insipidus,21,22 which usually occurs later in gestation, and peripartum for VWD. There are some data regarding use before completion of organogenesis as well, but it is limited.23,24 With desmopressin use antepartum, hyponatremia remains a concern, especially with recurrent dosing, and later in gestation, the potential blood pressure impact should be considered in the context of preeclampsia or risk for preeclampsia. Antifibrinolytics cross the placenta, though teratogenicity has not been noted. Use of TXA peripartum has been extensively studied, as discussed subsequently, but data earlier in gestation are absent. Patients often benefit from having a prescription for TXA (or another alternative as discussed previously) on hand when stopping OCPs to avoid delays of care should HMB recur as anticipated.

Case 2 reflects a common challenge for patients diagnosed with VWD in childhood: their adult hematologist may not have access to prior laboratory studies. Rates of this scenario are likely to decrease with the expansion of electronic medical records, but this is unlikely to resolve entirely. In such circumstances, obtaining nonpregnant VWF levels is highly useful to confirm diagnosis, subtype, and help predict likelihood of needing interventions. An ongoing area of controversy remains whether modern OCPs really affect VWF levels significantly.25 Higher doses of estrogen are well established to raise VWF, but these doses exceed that used in currently available OCPs.26,27 The greatest data limitation in this area is that studies cited to support the idea that OCPs do not increase VWF levels have been conducted in patients without VWD.28, 29, 30, 31, 32 Even with this uncertainty in mind, testing on OCPs is still useful: low values are readily interpretable and values that are on the lower end of normal can be reevaluated over time.

As with any medical condition, patients may face concerns about the negative impact of VWD on fertility itself. Several studies have demonstrated no increase in pregnancy loss or poor fetal outcomes even in more severe forms of VWD.33, 34, 35, 36 Antepartum bleeding has been more variable across these studies with some finding increased rates of bleeding,35,37 whereas others do not.36 One hypothesis for the discrepancy is that bleeding itself may not be more common, but due to higher volume of blood loss attributable to VWD, patients are more likely to come to medical attention for antepartum bleeding. Ultimately, patients should be reassured that VWD will not compromise the pregnancy, but that involvement of their hematologist is important should bleeding occur, even of a nature that is often managed by their obstetrician, to allow for thoughtful augmentation of hemostasis when appropriate. The hematologist should also be involved in planning when pursuing assisted reproduction to assure that bleeding risk is addressed. Although there is a paucity of evidence on best practices, the authors’ approach is to provide hemostatic support with an agent known to be effective for the individual patient for egg retrieval but not for an embryo transfer, unless the patient has type 3 or a severe type 2.

Finally, iron deficiency with or without anemia occurs at an increased rate in patients with bleeding disorders and HMB. All patients with HMB regardless of etiology should be regularly screened for iron deficiency. Pregnancy represents a substantial demand on iron stores, so patients anticipating pregnancy are well served by assuring that they enter conception iron replete. Initiation of folic acid when trying to conceive is also recommended.38

Return to case 2

Laboratory testing confirms that the patient has VWD type 1. She is happy to hear that pregnancy can be proactively managed and that she is not expected to be at increased risk of a poor outcome. She is given a prescription for TXA in anticipation of stopping OCPs. At her return visit, she expresses that menses were markedly heavier during the first cycle off OCPs and that TXA was effective at curbing the intensity of HMB.

Case 3

A 32-year-old woman who is G1P0 at 14 weeks gestation is referred to hematology by her obstetrician for concern of undiagnosed VWD. The patient’s sister was recently diagnosed with VWD, and the patient notes HMB and blood-soaked gauze requiring exchange several times per day after wisdom tooth extraction. She asks about delivery planning and whether she can safely use epidural anesthesia. A VWD panel drawn at the initial hematologist visit demonstrates VWF antigen 54 IU/dL, VWF activity 52 IU/dL, and Factor VIII 67 IU/dL.

VWD testing during pregnancy

VWF is well established to rise in pregnancy.28,34,39,40 This creates challenges for interpretations of levels for both diagnosis and management during pregnancy. The rise in VWF starts in the first trimester, though levels continue to rise during gestation.40 The reference range reported by laboratories does not reflect the physiologically normal state for pregnancy, as upregulation of VWF is hypothesized to reflect an evolutionary adaptation to reduce risk of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). False reassurance can be taken from values labeled as “normal” but do not reflect truly normal hemostasis for pregnancy and parturition. Similarly, a value marked as “normal” measured during pregnancy does not exclude VWD, particularly if on the lower end of the reference range.

Patients should have a third trimester VWD panel to determine what interventions are needed for delivery. We typically collect this ∼32 to 34 weeks gestation to assure that values are back for delivery planning. Patients with severe type 2 or type 3 are a reasonable exception to the role for factor levels in the third trimester, as the need for hemostatic support with factor replacement is unambiguous. In the event of delivery before third trimester measurement, last available values should be used. Given the overall low risk of these interventions, management should err on the side of use of factor replacement and concurrent antifibrinolytics, as discussed subsequently, if recent levels are not available.

Peripartum management

Given the increased rates of PPH including notably delayed PPH,41,42 we advocate for some degree of hemostatic support for all patients with VWD around the time of delivery (Figure 2). This is in keeping with guidelines.14 The type of support should be tailored to VWD subtype and levels in the third trimester. Although research is limited (Figure 3), we think that most patients with VWF activity <100 IU/dL in the third trimester should receive hemostatic support for delivery itself. Other experts advocate for VWF activity of 80 IU/dL to determine interventions.43 Controversy remains about the ideal cutoff, as there is a lack of high-quality data, though use of 50 IU/dL (ie, the lower limit of reference in most laboratories) is not an acceptable practice. Our practice is to recommend VWF replacement at time of delivery for patients with third trimester levels <100 IU/dL, though desmopressin remains an alternative. Patients who correct to VWF activity levels >100 IU/dL remain at increased risk of bleeding but are less likely to need augmentation of factor levels. For patients with VWD type 3, severe type 2, or any patient whose levels fail to correct to an activity >80 IU/dL in the third trimester, we recommend delivery in a specialized center with ready access to factor replacement. When this need can be anticipated earlier in pregnancy based on the VWD type or the VWF activity before pregnancy, it is helpful to communicate this recommendation to patients and collaborating obstetricians in advance of the third trimester. When it is truly infeasible to deliver at a facility with VWF concentrate and the patient has a known response to desmopressin, one may consider desmopressin as an alternative to VWF concentrate even for levels <80 IU/dL. However, this should not be attempted for more severe forms of VWD and raises concern for an inability to manage PPH at these hospitals should it occur.

Use of neuraxial anesthesia including epidural catheters and spinal blocks merits caution, including consideration of target VWF activity levels. American Society of Hematology guidelines suggest a VWF activity level of 50 to 150 IU/dL for VWF activity for neuraxial anesthesia.14,44,45 In our practice, we usually do not separate the goals for delivery and neuraxial anesthesia, as initiating the hemostatic plan at the earlier of either neuraxial anesthesia or active labor is easier to enact and unlikely to have a downside. Alternatives to factor replacement to augment hemostasis may be appropriate for low-risk patients.46 Specifically, for patients with third trimester values nearing this level (ie, 80-100 IU/dL), use of antifibrinolytics alone may be an acceptable alternative, especially if delivering in a setting without ready access to factor products. Attention should be paid to epidural catheter removal and not just placement. As noted, relative rates of bleeding and complications in this setting lack data. Clinical trials addressing the impact of different hemostatic measures peripartum and the optimal factor levels for both delivery and neuraxial anesthesia are sorely needed.

There are advantages and disadvantages to both factor replacement and desmopressin. Factor replacement is efficacious regardless of underlying VWD subtype, does not require prior testing to predict response (though factor level monitoring is advisable), can be redosed indefinitely, and has no off-target effects. However, factor replacement is more expensive and not available at all hospitals. In contrast, desmopressin is less expensive and readily available in most settings but has limitations that may be particularly problematic during labor: limited response to redosing, which diminishes utility in prolonged labor or PPH; increased blood pressure; and hyponatremia. Hyponatremia has long been invoked as a particular concern because of the routine administration of IV fluids during labor and potential decreased seizure threshold already present. Studies of desmopressin given for labor have revealed overall good outcomes and low complication rates, but they are smaller and not randomized.46,47 Likely, the avoidance of complications relates in part to judicious patient selection and attention paid to the ongoing use of IV fluids. For patients with partial correction in pregnancy (ie, factor levels substantially improved but not >100 IU/dL), desmopressin likely remains a good option, so long as blood pressure and hyponatremia are considered. For patients with minimal improvement, especially those well below 80 IU/dL, desmopressin risks inadequate correction and ongoing bleeding risks, especially with prolonged labor, conversion to cesarean delivery, or PPH. With this in mind, it is our practice to use factor replacement for VWD delivery management for all patients with VWF activity <80 IU/dL and most patients <100 IU/dL. Local practice may vary, and centers with less access to factor replacement may safely use desmopressin with monitoring of blood pressure and sodium for patients with VWD type 1.48 Patients with VWD type 2 without a proven response to desmopressin or with a contraindication to desmopressin (ie, type 2b) or with type 3 should not be attempted to be managed with desmopressin.

Patients who have required VWF concentrates or have clinical evidence of increasing bleeding postpartum should have factor levels monitored to identify any need for further hemostatic support including continued or initiation of VWF concentrates and to inform the duration of antifibrinolytic use.

We usually administer antifibrinolytics as an adjunct for patients receiving desmopressin or factor replacement and as the sole intervention for patients not receiving either because of factor activity >100 IU/dL in the third trimester. TXA or aminocaproic acid should be given for 5 to 14 days postpartum tailored to the patient’s ongoing bleeding. We recommend prescribing the antifibrinolytic and having patients fill this prescription antepartum to reduce the burden of the postpartum period and assuring there is no delay in availability in the face of PPH. Patients may monitor their own bleeding and stop before 14 days if lochia has slowed significantly or stopped, with the option to resume if bleeding increases again. This approach is driven by the already noted increased rates of PPH that is often delayed PPH due to significant drop in VWF levels within 1 to 3 weeks postpartum.41,42 Antifibrinolytics have been found to be effective in treating PPH and reducing blood loss after cesarean delivery.49,50 Equally as important, multiple large clinical trials have revealed no increase in thrombosis rates after both vaginal and cesarean deliveries.49, 50, 51, 52 Although these seminal studies of antifibrinolytics were not restricted to patients with bleeding disorders, additional research has validated the benefit in patients with inherited bleeding disorders.53,54 Due to the potential for delayed PPH, a 2-week postpartum visit should be considered, especially for patients with lower VWF levels or prior PPH.

Postpartum testing

For patients without a diagnosis before pregnancy, repeat testing should occur at least 12 weeks postpartum. Although levels can decline as soon as a few days postpartum, some patients remain above baseline for several months.40 Accurate postpartum testing both confirms a suspected diagnosis and characterizes baseline levels for future medication dosing. If there are no expected interceding procedures, we often conduct this visit ∼4 to 6 months postpartum to minimize the demands on patient time during the postpartum period. This visit also assures that time can be devoted to counseling the patient about VWD implications outside of pregnancy, which is unlikely to be well addressed or retained during gestation and avoids patients getting lost to follow-up until pregnant again, when the same diagnostic limitations recur. For patients who may benefit from a desmopressin challenge, this visit provides an opportunity to coordinate testing.

Medication safety during breastfeeding

There is no evidence of harm to neonates from use of any of the prohemostatic measures discussed. Desmopressin is minimally excreted in breastmilk and not expected to be bioavailable in concentrations that have a physiological effect on the infant.55 Although rates of excretion of VWF concentrate in breastmilk are not available, there is no significant expectation of oral bioavailability. As noted previously, TXA has been extensively studied in the peripartum period. These studies did not exclude patients based on breastfeeding status or plans, and there have been no reported adverse neonatal outcomes including specifically no reported neonatal thrombotic events.49, 50, 51, 52

Return to case 3

The patient’s VWD panel with values at the lower end of the reference range despite being in the second trimester of pregnancy is highly suggestive of VWD type 1. Repeat VWD panel measurement at 32 weeks gestation revealed VWF level 72 IU/dL, VWF activity 76 IU/dL, and Factor VIII 98 IU/dL. She received VWF concentrate at 40 U/kg to target a level of ∼150 IU/dL for neuraxial anesthesia and delivery, which occurred at 39 weeks gestation. Estimated blood loss was 600 mL. She was redosed VWF concentrate 20 U/kg every 12 hours for 3 days, including timing 1 dose shortly before removal of the epidural catheter. She received adjunctive TXA initially 1 g IV every 8 hours starting with active labor and then transitioned to 1300 mg orally every 8 hours. She reported that there was an uptick in bleeding around postpartum day 5, but that it was manageable with TXA and did not exceed saturation of a pad every hour. Lochia gradually slowed, which prompted holding of TXA after 10 days, and stopped by ∼4 weeks postpartum. She was doing well at her 2-week and 6-week postpartum visits with obstetrics. At 5 months postpartum, she had follow-up with hematology where repeat testing confirmed VWD type 1.

Menopause and aging

Case 4

A 48-year-old woman with VWD is referred back to hematology for worsening anemia. She was last seen by a hematologist ∼15 years ago for management of her pregnancies, one of which was complicated by severe delayed PPH. Previously, she had adequate menses control with OCPs but was taken off at age 45 years due to concerns about increasing thrombosis risk with aging. Prior records demonstrate that at diagnosis in her 20s, she had a VWF antigen 38 IU/dL, VWF activity 40 IU/dL, and Factor VIII 81 IU/dL. Due to recurrent epistaxis, prolonged bleeding with wisdom tooth extraction, and HMB, she was considered to have VWD type 1. Repeat VWD panel at this visit reveals VWD type 1 with VWF antigen 52 IU/dL, VWF activity 56 IU/dL, and Factor VIII 90 IU/dL. She is considering hysterectomy for definitive management of HMB.

Diagnosing HMB in perimenopause

Increased or new onset of HMB in women in their 40s and 50s has multiple etiologies, most of which do not relate to VWD. Therefore, it is key not to limit the differential diagnosis in patients with VWD or any other bleeding disorder. For example, development of fibroids or anovulatory cycles due to perimenopause is more common as women age.56,57 Thus, any evaluation for worsening HMB requires interdisciplinary collaboration with gynecology. Ultimately, if HMB is new in middle age, although VWD may accentuate the bleeding, it is not likely to account for the symptom on its own. Similar to menarche, counseling in advance on the potential increase in HMB due to hormonal shifts with perimenopause is important. The median age for menopause is 51.4 years with ∼90% occurring between 45 and 55 years of age.58 Although irregular bleeding may occur for years prior, 12 consecutive months without menstruation in the absence of alternative causes are required to define menopause. The possibility of increased bleeding before cessation of menses is not common knowledge for patients, nor is the option to intervene. There is a common misperception that menses will peter out uneventfully, which occurs only in a subset of individuals. As such, proactive education can improve awareness of available strategies to mitigate bleeding and bring patients to care sooner. Hematologists caring for women with VWD in this age range should also solicit any change in menstrual patterns as part of an annual assessment.

Managing HMB in perimenopause

All therapies for HMB discussed previously are appropriate for the emergence of HMB in perimenopause. Selection of therapies should account for not only VWD severity and previous tolerance and efficacy but also any factors that may increase thrombosis risk. Age is a risk factor for thrombosis,59 but age alone should not be considered a contraindication to OCP use, especially because OCPs may address multiple factors contributing to HMB in perimenopause.60 Caution with use of desmopressin is not usually merited without concern for cardiovascular disease or more advanced age.

Many women with HMB of any etiology consider surgical management. Weighing the risks and benefits for surgery for HMB during perimenopause is often complicated by the uncertain duration of remaining menses. Perimenopause lasts on average 5 years,61 which may help inform patients preference and the viability of waiting for cessation of menses, though individual courses vary.

Increasing VWF levels with age

VWF levels increase with age, rising 10 IU/dL or more per decade after age 40 years by some estimates.62, 63, 64, 65, 66 For patients with VWD type 1, this will move nearly a quarter of patients into the normal range67 concurrent with a stage of life when medical procedures and other hemostatic challenges are more common. Most population prevalence studies focus on the pediatric population and do not account for this shift.2, 3, 4,6 What remains unclear is whether this age-related increase is sufficient to normalize hemostasis and whether hemostatic support for procedures can be safely withheld. Limited available studies vary as to whether the increased factor levels correlate with reduced bleeding symptoms.68, 69, 70 We do not typically remove the label of VWD for patients with confirmed disease at a younger age, as we are concerned that this may lead to a cavalier approach to management. However, we do tailor therapy to current factor levels and competing thrombosis risk. We favor the use of antifibrinolytics and/or factor concentrates over desmopressin because of the relative side effect profile. Given inadequate data proving it is safe to withhold intervention, our practice is to err on the side of hemostatic support with antifibrinolytics or factor replacement for patients just entering the normal range (ie, 50-70 IU/dL) but are unlikely to recommend intervention with patients whose levels are 80 IU/dL or more.

For select patients with a dubious proceeding VWD diagnosis (eg, absence of a bleeding history; childhood diagnosis made by bleeding time alone), we encourage revisiting the diagnosis.

Return to case 4

The option to resume OCPs was discussed and trialed but was inadequate for control of HMB. The option to try IUD alone or in combination with TXA was offered. Gynecologic evaluation did not identify any anatomic driver of bleeding. Given that she was not yet having irregular cycles or other overt perimenopause symptoms, she was estimated to have at least 5 years expected for cessation of menses and opted to pursue hysterectomy instead. In the context of her barely normalized VWF activity and her bleeding history, VWF factor concentrate was used for hysterectomy, which was uneventful.

Conclusion

VWD and female reproductive health are intertwined. The large mucosal surface of the endometrium is reliant on primary hemostasis and VWF function. Unlike most foci of bleeding, uterine bleeding can be anticipated, which allows advance planning that cannot not only minimize bleeding but also reduce anxiety and improve quality of life. The impact of estrogen on VWF levels much be considered in the management of women with VWD, and ideal target VWF levels through the phases of reproductive health merit further investigation.
