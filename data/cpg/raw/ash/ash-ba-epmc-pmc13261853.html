Interferon in early MF: intersection of biology and evidence

John Mascarenhas

John Mascarenhas

In this issue of Blood Advances, Gill et al1 evaluated the outcomes of patients with prefibrotic primary myelofibrosis (prePMF) and lower-risk overt MF, treated with ropeginterferon, a monopegylated proline form of interferon alfa-2b (IFN-α2b) in a phase 2 trial (CITE). Ropeginterferon is currently approved by the Food and Drug Administration for polycythemia vera (PV), based on the results of the PROUD-PV and CONTINUATION-PV trials,2 although its approval is anticipated for essential thrombocythemia (ET) based on the more recent results of the phase 3 SURPASS trial as second-line therapy after hydroxyurea failure.3

IFN therapy has been tested in myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) for nearly 40 years as a nonchemotherapeutic option, and one in which tolerability was significantly improved with pegylation.4 Enhanced understanding of the molecular underpinnings of MPNs in 20055 ushered a renewed interest in IFN and drove the phase 3 evaluation of pegylated–IFN-α2a as initial therapy for ET/PV by the MPN Research Consortium.6 Preclinical data established the potential of IFN to target the hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) through (1) activation and induction of cell cycling of dormant HSC by JAK-STAT pathway activation, (2) depletion of disease-initiating JAk2V617F-positive MPN HSCs in murine modeling, and (3) chronic IFN exposure that leads to HSC exhaustion.7,8 Seminal observations by a French study provided important insights into the clonal dynamics and MPN HSC-depleting capability of this biologic in a cohort of patients with MPN prospectively treated with IFN.9 Irrespective of a therapeutic agent, studies in PV have also reported a relationship between reduction in the JAK2V617F variant allele fraction (VAF) and clinical outcome measures, including hematologic response and event-free survival.10,11

In this phase 2 trial, 72 Asian patients with prePMF and low-/intermediate-risk MF requiring cytoreduction were treated with an accelerated dosing strategy of ropeginterferon. At a median follow-up of ∼2.5 years, ropeginterferon treatment resulted in hematologic responses of 70%, with the highest response rate found in prePMF at 78%, PMF at 33%, and post-ET/PV MF at 50%. Thrombohemorrhagic events, a major contributor to morbidity and the primary indication for cytoreductive therapy in ET/PV/prePMF, were not observed. However, the more compelling reason for therapeutic intervention in this subset of patients with MPN may be the potential for disease course modification, a therapeutic objective in these chronic and progressive neoplasms which has been challenging to prove in the absence of a validated biomarker.

The primary end point for this single-arm trial was clinicohematologic complete response (CHCR) defined as white blood cells <10 ×109/L, platelets <400 ×109/L, and absence of MPN symptoms and palpable spleen for 12 consecutive weeks. Key secondary end points included change in driver VAF and thrombohemorrhagic and progressive events. Important baseline characteristics to note are that all patients were receiving hydroxyurea at enrollment, 20% had palpable splenomegaly, and high molecular risk mutations were documented in 20%.

Reductions in JAK2V617F VAF were more frequent and deeper than CALR VAF, and reduction in subclonal mutations, including high molecular risk mutations, was also reported, whereas increases in driver mutation VAF and emergence of new subclonal mutations were found in patients who experienced progressive disease. Although this trial was not designed and powered to establish changes in driver mutation VAF as a surrogate for disease course modification in this MPN subset, the authors did correlate reduction in JAK2V617F VAF with CHCR response, and leukocytosis has been identified as an adverse prognostic variable.

Toxicity is of utmost concern in patients with earlier forms of MPN in whom the tolerance for treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs) may not be similar to therapeutics used in higher risk MF. Not unexpectedly, the usual toxicity profile was found with mostly low-grade transaminase elevation, malaise, alopecia, myalgias, arthralgias, and myelosuppression. Only 3 patients discontinued therapy due to AE. Interestingly, and in contrast to what is found and reported in US patients treated with IFN, only 1 patient experienced mild mood disorder. It is a unique IFN-associated toxicity that I am particularly attuned to, because it can be significant in some patients and there are no laboratory correlates for this insidious issue.

An important consideration when contextualizing this trial was the eligibility requirement of hydroxyurea as the primary therapeutic intervention for this patient population, which may not fully reflect the US experience in which ruxolitinib is often used to address symptoms and the spleen, even in lower-risk individuals.

The lesson we have learned in several decades of formal prospective evaluation of IFN in MPNs is that both provider and patient must be persistent in approach, as the clinicohematologic responses can take time to achieve, and clinical decision-making is not yet fully informed by available data. For example, if one were to achieve a hematologic response and not a deep molecular response, is this considered an inadequate response? If one were to achieve a deep molecular response and not a bone marrow histopathologic remission, does that alter the perception of response and risk of disease progression over time? The optimal dosing strategy of ropeginterferon is also not clear in this patient population in which balancing therapy-related toxicity and attaining deep molecular responses may favor treatment duration over dose intensity.

Although certain questions remain surrounding optimal dosing, response monitoring, and disease modification with IFN therapy, the wealth of laboratory data supports an MPN HSC-directed biologic effect, and prospective clinical data confirm activity across the MPN disease spectrum. The randomized, placebo-controlled, phase 3 HOPE trial (NCT06468033) is now active and enrolling across the world, and it will provide an opportunity to validate the results found in this phase 2 trial of ropeginterferon in prePMF and lower-risk MF across a diverse patient population.12 In the meantime, inclusion of this nonchemotherapeutic option to the MPN treatment arsenal is already endorsed by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines in select patients with low-risk symptomatic MF. Whether sufficient follow-up will also allow for correlation of changes in biomarkers (driver VAF, bone marrow fibrosis, cytokine levels) with clinically meaningful outcome measures, such as event-free survival, will need to be documented.
