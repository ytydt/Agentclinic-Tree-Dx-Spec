Key Points

  • •

    Hypertension is associated with shorter survival following both CLL diagnosis and treatment for patients with CLL aged <75 years.

  • •

    The increased mortality is primarily driven by infections and cardiocerebrovascular causes of death.

Visual Abstract

Abstract

Hypertension is the most common comorbidity in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), but its impact on the disease course of CLL remains poorly understood. In this nationwide cohort study of Danish patients with incident CLL (2008-2022), we examined the association between hypertension and survival from CLL diagnosis and treatment, and compare with the general population. Cox proportional hazard regression was applied for survival analyses, and the Aalen-Johansen estimator for cumulated incidence. At diagnosis of CLL, the hypertension prevalence was 46% in the CLL cohort and 45% in the general population. Hypertension was associated with shorter overall survival (OS) following CLL diagnosis (hazard ratio [HR], 1.10; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.00-1.20) and first-line treatment (HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.18-1.86), primarily driven by infections and cardiocerebrovascular causes of death. The difference was seen for patients aged <75 years both following diagnosis (HR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.25-1.64) and first-line treatment (HR, 1.89; 95% CI, 1.34-2.66). For patients treated with Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors, we observed a trend toward poorer OS in patients with hypertension (HR, 1.97; 95% CI, 0.90-4.34). No interaction between CLL in general and hypertension was observed (HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.79-1.02), meaning that hypertension did not confer a higher additional risk in patients with CLL than in individuals without CLL. This study identifies an important subgroup of patients with CLL aged <75 years with coexisting hypertension who have a poorer prognosis. Our findings support encouraging patients to maintain regular contact with primary care and adhere to preventative health measures, as hypertension and related comorbidities remain important for survival even after CLL diagnosis.

Introduction

Comorbidities are common and affect the prognosis in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) that mostly affects older patients, with a median age of 70 years at diagnosis. Comorbidities such as diabetes, cardiac disorders, chronic pulmonary disease, and cerebrovascular disease are common.1,2 Approximately 90% of patients have ≥1 comorbidity at the time of CLL diagnosis.3,4 Because comorbidity is associated with both increased CLL-related and overall mortality,1, 2, 3,5, 6, 7 understanding the impact of specific comorbidities on treatment outcomes and cause-specific mortality is essential to provide patients with optimal monitoring and treatment.8

We previously showed that type 2 diabetes increased the risk of CLL-related mortality.9 This finding prompted further investigations into the significance of other comorbidities belonging to the group of metabolic syndrome manifestations. Among these, the lifetime risk of hypertension is >90% in industrialized countries, and hypertension is one of the major contributors to morbidity and mortality worldwide.10,11 This is also true among patients with CLL, among whom hypertension is the most prevalent comorbidity, affecting ∼45% of patients at time of CLL diagnosis,3,5 increasing to ∼60% by initiation of first-line treatment.12

Despite the high prevalence of hypertension among patients with CLL, no guidelines concerning the management of this large subgroup exist, except in the context of Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, which may induce or exacerbate hypertension.4,12, 13, 14, 15 Patients receiving BTK inhibitors should undergo baseline assessment, electrocardiogram, and blood pressure measurements, and be monitored throughout treatment according to guidelines.16

To increase our understanding of the significance of hypertension in CLL care and treatment, we present characteristics of patients with coexisting hypertension and CLL, and assess the impact of hypertension on treatment and survival outcomes, both at CLL diagnosis and following treatment.

Methods

Data sources and study population

All Danish residents have access to free health care services. The usage is monitored in national registers using a unique personal identification number for each resident, enabling identity-secure data linkage.17 Individuals diagnosed with CLL since 2008 were identified in the Danish National CLL Registry,18 and enriched with data (since 2002) from the Danish Lymphoid Cancer Research (DALY-CARE) resource.19 To study the clinical significance of hypertension among patients with CLL, the abovementioned patients with CLL in DALY-CARE diagnosed before May 2022 were followed, henceforth referred to as the DALY-CARE cohort. To evaluate potential interactions between hypertension and CLL, we used Statistics Denmark to identify a similar cohort of patients with CLL diagnosed before June 2021. In this, each patient was matched on age and sex, with ≤5 individuals from the general CLL-free population. Comparators were assigned a date of pseudodiagnosis, corresponding to the date of CLL diagnosis in the index patient with CLL. This cohort is henceforth referred to as the Matched cohort. For cohort differences, see supplemental Figure 1.

Individuals were followed through the Civil Registration System from date of (pseudo-)diagnosis until death, emigration, or September 2022 (December 2019 for cause-specific death), whichever occurred first.17

Data on comorbidities were obtained from the Danish Prescription Register, containing information on all prescription drugs sold in Denmark since 1995,17 and the Danish National Patient Register, containing information on diagnoses for all inpatient contacts since 1977, and all outpatient contacts since 1995.17 For the DALY-CARE cohort, we also included diagnosis codes from electronic hospital records. According to current international and Danish guidelines, largely consistent since 2009, the diagnosis of hypertension requires an in-office systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg.20, 21, 22 Confirmation should be obtained through repeated measurements using either home blood pressure monitoring (systolic ≥135 mm Hg and/or diastolic ≥85 mm Hg) or 24-hour blood pressure monitoring (systolic ≥130 mm Hg and/or diastolic ≥80 mm Hg). Exceptions apply if the initial blood pressure is ≥180/110 mm Hg or if there is evidence of hypertension-mediated organ damage. Antihypertensive drugs are indicated when these criteria are met and lifestyle interventions alone are insufficient to reduce blood pressure.20 In this study, hypertension was defined as having either ≥2 hypertension codes registered at least 7 days apart, or ≥2 prescriptions of antihypertension drugs before the date of CLL (pseudo-)diagnosis or treatment (supplemental Figure 2A; supplemental Table 1). We modified previous definitions23, 24, 25, 26 to ensure that mild hypertension treated in primary care was correctly classified (supplemental Table 1). Comorbidities were defined according to comorbidities in the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), excluding AIDS and cancer diagnoses (supplemental Table 1).27,28

Causes of death were categorized according to previous work, using the Danish National Cause of Death Register (supplemental Table 1).29 To avoid bias from COVID-19–related deaths during the pandemic, follow-ups in cause-specific death analyses were limited to data up to 1 January 2020.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcomes were overall survival (OS) from (pseudo)diagnosis and first-line treatment (DALY-CARE cohort). Secondary outcomes were time to first treatment and cause-specific death. Kaplan-Meier estimates and Cox regression models provided OS and hazard ratios (HRs) for death, respectively. The Aalen-Johansen estimator and Cox regression models were used to estimate cumulative incidence in settings with competing events.

The DALY-CARE cohort was analyzed using multivariable Cox regression models, adjusted for age, sex, and comorbidities. Analyses from first-line treatment were additionally adjusted for the International Prognostic Index for CLL (CLL-IPI)30 at diagnosis, excluding the age score. In the Matched cohort, multivariable Cox regression models were used, time-dependently adjusted for comorbidities and municipality of residence. Numbers permitting, individual comorbidities were included as covariates; otherwise, the CCI score was used.31 Age was adjusted for using categories.

Data were analyzed in R (version 4.2.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics

The study was approved by the Danish Health and Medicine Authorities (jr. no. 3-3013-1141/1), Danish National Ethics Committee (1804410), and the Danish Data Protection Agency (jr. no. P-2020-561). No consent was required as all data used for the Danish cohort were from existing Danish national registers. In accordance with Danish legislation, all analyses were performed on pseudonymized data.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In the DALY-CARE cohort, we identified 6557 patients with CLL (61% male), of whom 3035 (46%) had hypertension at the time of CLL diagnosis. Patients with CLL with hypertension were older (median age 74 years) than patients with CLL without hypertension (median age 68 years) at diagnosis (Table 1). Patients with both CLL and hypertension had modestly higher CLL-IPI scores than those without hypertension. β2-Microglobulin (B2M) significantly increased with age (supplemental Table 2). The distributions of immunoglobulin heavy chain mutational status, fluorescence in situ hybridization status, and Binet stage were similar in patients with and without hypertension. However, patients with both CLL and hypertension had a higher prevalence of all recorded comorbidities than those without hypertension, particularly cardiac disease (18% vs 3%) and diabetes (16% vs 3%). Additionally, a higher proportion of patients with CLL and hypertension had a CCI score ≥1 (41%) compared with those without hypertension (29%). Data coverage for the DALY-CARE cohort was adequate for all included International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) and Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes to support reliable identification of hypertension cases and comorbidities throughout the study period (supplemental Figure 2B-C).

In the Matched cohort, distributions of hypertension status were comparable to the DALY-CARE cohort. Among individuals without CLL, 45% had hypertension, and the median age was 74 and 68 years for those with and without hypertension before (pseudo)diagnosis, respectively. Among individuals with CLL, the corresponding figures were 46% with hypertension, and median ages of 74 and 67 years (Table 1).

OS

The median follow-up time was 4.4 years for patients with CLL and hypertension, compared with 5.7 years for those without hypertension. The median OS was shorter among patients with CLL with hypertension (8.6 years) than among those without (13.8 years; Figure 1A). This increased risk of death was statistically significant in a multivariable Cox regression model (HR, 1.10; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.00-1.20; Table 2). Patients with both CLL and hypertension had inferior survival compared with those without hypertension, regardless of CCI score (supplemental Figure 3A). However, this association was no longer statistically significant when adjusting for individual comorbidities, including cardiac conditions (supplemental Figure 4G).

In patients with CLL aged <75 years, those with hypertension had reduced OS compared with patients without hypertension (5-year OS: 81% vs 89%; HR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.25-1.64). For patients aged >75 years, OS was similar for those with and without hypertension (5-year OS: 52% vs 54%; HR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.87-1.10; Table 2; Figure 1B).

As hypertension was associated with reduced OS in patients with CLL, we investigated whether this effect differed from that in the general population by comparing patients with CLL with individuals without CLL from the general population. In the Matched cohort, the median OS was 8.8 years for patients with CLL with hypertension, 14.1 years for patients with CLL without hypertension, and 11.4 years for comparators with hypertension, whereas the median OS was not reached for those with neither condition (Figure 1C). When adjusting for municipality of residence and individual comorbidities, the difference in OS between people with and without hypertension was attenuated both for individuals without CLL (HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.99-1.13) and with CLL (HR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.83-1.06; supplemental Table 3). The interaction term between CLL and hypertension was 0.89 (95% CI, 0.79-1.02), indicating that the adverse effect of hypertension on OS was comparable in patients with CLL and in the general population.

Next, we investigated the impact of different antihypertensive medications, CCI score, and sex (supplemental Figure 3; Table 2). A higher number of different antihypertensive medications prescribed was associated with poorer OS among patients with CLL (P = .0001; supplemental Figure 3B). This association was not observed in multivariable analysis adjusted for sex, age at CLL diagnosis, and individual comorbidities (Table 2). For patients aged >75 years with CLL and hypertension, an increased risk of death was seen for patients receiving diuretics as compared with renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (HR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.01-2.05), whereas this did not affect patients aged <75 years (Table 2; supplemental Figure 3C). The association between hypertension and OS after CLL diagnosis was similar for females and males (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.94-1.26; vs HR, 1.10; 95% CI, 0.98-1.23; Table 2; supplemental Figure 3D).

Cause-specific survival

The 5-year cumulative risk of death from infections following CLL diagnosis was higher for patients with CLL with hypertension than for patients with CLL without hypertension (11% vs 6%; Figure 2A). However, the difference did not retain statistical significance after adjustment for sex, age at CLL diagnosis, and CCI score (HR, 1.16; 95% CI, 0.97-1.39; supplemental Table 4). In the Matched cohort, the infection-related mortality was higher for individuals with hypertension than for people without either CLL or hypertension in crude analysis (6% vs 3%; supplemental Figure 5A). This difference was not statistically significant in multivariable models (supplemental Table 5). To assess whether the COVID-19 pandemic affected the distribution of registered causes of death, we performed cause-specific survival analyses for the entire follow-up period. The results remained unchanged (supplemental Table 6).

Stratification by age (<75 vs ≥75 years) revealed that the higher infection-related mortality was primarily driven by patients aged <75 years (Figure 2B-C; supplemental Table 4). In patients with CLL, hypertension was not associated with risk of death due to hematological malignancies or other malignancies, even after stratification by age (Figure 2D-F; supplemental Table 4). Patients with CLL with hypertension had an increased risk of death due to cardio- and cerebrovascular disease compared with patients with CLL without hypertension, also in multivariable modeling (HR, 1.83; 95% CI, 1.25-2.69; supplemental Figure 6A; supplemental Table 4). Similar associations between hypertension and risk of death due to cardio- and cerebrovascular disease were also seen in the general population (supplemental Figure 5C).

Time to first treatment

Patients with CLL and hypertension had statistically significantly longer time to first treatment than patients with CLL without hypertension (5-year cumulative incidence: 27% vs 31%; P = .0009; supplemental Table 7). This association was observed both in competing risks settings treating death as a competing event (supplemental Figure 7) and in cause-specific Cox regression models censoring for death (supplemental Table 7). However, this association was no longer significant after covariate adjustment (supplemental Table 7).

Survival from first-line treatment

A total of 1966 patients received first-line treatment for CLL, of whom 884 (45%) had hypertension before treatment (supplemental Table 8). For the subgroup analyses, treatment regimens were categorized into 6 treatment groups comprising the most common treatment regimens. Chlorambucil-based treatments were most common (n = 516 patients), and more frequently given to patients with CLL with hypertension than to those without (34% vs 20%). The opposite was seen for fludarabine-based treatments (n = 379 patients, 11% vs 26%). The use of bendamustine (n = 459 patients) was comparable between the 2 groups (23% vs 24%). Overall, 13% of patients with CLL received targeted agents, with similar distributions among patients with and without hypertension for BTK inhibitors (n = 112 patients, 7% vs 5%), venetoclax-based therapy (n = 113 patients, 5% vs 7%), and combination therapy with ibrutinib and venetoclax (n = 32 patients, 2% vs 1%).

Following first-line treatment, the 5-year OS for patients with CLL with hypertension was 55% compared with 71% for patients with CLL without hypertension (HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.18-1.86; supplemental Table 9). Among patients aged <75 years, hypertension was associated with a significantly higher risk of death in multivariable models (HR, 1.89; 95% CI, 1.34-2.66; Figure 3A; supplemental Table 9).

There was a small nonstatistically significant lower survival for patients with hypertension treated with targeted agents than for patients without hypertension (Figure 3B-C; supplemental Table 9). Hypertension was associated with significantly shorter OS in patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy (HR range, 1.25-1.48; supplemental Figure 8A-C; supplemental Table 9). For patients treated with BTK inhibitors, those with hypertension also had poorer 5-year OS compared with those without hypertension (60% [0.48-0.76] vs 84% [0.75-0.95]; HR, 1.97; 95% CI, 0.90-4.34), although estimates were imprecise (supplemental Figure 8D; supplemental Table 9). With <10 events, we could not stratify OS for venetoclax-based or combination treatments with ibrutinib and venetoclax (supplemental Figure 8E-F). For patients with CLL and hypertension, we observed similar shorter survival for patients treated with BTK inhibitors and chlorambucil than for those receiving other treatment regimens (Figure 4A).

Finally, we analyzed cause-specific mortality following first-line treatment. In univariable cause-specific Cox regression models, hypertension was associated with an increased risk of death from infections, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular causes, hematological malignancies, and other causes, but not from other malignancies. These associations did not retain statistical significance in multivariable analyses (supplemental Figures 6D-F and 9; supplemental Table 10).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this nationwide cohort study is the first population-based study investigating the impact of hypertension on survival outcomes in patients with CLL. Our findings suggest that hypertension is associated with a poorer prognosis in patients with CLL, both from time of diagnosis and following first-line treatment for CLL, particularly among patients aged <75 years. This increased risk of death was primarily driven by death from cardiac disease, cerebrovascular disease, and infections. Our results suggest that hypertension is associated with susceptibility to infectious complications in younger patients with CLL, but not in older patients with CLL. In younger patients, the impact of hypertension possibly reflects additional underlying risk factors associated with hypertension, such as cardiometabolic comorbidities, smoking, obesity, or dyslipidemia.32,33

Although hypertension was associated with poorer outcomes overall, patients with CLL and coexisting hypertension did not experience a greater excess risk than individuals with hypertension in the general population, suggesting no synergistic effect between CLL and hypertension on adverse outcomes. Although the effect of comorbid diseases on mortality might be minor if the cancer prognosis is poor,34,35 Read et al argue that with longer survival, comorbidity assessment should be included in prognostic cancer research.36 However, hypertension is rarely accounted for in comorbidity indices used in hematological research, even though the survival of patients with CLL is approaching the survival in the background population.27,29,37 Evidence in cancer populations suggests that preexisting hypertension is prevalent, often undertreated, and associated with clinically meaningful increases in all-cause and cancer-specific mortality in some cancer types.33,38 With the increased risk of cardiovascular events with BTK inhibitors, understanding cardiometabolic comorbidities with long-term impact is becoming increasingly important for the CLL-treating physician.

Furthermore, we investigated the impact of hypertension on survival following first-line treatment across different treatment regimens. Overall, patients with CLL and hypertension had poorer survival than those without hypertension regardless of treatment type. Hypertension had a more pronounced adverse effect on survival following first-line treatment with targeted therapy compared with chemoimmunotherapy. Reflecting contemporary guidelines in place during the study period, only 13% of patients received targeted drugs. There was a trend toward shorter survival among patients with hypertension treated with BTK inhibitors than for patients without hypertension receiving the same treatment. No other treatment regimen showed a similar pattern. Clinical studies have shown that patients with hypertension are at increased risk of cardiotoxicity during treatment with BTK inhibitors.12,14,15 In the FLAIR trial, intensified monitoring of cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension was introduced to manage the incidence of sudden cardiac death during BTK inhibitor therapy.39,40 The relevance of this observation is supported by emerging evidence from the CLL17 trial, where frail patients treated with continuous ibrutinib monotherapy had poorer progression-free survival than those receiving fixed-duration venetoclax-based therapy.41 In addition, the cumulated risk of new-onset hypertension continued to rise during ongoing ibrutinib treatment, in contrast to the plateau observed after completion of fixed-duration regimens. This suggests that our estimates are likely conservative.

Survival among patients with CLL and hypertension treated with BTK inhibitors or chlorambucil was comparable. This contrasts with the superior outcomes of ibrutinib over chlorambucil in the Resonate-2 trial, which enrolled a frail population without del(17p).42 The difference likely reflects treatment selection bias inherent to our nonrandomized cohort, where first-line ibrutinib use was largely restricted to patients with del(17p) or TP53 aberrations, consistent with contemporary clinical guidelines.13,43,44 In the ELEVATE-TN trial, the 4-year OS for patients with CLL with del(17p), TP53 aberrations, and/or unmutated immunoglobulin heavy chain status receiving acalabrutinib-based therapy ranged from 75% to 86%.45 The patients in these subgroups are comparable with the patients in our cohort receiving targeted treatment. We observed shorter survival for patients with hypertension treated with BTK inhibitors, whereas patients without hypertension had OS comparable to that of the ELEVATE-TN trial. Our results highlight the importance for real-world evidence with longer follow-up, including frailer patients usually excluded from trials. However, because treatment choices in our study were determined by clinical indication, this study design is not appropriate to determine superiority of one treatment regimen over another.

Preexisting hypertension has been linked to increased mortality upon sepsis, and hypertension emerged as one of the most common comorbidities among patients with severe disease during the COVID-19 pandemic.46, 47, 48 In our study, patients aged <75 years with hypertension showed a higher cumulative incidence of infection-related death in crude analyses. However, this association was not statistically significant in multivariable models adjusted for sex, age at CLL diagnosis, and CCI score, likely due to the small sample size and few infection-related events in subgroups. Results from a meta-analysis suggested that the risk of death from COVID-19 in patients with preexisting hypertension was particularly evident in younger patients, aligning with our findings in CLL.49 As secondary immune deficiency is inherent to CLL, infection-related mortality is broadly perceived as a CLL-specific cause of death.50 Our findings support that hypertension, coexisting with CLL in almost 50% of our patient population, affects the risk of infection-related mortality. These findings merit hypertension as an important comorbidity to access in patients with CLL, and may be useful to inform antibiotic treatment strategies.

Interestingly, patients with CLL and hypertension had seemingly longer time to CLL treatment initiation, although this difference was not statistically significant in multivariable models. This observation is consistent with previous findings in patients with type 2 diabetes.9 We speculate whether individuals receiving routine care for hypertension are also diagnosed with CLL earlier due to surveillance bias, resulting in a longer interval between CLL diagnosis and treatment.51,52 However, if earlier detection was the driving factor, improved survival and lower CLL-IPI at diagnosis would be expected, neither of which was observed in our data. In our study, the prevalence of hypertension at CLL diagnosis (46%) was comparable to that of the matched population. As such, we did not observe an increased prevalence of CLL among patients with hypertension, further arguing against diagnostic bias. Overall, the longer time to CLL treatment combined with the increased mortality in patients with CLL with hypertension, highlights the complex relationship between hypertension and CLL progression, and underscores the importance of not deferring therapy in these patients when meeting International Workshop on CLL criteria for treatment.

We found no differences in survival based on treatment with different single-drug antihypertensive drug classes (inhibitors of the renin-angiotensin-system, calcium channel blockers, or thiazide-like diuretics). This is consistent with multiple previous studies showing mixed or neutral effects of different antihypertension drugs on cancer risk53,54 and outcomes.54, 55, 56 Upon cancer treatment, thiazide-like diuretics are not recommended due to side effects, for example increased risk of hypokalemia, hyponatremia, and potential worsening of hypovolemic states or dehydration, all unfavorable when initiating CLL treatment.20,32

Our study has limitations inherent to its observational and retrospective design. Although we adjusted for key confounders, there may be residual confounding from unmeasured lifestyle-related factors.10 Further studies investigating obesity and smoking are ongoing. We also observed that patients with CLL and hypertension were more likely to have elevated B2M levels than patients without hypertension. Of note, B2M levels varied significantly across age groups, consistent with an age-related decline in renal function.57,58 The median follow-up time was predictably shorter than in trial settings, consistent with the shorter survival in real-world cohorts that include patients who are older, frailer, and more comorbid.59 Nevertheless, the maximum attainable follow-up was substantial for patients who remained alive throughout the study period.

Comorbidities in this study were based on hospital discharge diagnoses. Meanwhile, hypertension diagnoses were based on both ICD-10 codes and prescriptions of antihypertensive drugs, with most patients with hypertension identified using the latter. Our definition of hypertension may have included some patients treated for nonhypertensive cardiac conditions. To address this potential misclassification, we conducted a sensitivity analysis adjusting for cardiac comorbidities. In this analysis, the association between hypertension and OS was slightly smaller and no longer statistically significant. This suggests that part of the observed effect of hypertension on survival may be explained by coexisting cardiac conditions. The study design did not permit quantification of hypertension beyond the number of antihypertensive medications used. Furthermore, assessment of hypertension control during follow-up was not feasible, as available blood pressure measurements were limited to in-hospital contacts, and thus potentially confounded by acute illness, infection, or white-coat syndrome.

This study provides evidence from a large population-based cohort, including frail and comorbid individuals, highlighting the impact of hypertension on outcomes in CLL. Our findings demonstrate that hypertension is associated with poorer survival following CLL diagnosis and first-line treatment, particularly among individuals aged <75 years who are at increased risk of infection-related death. A similar increased risk of death associated with hypertension was observed among individuals without CLL in the general population, indicating that the effect among patients with CLL is attributable to hypertension itself rather than a synergistic effect between CLL and hypertension. The findings underscore the importance of comprehensive comorbidity management in patients with cancer and the need to consider the clinical impact of specific comorbidities. Recognizing the risk associated with hypertension may guide infection management and support regular monitoring in primary care for patients with CLL and coexisting hypertension. In the future, these results should be reassessed in a population of patients treated with targeted therapies to confirm whether the lack of interaction between CLL and hypertension on survival persists in the modern treatment era.
