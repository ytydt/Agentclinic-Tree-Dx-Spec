Key Points

  • •

    Dynamic on-treatment cfEBV-DNA is significantly associated with prognosis in patients with early-stage NKTCL.

  • •

    cfEBV-DNA response phenotype serves as a prognostic biomarker and may inform risk-adapted treatment strategies in early-stage NKTCL.

Visual Abstract

Abstract

Although circulating tumor DNA has shown promise in real-time monitoring of recurrence risk in various solid tumors, the clinical relevance of cell-free Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) DNA (cfEBV-DNA) in natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (NKTCL) remains unclear. In this study, 710 consecutive patients with early-stage NKTCL were included from 5 hospitals between January 2014 and December 2022, all of whom underwent longitudinal monitoring of cfEBV-DNA. All patients received asparaginase-based induction chemotherapy (IC) followed by radiation therapy (RT). The pretreatment cfEBV-DNA was positive in 487 patients (68.6%), 258 of whom became negative after the first cycles of IC. During chemotherapy, the percentage of patients becoming cfEBV-DNA–negative increased, whereas the velocity of cfEBV-DNA clearance decreased. Achieving cfEBV-DNA negativity during different treatment phases (post–IC cycle 1 [post-IC1] to post-IC6, and post-RT) was significantly associated with better progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared with those who remained positive (all P < .01). Subsequently, using unsupervised clustering analysis, patients were classified into 4 cfEBV-DNA response phenotypes: (1) consistently negative, (2) early response, (3) late response, and (4) no response. These cohorts had significantly different 5-year PFS (94%, 83%, 57%, and 18%, respectively; P value for trend < .001) and OS (98%, 91%, 70%, and 33%, respectively; P value for trend < .001). In the Cox multivariable analyses, cfEBV-DNA response phenotype was still independently correlated with PFS and OS. In conclusion, dynamic monitoring of on-treatment cfEBV-DNA provides longitudinally prognostic information in patients with early-stage NKTCL and may have therapy implications.

Introduction

Natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (NKTCL) is a subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma primarily originating in the upper aerodigestive tract.1 It is highly heterogeneous, with a 5-year cumulative survival rate that varies widely from 30% to 90%.2,3 The prognosis for Ann Arbor stage I and II NKTCL has improved with the adoption of asparaginase-based chemotherapy and improved radiation therapy (RT).4, 5, 6 However, certain tumors adapt to the selective pressures of treatment, resulting in increased resistance in tumor cells and ultimately therapeutic failure.7 There are several prognostic models, including the Korean Prognostic Index, the Prognostic Index of Natural Killer Lymphoma, and Nomogram-Revised Risk Index, use pretherapy covariates to estimate survival.8, 9, 10 However, none of them are dynamic, limiting their utility in evaluating progression risk longitudinally.

Measuring circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is gaining widespread use because of its minimally invasive nature and rapid turnaround, which shows diagnostic and prognostic value before treatment.11,12 Dynamic changes in ctDNA usually occur earlier than changes in tumor diameter during the treatment course; therefore, ctDNA has been incorporated in evaluating response in solid tumors.13 Longitudinal monitoring of on-treatment ctDNA allows for real-time prediction of recurrence risk, enabling risk-adapted and personalized patient management.14,15

In Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)–associated malignancies, such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma and NKTCL, EBV genomes are typically maintained in an episomal form within tumor cells. Cell-free EBV DNA (cfEBV-DNA) is predominantly derived from EBV-infected malignant cells and released into the circulation as cell-free DNA during tumor cell apoptosis and necrosis, and it is commonly regarded as a surrogate of ctDNA.14,16,17 cfEBV-DNA concentration correlates with tumor burden, making it a useful marker for monitoring therapy response and estimating prognosis.18,19 In EBV-associated nasopharyngeal carcinoma, dynamic cfEBV-DNA monitoring has proven valuable for real-time risk assessment and treatment response evaluation.14,20 In NKTCL, cfEBV-DNA at diagnosis is a prognostic covariate and has been incorporated into several prognostic models.9,21 Beyond baseline assessment, a small retrospective study reported that the dynamic monitoring cfEBV-DNA after remission predicts relapse,22 and a recent study demonstrated that interim cfEBV-DNA positivity is an independent predictor of poor survival in early-stage NKTCL.23 However, existing studies in NKTCL have largely relied on single or limited on-treatment time points, and the prognostic significance of longitudinal cfEBV-DNA dynamics throughout the entire treatment course has not been systematically evaluated.

In this study, we studied the predictive value of dynamic cfEBV-DNA monitoring in 710 consecutive patients with early-stage NKTCL receiving asparaginase-based chemotherapy and RT. We further identified 4 response patterns correlating with progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). This stratification highlights the potential of using risk-based patient grouping to inform treatment adjustments, enabling more personalized therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Patients

The medical records of 1825 consecutive patients diagnosed with NKTCL between January 2014 and December 2022 were collected from 5 hospitals of the Chinese Southwest Oncology Group. A CONSORT flow diagram is displayed in supplemental Figure 1. The investigators were experts in lymphoma management, and each center had specialized lymphoma pathologists. Pathology slides were reviewed by at least 2 expert pathologists at each participating center. Discordances were resolved by central review. Study entry criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed NKTCL with EBV positivity by EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization, according to the fourth World Health Organization Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours criteria24; (2) Ann Arbor stage I or II; (3) asparaginase-based induction chemotherapy (IC) followed by RT; (4) availability of cfEBV-DNA quantification at baseline, after each chemotherapy cycle and within 1 month after RT; and (5) complete clinical and survival data. A total of 710 patients met these criteria. Staging were as follows: (1) panendoscopy of the upper aerodigestive tract; (2) magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography (CT) of the head and neck; (3) positron emission tomography–CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis; and (4) bone marrow aspirate and multiparameter flow cytometry.

cfEBV-DNA testing

Blood samples (5 mL) were collected in EDTA tubes, centrifuged, and the plasma viral DNA was extracted and stored at −80°C until further analysis.20 cfEBV-DNA was quantified using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction.25 Concentration was calculated according to the standardized procedures employed by the participating hospitals. Any detectable cfEBV-DNA was scored as positive, whereas undetectable results were interpreted as values below the assay-specific detection limit.9

Therapy

Patients received 1 to 6 courses of asparagine-based chemotherapy followed by RT as recommended in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines.26 RT was given in accordance with the International Lymphoma Radiation Oncology Group guidelines for NKTCL.27 The clinical target volume included the primary site and high-risk regions. Median dose was 54 Gy (range, 40-64).

Follow-up

Patients were followed every 3 months during the first 2 years, every 6 months for the next 3 years, and annually thereafter. At each visit, routine clinical evaluations and cfEBV-DNA analyses were conducted. Disease progression or recurrence was identified through a combination of clinical symptoms, imaging methods (including CT, magnetic resonance imaging, or positron emission tomography–CT) conducted every 6 months during the first 2 years and annually thereafter. A biopsy was conducted when feasible.

Study end points and statistical analysis

χ2 tests were used to assess associations between categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. To depict the dynamic changes in on-treatment cfEBV-DNA, we calculated the cfEBV-DNA clearance proportion at each time point defined as the percentage of patients with undetectable cfEBV-DNA among those previously cfEBV-DNA–positive. The cfEBV-DNA change rate (△cfEBV-DNA) was defined as the difference in clearance proportion between consecutive therapy courses adjusted for the interval between cycles.

PFS was defined as the interval from diagnosis to relapse, progression, death from any cause, or censored at last follow-up. OS was defined as the interval from diagnosis to death from any cause or censored at the date of the last follow-up. In survivors at the time of data cutoff, their data were included until the last documented follow-up. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Landmark analyses were performed at on-treatment time points. Only patients who were event-free (for PFS) and alive (for OS) at the corresponding cfEBV-DNA assessment time point were included, and survival was evaluated from the landmark forward. Treatment response, including complete remission (CR), partial remission (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD), was assessed according to the Lugano 2014 criteria.28 Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was conducted to identify independent prognostic covariates. Hierarchical clustering analysis based on P values and hazard ratios was conducted to identify discrete patterns of cfEBV-DNA changes during therapy.14 The prognostic significance of different phenotypes was assessed using the log-rank test and validated in multivariable analyses as previously described. Statistical significance was defined as a P value of <.05 in a 2-sided test. Statistical analyses used R software (version 4.2.1; Institute for Statistics and Mathematics, Vienna, Austria).

This study on human patients was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of Declaration of Helsinki II and approved by the participating hospitals.

Results

Covariates of patients are displayed in Table 1. A total of 495 patients (70%) were male. In addition, 39 patients (5%) received 1 chemotherapy course, 93 (13%) received 2, 185 (26%) received 3, 273 (39%) received 4, 36 (5%) received 5, and 84 (12%) underwent 6. cfEBV-DNA was positive at study entry in 487 patients (69%), with a median concentration 1.9 × 103 copies per mL (IQR, 0.30 × 103 to 7.69 × 103 copies per mL), which was significantly correlated with Ann Arbor stage (median, 0.23 × 103 in stage I vs 1.12 × 103 in stage II; P < .001, Mann-Whitney U test).

On-treatment cfEBV-DNA as an independent prognostic marker

At the time of the last follow-up, 171 patients (24%) had relapsed or progressed and 114 (16%) had died. Median follow-up was 50 months (IQR, 33-95). The 5-year PFS was 72% (95% confidence interval [CI], 68-76) and 5-year OS was 81% (95% CI, 78-84).

We evaluated the prognostic accuracy of cfEBV-DNA at various time points. Pretreatment cfEBV-DNA positivity was significantly associated with worse 5-year PFS (64% [95% CI, 59-69] vs 90% [95% CI, 85-94]; P < .001) and 5-year OS (75% [95% CI, 70-79] vs 94% [95% CI, 91-98]; P < .001; Figure 1A). In addition, becoming cfEBV-DNA–negative after the first cycle of IC (post-IC1), post-IC2, and at the end of treatment was each associated with significantly improved PFS and OS compared with those remaining positive (P < .01; Figure 1B-D). Similar associations were observed at the remaining on-treatment time points (all P < .01; supplemental Figure 2). These associations remained consistent in landmark analyses, in which only patients who were alive or event-free at the corresponding on-treatment time points were included. Across different on-treatment time points, becoming cfEBV-DNA–negative remained significantly associated with improved PFS and OS (all P < .01; supplemental Figure 3). In the multivariable analyses, cfEBV-DNA status after each chemotherapy course and RT was still an independent prognostic covariate for PFS and OS (Table 2). Diagnostic performance analyses further showed that cfEBV-DNA negativity at on-treatment time points was associated with a high negative predictive value for both disease progression and OS, whereas persistent positivity demonstrated high specificity and increased positive predictive value (supplemental Tables 1 and 2).

We next explored the association between imaging-based treatment response and cfEBV-DNA status after IC and at the end of treatment. After IC, patients with unfavorable imaging responses (SD/PD) were significantly more likely to have detectable cfEBV-DNA than those achieving CR/PR (55.6% vs 16.3%; odds ratio, 6.43; 95% CI, 3.44-12.04; P < .001; supplemental Table 3). This association became even more pronounced at the end of treatment, with detectable cfEBV-DNA observed in 70.7% of patients with SD/PD compared with 9.8% of those with CR/PR (odds ratio, 22.12; 95% CI, 10.77-45.44; P < .001; supplemental Table 4). We also observed that favorable imaging responses did not fully eliminate prognostic heterogeneity. Even among patients achieving CR/PR, cfEBV-DNA status remained informative for survival stratification. After IC, patients with undetectable cfEBV-DNA experienced significantly improved PFS and OS compared with those who remained cfEBV-DNA detectable (both P < .001; supplemental Figure 4A-B). Consistent findings were observed at the end of treatment. Among patients classified as CR/PR on end-of-treatment imaging assessment, cfEBV-DNA negativity continued to be associated with significantly more favorable PFS and OS compared with that in those with persistent cfEBV-DNA detectability (both P < .001; supplemental Figure 4C-D).

Dynamic monitoring

We also assessed cfEBV-DNA dynamics during therapy. Overall, 223 patients (31%) were cfEBV-DNA–negative before therapy, whereas487 patients (69%) were cfEBV-DNA–positive before therapy. Of the 487 patients, 258 (36%) became cfEBV-DNA–negative after the first chemotherapy course. Among 229 patients who remained cfEBV-DNA–positive after the first chemotherapy course (94%), 217 received 2 to 6 more courses of chemotherapy, whereas 12 transitioned directly to RT. Of the 217 patients who received more courses of chemotherapy, 107 (49%) became cfEBV-DNA–negative. During chemotherapy, the percentage of patients becoming cfEBV-DNA–negative increased (Figure 2A), whereas the velocity of cfEBV-DNA clearance (△cfEBV-DNA) decreased (Figure 2B). Interestingly, 62 patients experienced a transition from cfEBV-DNA–negative to cfEBV-DNA–positive during the IC phase. This resolved in 31 as the subsequent IC continued. Overall, 153 patients (22%) who were cfEBV-DNA–positive after chemotherapy subsequently received RT, of whom 68 (44%) became cfEBV-DNA–negative. In contrast, 12 patients who were cfEBV-DNA–negative before RT subsequently became cfEBV-DNA–positive. At the end of therapy, 57 of 97 patients who were cfEBV-DNA–positive remained cfEBV-DNA–positive throughout therapy. In addition, 40 patients who became cfEBV-DNA–positive after becoming cfEBV-DNA–negative never became cfEBV-DNA–negative again.

Based on these data, we identified 8 cfEBV-DNA response patterns: (1) C1, patients who were cfEBV-DNA–negative before therapy and remained negative (n = 206 [29%]); (2) C2, patients who were cfEBV-DNA–positive before therapy became cfEBV-DNA–negative after 1 course of chemotherapy and remained negative (n = 233 [32%]); (3) C3, patients who were cfEBV-DNA–positive before therapy became cfEBV-DNA–negative after the second course and remained negative (n = 55 [8%]); (4) C4, patients who were cfEBV-DNA–positive before therapy became cfEBV-DNA–negative after 3 to 6 therapy courses and remained negative o (n = 38 [5%]); (5) C5, patients who were cfEBV-DNA–positive before therapy became cfEBV-DNA–negative only after RT (n = 47 [7%]); (6) C6, patients who exhibited a transient shift from cfEBV-DNA–negative to cfEBV-DNA–positive and ultimately reverted to cfEBV-DNA–negative after subsequent therapy (n = 34 [5%]); (7) C7, patients who were transiently cfEBV-DNA–negative but became cfEBV-DNA–positive despite continued therapy (n = 40 [6%]); and (8) C8, patients who were persistently cfEBV-DNA–positive (n = 57 [8%]).

cfEBV-DNA response patterns and prognoses

Subsequently, we studied correlations between cfEBV-DNA response patterns and PFS and OS. Overall, patients who achieved earlier and sustained cfEBV-DNA clearance experienced more favorable PFS and OS, whereas delayed clearance, cfEBV-DNA recurrence, or persistent positivity were associated with inferior outcomes (supplemental Figure 5).

We further identified 4 distinct response phenotypes from 8 patterns using unsupervised clustering analysis (supplemental Figure 6A). Cohorts were classified as consistently negative (n = 206 [29%]), early response (n = 288 [40%]), late response (n = 119 [17%]), and no response (n = 97 [14%]). Next, we investigated potential associations between response phenotypes and clinical covariates including disease stage and pretreatment cfEBV-DNA status. Baseline cfEBV-DNA positivity and stage II disease were significantly associated with a reduced likelihood of early response and an increased risk of not responding (supplemental Figure 6B). The 4 response phenotypes had significant differences in 5-year PFS (94% [95% CI, 90-98], 83% [95% CI, 77-88], 57% [95% CI, 47-68] and 18% [95% CI, 11-29]; pairwise comparisons all P < .05; P for trend < 0.001) and 5-year OS (98% [95% CI, 96-99], 91% [95% CI, 87-95], 70% [95% CI, 60-81], and 33% [95% CI, 23-45]; pairwise comparisons all P < .05; P for trend < 0.001) (Figure 3). Response phenotype remained independently associated with PFS and OS in multivariable analyses (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we present comprehensive data on cfEBV-DNA throughout the entire course of treatment, with a particular emphasis on clearance patterns observed during therapy. To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date focusing on longitudinal cfEBV-DNA detection in patients with early-stage NKTCL. Comprehensive profiling of molecular response dynamics to IC and RT revealed that nearly half of patients with detectable baseline cfEBV-DNA achieved molecular remission after just 1 IC cycle, reflecting marked chemosensitivity and an associated favorable prognosis. Notably, the most substantial decline in cfEBV-DNA occurred after IC1 across all treatment arms, implying that dominant tumor clones harbor truncal mutations sensitive to asparaginase-based regimens. In contrast, the subsequent slower clearance trajectory suggests that residual cfEBV-DNA after IC may originate from subclones with inherent resistance to standard therapies. The results highlight the significance of longitudinal cfEBV-DNA in real-time risk monitoring and its potential as a biomarker to inform risk-adapted therapeutic strategies in NKTCL.

Recent studies in nasopharyngeal carcinoma have demonstrated that the longitudinal tracking of cfEBV-DNA levels during treatment can predict real-time progression risk, enabling more personalized and risk-adapted management strategies.14 However, the dynamics of cfEBV-DNA clearance during NKTCL treatment remain insufficiently explored. Prior studies in NKTCL have predominantly assessed cfEBV-DNA at a single on-treatment time point during sequential chemoradiotherapy,21,23,29,30 limiting insight into its longitudinal biological significance. A further challenge is the lack of standardized sampling schedules, which compromises the consistency and interpretability of longitudinal cfEBV-DNA data across patients. In this study, we present the most comprehensive data on cfEBV-DNA throughout the entire course of treatment, with a particular emphasis on the clearance pattern observed during the IC phase. We found that, similar to nasopharyngeal carcinoma, the dynamic monitoring of cfEBV-DNA in NKTCL could predict the real-time risk of recurrence and progression. Overall, our results suggest that the dynamic monitoring of cfEBV-DNA could offer shared clinical value for managing EBV-associated malignancies.

Given that ∼5% of healthy individuals harbor detectable cfEBV-DNA in plasma,31 false positivity has been a concern in the use of cfEBV-DNA as a tumor biomarker. Prior studies have demonstrated that tumor-derived cfEBV-DNA is typically persistent, and that longitudinal EBV-DNA follow-up can reduce false-positive interpretations.32 Consistent with these observations, our diagnostic performance analyses showed that persistent cfEBV-DNA positivity was associated with high specificity and an increased positive predictive value. Moreover, longitudinal cfEBV-DNA changes were largely concordant with radiographic tumor evaluations. Nevertheless, cfEBV-DNA dynamics were not always concordant with imaging-based assessments. Among patients exhibiting transient cfEBV-DNA positivity, only a small proportion showed concurrent radiological progression; however, these patients had significantly poorer outcomes than those who remained consistently cfEBV-DNA–negative. This discrepancy was even more pronounced in patients with persistent cfEBV-DNA positivity who experienced significantly inferior outcomes compared with cfEBV-DNA–negative patients despite the absence of radiographic progression. Overall, these findings suggest that the integration of imaging assessments with cfEBV-DNA dynamics provides earlier or complementary signals of disease relapse compared with that with imaging alone.

The dynamic monitoring of cfEBV-DNA provides increased value in detecting the risk of progression in real time compared with determinations at 1 time point, enabling timely adjustments to treatment strategies that can further improve patient survival. First, for patients with detectable cfEBV-DNA at baseline, most became cfEBV-DNA–negative after the first 2 cycles of IC (early response group). These patients exhibited a favorable prognosis, highlighting the strong sensitivity of tumor clones to IC. The rate of cfEBV-DNA clearance slowed over time during IC. For these patients, short-course IC combined with RT may be sufficient, eliminating the need for additional intensification of IC. Moreover, a subset of patients failing to achieve early cfEBV-DNA clearance subsequently achieved cfEBV-DNA negativity after treatment modification or radiotherapy and had more favorable outcomes than those with persistent cfEBV-DNA positivity. Therefore, for patients with delayed cfEBV-DNA clearance (late response group), adjusting the treatment strategy, such as switching to RT, may enhance tumor control more effectively. Finally, patients with cfEBV-DNA positivity after treatments (no response group) are associated with the worst prognosis, suggesting the presence of drug-resistant tumor clones. For this subset of patients, intensifying systemic treatment, such as the incorporation of immunotherapy, may be needed to target the remaining drug-resistant clones. Further prospective studies are needed to evaluate whether monitoring on-treatment cfEBV-DNA at different time points can guide risk-based therapy and optimize treatment strategies for patients with NKTCL.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As a retrospective study, physician selection bias inevitably contributes to treatment heterogeneity. To mitigate this, we standardized the treatments administered to patients, ensuring adherence to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines for first-line therapy, thereby minimizing treatment variability. Moreover, the treatment choices during the IC and RT phases may have been influenced by cfEBV-DNA results. However, multivariate analyses adjusted for confounding variables consistently confirmed the prognostic relevance of our response phenotypes. Finally, the EBV-DNA polymerase chain reaction–based assay lacks a universally established standard protocol. Nonetheless, we have employed rigorous quality control measures to minimize assay variability, and in our primary analysis, we adopted the most widely accepted classification, detectable vs undetectable cfEBV-DNA,9 to increase the generalizability of our conclusions.

In conclusion, we found that the dynamic monitoring of cfEBV-DNA response and pattern thereof correlates with PFS and OS in patients with early-stage NKTCL. Our study highlights the unique patterns of longitudinal on-treatment cfEBV-DNA and underscores its potential for real-time risk monitoring, providing valuable guidance for risk-based treatment strategies in patients with NKTCL.
