Treatment of von Willebrand disease

Abstract

Treatment for von Willebrand disease (VWD), the most common inherited bleeding disorder, has advanced considerably since its initial description by Erik von Willebrand who first described a family in the Åland Islands with severe bleeding. Infusion of von Willebrand factor (VWF)–containing concentrates continues to serve as the foundation of management, in addition to desmopressin, hormonal therapies, and antifibrinolytic agents such as tranexamic acid. Recent advancements in VWD management are underscored by the publication of evidence-based international guidelines addressing critical aspects such as VWF prophylaxis, the use of anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents for cardiovascular disease, and comprehensive management during surgical as well as obstetric and gynecologic scenarios. This review provides an overview of modern treatment strategies, discusses management in special populations, and explores emerging therapies and future directions for improving VWD care.

Introduction

Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder and is characterized by either a quantitative (types 1 and 3) or qualitative defect (type 2).1 Although inherited equally by both sexes, VWD disproportionately affects those assigned female at birth due to the presence of reproductive-related bleeding, such as heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) and postpartum hemorrhage. Treatment of VWD has evolved significantly in the 100 years since Erik von Willebrand described a family in the Åland Islands with severe bleeding,2 first with the recognition that bleeding episodes could be treated with plasma infusions, then with cryoprecipitate, followed by the development of purified plasma derivates with varying ratios of von Willebrand factor (VWF) and factor VIII (FVIII).3 In recent years, there has been an explosion of research into the diagnosis and management of VWD, stimulated by increasing recognition of the underdiagnosis of the disorder as well as ongoing investment in the scientific and clinical pipeline development of emerging therapies for inherited bleeding disorders.4,5 Advances over the past 100 years have led to serial iterations of clinical practice guidelines from groups such as the US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute,6 the UK Haemophilia Centre Doctors’ Organization,7 and the Italian Association of Haemophilia Centres.8 In 2021, through a collaborative effort between the American Society of Hematology, the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis, the National Bleeding Disorders Foundation (formerly the National Hemophilia Foundation), and the World Federation of Hemophilia, international evidence-based guidelines on the management of VWD were published,9 along with systematic reviews informing recommendations on the use of prophylaxis in VWD,10 surgical management of VWD,11 and gynecologic and obstetric management of VWD.12 A monitoring report with updated searches using terminology from the original systematic reviews was recently published, and affirmed that the recommendations from the 2021 guidelines continue to stand based on available evidence through 2024.13

This review covers the principles of management, modern treatment strategies, and paradigms, navigating special populations and scenarios such as surgery or prophylaxis, emerging therapies, and future directions for research for improvement management.

Principles of management

The most important principle of management is to create an individualized therapy plan addressing the impact of bleeding on the health, safety, and quality of life of the affected person and their family. Although clinicians may believe that a specific form or anatomic site of bleeding may be minor in nature, the effect may dramatically impact quality of life and function. Therapeutic options include those that increase circulating endogenous VWF levels, replacement therapy with exogenous VWF concentrates, and use of adjunctive pan-hemostatic measures.

Increasing endogenous VWF levels

Several therapies can increase circulating endogenous VWF levels. Desmopressin (1-deamino-8-d-arginine vasopressin) is a synthetic analog of arginine vasopressin, and induces release of stored VWF and FVIII from the vascular endothelium.14 The therapy may be given IV/subcutaneously (0.3 μg/kg with a maximum dose of 20 μg) or intranasally (150-300 μg depending on weight). It is important to note that the lower-dose enuresis products are not appropriate, as they only deliver a dose of 10 to 20 μg, and that attention to specific product prescription is needed to avoid dosing errors. Although usually very effective in type 1 VWD, particularly those with levels >0.30 IU/mL, and in some patients with type 2A and type 2M VWD with confirmed responsiveness, desmopressin is contraindicated in type 3 VWD due to lack of stored VWF for release, and in type 2B VWD due to the risk of worsening thrombocytopenia and further bleeding. Tachyphylaxis is also an issue with desmopressin, with repeated doses leading to decreasing efficacy. Individuals receiving desmopressin should have free water intake restrictions clearly outlined along with serial monitoring of serum sodium levels due to the risk of hyponatremia. Common side effects including flushing, dizziness, headache, nausea, and fluid retention, and some centers will limit use of desmopressin in individuals aged >60 to 70 years or in those with known cardiovascular disease (CVD).15 Additionally, desmopressin is contraindicated in children aged <2 years due to the risk of hyponatremia and seizures.

In 2020, a recall affected intranasal desmopressin due to production issues by the primary company manufacturing the therapy. Compounding pharmacies in the United States and The Netherlands have maintained a limited supply; however, these compounded versions have short shelf-lives (typically 30-90 days) and access remains limited.16

The 2021 VWD guideline panel proposed a standard definition of desmopressin responsiveness as, “…an increase of at least >2 times the baseline VWF activity level and a sustained increase of both VWF and factor VIII (FVIII):C levels >0.50 IU/mL for at least 4 hours.”17(p565) This was simultaneously intended to assist in the recognition and diagnosis of type 1C VWD, in which there is increased clearance, and also document that VWF levels remained adequate throughout the typical time period in which minor surgical procedures may be performed after administration of desmopressin.

Replacement therapy: VWF-containing concentrates

Most of the commercially available VWF concentrates are plasma derived (pdVWF), include a multistep process for viral inactivation, and contain varying ratios of VWF to FVIII. pdVWF products classified under the generic name antihemophilic factor/von Willebrand factor complex (human) include the brand products Humate-P with a VWF to FVIII ratio of 2:1, wilate with a ratio of 1:1, Fanhdi with a ratio of 1.58:1, and the highly-purified product Wilfactin (only available in Europe) with a ratio of 50:1.

Recently, vonicog alfa (Vonvendi), a recombinant VWF (rVWF) product, has been developed and licensed in limited jurisdictions. This product does not contain any FVIII, therefore, if the baseline FVIII level is <0.4 IU/mL or is not known at the time of treatment, coadministration of recombinant FVIII is recommended to ensure initial hemostasis when treated for on-demand bleeding. Because rVWF has not been exposed to circulating ADAMTS13, it is enhanced in terms of high-molecular weight multimer composition.18,19 Although rVWF undergoes glycosylation during production, in a similar manner to endogenous VWF, there are differences in sialylation and blood group antigen expression that contribute to differences in half-life.20 Because rVWF is not derived from human blood, it is often acceptable to those who decline blood products for personal or religious reasons, such as Jehovah’s Witnesses.

In general, the half-life of pdVWF is ∼10 to 12 hours, whereas the half-life of rVWF approaches 20 hours; however, patient and situational use factors may lead to significant variability in the half-life, and therefore dosing frequency requirements. Availability of specific VWF concentrates and approved indications varies widely by geographic region.

Adjunctive therapies

Several adjunctive therapies are available for use in VWD, not because of their ability to increase VWF levels, but because they work across many different types of bleeding. The antifibrinolytic agents tranexamic acid (TXA) and -aminocaproic acid, both derived from the amino acid lysine, block enzymatic action of the fibrinolytic protein plasmin, thereby delaying fibrinolysis and clot clearance.

Recombinant FVIIa is an option for refractory bleeding in spite of other treatment options. This is also effective in the rare scenario in which an individual has a VWF or FVIII inhibitor.

Therapies directed against vascular endothelial growth factor may be useful to address arteriovenous malformations, such as those commonly seen in people with type 2A VWD or the acquired von Willebrand syndrome.21 Additionally, various case reports and case series describe the efficacy of statin therapy, particularly high-dose atorvastatin, in people with VWD and refractory bleeding from arteriovenous malformations.22

Modern treatment strategies and paradigms

On-demand therapy

Most people with VWD will only require on-demand therapy to treat bleeding episodes. Major bleeding was defined by a 2021 expert panel as, “episodes requiring hospital admission, surgical intervention, blood transfusion, hemoglobin drop of ≥2 g/dL, bleeding involving critical areas (eg, intracranial, intraspinal, intraocular, retroperitoneal, intra-articular, pericardial, or intramuscular with compartment syndrome), or recurrent bleeding affecting the ability to attend normal schooling, working, or social activity.”17(p566) For major bleeding, dosing VWF concentrate of at least 0.80 to 1.00 IU/mL to achieve both peak FVIII and VWF activities, and maintaining trough levels of at least 0.50 IU/mL is reasonable until hemostasis is achieved. All other episodes of bleeding would be considered minor bleeding, and include a heterogeneous mixture of bleeding events, therefore data to guide therapy are limited. Desmopressin can be considered for minor bleeding events in which the patient is known to be desmopressin responsive. Antifibrinolytic therapy may be particularly effective for minor bleeding involving mucosal sites such as epistaxis or heavy menstrual bleeding; however, escalation to VWF concentrate should be considered for persistent bleeding that does not respond quickly to more conservative therapies and supportive measures.

Prophylaxis

For those with severe or frequent bleeding events, administration of VWF concentrate prophylaxis is suggested by the 2021 VWD guidelines. An expert panel proposed a definition of prophylaxis in VWD as, “a period of at least 3 to 6 months of treatment consisting of VWF concentrate administered at least once weekly, or for women with HMB, use of VWF concentrate administered at least once per menstrual cycle.”17(p566)

In the systematic review informing the 2021 international guidelines, there was a single randomized controlled trial of 19 patients and 13 pre-post analyses with varying levels of explicit or implicit comparison.10 Prophylaxis for VWD was shown to reduce the overall risk of bleeding, improved the time of the first bleeding event after initiation, reduced episodes of epistaxis, and reduced the risk of bleeding episodes, need for hospitalization, and burden of heavy menstrual bleeding. Since the publication of the guidelines and the systematic review, there have been several other studies that provide further support for use of prophylaxis in VWD. The VWDMin study was a phase 3, open-label, randomized crossover trial comparing rVWF with TXA for heavy menstrual bleeding. The study was stopped early due to slow patient accrual and did not show either treatment to be superior to the other; however, there were no serious adverse events.23 In another phase 3 study, regular prophylaxis with rVWF led to a 91% reduction in the spontaneous annualized bleeding rate compared with on-demand therapy, and those switching from pdVWF to rVWF continued to demonstrate good control of bleeding symptoms.24 The WIL-31 study evaluated the efficacy and safety of the pdVWF product wilate, demonstrating an 84% reduction in mean annualized bleeding rate compared with on-demand therapy, and specifically there were no serious adverse events or FVIII accumulation.25 Additionally, 5 patients in the WIL-31 study had significant improvement in heavy menstrual bleeding with regular prophylaxis compared with on-demand therapy.

Of note, most individuals with VWD do not learn to self-infuse at an early age as is typically done with people who have the more severe forms of hemophilia; therefore, it is important to assess logistics and patient values through shared decision-making.

In the hemophilia space, there is a growing body of evidence to support pharmacokinetic (PK)-guided dosing prophylaxis strategies to optimize outcomes by ensuring that safe trough levels are maintained. In VWD, PK-guided dosing of VWF concentrate during elective surgery demonstrated feasibility, efficacy, and safety26; however, its application in long-term prophylaxis is less clear. Future studies aimed at evaluating the utility of PK-based models for personalized long-term VWF prophylaxis are needed.

Navigating special populations and scenarios

Surgical management

Perioperative management of VWD can be a particularly difficult anxiety-provoking time for both patient and hematologist. For emergency situations, there may not be much time to create and enact a perioperative hemostasis plan, so patients should carry wallet cards or have information about their type of VWD stored in their smartphone medical IDs, with details on diagnosis, emergency treatment, and contact information for their bleeding disorders center.

Planning for elective surgery should be communicated with a multidisciplinary team, including the surgeons, hematologists, nursing, anesthesia, pharmacy, and coagulation laboratory. Whenever possible, the surgery should be coordinated with a hematologist experienced in managing VWD, or in consultation with a hemophilia treatment center. If possible, surgery should be performed in a center with access to regular access to both FVIII and VWF activity testing, including on the weekends. Pharmacy staff should be notified in advance to ensure an adequate supply of VWF-containing products as well as various adjunctive therapies.

For major surgery, the 2021 VWD guidelines recommend maintaining both the FVIII activity and VWF activity at least 0.50 IU/mL for at least 3 days postoperatively.9 Higher trough values and longer time frames may be necessary for certain surgeries or procedures such as neurosurgery or spinal procedures. For minor surgeries, the guidelines suggested increasing VWF levels to at least 0.50 IU/mL and adding TXA to reduce the risk of bleeding. These recommendations were informed by 2 systematic reviews of the literature, which included 7 case series for major surgery, along with 2 randomized controlled trials and 12 case series for minor surgery.11

Obstetric and gynecologic management

As noted previously, obstetric and gynecologic management is especially important in those with VWD given the high prevalence of mucocutaneous bleeding. Heavy menstrual bleeding is common in the setting of VWD, and iron deficiency, even without anemia, is common.27 Treatment options include estrogen-containing combined hormonal contraceptives, the levonorgestrel intrauterine device, desmopressin, and VWF concentrate. For those not wishing to conceive at the current time, hormonal-based therapies are considered first-line therapy as they are very effective in reducing the burden of heavy menstrual bleeding, and generally acceptable to patients in terms of risk and side effects. Although estrogen is the most effective option for control of menstrual bleeding, progestin-only options, including oral, injectable, and implantable versions, are alternatives for those with contraindications to estrogen. For those wishing to conceive, hormonal therapies cannot be used, so the 2021 international guidelines recommend use of antifibrinolytic therapy over desmopressin due to lack of substantive side effects. Any patient with heavy menstrual bleeding should be assessed for iron deficiency, with a goal ferritin of ≥50 ng/mL based on both randomized clinical trial data28 and correlative physiology data.29

During pregnancy, VWF levels increase in almost all individuals, except those with the most severe forms of VWD. It is not uncommon for someone with type 1 VWD to have VWF levels increase to >0.50 to 0.80 IU/mL in the third trimester, but it appears that this “normalization” of circulating VWF may not be sufficient to prevent postpartum hemorrhage.30,31 During pregnancy in those without VWD, VWF and FVIII levels often increase to >1.50 IU/mL, suggesting there may be some physiologic or evolutionary need for higher levels in the peripartum setting. Particularly in those with type 2 VWD, the qualitative defect in VWF activity or FVIII binding persists, even with an increase in VWF antigen during pregnancy.

Ideal levels for neuraxial anesthesia are not known. The 2021 VWD guidelines suggest VWF levels of ≥0.50 to 1.50 IU/mL should be targeted to allow for neuraxial anesthesia, but this was a conditional recommendation based on very low certainty in the available evidence.9,12 Furthermore, a level of 0.50 to 1.50 IU/mL only suggests safety for neuraxial anesthesia from a VWD perspective, and there may be other reasons to target a higher level for the procedure. The ultimate decision on safety of neuraxial anesthesia should rest with the obstetric anesthesiologist and obstetrician managing the delivery, but simply having a diagnosis of VWD is not in and of itself a contraindication for neuraxial anesthesia if a person desires to have 1 for childbirth, provided VWF replacement can be safely delivered with appropriate laboratory monitoring available prior to insertion and removal of the epidural catheter.32

However, VWF levels may need to be higher to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. As noted, VWF levels increase starting in the second trimester and peak early in the third trimester, maintaining this plateau until ∼48 to 72 hours after delivery. Typically, VWF levels have returned to baseline by ∼7 to 21 days postpartum.31 This decrease in VWF levels coincides with the sloughing of the placental scar, the attachment spot of the placenta to the uterus, which typically occurs at around days 7 to 10. Antifibrinolytic therapy should be prescribed for ≥10 to 14 days post partum to cover this high-risk period.

One suggested approach to VWD in pregnancy is to check VWF and FVIII levels at ∼30 to 32 weeks of gestation to assess peak levels, and to aim for a minimum VWF activity of ≥0.80 IU/mL to allow neuraxial anesthesia to reduce the risk of postpartum hemorrhage. A detailed hematologic plan for labor and delivery should be entered in the chart and communicated to the multidisciplinary team, including obstetrician, obstetric anesthesiologist, primary care physician, and pediatric hematologists, including the recommendation to administer TXA 1000 mg IV at the time of delivery, with ultimate timing to be determined by the obstetrician, but typically given at the time the umbilical cord is clamped. TXA 1000 to 1500 mg may then be given orally every 8 hours thereafter for ≥10 to 14 days post partum, or as long as lochia is heavy. If VWF concentrate is needed, this should be administered for ≥72 hours post partum for a vaginal delivery, and 5 days if there has been a lower-segment cesarean delivery, including ≥24 hours after the epidural is removed.33

Postpartum thromboprophylaxis is a complex scenario that requires consideration of the patient’s thrombosis risk balanced against the risk of bleeding. Given that the risk of thrombosis carries significant long-term morbidity and mortality in the peripartum setting, normalization of VWF and FVIII activity during pregnancy likely warrants use of postpartum chemical thromboprophylaxis per typical institutional protocols and obstetric society guidelines, especially if there is prolonged immobility or cesarean birth34,35

The VWF in Pregnancy study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04146376) in the United States, and the Pregnancy and Inherited Bleeding Disorders Study in The Netherlands aim to assess the impact of specific target VWF levels on bleeding outcomes in the peripartum setting, and these studies are expected to provide more data for more specific guidance in the future.

Cardiovascular disease

CVD presents a particular challenge in people with bleeding disorders. Although CVD is fortunately less prevalent than in the general population, people with bleeding disorders can and do end up developing clinically significant disease, necessitating consideration of anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy.36, 37, 38, 39 This is often difficult for patients and families to comprehend, as they may have been told that they should avoid these therapies. Coordinated communication between the hematology, cardiology, and cardiac surgery teams is needed when these scenarios arise.

Due to a paucity of data, the 2021 VWD guidelines were only able to issue a conditional recommendation that people with VWD who required anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy should receive these therapies over the alternative of not receiving them, if needed to treat CVD.9 To safely provide these therapies, shared decision-making is necessary, along with consideration of an individualized hemostatic plan. Although there are no specific recommendations about goal VWF activity or FVIII activity available specifically for people with VWD, indirect evidence from the hemophilia literature suggests a FVIII activity of ≥0.05 IU/mL may be needed for single antiplatelet therapy, whereas dual antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation would require a FVIII activity of ≥0.15 to 0.30 IU/mL.40,41 In the absence of VWD-specific data, it is reasonable to maintain VWF activity of ≥0.05 to 0.10 IU/mL for single antiplatelet therapy, and FVIII activity of ≥0.20 to 0.30 IU/mL for dual antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation.

Emerging therapies and future directions

Novel therapies

Just as novel therapies have changed the standard of care for people living with hemophilia, there is the potential for these therapies to also have a role in the treatment of VWD. Emicizumab, a subcutaneously administered bispecific monoclonal antibody that mimics and catalyzes the reaction between coagulation FIX and FX, has shown tremendous efficacy and safety for the prevention of bleeding in people with hemophilia A both with and without inhibitors.42,43 It has shown efficacy in people with severe type 3 VWD,44 as well as other subtypes of severe VWD.45 Although emicizumab partially replaces the activity and function of FVIII, this therapy does not perform the platelet-binding function afforded by VWF, so its use may be limited to those with severely low FVIII levels such as that seen in severe types 1, 2N, or 3 VWD. There are ongoing studies to evaluate the efficacy and safety of emicizumab for the treatment of people with type 3 VWD (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06998524) and severe VWD (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05500807).

Efanesoctocog alfa, a novel bioengineered protein that fuses the DʹD3 region of VWF to FVIII to extend the half-life of the FVIII molecule, is approved for prophylaxis and treatment of bleeding events in people with hemophilia A.46 This molecule may be useful in type 2N VWD in which platelet-binding activity of VWF is intact, but FVIII half-life is short due to an inability to bind to endogenous VWF, and is currently undergoing clinical evaluation (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04770935). Rondoraptivon pegol is a pegylated aptamer that binds to the A1 domain of VWF, reducing clearance by macrophage low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein, leading to higher circulating levels of VWF.47 Additionally, it blocks the abnormal VWF platelet-binding activity that occurs in type 2B VWD, offering a potentially targeted therapy for this subtype with correction of thrombocytopenia. In a prospective phase 2 study in people with type 2B VWD, rondoraptivon pegol caused a rapid increase in both circulating VWF levels and platelet counts.48

HMB-002 is an investigational subcutaneous monovalent antibody therapy, which binds to the C-terminal cystine knot (CK)-domain of VWF, delaying clearance of VWF through direct shielding and via neonatal Fc receptor (FcRN) recycling, thereby increasing levels of both VWF and consequently FVIII. The VELORA Pioneer study is a phase 1/2, open-label study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of HMB-002 in type 1 VWD (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06754852) and recently presented preliminary data at the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis 2025 Congress.49

Also of note, VGA039, a monoclonal antibody against protein S that act as a rebalancing agent in secondary hemostasis, is currently undergoing clinical investigation for prophylaxis in VWD (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05776069). Other rebalancing agents such as marstacimab, concizumab, and fitusiran are theoretical therapeutic options, but rigorous evaluation of efficacy and safety is needed with these agents given the risk of thrombosis if not properly managed. The nanobody KB-V13A12, which crosslinks VWF to albumin, has the potential to significantly extend the half-life of VWF. Ongoing areas of work are evaluating small interfering RNA (siRNA)-based methods to silence mutant alleles specifically within VWF.

Gene therapy

Although gene therapy has been in development for several decades for hemophilia A and B, with commercial products now approved for both of these bleeding disorders, gene therapy for VWD has been more challenging. The VWF gene is very large, ∼178 kilobases in length, with 52 exons,50 precluding it from fitting inside most common gene therapy vectors. The VWF protein undergoes extensive post-translational modification, packaging, and cellular trafficking, all governed by separate genes that would not be included in a standard gene therapy product. Many gene therapy vectors target hepatocytes, which are not the native cell for synthesis of VWF and FVIII. Furthermore, gene therapy studies in mice show loss of multimerization, which provides most of the hemostatic effect of VWF. Several scientific labs are working to overcome these barriers, and move VWD toward gene therapy and gene editing as a potential cure for the future.51,52

Models of care

The comprehensive management of VWD has advanced significantly, yet its inherent complexities necessitate evolving models of care. Drawing lessons from hemophilia, the establishment and evaluation of multidisciplinary clinics represent a critical next step for VWD. These clinics could be embedded within existing comprehensive hemophilia and bleeding disorder treatment centers, or such as those embedded within another specialty focused on the unique needs of women and girls with bleeding disorders, a model championed by the Foundation for Women and Girls with Blood Disorders.53 Novel clinics serve as crucial entry points to the care system for undiagnosed individuals, facilitating a diagnosis and then subsequently offering access to treatment. Adapting such comprehensive care models, with appropriate evaluation of the efficacy of the approach, will be essential to optimize clinical outcomes and ensure equitable access to specialized expertise for all individuals with VWD.

Despite considerable progress, significant global inequities persist in VWD diagnosis and management. The World Health Organization’s Essential Medicines List has been criticized for lack of safe treatment options for people with inherited bleeding disorders; however, tremendous advocacy efforts have led to change for hemophilia therapies, which will hopefully translate to inclusion of safer therapies for VWD as well.54,55 The World Federation of Hemophilia’s World Bleeding Disorders Registry is a vital initiative, actively collecting comprehensive global data on the diagnosis and management of people with inherited bleeding disorders, including limited-resource settings.56 The inclusion of VWD in the registry has already collected initial data on >1000 people with VWD, including >500 people with type 3 VWD, and will be instrumental in informing evidence-based policy advocacy and strategically steering resources to areas of greatest need.

Conclusions

The last century has shown substantial progress in the treatment of VWD since its initial description, with current therapeutic approaches encompassing strategies to increase endogenous VWF levels and the use of VWF-containing concentrates, available in both plasma-derived and recombinant formulations. Although the 2021 international VWD guidelines provide invaluable recommendations developed for high-resource settings, their adaptation and implementation in resource-constrained environments are critical steps toward addressing these disparities and fostering truly equitable VWD care worldwide. Despite these advancements, the spectrum of therapeutic options for VWD notably lags those available for less common bleeding disorders, such as hemophilia A and B. As clinical trials have largely focused on hemophilia-derived outcomes such as joint bleeding and surgical hemostasis, many studies have neglected to include reproductive tract bleeding as a meaningful outcome, even though it is the most reported bleeding symptom in VWD. This disparity underscores the imperative for continued innovation. Given the nature of rare disease research, a proposed set of common definitions and core outcomes has been published to facilitate clinical studies in VWD.17,57 The coreVWD initiative specifically calls for inclusion of reproductive tract bleeding outcomes, such as menstrual blood loss, menstrual period duration, need for transfusion due to menstrual bleeding, and postpartum hemorrhage, in addition to the standard outcomes of interest in prophylaxis and perioperative management. Although the past century has brought significant improvement in care for people with VWD, there is still a large gap in therapies compared with other hematologic conditions. Future research efforts must continue to address these scientific hurdles to further refine and expand therapeutic options, ultimately striving for more personalized and effective care for all individuals living with VWD.
