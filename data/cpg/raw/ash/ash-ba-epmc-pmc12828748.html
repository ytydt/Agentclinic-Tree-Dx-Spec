Myeloproliferative neoplasm subtypes have distinct impact on risk of incident osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures

Visual Abstract

TO THE EDITOR:

Osteoporosis (OP) is a preventable disease defined by progressive loss of bone mass and mineral density that leads to an increased risk of fractures and premature mortality.1 In women aged ≥55 years, osteoporotic fractures (OFs) account for more hospitalizations than diabetes, myocardial infarction, and breast cancer combined.1,2 Guideline-concordant radiographic screening is a cost-effective method to facilitate early detection and treatment of OP to prevent associated morbidities.3 Subsequently, further identifying specific populations at a high risk of OP development and OFs is critical.

Increasing evidence suggests an association between abnormal bone homeostasis and myeloid disorders.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) such as polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), myelofibrosis (MF), and MPN-unclassifiable (MPN-U) are clonal hematopoietic malignancies characterized by somatic mutations in Janus kinase 2 (JAK2), calreticulin (CALR), and myeloproliferative leukemia virus oncogene (MPL). Dysregulated JAK-STAT signaling along with overproduction of inflammatory cytokines in MPNs may contribute to the pathogenesis of bony disease.12 Hence, we examined the association between MPNs and OP and OFs in a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)–based case-control study.

We analyzed 2022 SEER-Medicare linked data that represent 45.9% of all patients with cancer aged ≥65 years in the United States.13 We identified ET, PV, MF, and MPN-U cases between 1 January 2006 and 31 December 2019 with the help of 3rd edition of International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O) codes. Exclusion criteria included lack of continuous Medicare coverage, Health Maintenance Organization (HMO) enrollment during months −13 to −1 prior to MPN diagnosis or index date, concurrent active hematologic and/or solid malignancies ± 6 months of index date, and MPN diagnosis at time of death or autopsy.

A representative Medicare control population, comprising those who did not have cancer, matched in a 3:1 ratio with MPN cases by birth year, sex, and registry location was derived from a random sample of 5% of Medicare recipients as specified in the SEER-Medicare–linked database. The control population was given the MPN diagnosis date of their matched case as a pseudoindex date, and matching was restricted to those who survived ≥6 months after the assigned pseudoindex date. Those with HMO coverage or lack of continuous Medicare coverage during months −13 to −1 relative to the pseudoindex date were excluded. In both cohorts, adults with a diagnosis of OP or OF ± 6 months of the index/pseudoindex dates, and those who died within 6 months after their index/pseudoindex date were excluded. The primary end point was the development of OP or occurrence of OFs in 6 months after MPN diagnosis and 6 months before death. Additional details on cohort selection and statistical analyses are available in supplemental Methods.

We identified 10 955 patients with MPNs and 13 560 matched control individuals (Table 1); majority (63%) were male with a median age of 75 years (interquartile range [IQR], 70-81). The most common MPN diagnosis was ET (42%) then PV (41%), MF (14%), and MPN-U (3.3%). Compared to the control group, a large proportion of MPN patients were aged <75 years (53% vs 50%; P < .001), White (87% vs 83%; P < .001), non-Hispanic (99% vs 97%; P < .001), of higher socioeconomic status (SES) by quintiles (Q) living below federal poverty line (48% in Q1-Q2 vs 40%; P < .001), and had higher comorbidity burden by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (score ≥3 in 21% vs 15%; P < .001).

At a median follow-up of 89 months, the overall survival (OS) was 44.5% (95% confidence interval [CI], 43.3-45.6) in the MPN cases and 67.5% (95% CI, 66.6-68.5) in the controls. In the entire study cohort, 2188 individuals (9.1%) were diagnosed with OP and 304 (1.3%) experienced OF (supplemental Results; supplemental Table 1). The 5-year cumulative incidence of OP/OFs was significantly higher among MPN patients vs controls (9.2% vs 7.0%; P < .001; supplemental Results; supplemental Figure 2). By MPN subtypes, the 5-year cumulative incidence of OP/OFs (Figure 1) was the highest for MPN-U (12%) followed by ET (10%), PV (8.7%), and MF (6.2%). Factors associated with OP/OFs for the entire cohort included female sex and Asian race. Per univariable analyses (supplemental Results; supplemental Table 2), MF was associated with a lower incidence of OP/OFs compared to matched controls (hazard ratio [HR], 0.79; 95% CI, 0.64-0.98), whereas PV (HR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.11-1.39), ET (HR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.30-1.60), and MPN-U (HR, 1.66; 95% CI, 1.26-2.19) were associated with an increased risk. After adjusting for all covariates, PV (adjusted HR [aHR], 1.17; 95% CI, 1.05-1.31) and ET (aHR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.05-1.30) maintained their independent association with an increased risk of OP/OFs whereas MF was associated with a decreased risk (aHR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.69-1.05).

The association among PV, ET, and MPN-U with an increased incident OP/OFs reinforces 2 population-based studies reporting increased femoral, hip, and vertebral fracture risk in patients with MPNs.14,15 We are the first to describe a decreased incidence of OP/OFs with MF. Previous studies have shown that MF is characterized by osteosclerosis rather than loss of bone trabeculae as seen in case of OP.5,16 However, the association between MF and incident non-OP fractures, and whether osteosclerosis leads to under-reporting of OP on a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scan is unknown. In our study, an increased incidence of fractures was not observed with MF. However, missing ICD codes for osteosclerosis and the small numbers of reported fractures (OF or otherwise) in patients with MF limit exploring the relationship between osteosclerosis and fractures using this data set. Additional studies are required to understand if patients with MF are at increased risk of fractures despite not meeting traditional OP diagnosis.

A potential mechanism for abnormal bone homeostasis in MPNs is dysregulation of inflammatory pathways and perturbation of the osteohematopoietic niche by clonal hematopoiesis (CH).17,18 In MPNs, the constitutive activation of JAK/STAT signaling leads to overproduction of inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin 1 (IL-1), and interleukin 6 (IL-6), which have been implicated in osteoclastogenesis and OP.12,19,20 Whether the anti-inflammatory effects of JAK inhibitors can reduce incident OP and osteosclerosis remains an important question.

Our study is limited by the variability in documentation that is not unique to the SEER and Medicare claims–based database.21 Moreover, SEER does not collect information on behavioral risk factors, and the sensitivity of ICD codes related to substance use or social determinants of health are low. Our analyses were restricted to patients aged ≥65 years. Furthermore, we did not include body mass index, cigarette smoking, or alcohol dependence as covariates despite their known association with OP/OFs, as these are not reliably included in SEER. The database lacked mutational data or information on treatment patterns. Finally, we may not have captured the data pertaining to patients with MPNs with lower SES using this database depending on where the patients are seeking MPNs care. However, the strength of our work includes the use of a large, population-based registry to investigate rare leukemias that impact <20 000 adults per year.21,22

Compared to the general healthy population, we observed a near 20% increased hazard of OP/OFs among patients with PV, ET, and MPN-U. Currently, the US Preventative Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommends a DEXA screening for women aged ≥65 years and for younger women at increased risk of fractures.3 However, studies of Medicare beneficiaries have reported screening adherence as low as 30%, especially among women aged ≥75 years who represent a large proportion of our cohort.23 Underuse of screening likely underestimates the true incidence of OP/OFs in MPNs; efforts to improve adherence to guideline-concordant practices may be cost- and life-saving for our older patients with MPNs. Finally, prospective studies are needed to evaluate the effect of early therapeutic intervention with JAK inhibitors or interferons on both MPN symptoms and risk of OP/OFs.
