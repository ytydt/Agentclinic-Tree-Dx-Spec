Key Points

  • •

    Bemiltenase alfa is well tolerated at IV doses of 0.10 U/kg in HAwI and HBwI.

  • •

    Bemiltenase alfa has potential to be used as a hemostatic treatment for bleeding episodes in patients with HAwI and HBwI.

Visual Abstract

Abstract

Bemiltenase alfa, a factor X (FX) activator derived from Daboia russelii siamensis venom, was developed as a hemostatic agent for hemophilia A and B with inhibitors (HAwI and HBwI). We conducted 2 consecutives multicenter, open-label clinical trials. The phase 1b/2a study assessed the safety, hemostatic efficacy, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic characteristics of bemiltenase alfa at a dose of 0.1 U/kg. The phase 2b study further evaluated the safety and efficacy at a dose of 0.1 U/kg. Primary efficacy end points encompassed the effective hemostasis rate, safety assessment, and antidrug antibody (ADA) development. There were 6 participants enrolled in the phase 1b study, 20 in phase 2a, and 25 in phase 2b. Patients received bemiltenase alfa for bleeding episodes in the phase 2a and 2b studies. In phase 2a, the effective hemostasis rate was 94.1% (95% confidence interval [CI], 88.7-97.4). Phase 2b showed a rate of 81.9% (95% CI, 71.0-92.9). Most adverse events were mild with grade 1 severity, with no serious events. ADA was detected in 5 patients, but no impact on efficacy and safety was found. Pharmacokinetic findings showed repeated doses of bemiltenase alfa resulted in progressive drug concentration, as indicated by the accumulation ratio index. Pharmacodynamic results indicated a reduction in activated partial thromboplastin time and an increase in thrombin generation peak. Furthermore, a mild decline in FX activity was observed postadministration. The study demonstrates FX activation as a novel hemostatic strategy, with snake venom–derived bemiltenase alfa showing promising safety and efficacy for treating bleeding episodes in patients with HAwI and HBwI. These trials were registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as #NCT05027230 and #NCT06289166.

Introduction

Hemophilia A (HA) and B (HB) are rare X chromosome–linked bleeding disorders caused by deficiencies or dysfunctions of coagulation factors VIII (FVIII) and IX (FIX), respectively. Exogenous clotting factor concentrates have long been the cornerstone of treatment. A major challenge is the development of neutralizing antibodies, which can render these therapies ineffective.1 Notably, the incidence of inhibitors can reach 30% in patients with severe HA and 5% in those with severe HB.

Although immune tolerance induction can eliminate inhibitors in some patients, ∼30% of patients with HA and 70% of those with HB show limited responsiveness to this approach.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Nonfactor therapies, including emicizumab for HA, and rebalancing agents, such as fitusiran, concizumab, and marstacimab for HA and HB, have demonstrated good efficacy in prophylaxis for patients7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13; however, none of these agents can be used to treat active bleeding.1,8,9

For patients with HA or HB with inhibitors (HAwI or HBwI), acute bleeding is typically treated with bypassing agents, such as activated recombinant FVII (rFVIIa) and activated prothrombin complex concentrate (APCC). However, the effectiveness of these agents varies significantly across studies.14 APCC may trigger an anamnestic response in both patients with HAwI and HBwI. Furthermore, FIX in APCC can lead to allergic reactions or nephrotic syndrome in patients with HBwI, posing significant challenges for these patients.1 A real-world analysis conducted in China, where APCC is unavailable, revealed that only 29% of individuals with HAwI received rFVIIa due to its prohibitive expense.15 This highlights the urgent need for the development of novel, effective and more accessible therapies for managing bleeding episodes in these patients.

FX, a central component in the common pathway of the coagulation cascade, plays a critical role in thrombin generation (TG).16 The activation of FX through either the intrinsic or extrinsic pathway initiates a rapid and robust thrombin burst, positioning FX as an attractive target for patients with hemophilia, regardless of inhibitor status.

Snake venom, particularly from Daboia russelii siamensis (Russell’s viper), is widely recognized for its potent coagulation-enhancing properties, particularly by activating FX.17 Currently, Russell’s viper venom (RVV) is widely employed as a laboratory reagent for antiphospholipid syndrome screening. Despite its established role in coagulation assays, the therapeutic potential of RVV in hemophilia models and clinical trials has only been recently explored.18, 19, 20

Bemiltenase alfa, an FX-activating enzyme purified from the venom of Daboia russelii siamensis, represents a novel therapeutic approach. Our preclinical studies demonstrated its capacity to selectively activate FX independent of FVIII or FIX, without significantly affecting FVII, FII, plasminogen, or anticoagulant proteins antithrombin and protein C.19 Bemiltenase alfa has been shown to enhance TG and peak thrombin levels in FVIII-deficient human plasma, as well as shorten activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and improve survival in a mouse model following tail docking injury.19 Our phase 1a clinical trial in adult patients with HAwI and HBwI established the safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) of single ascending doses of bemiltenase alfa.19 After our study, RVV-derived FX activation was commented to be a promising strategy in the hemostatic treatment of patients with hemophilia with inhibitors.18,20 Therefore, FX activators purified from snake venom have opened a new therapeutic avenue for managing bleeding in patients with hemophilia. In June 2024, bemiltenase alfa received orphan drug designation from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of HA and HB.21

Here, we report the findings from a sequential phase 1b/2 trial designed to further validate the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of bemiltenase alfa in treating bleeding episodes among adult patients with HAwI or HBwI. This study marks a significant step forward in developing innovative therapies for this challenging patient population.

Methods

DP

Bemiltenase alfa is a heterotrimeric protease purified from the venom of Daboia russelii siamensis. Manufacturing involved the following steps: (1) venom collection and lyophilization, during which the snake venom was first collected using the bite plate method, subpackaged, and directly lyophilized to obtain the lyophilized snake venom (LSV); (2) preparation and purification of the drug substance (DS), during which the LSV was dissolved, centrifuged, and subjected to viral inactivation. The material was then isolated and purified through 4 chromatographic steps that relied on different purification mechanisms, including ionic exchange chromatography, mixed-mode chromatography, and gel filtration. Finally, the DS was obtained after viral filtration and sterile filtration; (3) formulation of the DP, during which the DS was formulated with excipients, subjected to sterile filtration, filled into vials, lyophilized, and capped to produce the final DP, specified as 5 U (0.15 μg) per vial.

The process validation for the LSV, DS, and DP was performed across 3 consecutive batches in compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice standards. Validation data confirmed robust process control and consistent interbatch quality. The purity of all 3 batches of the DS exceeded 99%.

The viral inactivation condition was defined as follows: final inactivator concentration of 1% (weight-to-weight ratio) polysorbate 80 and 0.3% (weight-to-weight ratio) tri-n-butyl phosphate were maintained at 25°C to 26°C for a duration of 6.0 to 6.5 hours. In addition to this viral inactivation step, bemiltenase alfa manufacturing process incorporates 2 validated chromatography steps and 1 viral clearance nanofiltration step. Comprehensive process validation was performed for all 4 virus clearance steps, and the results demonstrated full compliance with the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use Q5A requirements.

Study design and participants

Both the phase 1b/2a and phase 2b studies were prospective, multicenter, open-label, multiple-dose clinical trials, aiming at evaluating bemiltenase alfa as hemostatic treatment for patients with HAwI or HBwI.

Eligible participants for all the studies included adult male patients with hemophilia with confirmed FVIII or FIX inhibitors, as determined by the Bethesda assay or the Nijmegen-modified Bethesda assay. Key exclusion criteria included the presence of coagulation disorders other than HAwI or HBwI or a history of thromboembolic diseases (supplementary criteria).

Procedures

In the phase 1b/2a study, we aimed to evaluate the PK and PD characteristics and the hemostatic efficacy of administered bemiltenase alfa. In the phase 1a study, eligible subjects received up to 6 consecutive IV injections of bemiltenase alfa at a dose of 0.10 U/kg, administered at 4-hour intervals with a maximum of 6 doses for PK and PD assessments. In the phase 2a study, bemiltenase alfa was administered at 4-hour intervals to treat a bleeding episode at a dose of 0.10 U/kg or 0.16 U/kg, with a maximum of 6 doses until hemostasis was achieved. However, in this report, we only present the phase 2a data for bemiltenase alfa at a dose of 0.1 U/kg. Data for 0.16 U/kg will be reported separately.

The phase 2b study further explored the efficacy at a dose of 0.10 U/kg. Bemiltenase alfa was also administered at 4-hour intervals to treat a bleeding episode, with a maximum of 6 doses until hemostasis was achieved.

Rescue therapy for uncontrolled bleeding during bemiltenase alfa treatment involves dose escalation (eg, increasing from 0.10 U/kg to 0.16 U/kg) or adjunctive hemostatic agents such as PCC or rFVIIa.

The reason why the dosage of 0.10 U/kg was chosen was that phase 1a study results demonstrated that both the 0.08 U/kg and 0.16 U/kg doses effectively improved PD parameters in HAwI/ HBwI, including TG and APTT.19 Hence, a 0.08 to 0.16 U/kg dose range was considered suitable for evaluating the efficacy and safety of on-demand bleeding treatment in phase 2 clinical trials. For the convenience of dosing calculation in clinical practice, 0.10 U/kg was selected in phase 1b and phase 2 studies.

Outcomes

In the phase 1b/2a study, the primary end points were hemostatic efficacy and safety of bemiltenase alfa and the secondary end points were the PK and PD characteristics of multiple doses of bemiltenase alfa at 0.10 U/kg.

Efficacy was assessed at each bleeding episode. Participants may have >1 bleeding site at each bleeding episode, and hemostatic response was calculated per bleeding site. Hemostatic responses were classified as excellent, good, moderate, or ineffective based on the efficacy evaluation chart, with the first 3 categories defined as effective hemostasis (supplemental Table 1).22 Clinical resolution at 8 hours following the initial dose and at 24 hours after the final dose served as the principal benchmarks for efficacy. Other primary end points for efficacy included the total dosage required to achieve effective hemostasis and the proportion of cases achieving complete or significant resolution of bleeding symptoms or signs at 4, 8, and 24 hours after first administration. The primary end points for safety encompassed adverse events (AEs), including venous and arterial thromboembolism events, abnormal laboratory results, and abnormalities in vital signs. The presence of antidrug antibody (ADA) was also tested (supplemental Methods). There were no allergic events.

In the phase 2b study, the primary end points were satisfactory and sustained therapeutic response and the safety of bemiltenase alfa administered at a dose of 0.10 U/kg. Efficacy was assessed at each bleeding episode. However, in contrast to the phase 2a study, effective hemostasis for a bleeding episode in the phase 2b study was defined as achieving satisfactory and sustained hemostasis in all bleeding sites. If a participant experienced bleeding at >1 site during a single bleeding episode, the bleeding episode was deemed successful hemostasis only if all bleeding sites achieved effective hemostasis. If hemostasis was successful at 1 site but failed at another, the entire episode was classified as unsuccessful. At a given time point (8 and 12 hours), satisfactory and sustained therapeutic efficacy was determined to be an excellent or good bleeding symptom resolution without requiring additional treatment for up to 72 hours after the final dose (supplemental Table 2). The key primary efficacy end point was the effective hemostasis at 12 hours following the first dose in all episodes. The rationale for setting primary efficacy end points at 12 and 72 hours in efficacy assessments was based on 2 key factors. First, results from the phase 2a trial results demonstrated that most hemorrhages resolved within this time frame following 3 doses. Second, this end point selection aligns with requirements issued by the Chinese Center for Drug Evaluation after the phase 2a trial, which referenced guidelines from the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis.22 Other primary end points for efficacy included: (1) the total dosage of bemiltenase alfa required to achieve 12-hour (from the first dose) effective hemostasis, (2) effective hemostasis within 8 hours from the first dose, (3) effective hemostasis of target joint bleeds (≥3 bleedings within a 6-month period) within 12 hours of the first dose, (4) time from bleeding onset and from the first dose to excellent or good clinical symptom resolution, and (5) the frequency of rescue therapy utilization throughout the observation period. The primary end points for safety included AEs, and ADAs, similar to those of the phase 1b/2a study.

Statistical analysis

The planned enrollment for the phase 1b/2a study was ∼26 eligible participants, determined based on feasibility and clinical considerations. Among them, 6 participants were designated for PK and PD data collection in phase 1b, whereas 10 to 20 participants were allocated for safety and efficacy assessments in phase 2a. Efficacy analyses included the calculation of effective hemostasis rates with 2-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using either the normal approximation method or the Clopper-Pearson method.

The phase 2b study aimed to enroll 25 participants, targeting the collection of at least 100 bleeding episodes for efficacy evaluation. Assuming that the average number of bleeding episodes per patient during the 3-month follow-up period is 4, the correlation coefficient between bleeding visits is 0.1. The effective hemostasis rate is estimated to be 73%, compared with the objective performance criterion of 55%. The objective performance criterion was derived from the success rates used to support eptacog alfa registration or reported in the inhibitor literature between 1998 and 2013.23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 The 1-sided, single-sample normal approximation method (α = 0.025, 1 − β ≥ 80%) is used to test the null hypothesis that P ≤.55 vs the alternative hypothesis that P >.55. The effective hemostasis rate at 12 hours after the first dose was assessed, with its 2-sided 95% CI calculated using the normal approximation method. -The standard error accounted for a given patient correlation across bleeding episodes, ensuring appropriate adjustment for repeated measurements in the statistical analysis. Time to clinical symptom resolution was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with 2-sided 95% CIs calculated using the Brookmeyer-Crowley method and log-log transformation.

The primary end points for efficacy analyses were conducted on the full analysis set population of treated patients in the phase 2a and 2b study.

For demographic and baseline analyses of the phase 1b/2a and the phase 2b study, categorical variables were summarized as proportions, whereas continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges. All analyses were based on the intention-to-treat principle. Analyses were performed using SAS software 9.4 (SAS Inc, Cary, NC).

The trials were conducted in 9 centers in China and approved by the ethics committees at each participating center (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT05027230 and NCT06289166). Written informed consents were obtained from all participants.

Results

Efficacy, safety, and PK/PD results in the phase 1b/2a study

Between October 2021 and July 2023, 32 patients were screened for eligibility in the phase 1b/2a study, with 26 ultimately enrolled. In the phase 1 study, 6 patients (5 with HAwI and 1 with HBwI) were included, with a median age of 42 years (range, 25-49) (Table 1; Figure 1). In the phase 2a study, 20 participants (17 with HAwI and 3 with HBwI) were enrolled, with a median age of 35 years (range, 19-58). All participants received bemiltenase alfa for bleeding events.

In the phase 2a study, participants experienced 135 bleeds, predominantly in the joints (51.9%) and muscles (29.6%). The overall hemostatic efficacy was 94.1% (95% CI, 88.7-97.4), with joint and muscle efficacies of 92.9% and 95.0%, respectively (Table 2). Among all treated episodes, 90.4% were rated as having excellent or good outcomes. The rates of complete or significant clinical symptom resolution at 4, 8, and 24 hours after the first dose of bemiltenase alfa were 77.8%, 88.9%, and 97.0%, respectively. The mean number of administrations required for effective hemostasis of 1 bleeding site was 2.1 ± 1.1 doses, with a corresponding mean dosage of 0.21 ± 0.11 U/kg. Most bleeds (90.6%) achieved effective hemostasis within 3 doses and 70.9% responded within 1 to 2 doses (supplemental Table 3). Five bleeds (3.7%) received rescue therapy. Four bleeds resolved with rFVIIa. One bleed unresponsive after 4 doses of 0.10 U/kg resolved after 2 doses of 0.16 U/kg.

No cases of disseminated intravascular coagulation, thrombosis event, serious AEs related to bemiltenase alfa or AEs resulting in dose reductions, drug discontinuation, or withdrawal were reported in the phase 1/2a study. In the phase 1b study, 6 participants experienced a total of 15 AEs (Table 3; supplemental Table 4). Of these, 5 AEs in 3 participants (50.0%) were classified as drug-related. These included 4 grade 1 events and 1 grade 2 event. The most commonly reported AEs included mild increased D-dimer level (2 cases) (with a mean ± SD elevation of 0.81 ± 0.38 mg/L from the baseline level after the sixth dose; supplemental Table 5), elevated fibrinogen degradation product (FDP) level (2 cases), and reduced blood fibrinogen level (1 case). Importantly, no thromboembolic events were observed. In the phase 2a study, 18 of 20 participants reported 56 AEs (Table 3; supplemental Table 6). Of these, 42 events in 15 participants were classified as treatment-emergent AEs and 18 (42.9%) in 8 participants were classified as related to investigational drug. All drug-related AEs were grade 1. The most frequent drug-related events were elevated D-dimer (44.4%) and FDP (33.3%) levels. Immunogenicity testing in the phase 1b/2a study did not detect any positive ADAs (supplemental Table 7).

TEAEs, treatment-emergent adverse events.

The PK/PD study showed repeated doses of bemiltenase alfa resulted in progressive drug concentration, as indicated by the accumulation ratio index (Rac) (Figure 2A; supplemental Table 8). Multiple administrations led to a notable reduction in APTT and an increase in TG peak. Specifically, 10 minutes after the first dose, APTT decreased by an average of 6.4 ± 2.2 seconds, reaching a minimum of 27.0 ± 10.7 seconds after 6 doses (Figure 2B; supplemental Tables 9 and 10). Concurrently, the TG peak height increased significantly, rising by 27.1nM ± 3.6nM within 10 minutes of the first dose and peaking at an increase of 58.4nM ± 18.7nM after the sixth dose compared with that at baseline (Figure 2C; supplemental Tables 11 and 12). The endogenous thrombin-generating potential (ETP) also increased progressively, peaking at 865.7nM·minute ± 145.1nM·min after the sixth dose (Figure 2D; supplemental Table 13). In addition, FX activity (FX:C) showed a slight decline of 9.2% ± 2.6% at 8 hours after the final dose (supplemental Table 14).

Efficacy and safety in phase 2b study

From March 2024 to October 2024, 36 patients were screened and 25 patients with severe hemophilia (21 with HAwI and 4 with HBwI) were enrolled in the phase 2b study, with a median age of 41 years (range, 18-60) (Table 1; Figure 1). All participants received bemiltenase alfa for bleeding events.

In the phase 2b study, 144 bleeding episodes resulted in 158 documented bleeds. The 12-hour effective hemostasis rate of all bleeding episodes was 81.9% (95% CI, 71.0-92.9) (Table 2), with 77.1% of bleeding episodes achieving hemostasis with just 1 or 2 doses of bemiltenase alfa. The mean dosage required was 0.19 ± 0.07 U/kg (supplemental Table 15). The 8-hour effective hemostasis rate was 61.8% (95% CI, 46.6-77.0) (supplemental Table 15). Joint bleeding accounted for most cases (64.6%), with 82.4% (84/102; 95% CI, 72.2-92.5) achieving effective hemostasis within 12 hours. Among the 23 target joint hemorrhages, 20 bleeds (87.0%; 95% CI, 66.4-97.2) achieved 12-hour effective hemostasis. Across all bleeding episodes, the median time to excellent or good clinical symptom resolution was 5.47 hours from the first dose and 9.12 hours from the onset of bleeding. Rescue therapy with rFVIIa was required in only 2 bleeding episodes (1.4%).

No cases of disseminated intravascular coagulation, serious AEs related to bemiltenase alfa, or AEs leading to dose reductions, drug discontinuation, or withdrawal were reported in the phase 2b study. Only 1 patient experienced grade 1 asymptomatic intramuscular venous thrombosis, which was identified by regular ultrasound scan after bemiltenase alfa administration, lasted for 82 days, and resolved spontaneously without intervention. In total, 16 participants experienced 34 AEs, with 11 (32.4%) AEs classified as drug-related (Table 3; supplemental Table 16). The most common drug-related AE was elevated D-dimer level (63.6%) and all were grade 1, except for a single case of grade 2 liver dysfunction. The mean ± SD elevation of D-dimer level from the baseline level after the fourth dose was 0.09 ± 0.23 mg/L.

In the phase 2b study, 5 patients in total tested positive for ADA. Among them, 1 patient tested positive for ADA before treatment but became ADA-negative 1 month after the first administration, whereas the remaining 4 developed ADAs after treatment. By the final follow-up, 3 reverted to ADA-negative, whereas the one with neutralizing antibody remained ADA-positive after 3 months (supplemental Table 7). For this participant, 1 month after bemiltenase alfa infusion, the titer of his binding antibody was 30.21 and neutralizing antibody was 3.57. Three months later, the binding antibody remained positive but the titer decreased to <15, whereas the neutralizing antibody titer became negative. Notably, the presence of ADAs had no significant impact on safety or hemostasis efficacy (supplemental Table 17).

Discussion

These trials provide detailed evaluation of bemiltenase alfa, an FX-activating enzyme derived from snake venom, as an effective hemostatic treatment for patients with HAwI and HBwI.

In the phase 2 studies, bemiltenase alfa demonstrated high hemostatic efficacy rates (94.1% in phase 2a and 81.9% in phase 2b). These efficacy rates align with those reported for other established treatments such as rFVIIa and APCC, which show overall efficacy rates of 80% to 90%.28,29

Most bleeding (70.9%) achieved effective hemostasis with 1 to 2 doses in phase 2a, and this number reached 77.1% in phase 2b. This suggests that bemiltenase alfa facilitates a fast thrombin burst, offering potential advantages in hemostatic treatment of patients with HAwI/HBwI, especially for severe or life-threatening bleeding events that require rapid hemostasis. Our previous studies have indicated that coagulation parameters improve with increasing dose levels and administration frequency.19 Although higher doses may lead to more rapid and effective hemostasis, the efficacy and safety of such regimens are under ongoing investigation (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03384277).

The PK study demonstrated that the half-life of bemiltenase alfa was ∼8 hours. The dosing interval that we used was 4 hours, based on our previous clinical study of TG assay (TGA), which showed that TG potential peaked at 4 hours and subsequently exhibited a declining trend.19 We will investigate further to confirm the efficacy of an 8-hour dosing interval in subsequent studies. PD analysis of the 0.10 U/kg cohort demonstrated significant improvements in coagulation parameters, including APTT, peak, and ETP in the TGA. These enhancements suggest that bemiltenase alfa robustly activates the coagulation cascade, driving TG and promoting effective blood clot formation, critical processes for achieving hemostasis. This provides a scientific basis for the hemostatic efficacy observed in our patients.

The phase 1b/2 trial findings demonstrated that bemiltenase alfa was well tolerated. The most common AEs involved transient changes in coagulation parameters, such as increased D-dimer, elevated FDP, and decreased blood fibrinogen levels. These events were mild and self-resolving and did not compromise the overall safety profile of bemiltenase alfa. However, albeit moderately elevated, the levels of D-dimer and FDP suggest a degree of coagulopathic consumption. This finding warrants caution in clinical practice, particularly regarding thrombotic risks in patients with high-risk factors for thrombosis. It is recommended to monitor patients for any signs or symptoms of thrombosis during treatment. For high-risk individuals, relevant biomarkers such as D-dimer, FDP, and thrombin-antithrombin complex should be closely monitored. In our ongoing phase 3 clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06922045), we are maintaining rigorous surveillance of these parameters to ensure patient safety. Four patients developed ADA after the administration of bemiltenase alfa. Among them, 3 ADAs were negative at the 3-month follow-up. One patient remained persistently positive; however, his binding antibody titer improved over time and the neutralizing antibodies turned negative. In our study, no significant differences in efficacy were observed between ADA-positive and ADA-negative participants. However, additional data are required to confirm this finding.

The percentage of patients requiring rescue therapy was relatively low (3.7% in phase 2a and 1.4% in phase 2b). However, thrombotic risk should be carefully monitored when higher doses of bemiltenase alfa are used, particularly among patients with predisposing factors such as diabetes, hypertension, or obesity. In addition, in cases of breakthrough bleeding during treatment with other agents (eg, emicizumab or rebalancing therapies) for which combination with bemiltenase alfa is considered, the close evaluation of thrombosis-related safety parameters is warranted. Further real-world evidence and data from in vitro assays, including TGA, will be valuable for optimizing the dosing strategy of bemiltenase alfa, including drug dosage and administration intervals.

Through a high-yield process, bemiltenase alfa achieves an intermediate concentration exceeding 6 mg/mL, yet the final DP contains only 0.15 μg per vial (5 U). This efficiency enables the production of millions of therapeutic doses from minimal venom volumes. To standardize venom quality and ensure supply chain stability, the snakes are bred in a controlled, proprietary facility. Collectively, these optimizations translate to significant manufacturing cost advantages.

This study has some limitations. The relatively small patient sample size and short follow-up period restrict the generalizability of the findings. Future investigations with larger cohorts and extended observation periods are needed to further verify the hemostatic efficacy and long-term safety of bemiltenase alfa.

Our study indicates that bemiltenase alfa has high hemostatic efficacy and a favorable safety profile. These findings highlight bemiltenase alfa as a promising new therapeutic option for the treatment of acute bleeding episodes in patients with HAwI/HBwI.
