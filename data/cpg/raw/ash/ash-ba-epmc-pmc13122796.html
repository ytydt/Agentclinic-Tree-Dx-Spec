Hypertension and CLL: a common and hazardous relationship

Stephen P Mulligan

In this issue of Blood Advances, Vainer et al1 evaluate the impact of hypertension in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). The authors evaluated the national Danish CLL and lymphoid cancer registries between 2008 and 2022. In the study period, they identified 6557 patients with CLL in total, of whom 3035 (46%) had coexistent hypertension. Each patient was matched with up to 5 individuals from the normal population in whom the incidence of hypertension without CLL was highly comparable at 45%. Patients with CLL when compared with those without hypertension were older and had higher prevalence of comorbidities, particularly cardiac disease and diabetes.

Vainer et al document an adverse survival impact of hypertension in patients with CLL; both from time of diagnosis and from time of first-line therapy.1 Median overall survival (OS) for patients with CLL was statistically lower at 8.6 years with hypertension vs 13.8 years without hypertension (hazard ratio [HR], 1.10), regardless of comorbidity score. By comparison with the matched population, median OS was 11.4 years with hypertension and not reached for the general population without hypertension, a significant detrimental effect for those with CLL and hypertension compared with the general population with hypertension (see figure).

The study time frame of 2008 to 2022 bridged the immunochemotherapy to targeted therapy eras during which 1966 of the total cohort received frontline therapy, 45% with existing hypertension. Chlorambucil was the most often used (n = 516) and used more frequently in those with hypertension (34% vs 20%), followed by bendamustine (n = 459) and fludarabine (n = 379). Targeted therapy was used in 13% overall with Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKis) in 112, venetoclax-based therapy in 113, or the combination in 32. In patients with CLL after their first-line therapy overall, those with hypertension had 5-year survival of 55% compared with 71% without (HR, 1.48). Hypertension was associated with shorter OS in patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy (HR range, 1.25-1.48), predating the BTKis. For those treated with BTKis, 5-year survival was 60% vs 84% with and without hypertension, respectively.

In both settings (from diagnosis and after frontline therapy), cause-specific mortality was due to infections and cardiac and cerebrovascular events.1 The rate of infection mortality with CLL was 11% vs 6% and for the general population 6% vs 3% with and without hypertension, respectively. Hypertension increased the risk of death by cardiac and cerebrovascular causes (HR, 1.83), but with a similar association in the general population.

The impact of infectious deaths was mainly driven by patients younger than 75 years and lost its significance for those older than 75 years suggesting that competing risks in the older group may obscure its effect. CLL-immune defects such as hypogammaglobulinemia and T-cell exhaustion will likely be playing an important role in this group. Although this study was not designed to identify the contributing factors involved in the increased risk of infection deaths, further evaluation of these would be useful and whether they can be modified by treatment of the hypertension or other risk factors. As noted by the authors, links between hypertension and infection have been noted previously. In any event, the clinical ramifications of hypertension in patients with CLL are far from benign.

Hypertension is a cause or contributor risk for atrial fibrillation (AF), ischemic heart disease, cardiac failure, stroke, renal impairment, atherosclerotic peripheral vascular disease, aortic aneurysm, eye disease, cognitive decline, and dementia.2, 3, 4 It is extraordinarily common, globally pervasive, and present in more than a billion adults worldwide, affecting some 45% of the total global population, ∼60% older than 60 years of age, and rising in incidence.5 The Global Burden of Disease 2023 study ranked hypertension as the highest risk for disability-adjusted life-year impairment.6 Notwithstanding its enormous prevalence, it is remarkably under recognized and inadequately managed despite a multitude of authoritative guidelines to assist management.2, 3, 4

CLL is the most common form of leukemia, at least in Western countries, and with hypertension the most common chronic, noncommunicable medical condition globally, it is hardly surprising that the 2 conditions often coexist. The issue of hypertension in CLL has rightly become extremely topical and highly pertinent for patient care in the course of recent years with the advent, and now near universal, adoption of targeted therapies, due to the fact that BTKi, as a class effect, causes or exacerbates hypertension in a significant proportion of patients.7,8 This highlights the necessity of addressing hypertension in our patients.

Hypertension and CLL frequently coexist, and in the targeted therapy era, we are now adding BTKi-induced iatrogenic hypertension, in addition to the equally well-known arrhythmic risk and cardiac toxicity profile of BTKis. To use tautology for emphasis, the very treatment we are now giving virtually every patient with CLL as either frontline or subsequent therapy has a high likelihood of causing more hypertension, in addition to the arrhythmogenic risk for AF, and other cardiac toxicities, both of which will be exacerbated by hypertension. This Danish study demonstrates that hypertension in CLL is hazardous and impairs survival.

In the Danish cohort, there was a trend toward poorer survival in patients with hypertension on BTKi despite relatively small numbers likely making the trend not currently significant. Although the ALPINE and other clinical trials8 have revealed that the impact of BTKi on hypertension is more marked with ibrutinib than with the more selective covalent BTKis such as zanubrutinib, nevertheless it appears clear that hypertension can occur with all BTK inhibition albeit with sometimes lower incidence and later onset, including the noncovalent BTKis, such as pirtobrutinib,9 and the emerging BTK degraders.10

Where does this lead us as hematologists and clinicians managing patients with CLL? Adequate management of hypertension with the aim of modifying its adverse outcomes will require multidisciplinary collaboration between hematologists, cardiologists, general practitioners, and pharmacists. Regular monitoring of blood pressure, with early recognition and intervention, and patient education regarding the risks and management of hypertension must become standard practice in CLL care, especially in the context of targeted therapies with BTKis.
