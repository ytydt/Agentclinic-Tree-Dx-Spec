Abstract

Approximately 80% of patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma achieve a complete response after chemoimmunotherapy as defined by positron emission tomography (PET) scan. However, not all patients who fail to achieve a metabolic complete response are destined to relapse. In a number of these patients, the PET scan is falsely positive (ie, 20%-40% in several large series), particularly when the PET scan has a Deauville score of 4. Reflexively intervening with salvage systemic therapy or radiotherapy in these patients is potentially harmful. It seems that very sensitive measurements of circulating tumor DNA might be able to differentiate between patients who are unlikely to relapse and those with residual lymphoma. It is important to remember that lymphoma is not the only condition that can make a PET scan abnormal and to consider an alternate diagnosis that should be managed differently.

Introduction

The disease that we now call diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) has gone by many names in the past, including reticulum cell sarcoma, diffuse histiocytic lymphoma, and diffuse large cell lymphoma.1, 2, 3 Currently, DLBCL is subdivided based on gene expression profiling (eg, germinal center vs activated B-cell type), immunohistochemistry applying the Hans algorithm (germinal center B-cell vs non–germinal center B-cell type) (new reference), specific genetic abnormalities (eg, combined BCL 2 and MYC gene rearrangements), and unique primary sites (eg, primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma).4, 5, 6 Recently, 2 new molecular classifications of DLBCL have been proposed.7 However, all subtypes are potentially curable.

It is axiomatic that to achieve a cure a patient must achieve a complete response (CR) (ie, no identifiable residual lymphoma). Sometimes this goal has been problematic with DLBCL because, after eradication of the lymphoma cells, residual fibrotic or inflammatory masses may persist, making it difficult to define a CR. However, the development of the positron emission tomography (PET) scan provided a way to tell which of these residual masses is most likely to contain active lymphoma. By combining a PET scan with a computed tomography scan and after the development of the Deauville score for semiquantitative assessment (Table 1),8 a standard for identifying patients in CR was adopted in the Lugano criteria.9 Patients with a score of 1, 2, or 3 are considered to be in CR, and patients with a score of 4 or 5 are considered not to be in CR and to reflect failure of treatment.

Since the report by DeVita et al10 in 1975 that showed this lymphoma to be potentially curable using chemotherapy even for patients with widespread disease, several advances in treatment have been made. These include incorporating the most active single agent, doxorubicin, into the standard treatment (ie, the CHOP [cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone] regimen)11; the addition of rituximab to standard chemotherapy regimens10,12; and, most recently, the use of polatuzumab vedotin in the Pola-R-CHP (CHOP plus rituximab [CHOP-R] plus polatuzumab vedotin) regimen.13 At present, most patients with DLBC are cured including, in most series, 80% to 85% of those who achieve a metabolic CR as defined by PET scan.

Outcome for patients not in a metabolic CR

However, not all patients in whom treatment fails to achieve a CR are doomed to progress. In fact, the number of patients from several series who did not achieve a CR on end-of-treatment PET scan and were monitored without further therapy is presented in Table 2. Most of the papers were describing the prognostic value of PET scans in determining treatment outcome. The authors reported progression-free survival defined as surviving (in 1 paper, deaths from causes other than lymphoma were censored), being free from progression, and not receiving second-line therapy. However, in the paper by Freeman et al,14 an uncertain number of patients received salvage therapy, and in the paper by Dabaja et al,15 the Deauville score was not used to determine a CR. We frequently estimated the progression-free survival from progression-free survival curves.

Broglio et al15 reported pooled data from 420 patients treated with CHOP plus obinutuzumab, CHOP-R, or CHOP-R plus lenalidomide22; 20% to 35% of these patients who did not achieve a metabolic CR (ie, Deauville score of 4 or 5) continued free of progression. Kostakoglu et al16 reported 131 patients treated with CHOP plus obinutuzumab or CHOP-R in a randomized trial,23 in whom a metabolic CR was not achieved. Approximately 40% of these patients continued free of progression at 5 years. Freeman et al17 reported 97 patients treated with CHOP-R in whom a metabolic CR was not reached.14 The 5-year freedom from progression for these patients was ∼30%. Dabaja et al15 reported 70 patients with localized and advanced-stage DLBCL treated with CHOP ± rituximab with a positive end-of-treatment PET scan (ie, the Deauville scale was not used in this study). The 5-year progression-free survival was ∼35%. Li19 reported 56 patients treated with CHOP-R whose treatment failed to achieve a metabolic CR. Approximately 48% of the patients continued free from progression. Interestingly, they reported the results using the Deauville score, and 65% of patients with a Deauville score of 4 continued without progression compared with 20% of patients with a Deauville score of 5. Lee et al20 reported 24 patients with stage 1 or 2 DLBCL treated with CHOP-R with or without radiotherapy who did not achieve a metabolic CR. There was no difference in outcome between patients who did and did not receive radiotherapy, and 67% continued without relapse. Micallef et al21 reported 8 patients treated with epratuzumab (anti-CD22), rituximab, and CHOP who were unable to achieve a metabolic CR; 50% of these patients did not relapse.

Management of patients not in a metabolic complete remission after primary therapy

It is clear that not all patients who fail to achieve a metabolic CR after potentially curative chemoimmunotherapy are destined to relapse. The continuing abnormalities on the PET scan might reflect a slowly resolving PET scan in sites of known involvement by lymphoma (ie, Deauville score of 4), new sites of PET avidity that might reflect another disease process (ie, interpreted as a Deauville score of 5), or residual or progressive lymphoma (ie, Deauville score of 4 or 5). New sites of uptake are relatively more likely to reflect an unrelated disease process because DLBCL generally progresses in previously involved sites of disease.

The response of patients with primary refractory DLBCL to subsequent therapy is an important factor in the treatment decision. If primary refractory patients with DLBCL cannot be cured, there would be no reason to do anything for asymptomatic patients but watch and wait, hoping that the patient is already cured, and to start palliative therapy if residual lymphoma becomes apparent. Progressive disease is usually associated with symptoms. The initial report of using autologous bone marrow transplantation for patients with primary refractory or relapsed DLBCL showed a significant cure rate for patients who had been in remission and had chemosensitive disease after relapse.24 Unfortunately, longer follow-up found that no patient who never achieved an initial CR was cured (Figure 1). More recent data suggest that some patients with DLBCL who fail to achieve an initial CR but remain chemotherapy sensitive can be cured with autologous bone marrow transplantation.25 Newer intensive salvage therapies, including chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy, can cure primarily refractory patients.26,27 Thus, the calculus for these patients has changed because cure, although remote, is possible. However, the toxicity of these salvage therapies requires that residual lymphoma be documented.

The options for approaching a patient with a Deauville score of 4 or 5 on the end-of-treatment PET scan are presented in Table 3. The obvious approach to rule in or out resistant DLBCL is to do a biopsy. Previous studies have found negative biopsies in a high proportion of areas of residual PET avidity.28 Unfortunately, several issues complicate taking this approach. The location of the areas of residual PET avidity can limit the use of an excisional biopsy. Today in oncology, most biopsies in similar settings are done by interventional radiology. These biopsies are often small and sometimes difficult to interpret. Particularly with small biopsies, only a biopsy with residual DLBCL or an alternate diagnosis is helpful. A negative biopsy might simply mean that the residual lymphoma was missed.

Given the sizable number of false-positive end-of-treatment PET scans, watch and wait is a reasonable approach. As noted previously, this would be a justifiable approach for otherwise asymptomatic patients if curative salvage therapy were not available. However, potentially curative treatment options do exist, and many patients and providers are not comfortable with this passive approach. There is no standard approach to using imaging for patient follow-up. In practice, we do joint decision making with the patient that often involves another PET scan after ∼2 months. We always monitor the patients closely and do imaging if new symptoms, physical findings, or an elevated lactate dehydrogenase level is found.

The intensive treatments with the strongest evidence for cure in a primary refractory lymphoma are usually administered in the hospital, have considerable morbidity, are expensive, and have a risk of mortality. In the absence of definitive proof of residual lymphoma, a death in a patient who was already cured would be a tragedy. Of course, oncologists routinely give adjuvant chemotherapy to high-risk patients with breast cancer, colon cancer, and others in an attempt to eliminate minimal residual disease because randomized trials have shown that the net effect is to cure more patients, despite the risk. Even for tumors in which adjuvant therapy is employed, many patients are treated to benefit very few. No such studies are available for DLBCL and a Deauville score of 4 or 5 on the end-of-treatment PET scan after 6 cycles of chemoimmunotherapy, but these patients are often offered second-line therapy.

Patients with stage 1 and 2 DLBCL, particularly with a large mass, frequently receive “consolidation” involved field radiotherapy after initial chemotherapy. The treatment approach in these cases often involves a reduced number of cycles of systemic therapy (eg, 4 cycles of CHOP-R). Lamy et al29 reported a randomized trial of adjuvant radiotherapy for patients with nonbulky limited-stage DLBCL who were in CR after 4 or 6 cycles of R-CHOP-14 (14-day cycle of CHOP-R). An intent-to-treat analysis found no improvement in 5-year event-free survival (89% vs 92%, P = not significant).

In advanced-stage DLBCL, the use of adjuvant radiotherapy has been more variable, but most often used for patients with bulky masses. A special subset of patients with advanced-stage DLBCL and what seems to be localized residual lymphoma seen on PET scan after a course of chemoimmunotherapy is frequently treated with involved field radiotherapy, although no randomized data support its use. Freeman et al14 reported 723 patients treated with CHOP-R for at least 6 cycles. Of those who completed treatment, 517 had a negative end-of-treatment PET scan and were not offered radiotherapy; 206 had a positive end-of-treatment PET scan. Nonprogressing patients with a positive end-of-treatment PET scan were offered radiotherapy when it was feasible. Freedom from progression at least 3 years after treatment was 83% in the PET-negative patients and 56% in all patients with a positive end-of-treatment PET scan. However, for patients who received adjuvant radiotherapy, the 3-year progression-free survival was 76%. Interestingly, patients with bulky disease, defined as a 10-centimeter mass, who achieved a negative end-of-treatment PET scan and did not receive radiotherapy had the same outcome as those without bulky disease. Given that this was not a randomized trial, it is not possible to be certain about the contribution of the radiotherapy to the good outcome of the PET-positive patients who received radiotherapy and other data question its value. Patients who could not or did not receive radiotherapy were offered salvage systemic therapy.

Dabaja et al18 reported 294 patients with both stage 1 to 2 and stage 3 to 4 DLBCL treated with CHOP or CHOP-R. Patients received adjuvant radiotherapy at the attending physician’s preference. The report analyzed the effect of positive interim PET scans on treatment outcome. The Deauville score was not used in interpreting the scans. The authors found that patients who did not receive radiotherapy had a poorer progression and overall survival with a positive interim PET scan, but receiving radiotherapy negated the effect of a positive interim scan. Patients with a positive end-of-treatment PET scan had a 5-year progression-free survival of ∼35% with only chemotherapy and ∼70% with adjuvant radiotherapy. Again, the retrospective nature of the study and the lack of consistency in the criteria for receiving radiotherapy make it difficult to measure the contribution of the radiotherapy to the outcome.

It would be best if we had a way to tell which of these patients are already cured. It seems that circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) might lead to this possibility. In a series of 70 patients with end-of-treatment undetectable ctDNA measured by phased variant enrichment and detection sequencing, only 1 patient relapsed.30,31 Furthermore, 18 of 20 relapses had positive end-of-treatment ctDNA. In a study comparing end-of-treatment PET scan with end-of-treatment ctDNA measured by phased variant enrichment and detection sequencing, the 2-year progression-free survival was 88% vs 61% for a negative vs positive PET scan.32 The 2-year progression-free survival was 97% vs 29% for undetectable vs detectable ctDNA. For patients with a positive PET scan, the 2-year progression-free survival was 93% vs 30% for patients with undetectable vs detectable ctDNA. Particularly for patients in whom a biopsy is not possible or was not helpful, this approach might be used to identify which patients with an end-of-treatment PET scan with a Deauville score of 4 or 5 should be observed without further therapy. Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines suggest repeating all tests that were initially positive as part of the end-of-treatment determination of CR.

Finally, a good internist always considers the differential diagnosis. In this case, that means considering what other conditions might cause an abnormal PET scan other than residual lymphoma or slowly resolving PET uptake in a patient with cured lymphoma. The final diagnoses in our patients with apparently refractory or relapsed lymphoma and an abnormal PET scan that we have accumulated over the last few decades are presented in Table 4. Although these represent an unusual explanation for a Deauville score 4 or 5 PET scan at the end of therapy, they do occur and salvage treatment for the DLBCL would be inappropriate for these patients.

Is there a better way to interpret the PET scan and reduce false positives?

The simplicity of understanding and applying the Deauville score has made it the standard approach to PET scan interpretation, and it has been incorporated into guidelines.12 An end-of-treatment PET scan with a Deauville score of 1 to 3 is highly predictive of prolonged progression-free survival. The reproducibility of the Deauville score among radiologists blindly reading the same scans has been less studied. However, a report from the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group on patients with Hodgkin lymphoma found that 3 radiologists interpreting interim scans agreed on the Deauville score 71% of the time.33 In addition, another study from Iowa and the Mayo Clinic found that indeterminate scans were reported 20% of the time.34 Presumably, this has been less of an issue since the widespread use of the Deauville criteria.

Other methods of interpreting PET scans have been proposed to be more clinically relevant. These include total metabolic tumor volume,35 the lesion-to-liver maximum standardized uptake value ratio,36 and combining the change in maximum standardized uptake value and the Deauville score.37 It is possible that the use of artificial intelligence could improve the interpretation of the PET scan and reduce false-positive scans.

Conclusion

A number of patients with DLBCL and an abnormal end-of-treatment PET scan (ie, particularly those with a Deauville score of 4) are already cured. In various studies, the long-term freedom from relapse ranges from 20% to 67%. Reflexively intervening with radiotherapy or additional chemotherapy in these patients is potentially harmful. Although these patients are often given salvage systemic therapy, we lack clinical trials showing that this approach increases the ultimate cure rate. Better evidence exists for the use of involved field radiotherapy for patients with apparently localized residual lymphoma, but that evidence is not ideal. It seems that using very sensitive measurements of ctDNA might enable us to identify patients who are unlikely to relapse and those who are at high risk of progression. It is important to remember that lymphoma is not the only condition that can make a PET scan abnormal and to consider alternate diagnoses that should be managed differently.
