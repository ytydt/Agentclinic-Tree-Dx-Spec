Abstract

OBJECTIVE:

Early antibiotics are considered critical for bacterial sepsis treatment, although the benefit of this early timing may differ by the presence of shock. Little evidence exists from low- or middle-income settings. In patients referred from community hospitals to a tertiary center, we tested whether pre-referral antibiotic administration is associated with 28-day survival in sepsis, and whether this association differs by the presence of shock.

DESIGN:

Secondary analysis of a prospective cohort study that enrolled patients from 2013 to 2017 with a primary diagnosis of infection made by an attending physician and at least three Surviving Sepsis Campaign criteria for sepsis.

SETTING:

Tertiary care hospital in northeastern Thailand.

PATIENTS:

A total of 2593 adults with sepsis defined by primary diagnosis of infection and modified Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score greater than or equal to 2 who were referred from community hospitals.

INTERVENTIONS:

Antibiotics administered at the referring community hospital.

MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:

The median age was 59 years (interquartile range 44–72), 2233 (86.1%) were transferred the same day as initial presentation, and 1897 (73.2%) received antibiotics prior to referral. Blood cultures grew bacteria in 313 (12.1%). Twenty-eight-day mortality was 18.9%. In the propensity score-matched cohort (n = 722 sepsis without shock, n = 244 septic shock), shock modified the association between pre-referral antibiotics and death (interaction p = 0.001). In patients with septic shock, pre-referral antibiotics were associated with lower hazard of death (hazard ratio [HR], 0.38; 95% CI, 0.19–0.75) but in patients without shock there was no association with hazard of death (HR, 1.36; 95% CI, 0.96–1.92).

CONCLUSIONS:

In rural Thailand, antibiotic administration prior to referral was associated with lower hazard of death in patients with septic shock. Our findings extend to a resource-limited setting evidence supporting the benefit of early antibiotic administration in septic patients with shock.

Keywords: community hospitals, critical care, low- and middle-income countries, precision medicine, propensity score

Sepsis is a major cause of death and disability globally (1). Early antibiotics are considered critical for bacterial sepsis treatment; delays are associated with increased mortality (2–6). Current guidelines advise the administration of antibiotics immediately, ideally within one hour, in patients for whom sepsis is highly suspected with/without shock or when sepsis is possible in the setting of shock (7). In cases with a low-to-moderate risk of sepsis without signs of shock, the administration of antibiotics is recommended within three hours if concern for infection persists after performing an assessment of infectious vs. noninfectious causes (7, 8). These recommendations reflect a growing body of evidence demonstrating benefit of early antibiotics in patients with septic shock and challenging the benefit of early antibiotics in patients with suspected sepsis without shock given the diagnostic uncertainty common in the initial phases of sepsis care (7–10). Indiscriminate use of antibiotics can favor resistance and the occurrence of adverse effects (6, 10–13). Therefore, identifying the population with sepsis for whom early antibiotics has a favorable risk-benefit profile is paramount.

Little evidence exists regarding antibiotic timing in sepsis from low- or middle-income countries (LMICs), where the burden of sepsis is highest (1, 14–17). In high-income country (HIC) settings, up to 40% of patients with sepsis have a noninfectious condition or nonbacterial infection (18, 19). This may be even higher in Southeast Asia where sepsis etiologies include nonbacterial causes such as dengue and malaria (17, 20, 21). Further, increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance in this region threatens to undermine health gains in sepsis care (22–25). Compounding the challenges of implementing timely sepsis treatment, access to sepsis diagnostics and therapeutics are often limited in community hospitals of LMICs, and time to transfer to a referral hospital can be high (26–28). In Thailand, district hospitals (hereafter referred to as “community hospitals”) are public hospitals at the district level with approximately 10–150 beds and are a critical part of the country’s public health system (29, 30). Severely ill patients commonly receive initial treatment at community hospitals before being referred to tertiary care centers; 88% of patients receiving sepsis care at a tertiary facility in NE Thailand were referred from a lower acuity hospital (31). Very little data exist on treatment practices at referring hospitals, a critical gap given their first-line role in recognizing and treating sepsis. Few studies have assessed the impact of antibiotics administered prior to referral to a higher acuity hospital on outcome in patients with bacterial sepsis, and none have been performed in an LMIC (17, 32, 33).

Therefore, in this study we sought to determine if pre-referral antibiotic administration is associated with mortality in sepsis patients, considering shock as an effect modifier. We performed propensity score (PS)-matched analyses in a large prospective cohort study of adults presenting with sepsis to a community hospital and transferred to a referral hospital in northeastern Thailand (20).

METHODS

Study Population

The parent Ubon-sepsis cohort has been described previously (20, 31). In brief, subjects 18 years old or older who were admitted with suspected infection to Sunpasitthiprasong Hospital in Ubon Ratchathani, Thailand were sequentially and prospectively enrolled within 24 hours of admission between 2013 and 2017. Recruitment was performed by the study team by screening medical records of patients admitted to the emergency department, medical wards, and medical ICUs. Enrollment occurred if the patient had been admitted within the previous 24 hours with a primary diagnosis of infection made by the attending physician and had at least three Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2012 criteria for sepsis documented in the medical record (Table S1, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825) (34). Patients were excluded if they were diagnosed with hospital-acquired infections, had a previous hospitalization within the past 30 days, or were referred from other hospitals with a total duration of hospitalization greater than 72 hours. For this secondary analysis, we analyzed patients who were referred to the study hospital from a community hospital and who had sepsis, defined as infection plus the presence of organ dysfunction as per sepsis-3 guidelines (35). Organ dysfunction was determined by a modified Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score greater than or equal to 2 as described in the online supplement (20, 31, 35).

Study Measurements

Exposure.

The exposure in this study was administration of pre-referral antibiotics at the referring community hospital (directed acyclic graph provided in Fig. S1, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). The administration of antibiotics including type and dosage was recorded during the community hospital admission. Only the presentation date—and not time—was recorded, therefore we could not determine the precise interval between initial presentation and the first antibiotic dose. Because the pre-referral setting represents an early medical contact point for this population—and a key opportunity for interventions prior to arrival at the study hospital—antibiotic administration at this stage will be referred to as “early” throughout the article, although the precise timing relative to arrival at the community hospital is unknown.

Effect Modifier.

Shock was defined as receipt of IV vasopressors (norepinephrine, epinephrine, or dopamine) at any time at the community hospital prior to referral to the study hospital.

Potential Confounders.

The following variables that could confound the relationship between administration of antibiotics at the community hospital and subsequent mortality were considered: age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities (diabetes, heart disease, liver disease, kidney disease, lung disease, cancer, hypertension, dyslipidemia, stroke, HIV, tobacco use, heavy alcohol use, outpatient prednisone prescription), duration of symptoms, initial vital signs at the referring hospital, total WBC count, receipt of mechanical ventilation, and road distance between the referring hospital and the study hospital.

Outcome.

The outcome was death censored at 28 days.

Statistical Analysis

Propensity Score Matching.

To generate the PS, multivariable logistic regression models were made for the administration of pre-referral antibiotics in the septic shock and sepsis without shock groups, separately, which is recommended when effect modification is the question of interest (36–38). When calculating PSs, it is preferable to include variables associated with both the exposure and the outcome (confounder) and those only associated with the outcome (precision variable, Figure S1, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825) (39). Therefore, all 24 potential confounders described above were considered for inclusion in the PS calculation (Table S2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825), and multicollinearity was determined by a variance inflation factor (VIF) greater than five. With the PS derived from these variables, 1:1 greedy nearest neighbor caliper matching was performed without replacement and with exact matching on the presence of shock (40). A standardized mean difference (SMD) less than 10% was considered an adequate balance in the distribution between the groups (37, 41). For the evaluation of continuous variables, median and interquartile ranges were reported, which were compared by the Wilcoxon rank sum test; and categorical variables were described with proportions, compared by Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher exact test. To assess the generalizability of the matched cohort, the clinical characteristics of matched patients were compared with those of unmatched patients. Sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of results including varying the caliper width, alternative matching methods, and alternative propensity-based methods, which are described in the online supplement (40, 41).

Outcome Models.

In the primary analysis, an association of pre-referral antibiotic administration with hazard of death was determined using Cox regression adjusted for covariables that remained unbalanced (i.e., SMD ≥ 10%) after matching using a cluster-robust variance estimator and including an interaction term between pre-referral antibiotic administration and pre-referral shock (42, 43). A sensitivity analysis was performed in the full cohort without matching. There was no missingness for pre-referral antibiotic administration, shock, nor mortality censored at 28 days. Patients with any missingness in a variable used to generate the PS were excluded in the primary analysis. Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) was used in a sensitivity analysis (44, 45). Statistical analyses were performed with Stata, Version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and R, Version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Human Subjects.

The Ubon-sepsis study originally titled “Causes and Factors Associated with Outcomes in Community Acquired Sepsis and Severe Sepsis in Northeast Thailand” was approved on May 28, 2012 by the Mahidol University Faculty of Tropical Medicine Ethics Committee (MUTM 2012-024-01, MUTM 2018-046-01, and MUTM 2024-022-01), the Sunpasitthiprasong Hospital Ethics Committee (039/2556), the University of Washington Institutional Review Board (42988), and the University of Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee (OXTREC172–12). The procedures used in this study complied with the ethical principles outlined in the Belmont Report and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants or their representatives.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics Before Matching

Between March 1, 2013, and January 26, 2017, 4989 patients were enrolled in the parent cohort; 3328 patients had sepsis and were referred from a community hospital to the study hospital. Of these, 735 were missing data for at least one variable used to calculate the PS and were excluded (Table S2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). Therefore, 2593 patients were in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of the patients included in the analysis, 491 of 2593 (18.9%) died within 28 days of enrollment. The median age was 59.0 years (44.0–72.0), 1085 of 2593 (41.8%) were female. Median referral distance was 47.0 kilometers (39.0–90.0; Fig. 2), 2233 of 2593 (86.1%) arrived to the study hospital on the day of initial community hospital presentation, and 294 of 2593 (11.3%) arrived to the study hospital 1 day following presentation to the community hospital. The most common localized presenting syndrome was lung infection (623/2593 [24%]; Table S3, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). One thousand eight hundred ninety-seven of 2593 patients (73.2%) received antibiotics prior to referral, and the antibiotic most frequently administered was ceftriaxone (1602/1897 [84.4%]; Table S4, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). Blood cultures were obtained in all patients and positive for bacteria in 313 of 2593 (12.1%), predominantly with Gram-negative pathogens (n = 240 of 313 [76.7%]; Table S5, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). The SMD was larger than 10% in several variables in the cohort prior to matching (Tables 1 and 2; and Fig. S2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). In the sepsis without shock group, patients who received pre-referral antibiotics compared with those who did not receive pre-referral antibiotics were older, had more comorbidities including diabetes and chronic lung disease, higher total WBC count, and higher severity of illness indicated by higher modified SOFA score and higher heart rate (Table 1). Factors independently associated with pre-referral antibiotic use in multivariable logistic regression in this group included demographics (older age, male sex, underweight), comorbidities (diabetes, lung disease), more abnormal vital signs, and higher total WBC count (Table S6, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). In the septic shock group, patients who received pre-referral antibiotics compared with those who did not receive pre-referral antibiotics had higher body temperature and higher total WBC count (Table 2); only underweight, higher body temperature, and higher total WBC count were associated with pre-referral antibiotic administration (Table S6, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825), though sample size was smaller in this group (n = 1014) than the sepsis without shock group (n = 1579).

Participant Characteristics After Matching

All 24 potential confounders had VIF less than 5 and were therefore included in the logistic regression model to calculate the PS (Table S2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). For 1:1 greedy nearest neighbor caliper matching, a caliper of 0.01 was chosen empirically as it resulted in the best tradeoff between covariable balance and sample size (46). This caliper value was chosen prior to performing outcome analyses. After matching on the PS and exact matching on shock status, a population of 722 patients without shock and 244 patients with shock was obtained (Tables 1 and 2). 1627 patients did not have a match and were not analyzed (Table S7, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). For the sepsis without shock group, most covariables were balanced (SMD was < 10%) after PS matching except for lowest systolic blood pressure (SBP, SMD 18%) and referral distance (SMD 12% [Table 1; and Fig. S2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825]). In the septic shock group, all covariables were balanced (Table 2; and Fig. S2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). Distributions of the two unbalanced covariables are provided in Figure S3 (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825).

Survival Analysis

For the matched population, in the Cox model adjusted for variables that remained unbalanced (> 10% SMD: lowest SBP and referral distance) after matching, shock modified the relationship of pre-referral antibiotic administration and hazard of death (p value for interaction = 0.001) such that administration of pre-referral antibiotics was associated with lower hazard of death in the septic shock group (HR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.19–0.75) but not in the sepsis without shock group (HR, 1.36; 95% CI, 0.96–1.92; Figs. 3 and 4). Results were similar in the Cox model without adjustment for covariables (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity Analyses

Similar results were found in sensitivity analyses using different methods for PS matching. First, the caliper width was set to 0.2 × the sd of the logit of the PSs (caliper width = 0.234) (47). This resulted in 260 matched subjects with septic shock and 834 matched subjects with sepsis without shock. Referral distance and lowest SBP remained unbalanced (Fig. S4A, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825) and were included in the adjusted Cox model (Table S8, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). Optimal pair matching with caliper width equal to ∞ yielded similar results as well (Table S8 and Fig. S4B, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). Additional PS-based methods were performed and yielded results consistent with the main analysis including inverse probability of treatment weighted Cox regression (Fig. S4C, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825), Cox regression with PS adjustment, and Cox regression with stratification on PS quintiles (Table S8, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). Finally, the primary analysis was repeated using MICE with m equal to 20 imputations instead of complete case analysis. Results were consistent with the primary analysis (Table S9 and Fig. S4D, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825).

DISCUSSION

In this PS-matched analysis of a prospective cohort study of patients referred for sepsis in northeastern Thailand, pre-referral administration of antibiotics was associated with lower hazard of death in patients with septic shock, but no association was observed in sepsis without shock. These results were robust to sensitivity analyses. Our findings may have implications for pre-referral antibiotic practices at community hospitals in this region and in other LMIC settings.

This is the first study to assess the effect of pre-referral antibiotics for sepsis in an LMIC, where the burden of sepsis is highest and least well-studied (1, 17). The importance of pre-referral antimicrobials may be of particular importance in LMICs, where referral times may be substantial due to logistical challenges and distances between healthcare centers. For example, in African and South Asian patients with severe malaria, pre-referral artesunate reduced mortality risk, but only in patients with a delayed referral to a healthcare facility (48). A related paradigm to that investigated in our study is “pre-hospital” sepsis care, defined by sepsis diagnostics and treatments administered by paramedics in an ambulance. An RCT of pre-hospital antibiotic administration for suspected sepsis in an HIC setting did not show benefit (49). However, only 4% of patients in this study had septic shock, the population our analyses suggest would benefit the most from early antibiotics (49). Furthermore, a meta-analysis of pre-hospital antibiotics for sepsis reported a stronger benefit of pre-hospital antibiotics in patients with septic shock compared with sepsis without shock (33). Our findings reinforce—and apply to the pre-referral LMIC setting—the importance of early antibiotics for septic shock.

The lack of survival benefit observed in the sepsis without shock group is intriguing. This could reflect a modest benefit of pre-referral antibiotics among those with bacterial sepsis that is offset by harms associated with unnecessary antibiotics in those with nonbacterial etiologies or noninfectious conditions mimicking sepsis—a key challenge to sepsis management globally (6, 10, 18, 19). Our study included the real-world population clinicians encounter in this region, for whom antibiotic decisions are often made empirically before a definitive diagnosis can be established (6). An alternative explanation for the lack of benefit in the sepsis without shock group is indication bias due to residual confounding: providers may preferentially prescribe antibiotics to sicker patients, masking a benefit of antibiotics in this population (50). Indeed, accounting for this bias by balancing confounders between treatment groups is a key purpose of PS matching, and the lower hazard ratio for death observed in the matched cohort compared with the full cohort reflects this. However, patient characteristics including modified SOFA score and Charlson Comorbidity Index were similar in the matched group, and lowest SBP recorded was lower in patients not prescribed pre-referral antibiotics, suggesting severity of illness did not drive the signal. Finally, the survival benefit of pre-referral antibiotics in the septic shock group could be a proxy for better care quality provided to this group of patients at the community hospitals; our study is not sufficiently powered to exclude this possibility. Future studies should systematically investigate pre-referral sepsis practices in LMICs and other settings that heavily use referral networks for severe illness.

When applying our study findings to clinical practice, it is critical to consider the patient population. In the primary analysis, only a subset were matched and the unmatched patients were excluded (46). Although this approach reduces the generalizability of our findings, the matched subset is more reflective of a population that could be eligible for a theoretical randomized controlled trial of higher thresholds for antibiotic administration for suspected sepsis. Most unmatched patients had high likelihood of receiving antibiotics (Fig. S5, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). These patients for whom suspicion of infection was high would be ineligible to enroll in such a trial given the lack of equipoise in this group. Considering the group who were not prescribed pre-referral antibiotics, unmatched patients compared with matched patients were on average younger, higher proportion were female, had fewer comorbidities, and had lower total WBC count; considering the group prescribed pre-referral antibiotics, the matched and unmatched patients were similar (Table S7, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H825). To confirm the findings of this study, a randomized controlled trial of diagnostic and therapeutic sepsis protocols initiated in the community hospital setting, with a focus on antibiotic administration thresholds, is needed. Stratification by shock status would enable elucidation of a potential differential benefit and provide guidance for personalized sepsis management strategies.

Our study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to prospectively examine pre-referral antibiotics for sepsis in an LMIC and leverages a uniquely large and well clinically characterized cohort. Although the parent Ubon-sepsis cohort is single-center, the referral catchment area is wide: 48 community hospitals were represented in this analysis. We used rigorous PS methods and sensitivity analyses that corroborated our findings. Our study does have important limitations. As in all PS matched analyses, residual indication bias is possible despite our best efforts, particularly in unmeasured confounders such as those related to social determinants of health. Additionally, this study does not apply to all sepsis patients in this setting but only those who were referred to a higher level of care and who had an adequate match based on their PS. A pathogen was detected in a minority of patients as is common in clinical practice, and thus sepsis was not confirmed in the majority of patients. Due to limitations in the community hospital level data, we were unable to analyze time from community hospital presentation to antibiotic administration. However, time zero of sepsis is usually not known, and the relevance of granular time to antibiotic distinctions has been questioned (50). Twenty-eight-day mortality was similar in the septic shock group and the sepsis without shock group (18.5% vs. 19.2%) before matching, which is inconsistent with prior studies (35, 51). This could be due to survivorship bias as all patients were referred from a community hospital prior to enrollment, or due to differences in causative pathogens. Alternatively, septic shock may be more immediately recognizable and thus this group could have received more timely sepsis care. Finally, there may be clinical factors other than shock that modify the benefit of pre-referral antibiotic administration (e.g., comorbid metastatic cancer, other organ dysfunctions, bacterial species, etc.), which could be investigated in future studies (52, 53).

In conclusion, in rural Thailand, antibiotic administration prior to referral was associated with lower hazard of death by 28 days in patients with septic shock, but no association was observed in sepsis without shock. Our findings suggest early antibiotic administration in septic patients may be most critical to patients with shock, even in a resource-limited setting. Future studies that evaluate pre-referral protocols and practices in these settings are urgently needed.

KEY POINTS.

Question:

Do antibiotics administered for sepsis at a community hospital prior to referral to a tertiary care center in rural Thailand impact survival, and is septic shock an effect modifier?

Findings:

This propensity score-matched analysis of a prospective cohort study showed that pre-referral antibiotic administration was associated with lower hazard of death in patients with septic shock, but no association was observed in sepsis without shock.

Meaning:

Our findings extend to a resource-limited setting evidence supporting the benefit of early antibiotic administration in septic patients with shock.
