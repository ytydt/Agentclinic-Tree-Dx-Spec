The term “ICU” means different things to different people. For healthcare administrators, the ICU is where the hospital congregates the sickest patients, those with life-threatening organ failure in need of advanced life support. For healthcare payors, it is a place of high resource utilization, one of the costliest places in the hospital to receive care. For patients and their families, it is a marker of heightened awareness, simultaneously a place denoting concern and a place where they will be protected against harm. For healthcare providers, it is a place of business, where they come to work each day to do their jobs. It is also a source of identity and pride, a place where they obtain a unique sense of satisfaction and joy, as well a place where they too frequently experience strain and moral distress.

Given the central role of the ICU in modern healthcare, it is hard to imagine hospitals without ICUs. Yet 50 years ago, as Max Harry Weil, Peter Safar, William Shoemaker, and other visionaries met in Los Angeles to found the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM), intensive care medicine was still a fledgling specialty and the ICU as a fixture of hospital-based care was far from established. The first ICUs had appeared only a decade earlier in the forms of dedicated shock wards and expanded postanesthesia care units (1, 2). In addition, although, in 1970, many large hospitals had ICUs, most small- and medium-sized hospitals still did not (3). When ICUs did exist, they were largely disorganized—few were staffed by clinicians with special training in intensive care, almost none had a dedicated ICU director, and daily rounds by an interprofessional care team were nonexistent (4).

Over the last 50 years, much has changed. Stand-alone ICUs are now an integral part of all but the smallest hospitals, intensive care is a mature clinical discipline, and daily rounds by an interprofessional critical care team are increasingly the norm (5). There is also widespread agreement that the ICU is much more than a place where we congregate the hospital’s sickest patients. Rather, it is a complex adaptive system in which the myriad members of the interprofessional care team interact to make the ICU greater than the sum of its parts (6). There are also accepted standards for ICU organization and management, as well as a dynamic and growing evidence-base, demonstrating that ICU organization and management, that is, “the way in which we staff and run the ICU,” is a critical determinant of patient outcomes (7).

In this article, part of a series on the 50th Anniversary of SCCM in Critical Care Medicine, we review the evolution of ICU organization and management from the birth of the ICU to the present day. First, we discuss the history of hospital-based medicine and the motivators that led to the creation of the first modern ICUs in the 1950s. Next, we discuss how ICU organization and management have evolved since the founding of SCCM in 1970 up to today. Then, we review the evidence underlying modern-day ICU organization and management, including the most recent trends in staffing and care delivery. Finally, we discuss how we can continue to improve upon the current standards for ICU organization and management over the next 50 years, making the practice of critical care medicine more effective and efficient than the founders of SCCM ever imagined.

THE BIRTH OF THE ICU

From Florence Nightingale to Peter Safar

Hospitals have existed for most of recorded time, serving as community and religious centers focused on healing the sick, comforting the dying, and isolating the contagious (8). Yet it was not until the Crimean War (1853–1856) that hospitals began to take on their modern form, a change pioneered by the legendary nurse Florence Nightingale. Among Nightingale’s many innovations was moving hospitalized soldiers with the greatest injuries to a central area proximal to the nursing station where they could be closely observed (9). After the war, this concept evolved into specialized postoperative recovery units, where nurses could intensely monitor patients for complications. These units proliferated in the 1920s and 1930s as high-risk surgeries, particularly neurosurgery, became more widespread.

Nonsurgical critical care had its birth in the 1940s and 1950s with the polio epidemic and the advent of artificial respiration. Negative-pressure ventilators (i.e., “iron lungs”) were followed quickly by tracheal intubation and positive-pressure ventilators, enabling stable provision of respiratory support for patients with acquired neuromuscular weakness due to the polio virus (10). Although most polio patients would not be considered critically ill by modern standards, polio wards were unquestionably precursors to the modern ICU (Fig. 1).

By the mid 1950s, mechanical ventilators had become smaller and more technologically advanced, enabling true “life support” for patients unable to breathe on their own. At the same time, advances in applied physics and electrical engineering led to the creation of novel physiologic monitoring techniques such as the electrocardiogram and continuous monitoring of arterial and venous pressures. These innovations enabled intensive treatment of patients in shock. Under the direction of Max Harry Weil at the University of Southern California, a cohort of nurses trained in this type of care led to the creation of a “shock ward”—a 24/7 hospital ward dedicated to patients with life-threatening hemodynamic collapse (11).

Around that same time, the anesthesiologist Peter Safar expanded the operative recovery room at Baltimore City Hospital to create a resuscitation-oriented unit that he termed the “ICU” (1). At the time, Safar was developing theories around cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and he realized that these ideas could be most easily applied to critically ill patients if they were congregated in one place. In particular, he and his team recognized the need for at-the-ready nurses and respiratory therapists with skills to provide this type of care. He later wrote, “We experienced the frustrations of trying to provide respirator care for unconscious patients in various locations of the hospital without the help of trained inhalation therapists and specialty trained nurses. In 1958 therefore, we initiated the establishment of an Intensive Care Unit adjacent to the post-anesthesia recovery room” (4).

Key Drivers and Motivators

A major factor driving the creation of ICUs was technology. Invasive mechanical ventilation and continuous hemodynamic monitoring were both foundational technological advances that enabled modern critical care. A second major factor was the increasing complexity of hospital-based care, especially the development of high-risk surgeries. The fields of trauma surgery and neurosurgery in particular led to the creation of an extremely high-risk cohort that benefited from intensive monitoring by skilled nurses (12).

New technology and high-risk surgery created the conditions necessary for ICUs to exist. However, the sentinel innovation that made them succeed was more conceptual—the notion that we should group patients not according to their underlying diagnosis but according to their disease severity. This notion, pioneered by Florence Nightingale and modernized by the founders of SCCM, allowed hospitals to better take advantage of economies of scope and scale in healthcare. According to the principles of organizational science, teams are more adept at complex tasks the more they do them, a concept known as “organizational learning” (13). In critical care, the way teams gain experience is by grouping severely ill patients in a ward dedicated to caring for complex patients: the ICU.

The common denominator in this history is people. The ICU is, at its heart, an intervention based on human resources. Although we like to think of the ICU as a physical space, as Max Harry Weil wrote in 2009, the ICU is also an idea: “the commitment to dedicated care, on site, by physicians and specially trained professional nurses and technicians with preparedness for immediate lifesaving interventions for the most seriously ill and injured” (14).

ICUs in 1970

The ICU created in Baltimore City Hospital is arguably considered to be the first modern ICU, and it is archetypal of ICUs around the founding of SCCM (15–17). That unit contained 16 beds organized into two eight-bed wards with no private rooms, although there were side rooms available for isolation. The ICU’s mission was to provide critical care services to all hospitalized patients, regardless of underlying diagnosis. This meant a patient population that was both medical and surgical, although in practice the surgical patients far outnumbered the medical patients (93–7%) (1). Triage was an important concern, both at the high end and the low end of illness severity. It was necessary to “guard against admission of non-salvageable patients” as well as “guard against the tendency of house officers to keep patients in the unit far beyond the time of true need for intensive care” (1).

From the outset, the emphasis was on skilled nursing. Safar wrote, “we discovered nursing in this area required special skills not ordinarily available in the average hospital” (1). The British intensivist John Robinson wrote in 1966, “the success of the [ICU] has been due to insistence that intensive care should be made largely a nursing procedure” (15). There was also a major emphasis on ongoing nursing education—all nurses who worked in the Baltimore City Hospital ICU supplemented their traditional nursing education with additional formal training in the principles of mechanical ventilation and hemodynamic monitoring (1). This practice occurred despite concerns that increasing nursing knowledge may violate the unwritten rules of the healthcare hierarchy and historical gender norms (18).

Similar emphasis was placed on providing dedicated nurses for individual patients. At the time, the total number of nurses in an ICU was similar to general care wards, but the number of patients was fewer, thereby yielding a low patient-to-nurse ratio (19). In the words of Rosalie Lanius, a nursing leader who was involved in the creation of the ICU at Presbyterian Hospital in Pittsburgh, “[ICUs] were invented because of the problems that came from a patient being desperately ill and needing one nurse…. Finding a way to respond to that situation multiplied by thousands of times forced us to change the hospital” (19).

In contrast to nurse staffing, physician staffing was given relatively little thought. Each patient was under the responsibility of their individual admitting physician. There was no dedicated physician ICU director, and any overarching ICU policies were jointly established by the hospital’s various departments. Anesthesia house officers would consult for respirator care and resuscitation, but only when asked to do so by the attending physician.

This type of ICU was commonplace in 1970 at the founding of SCCM. Yet just like the practice of critical care, the organization and management of critical care would evolve dramatically over the next 50 years.

THE EVOLUTION OF CRITICAL CARE

Critical Care as a Physician Specialty

The founders of SCCM conceived of critical care medicine as a subspecialty building off multiple different primary specialties. In 1972, Weil wrote, “it is perfectly reasonable that [the intensivist] remains a competent surgeon, anesthesiologist, cardiologist, or an infectious disease specialist. However, [they] should apply [their] specialty skills to the care of the critical ill and build bridges to the conventional specialties” (14). Initially clinical training was ad hoc, with the first dedicated fellowship training in critical care medicine not appearing until 1963 (20). As more and more hospitals instituted ICUs and the complexity of critical care grew, the field of critical care medicine matured into an established physician specialty with dedicated fellowships, board certifications, and largely agreed upon core competencies (21–23). Central to these competencies is the focus on the whole patient, not just a specific organ system, as well as the focus on the leadership and communication skills required to lead an interprofessional team (24).

Intensivists gradually assumed a larger role in day-to-day management of critically ill patients. In 1970, nearly all ICUs operated under an “optional consult” model, whereby intensivists were available to consult on critically ill patients, but such consultations were not required (25). By the 1980s, there was steady movement toward “mandatory consult” and “closed” models in which physicians with intensive care training were required to consult on all patients in the ICU (26). Closed ICUs were initially born out of expediency, since it was impossible for physicians to provide care to critically ill patients while also attending to responsibilities in the operating room or outpatient clinic. However, there was eventually recognition that intensivist-directed care is associated with better clinical outcomes, which spurred more hospitals to hire intensivists to staff the ICU (27).

From Daily Consults to 24-Hour Care

As ICUs shifted to closed staffing models, there was an increasing emphasis on in-person care in real time and off hours (28). In academic hospitals, 24-hour care could be provided by residents and other trainees, with attending physicians available at home. However, the need for 24-hour care transcended academic hospitals with house officers. At first, community hospitals could rely on emergency department physicians to provide off-hours care. However, as emergency departments themselves became more complex, this solution became increasingly untenable. By the 1980s, there were the first instances of 24-hour staffing by trained intensivists in community hospitals (29). The rise of the internet and advances in audiovisual conferencing technology soon enabled nighttime coverage to be provided remotely via telemedicine (30). The first instance of ICU telemedicine as a strategy for nighttime coverage appeared in 2000 (31). Only 10 years later, over 10% of ICU beds in the United States were covered by telemedicine at night (32).

Nursing Specialization

Given the central role of nursing in the birth of critical care, it is not surprising that the approach to critical care nursing has also evolved over the last 50 years. As the practice of critical care became more complex, subspecialization of ICU nurses became more common, with nurses dedicating their time to surgical ICUs, neurologic ICUs, and other types of specialty units (33). As technology advanced, so too did nurses’ skills and training, with ICU nurses beginning to deliver continuous renal replacement therapy, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and other advanced therapies that transcended “routine” critical care (34). Another key development was the standardization and recognition of this expertise through critical care certification for nurses, which emerged in 1976 (35). Nurses also took on new leadership roles, both at the local and the national level. Maureen Harvey from the University of Southern California was the first nurse to become the president of SCCM in 2002, confirming the central role of nursing in the development of the field (36).

The Rise of Team-Based Care

The ICU has always been an interprofessional endeavor, with a strong emphasis on coordinated care among physicians, nurses, and respiratory therapists. This concept solidified over last 50 years, with growing awareness of the value of team-based care and an organizational culture that emphasizes communication and collaboration (37). Early studies of ICU culture failed to show a significant relationship between culture and outcome (38), likely because these studies approached ICU culture too broadly, without attention to the specific nuances of the ICU environment. Subsequently, more focused work demonstrated strong effects of infection control teams (39) and interprofessional rounding teams (40) on patient outcomes. In the 1980s, hospitals began incorporating clinical pharmacists on rounds, which resulted in lower risk of adverse drug events (41). At the same time, there was increasing recognition of the role of social workers, case managers, and pastoral care in improving patient and family outcomes (42, 43).

Advanced Practice Providers

The creation of Medicare and Medicaid in 1965 significantly increased the demand for primary care, giving rise to independent practice opportunities for nurses and the first nurse practitioner training programs (44). At the time, most nurse practitioners focused on primary care and rural health—in 1975, only 7% of nurse practitioners worked in the acute care setting (45). Eventually, limitations in the supply of intensivist physicians and duty-hour reforms for trainees created demand for nurse practitioners and physicians assistants—collectively referred to as “advanced practice providers (APPs)”—in critical care (46). The relatively low costs to train and maintain an APP workforce made this approach appealing to hospital administrators struggling to cope with limits on house officers’ hours. Eventually, data emerged that care models involving APPs yield similar clinical outcomes compared to care models without APPs (47–49). Recent estimates show that 68% of nurse practitioners and 24% of physician assistants practice in an ICU, highlighting the vital role APPs play in modern critical care (50, 51).

Introduction of Care Protocols and Bundles

The 1980s saw the establishment of intensivist groups with managerial and clinical oversights of the ICU, including the rise of ICU directors who individually provided oversight of critical care operations within a hospital (52). Around the same time, the Joint Commission began requiring a physician director for all ICUs in the United States (53). Increasing managerial oversight, along with the rise of the “evidence-based medicine” and patient-safety movements, led to the adoption of care protocols to standardize clinical practice, facilitate the use of evidence-based practice, and reduce medical errors in critical are. These protocols borrowed heavily from the fields of industrial process improvement and operations management, which had successfully transformed manufacturing in earlier decades (54). In the 1990s, data emerged supporting the use of many critical care protocols, including respiratory-therapy-driven weaning protocols (55) and protocolized sepsis resuscitation (56). Although controversy remains about the exact role of clinical protocols and bundles in intensive care, more and more hospitals are adopting these strategies in daily practice (57).

Attention to Architecture

ICUs in 1970 were generally “ward” style, with multiple patient beds in one or two large rooms with central nursing stations enabling direct views of every patient. Private rooms were reserved for patients in isolation due to contagious pathogens. This paradigm changed in the 1980s and 1990s, as clinicians paid more attention to the importance of the built environment in critical care. Open wards were replaced with private rooms configured with glass doors and large windows so that the patients were still easily visible. Designated family spaces became the norm as the role of families changed from mere “visitors” to integral support networks for critically ill patients (58). In turn, the very concept of visiting hours changed, moving away from brief intermittent time periods toward open, continuous visitation (59). This change necessitated space within the patients’ rooms where family members could be by the bedside 24 hours a day. ICUs also evolved to accommodate the collaborative, team-based model of care, with new types of workspaces designated for interprofessional interaction, informal communication between the care providers, and formal daily rounds (Fig. 2) (60).

The ICU Without Walls

Rapid response teams and ICU outreach teams were established with the goal of extending the expertise of intensivist physicians and nurses outside the ICU (61, 62). These teams sought to improve outcomes by rapidly identifying and treating patients at high risk of physiologic decompensation. These interventions were a response to several changes in hospital care, including an increase in illness severity of hospitalized patients due to shifts in care to the outpatient setting (63), nursing shortages that led to higher nurse-to-patient ratios on general care floors (64), and a relative lack of experience in the care of critical illness among general physicians and nurses (65). This idea came to be known as the “ICU without walls,” a name that is not without irony, since this concept was necessitated in part by the walls the field itself built by creating closed ICUs. Clinical studies never consistently demonstrated that rapid response teams are associated with improvements in patient outcomes (66). Nonetheless, rapid response teams staffed by ICU providers remain a common fixture of hospitals to this day.

Scientific Support to ICU Organization and Management

Perhaps the greatest change to ICU organization and management in the last 50 years is not in how we run the ICU, but in our approach to determining how we run the ICU. When SCCM was founded, the best practices for ICU organization management were determined by expert consensus (17). Today, we attempt to bring the same scientific rigor to the study of ICU organization that we bring to other areas of healthcare. This shift started in the 1980s with the first published studies of intensivist physician staffing (67). Subsequently, there emerged a large number of organizational studies culminating in several highly influential systematic reviews (68, 69), as well as the first SCCM evidence-based guidelines for ICU organization, which appeared in 2001 (70). Initially, this research was observational in nature, but in the present day, randomized clinical trials of ICU staffing are increasingly common (71). These studies helped usher in the current situation, in which a large and growing evidence-base provides guidance about how to organize and manage critical care optimally.

CURRENT BEST PRACTICES

Intensivist-Led, Interprofessional Care

Current best practice is a high-intensity physician-staffing model, in which patient care is at the discretion of an intensivist physician or consultation by an intensivist physician is required (7). Large multicenter studies as well as a recent meta-analysis containing over 50 studies demonstrate that this model is associated with lower mortality and lower ICU length of stay (Table 1) (72–74). Intensivist physicians are also associated with more consistent use of evidence-based practice, including lung-protective ventilation for acute lung injury (75) and prophylaxis against ICU acquired complications (76).

In addition to intensivist physicians, robust data support an interprofessional approach to care with the regular presence of respiratory therapists, clinical pharmacists, dieticians, and physical and occupational therapists (7). Respiratory therapists speed liberation of mechanical ventilation through the introduction of weaning protocols that emphasize early spontaneous breathing trials (77). Clinical pharmacists reduce adverse drug events and improve antibiotic prescribing (78–80). Dieticians play an integral role in ensuring adequate caloric intake and balance of macro-nutrients to enhance the recovery process (81). Physical and occupational therapists are fundamental to early mobilization that may improve respiratory and neuromuscular outcomes in the ICU (82, 83). To support communication and promote shared goals among the care team, interprofessional rounds should be conducted daily (84).

A notable limitation of this literature is the dearth of high-quality experimental evidence—the vast majority of studies are single-center and observational in nature, and, therefore, are subject to many potential biases. Furthermore, the findings are not entirely consistent across studies, with several studies showing no benefit to intensivists (85, 86) and at least one suggesting possible harm (87). A separate concern is that there is an insufficient supply of trained intensivists and ancillary care providers to enable this model in all ICUs (88).

In this context, we believe the best available evidence supports tailoring staffing models to local needs and contexts. Large academic medical centers and quaternary referral hospitals with highly sick patient populations are likely to require closed ICUs with robust interprofessional care teams. In contrast, many community hospitals might deliver excellent care with an intensivist ICU director, APPs overseeing day-to-day care, and pharmacists available for consult. Even less intense models with no intensivists are likely to suffice so long as there is appropriate nurse staffing and an ability to transfer rapidly sick patients to high-volume centers of excellence. Rather than a one-size-fits-all approach to ICU staffing, it is perhaps more important for each ICU to have an understanding of how it is staffed and why it is staffed in that manner, enabling a conscious and tailored approach (89).

Specialized Nursing

Nurse staffing should be maintained at a ratio of one nurse for one or two patients (Table 2). These staffing ratios provide nurses the time and opportunity to monitor patients closely for changes in patient status, manage the multitude of technology, and provide patient family–centered care (90). Low nurse-to-patient ratios are associated with lower mortality, particularly among high-severity ICU patients (91–95). In addition to maintaining low nurse-to-patient ratios, current best practice supports staffing ICUs with a high proportion of nurses with bachelor’s degrees in nursing, which is associated with lower mortality and fewer complications (96). Furthermore, an emphasis on healthy work environments for nurses is important—several studies demonstrate the benefits of a professional practice environment where nurses are engaged in quality improvement, have good working relationships with their physician colleagues, and are active members of policy and practice development in their hospital (96, 97). Less convincing data suggest value in staffing ICUs with nurses that have obtained critical care certification by a relevant specialty society, although this line of research is in its infancy (98).

Protocols and Care Pathways

Clinical protocols, care pathways, and checklists can be an effective strategy for facilitating the use of evidence-based practice, standardizing care across different providers, and supporting the autonomy of the interprofessional care team members (Table 3) (99). Many protocols can improve patient outcomes—for example, nurse-led sedation management (100, 101), respiratory therapist-led evaluation of extubation readiness (77), standardized management of acute respiratory failure (102), and protocolized sepsis resuscitation (103), among others (104–106). At the same time, protocols are not always successful at improving quality (107). Therefore, similar to the approach to staffing, the approach to protocols needs to be customized to each ICU—some units will benefit greatly from standardized care, while others, particularly those with more intense physician-staffing models, may benefit less.

Regional Care Coordination

A key lesson of the last 50 years is that hospitals vary widely in their critical care capabilities (108). One way to address this variation is through tiered regionalization akin to trauma or neonatal care, in which high-risk patients are routinely transferred to a limited number of centers with advanced capabilities. Indeed, some evidence exists that transferring patients with severe acute respiratory distress syndrome to regional centers of excellence leads to improved outcomes (109). However, potential downsides to this approach include the risk of adverse events during interhospital transport (110) and possibility that transportation over large distance will result in the separation of patients from their families and social support networks (111, 112).

An alternative strategy is to approach regionalization not as a hub-and-spoke model but as regional care coordination—a two-way exchange of information, education, and patients that ensures that each patient receives care matched to their specific needs (113). Indeed, informal transfer networks in the United States demonstrate that patient transfers usually flow toward better resourced hospitals (114). The most severely ill patients may still be best cared for in a large regional referral centers with advanced therapies, 24-hour intensivist staffing, and ready availability of expert consultation. However, with ready availability of ICU telemedicine, APPs, and collaborative networks for education and performance improvement, transferring all critically ill patients to regional referral centers is unlikely be either necessary or desirable (115).

Telemedicine in particular is a potentially valuable strategy for supporting smaller community hospitals. On the whole, adoption of telemedicine is associated with lower mortality for ICU patients (116). However, telemedicine care models vary greatly, and not all telemedicine programs are associated with improved outcomes. The most effective care programs appear to be those that are most responsive to local needs and are customized to local contexts rather than those that offer a “one-size-fits-all” approach (117).

THE ROAD AHEAD

ICU organization and management have changed substantially in the last 50 years, and it is certain to change further in the next 50. One probable change is the emergence of new types of care providers. Just as nurse practitioners are in some way a hybrid of nurses and physicians, we may see other hybrids such as nurses/respiratory therapists or respiratory therapists/physical therapists. These providers can make care more efficient and effective, particularly in small hospitals, such that nurses could adjust ventilators and respiratory therapists could also provide physical therapy. Indeed, in some ICUs in Australia and the United Kingdom, these roles are already in place, with nurses caring for only one patient while also managing their ventilator. Ultimately, these providers could substantially streamline critical care at a time when hospitals are under extreme financial pressures to reduce costs.

It is also likely that we will see entirely new provider types, such as care navigators who focus exclusively on the cognitive, emotional, and decisional well-being of patients and their families (118, 119). A defining change in healthcare over the last decade has been the focus on patient well-being and the patient as “customer.” Although the value of consumerism in healthcare is debatable, the goal of meeting the entirety of patient and family needs is undoubtedly good. One way to address this tension is through the introduction of providers specifically designed to meet these needs, building off the existing roles of social workers and clinical psychologists.

Another likely change is the emergence of novel care models that transcend the current standards for intensivist-led, interprofessional critical care. These might include pyramidal models where a single intensivist oversees multiple nonintensivists who in turn oversee multiple direct bedside providers or team-nursing models that provide both flexibility and scalability in times of staffing challenges. These meta-networks, first envisioned as strategies for disaster and pandemic preparedness and recently employed in the 2020 coronavirus pandemic (120), could provide high-quality critical care during routine circumstances. Another new care model might be specialty ICUs focusing on the phases of critical illness, such as dedicated ICUs for chronic critical illness focused on intensive rehabilitation rather than acute resuscitation (121).

We further expect that ICU organization and management will change through adoption of new sciences, such as social psychology and behavioral economics. Principles from these fields will provide new scientific underpinnings to strategies to get ICU teams to work together better, innovate more rapidly, retain knowledge in ways that are robust to turnover, and insulate team members against burnout. Key strategies will include an emphasis on leader inclusiveness and psychologic safety among team members (122), the use of nudge principles to harness heuristics to improve decision-making (123), and advanced scheduling procedures that pair providers based on personality type to increase the performance of ad hoc teams (124). These advances will be aided by technological innovations that help support teamwork, including augmented or virtual reality, which will allow care providers to view the electronic health record while simultaneously conversing with a live human being face to face (125).

Technology will change ICU organization and management in other ways, some of which may be profound. As machine learning and artificial intelligence improve, we will increasingly use them not just for targeted decision support but in all aspects of critical care such as triage and holistic patient management. Indeed, it is not hard to image the “computer as team member”—a hypersmart, hyperrational clinician with access to all the world’s knowledge and clinical skills that far exceed our own. It will then be our job to find the right place for this new colleague and navigate the inevitable ways that they will disrupt our traditional notion of intensivist-led, interprofessional care.

CONCLUSIONS

Through all these changes, a central challenge will be keeping patients front and center. Unfortunately, by adopting the intensivist-led interprofessional approach to ICU organization and management, we inadvertently made less room for the patient. ICU rounding teams now often include 10 or more people. This reality, coupled with single-patient rooms and the universal phenomenon of computers on rounds, has made it nearly impossible to round at the bedside. As a consequence, the critical care team often provides patient care with the patient nowhere in sight—a practice that the founders of SCCM surely would neither recognize nor endorse.

This is not to say that we should go back to open ICUs, ward-style units, paper charts, and exclusion of the interprofessional care team from clinical decision-making. Rather, we should continue to refine ICU organizational models such that we can keep the benefits of interprofessional care while returning to a world where the patient is the center of the ICU. The founders of SCCM were comfortable with technology, and it is not hard to envision them enthusiastically welcoming artificial intelligence, machine learning, and robotics into their daily practice. Yet they were also humanists who would have doubtlessly worked to prevent this new technology from obscuring the essential role of patients and their families in critical care.

In his presidential address at the SCCM’s first annual meeting, Max Harry Weil clearly recognized that patient outcomes and ICU organization were linked, stating, “it is the purpose of our society to improve the care of patients with acute life-threatening illness and injuries and to provide optimal facilities for this purpose. We commit ourselves to these ends by creating a good hospital environment with qualified teams of physicians, nurses, technicians, and medically oriented engineers. We commit ourselves to specific duties and obligations that will bring increased orderliness and expertise to the management of the critical ill” (126). Repeating these words to ourselves may be the best way to ensure that our approach to ICU organization and management continues to serve our patients for the next 50 years.
