Abstract

Objective:

Sepsis is a severe condition associated with high mortality, and hospital performance is variable. The objective of this study was to develop geospatial sepsis clusters, identify sources of variation between clusters, and test the hypothesis that redistributing sepsis patients from low-performing hospitals to higher-performing hospitals within a cluster will improve sepsis outcomes.

Design, Setting, and Patients:

We conducted a cohort study of age-qualifying Medicare beneficiaries using administrative claims data from 2013 to 2015. We calculated risk-standardized mortality for hospitals then used a clustering algorithm to define geospatial cluster boundaries based on care-seeking and inter-hospital transfer patterns. Finally, we used simulation to model the effect of reallocating sepsis patients to higher-performing hospitals within the same cluster.

Main Results:

We included 1,125,308 patients, and they were grouped into 222 regional clusters. High-performing clusters were located largely in the Midwest, and they tended to be in less urban regions with smaller hospitals. In our simulation, the most impactful strategy was reassigning cases from the lowest-performing hospital in a cluster to the highest-performing hospital in the cluster, which was predicted to prevent 1,705 deaths per year in the U.S. This aggregate benefit was lower than the 5,702 deaths predicted from reducing mortality by 1% absolute in hospitals in the lower half of the performance distribution.

Conclusions:

Geospatial clusters provide insight into regional approaches to system-based acute care. In a simulation study, targeted sepsis regionalization appears less effective than local performance improvement in reducing preventable sepsis deaths.

Keywords: Sepsis, regional medical programs, quality of health care, computer simulation

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome triggered by infection, and it is common, costly, and life-threatening.1–5 In fact, sepsis is now responsible for one-third of all in-hospital deaths, and caring for Americans with sepsis costs an estimated $64 billion in medical expenses annually.6–9 High-quality sepsis care requires early recognition, timely resuscitation, administration of antibiotics, and prevention of complications of critical illness, all of which necessitates a coordinated hospital approach to sepsis diagnosis and management.10–14 The Surviving Sepsis Campaign has recommended a structured care bundle, which outlines principles of sepsis management and have been associated with improved outcomes.10, 15–17 In the United States, this care bundle (Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Management Bundle [SEP-1]) will become a value-based purchasing metric in 2026, and current adherence varies substantially between hospitals.18, 19 Variations in quality of care, hospital mortality, and post-discharge mortality suggest that where sepsis patients are treated is a significant driver of clinical outcomes.20

Historically, one approach to managing variation in outcomes has been to identify standard practices in high-performing institutions and standardize care between hospitals.21–23 Another approach has been to incentivize performance, providing financial rewards to high-performing organizations.24–28 An alternative approach could be targeted regionalization, whereby sepsis patients are moved to high-performing hospitals to benefit from systems-of-care likely to provide the best outcomes. This type of targeted regionalization, such as is used for trauma and stroke, uses risk-stratification tools to match patients to hospitals with the tools to provide high-quality time-sensitive care.29

Our overall objective was to measure the potential impact of a sepsis regionalization strategy on sepsis outcomes. In this study, we (1) developed geographic clusters based on care-seeking and inter-hospital transfer behavior; (2) identified factors associated with variation in outcomes between clusters; and (3) tested the hypothesis that targeted outcome-based regionalization could reduce preventable sepsis deaths in the U.S. To accomplish this objective, we conducted an analysis of Medicare claims data and developed in silico simulations to estimate the impact of policy changes targeting regionalized sepsis care.

METHODS

We conducted a cohort study of fee-for-service age-qualifying Medicare beneficiaries hospitalized for sepsis between January 1, 2013 and September 30, 2015 (timed to correspond with the end of the use of the International Classification of Disease, 9th edition [ICD-9]). We used these data to develop geographic clusters, then we calculated observed-to-expected (O:E) hospital sepsis mortality and sepsis risk-standardized mortality (RSM) for individual hospitals and geographic clusters. Finally, we used these hospital performance data to simulate four strategies of sepsis patient reallocation within clusters and to estimate the projected effects of different scenarios of targeted regionalization for sepsis patients. The reallocation results were compared to overall hospital performance improvement. This study was approved under waiver of informed consent by the Mount Sinai School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (“Geography of Acute Care”, protocol STUDY-20–000657, approved 12/01/2020) and was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. It was reported using the Strengthening the Reporting of Empirical Simulation Studies (STRESS) guidelines.30 Additional methodologic details are included in Supplemental Appendix A.

Data Sources and Case Selection

We used fee-for-service Medicare claims to identify patients with sepsis treated in U.S. hospitals using the Dombrovsky method (ICD-9 codes) to identify sepsis cases requiring either a diagnosis of infection and organ failure or for septicemia as previously reported.20, 31–33 We obtained diagnosis codes from the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review (MedPAR) research identifiable files (ED and inpatient admissions). We excluded patients who were transferred between hospitals, those with a residential zip code outside the United States, and those in a hospital with fewer than 50 cases in the study period to ensure stability of RSM. Hospital characteristics were identified using the American Hospital Association (AHA) Annual Survey (2013) and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Provider of Services files, and population-level demographics were summarized from the U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey (2013, 5-year estimates). Missing data were rare, and this analysis was conducted as complete case analysis.

Outcome

Our primary performance outcome was survival to hospital discharge. Mortality was defined using the CMS Vital Status file, and we considered a patient to have survived if these 3 criteria were met: (1) inpatient stay status was “discharged;” (2) date of death (from CMS Vital Status file) was no earlier than 1 day after hospital discharge; and (3) discharge was not to hospice. Hospice was identified using the discharge disposition code on the final claim for the inpatient stay and was included with inpatient deaths to reduce the impact of rapid hospice transfers in patients expected to die from sepsis.

Performance Measurement

Our quality-of-care assessment was based on a risk-adjustment model to measure the O:E ratio for hospital mortality and the RSM. We used a generalized linear mixed effects model to risk-adjust for expected mortality, and we included a priori-defined covariates of age, gender, race, urban/rural, preceding skilled nursing facility (SNF) stay, sepsis present on admission, and comorbidities, with a random effect for hospital, as we have previously.20

O:E values for each cluster and each hospital were calculated by tabulating the number of actual deaths and dividing it by the summed predicted probability of death, using data from the entire study period. RSM was calculated by multiplying this value by the overall population mortality. A small percent of claims (<1%) were missing age and urban/rural information, and these claims were excluded from the calculation.

Development of Geographic Clusters

We developed geographic regional clusters to summarize boundaries that contain residential zip code tabulation areas (ZCTAs) within which patients were likely to seek care. We developed those regions for sepsis using a previously developed method.34

Factors Associated with Cluster Performance

We described characteristics of clusters using standard summary statistics. Then we compared cluster characteristics using t-tests and bootstrapping as appropriate, comparing characteristics for the top quartile vs. the bottom quartile (to find characteristics associated with being a top- vs. a bottom-performing cluster).

Simulation study

To estimate the effect of targeted regionalization, we performed four simulation studies combining various approaches to identify cases for simulated transfer and simulated recipient hospitals. To conduct this simulation, we assumed that reassigning a sepsis case to a new hospital changed the probability of death to reflect characteristics of the newly assigned treating hospital in the simulation. Cases for reassignment were identified either through: 1) random selection from hospitals in the upper half of RSM performance distribution within a geographic cluster; or 2) starting from the lowest-performing RSM performance hospital and reassigning cases until all cases (to a defined proportion threshold) had been reallocated. We used 3%, 5%, 10%, and 15% of the hospital-specific sepsis volume as the proportion thresholds, staying consistent with the proportion of reassigned cases. These cases were then assigned to a new recipient hospital in one of two ways: 1) to the top-performing hospital in a cluster by RSM performance (constrained to select the top-performing hospital from among only those in the top half of hospitals in the cluster by sepsis volume); or 2) to the top-performing hospitals by RSM performance up to the reassignment proportion (3%, 5%, 10%, or 15%) of each receiving hospital’s sepsis volume in our cohort, starting from the highest-performing RSM performance hospital in the cluster (constrained to ensure that we would never overwhelm a single receiving hospital related to capacity). Figure 1 shows how simulated reassignment occurred, divided into the 4 potential reassignment strategies.

Our base scenario reallocated 5% of the sepsis cases in each regional cluster from lower-performing hospitals to higher-performing hospitals. Then, we performed sensitivity analyses, reallocating 3%, 10%, and 15%, acknowledging that reallocating all cases was not feasible and wanting to understand the magnitude of reallocation that would be required to meaningfully improve clinical outcomes. We calculated the public health impact on a U.S. sepsis population of 1.85 million cases annually.

Hospital-level local improvement simulation

As a comparison, we simulated the effects of hospital-level performance improvement by reducing the absolute sepsis mortality rate of all RSM bottom-half hospitals by 1%, and calculated the total avoided deaths. For sensitivity analyses, the absolute sepsis mortality rate was decreased by 0.5%, 1%, 3%, and 5%.

RESULTS

From a total of 1,405,518 sepsis admissions for age-qualifying Medicare beneficiaries, 108,651 (7.7%) admissions were excluded due to hospital transfer. An additional 16,768 (1.2%) admissions were excluded because they were treated at low sepsis volume hospitals (fewer than 50 patients during the 3-year study period). This left a total of 1,125,308 age-qualifying Medicare beneficiaries who were included in the analysis and 1,280,099 admissions across 2,668 hospitals (Figure 2). A small percentage (0.07%) of cases had data missing. The median age was 80 years (interquartile range [IQR] 72 to 87). In our sample, 359,246 (28.1%) patients died during their hospitalization for sepsis. Supplemental Table S1 includes a full description of the case characteristics, stratified by regional cluster performance.

Characteristics of Sepsis Clusters

Sepsis cases were grouped into 222 regional clusters. The number of hospitals within each cluster ranged from 1 to 115 (median 6, IQR 3 to 15), with 34 (15.3%) clusters containing only a single hospital. The median population contained within each cluster was 701,522 people (IQR 242,886 to 1,739,290). Median RSM by hospital was 28.1% (IQR 27.6% to 28.6%), and median RSM by cluster was 28.0% (IQR of 27.4% to 28.5%, Figure 3). High-performing clusters (bottom quartile of RSM) were located predominantly in the Midwest, and low-performing clusters (top quartile of RSM) were mostly in the South (Figure 4). There was at least one teaching hospital located in 60 (27.0%) of the regional clusters.

High-performing clusters had similar population-based sepsis incidence to low-performing clusters (median 35.05 vs. 36.02 cases per 10,000 eligible population per year, difference −0.96, 95% CI −6.66 to 6.31), but they had lower mortality (median 21.1% vs. 30.6%, difference −9.47%, 95% CI −11.81% to −8.10%). A similar number of high-performing clusters had a hospital in the top decile of hospital volume compared to lower-performing clusters (median 17.9% of high-performing clusters vs. 25.0% of low-performing clusters, difference −7.1%, 95% CI −21.4% to 8.9%), and a similar number of hospitals per population (median 9.7 vs. 11.6 hospitals per 1 million population, difference −18.4, 95% CI −4.2 to 1.2). High-performing clusters had slightly lower population density (median 15.1 vs. 24.1 residents per square kilometers, difference −8.93, 95% CI −15.48 to −1.31), and slightly fewer hospitals (3 vs. 4 median number of hospitals, difference −1, 95% CI −3.50 to 0.01). High-performing clusters had fewer beds per population over age 65 (41.4 vs. 59.6 beds per 1000 residents over age 65, difference 18.2, 95% CI 5.7 to 25.8). High-performing clusters were more likely to have a top-performing (highest-performing decile) hospital than bottom-performing clusters (64.3% high-performing clusters vs. 25.0% low-performing clusters, difference 39.3%, 95% CI 32.1% to 48.2%). High-performing clusters had lower percentage of patients below the federal poverty level (median 14.1% vs 17.8%, difference −3.71%, 95% CI −4.98% to −2.37%), lower percentage of uninsured people (median 11.4% vs. 16.9%, difference −5.5%, 95% CI −7.1% to −3.8%), lower percentage of unemployed people (median 7.3% vs. 9.8%, difference −2.5%, 95% CI −4.0% to −1.4%), and greater percentage of population identifying as white (median 90.7% vs. 77.8%, difference 12.9%, 95% CI 6.5% to 17.8%, Table 1).

Variation within Clusters

Within clusters, there was variation in hospital sepsis RSM. Among hospitals with at least 50 cases, 81 hospitals (3.0%) had a hospital-specific RSM that was at least 10% greater than the cluster RSM (representing treatment of 6,483 [0.5%] cases of the total sample), and 129 hospitals (4.8%) had a hospital RSM that was at least 10% lower than the cluster RSM (representing treatment of 15,524 [1.2%] cases of the total sample). The median standard deviation (SD) for hospital RSM within clusters was 0.05, and the SD was similar in high-performing clusters and low-performing clusters. Among all cases, only 59,420 (4.6%) were treated initially at the top-performing hospital within a cluster, and 46 (20.7%) clusters had over 50% of the cluster volume treated at the top-performing hospital.

Simulation – Case Reassignment

We simulated reassignment of sepsis cases in all 188 clusters with greater than 1 hospital (85% of the 222 total clusters). When we simulated reassignment of cases that were treated in hospitals in the lowest-performing half of the cluster-specific mortality RSM (Simulation Methods A and B), 570,461 (44.6%) patients were eligible for reassignment. When we simulated reassignment of cases treated in the lowest-performing hospitals (lowest-performing hospitals first), all patients in these clusters were potentially eligible for reassignment. Cases in the remaining 34 clusters were retained in the analysis, but no reassignment occurred in those clusters.

Simulation A:

When we reassigned 5% of cases from hospitals in the higher half of RSM to the highest-performing RSM performance hospital in the cluster, the reassigned cases had a median decrease in absolute mortality of 1.02% (95% CI 1.01% to 1.03%). Averaged across the entire U.S. health system, this translated to a 0.054% (95% CI 0.054% to 0.055%) absolute sepsis mortality reduction, or an estimated 1,005 (95% CI 999 to 1,013) avoided deaths annually. The median distance from a patient’s residence increased 35.2 miles (95% CI 35.0 to 35.4) for patients who were reassigned. The volume in high-performing hospitals increased by a median of 44.9% (95% CI 44.9% to 44.9%), and the change in predicted mortality was proportional to the percentage reassigned (Table 2).

Simulation B:

Our second reassignment scenario reassigned randomly selected cases from hospitals in the higher half of RSM to the top-performing hospitals, limiting the number of reassigned cases to a portion of those hospitals’ sepsis volume, starting from the top-hospital and moving down in order of hospital RSM performance. When we reassigned a random sample of 5% of cases, the cases that were reassigned had 0.380% (95% CI 0.375% to 0.384%) decreased absolute mortality. Averaged across the entire U.S. health system, this translated to a 0.02% (95% CI 0.02% to 0.02%) absolute sepsis mortality reduction, or an estimated 374 (95% CI 369 to 379) avoided deaths annually. If 10% of cases from higher mortality hospitals were reassigned, the aggregate number of avoided sepsis deaths increased to 756 (95% CI 750 to 762). The median distance from a patient’s residence increased by 32.0 miles (95% CI 31.7 to 32.4) for patients who were reassigned. Furthermore, the volume in high-performing hospitals increased by a median of 4.58% (95% CI 4.55 to 4.60%) with 5% reassignment and 9.50% (95% CI 9.46 to 9.52%) with 10% reassignment.

Simulation C:

Our third simulation reassigned patients starting from the lowest-performing RSM performance hospital to the top-performing hospital that is also in the top-half hospitals in the cluster by sepsis volume. The reassigned cases had a 1.9% decreased absolute mortality (95% CI 1.9% to 1.9%) with 5% reassignment. Averaged across the entire US health system, this corresponded to a 0.09% (95% CI 0.09% to 0.09%) decreased mortality, and 1,705 avoided deaths (95% CI 1,702 to 1,708) each year. The median in increased distance was 39.7 miles (95% CI 39.7 to 39.7), and the volume at the top RSM hospital increased by a median of 44.9% (95% CI 44.9% to 44.9%).

Simulation D:

For our fourth simulation scenario, reassignment cases were selected starting from the lowest-performing RSM performance hospital in the cluster and reassigned to the bottom-half (highest-performing) RSM performance hospitals up to the threshold percentage. When 5% of cluster cases were reassigned, the predicted decrease in mortality in the reassigned cases had a median of 1.0% (95% CI 1.0% to 1.0%). Averaged across the US health system, 961 deaths (95% CI 958 to 965) were prevented. The median increased travel distance was 37.5 miles (95% CI 37.2 to 37.7), and the percent additional volume to the top hospital had a median of 4.9% (95% CI 4.9% to 4.9%).

Simulation: Hospital Performance Improvement

If all bottom-half hospitals improved their mortality rate by 1% absolute, we estimated that 5,702 sepsis deaths would be avoided. The number of avoided deaths increased proportionally with the percentage of mortality reduction, with 2,851 to 28,510 avoided with 0.5% to 5% mortality reduction, respectively.

DISCUSSION

In this analysis, we found that using algorithms for reassigning sepsis cases to higher-performing hospitals within geographic clusters led to minimal improvements in aggregate predicted survival. The biggest reason for this finding is that most hospitals were surrounded by other hospitals with relatively similar performance and high-performing hospitals had limited excess capacity, which limited the potential impact of performance-based reassignment. Even minimal aggregate improvements in hospital performance in lower-performing hospitals (without reallocation) greatly exceeded the predicted gains from patient reassignment.

Other authors have highlighted geographic disparities in sepsis outcomes. Prior researchers have shown that the population burden of sepsis deaths was higher in the U.S. Southeast—specifically in the Mississippi Valley, Middle Georgia, and Central Appalachia.35, 36 Some of that variation was related to racial/ethnic disparities, and outcome disparities have been associated with both intra-hospital and inter-hospital variation.36, 37 In a study using data from the REGARDS cohort, local poverty was a more important predictor of case-fatality than physician availability.38 Our maps focus more on hospital case-mortality than population sepsis-attributable mortality, but many regions previously identified as being low-performing overlapped. In contrast to prior studies, however, we identified cluster performance that did not depend solely on population incidence or hospital outcomes, and some of the clusters we identified had performance that differed substantially from that reported in adjacent clusters.

Currently, the only national sepsis quality measure publicly reported is the Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Management Bundle (SEP-1).18, 39 That measure is voluntarily reported at the hospital-level and its inclusion in the Hospital Value-Based Purchasing Program incentivizes health systems to optimize local care.40 Importantly, SEP-1 adherence excludes performance measurement of patients transferred between hospitals or in competing hospitals within a region. Future iterations of sepsis performance metrics might offer incentives both for high performance and for early transfer of patients to higher-performing centers. Other authors have advocated for more widespread regionalization for critical care services,41 and the Society of Critical Care Medicine published a proposed framework for critical care regionalization in 202242. While aggressive regionalization remains attractive in principle, though, our study found that even significant increases in inter-hospital transfer and reallocation yield only minimal population-level mortality benefits. Furthermore, reallocating cases to high-performing hospitals may tax resources with high-risk patients such that performance in these hospitals may not be maintained. In the face of concerns about the safety of early sepsis transfer and the uncertainty of unintended consequences, our data suggest that such an approach is unlikely to have meaningful impact at scale.43–48

This study also highlights the importance of local performance improvement activities to improve sepsis clinical outcomes. Such performance improvement systems are not ubiquitous, despite data suggesting that sepsis performance improvement is associated with better sepsis care.49, 50 In the most recent Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines, Recommendation #1 was a strong endorsement for hospitals and health systems to implement performance improvement, and previous Surviving Sepsis Campaign bundle implementation was effective at achieving dramatic performance improvement.10, 16, 17 Even regional programs, such as the Kansas Sepsis Project, have boasted impressive mortality reductions from performance improvement alone—improvements that may have greater impact than targeted regionalized care.51

This study has several limitations. First, our use of administrative claims precludes availability of a physiologic severity of illness metric (e.g., APACHE score). Second, we used community-level variables to account for regional differences, but some of these community factors are an imperfect surrogate for individual sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., education, income). Third, our simulations assume that when cases are reassigned to higher-performance hospitals, reassigned cases assume the outcome of those new hospitals—an assumption that may not strictly hold. Finally, we included only Medicare patients in this analysis. While the majority of U.S. sepsis cases are Medicare beneficiaries, this insured population may not fully reflect the experiences of non-Medicare sepsis patients.8

In conclusion, we identified geographic clusters in sepsis care that allow for simulation to estimate the effect of health system reorganization in impacting health outcomes. Future work will focus on other strategies to effect change most effectively, recognizing that performance variation in acute care outcomes is likely to persist.

Key Points:

Question:

Does treating sepsis patients in high-performing hospitals improve aggregate sepsis outcomes?

Findings:

In a simulation study of Medicare beneficiaries, reassigning sepsis patients to high-performing hospitals in geospatial clusters modestly improved sepsis survival, but local performance improvement targeted at low-performing hospitals had a greater effect.

Meaning:

While targeted regionalization appears to be an attractive strategy to improve sepsis outcomes, its impact is modest at best and limited due to the similarity in performance of hospitals across a region.
