Fluid Resuscitation in Septic Patients with Comorbid Heart Failure

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) recommends a 30 mL/kg bolus of crystalloid fluid within three hours of “sepsis induced hypoperfusion” (30by3), indiscriminate of comorbidities (1). Patients with heart failure (HF) receive less volume of fluid overall and reach 30by3 much later, if at all, when compared to patients without HF (2, 3). As patients with a broad diagnosis of HF are an especially heterogeneous population, therapeutic responses to fluid resuscitation may widely vary.

Literature on fluid resuscitation for septic patients with HF is scarce but seems to favor guideline recommendations. Administration of 30by3 did not increase the risk for intubation nor ventilator-free days for septic patients with HF, cirrhosis, and/or end-stage renal disease (4). Further, guideline-driven, bundled care has shown improved survival, with one study attributing larger fluid volumes in HF patients to be the driver of these findings (5, 6). An additional study has described HF as a risk factor for poor maintenance of normal hemodynamics after a fluid challenge, but cautions that, despite this, most HF patients sustain favorable responses to fluids (7).

Jones et al. have noted that there is limited literature evaluating the impact of early fluid resuscitation based on HF phenotypes (8). We elected to perform a secondary analysis of HF patients from our cohort of patients presenting with severe sepsis or septic shock to the emergency department (2). We aimed to determine whether subgroups of HF patients, including reduced ejection fraction (EF) (<50%, HFrEF), history of pulmonary hypertension (PH), and/or documentation of volume “overload” by providers affected fluid administration. We also aimed to assess whether these factors impacted the effects of 30by3 on clinical outcomes.

Information regarding patient selection, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and/or definitions can be found in the original study or in the Supplementary Material (2). Sixteen of the original 245 HF patients were excluded due to incomplete data (i.e., documented history of HF but without corresponding echocardiogram), leaving 229 patients for analysis. Analyses and covariates are outlined in Tables 1 and 2 and chosen a priori to mirror the covariates used in the original analysis (2).

Comparison of the effect of 30by3 on mortality, delayed hypotension, ICU admission and length of stay, and intubation across subgroups of HF. Logistic regression was used to assess relationships between achievement of 30by3 on various clinical outcomes. Interaction testing and subgroup analysis included MEDS, septic shock, and obesity to avoid overfitting. ICU length of stay was analyzed using Cox regression and accounted for censoring due to in-hospital deaths. A hazard ratio > 1 would indicate that the factor was associated with a greater likelihood (or hazard) of discharge (i.e., a shorter length of stay). Tests of the differences in the effect of reaching fluid goals by three hours across subgroups were performed fitting a model to the entire HF sample containing interaction terms between each covariate and the subgroup indicator.

Supplementary Table S1 highlights demographics, in-hospital metrics, in-hospital mortality, and time to antibiotics between subgroups of HF patients in detail. Mortality was 23.1% overall and 25.3% among patients with septic shock. Provider documentation of volume “overload” were least likely to achieve 30by3 (~7% vs. ~30% in HFrEF and ~12% in PH), received less fluid volumes at 3 hours (7.1 mL/kg vs. 13.3 mL/kg in HFrEF and 11.7 mL/kg in PH), and was associated with decreased odds of 30by3 (OR=0.17, 95% CI 0.07-0.43), while HFrEF and PH were not (Table 1). Receiving 30by3 did not impact mortality (OR=0.71; 95% CI 0.33-1.55), nor were there interaction effects of HFrEF, “overload,” or PH. Patients receiving 30by3 had shorter ICU stays by ~2 days (HR=1.96; 95% CI 1.24-3.08) compared to not receiving 30by3, with EF showing an interaction effect (p=0.03) (Table 2). Achieving 30by3 within subgroups did not impact ICU admission, delayed hypotension, or intubation.

Provider perception of volume “overload” appeared to be the primary factor associated with failure to achieve 30by3. It may be that physical exam and bedside diagnostic measures provide clinicians with immediate, real-time feedback on the volume status of the septic patient. These factors may change over time and the current status of the patient may be more important when making therapeutic decisions. Our original analysis demonstrated improved survival with 30by3, even after adjustment for HF, but with no interactive effect for volume “overload” status (2). However, this result may be subject to underpowering. Deciphering intravascular “overload” versus fluid that was merely “third-spaced” may be challenging, and deviation from guideline-based recommendations at this time may have deleterious consequences until further studies can be conducted.

No mortality impacts were observed for HF patients achieving 30by3, nor any interactive effects of the selected subgroups. This may support safety of guideline-based resuscitation, though our findings are limited by a small cohort. Prior studies observed improved survival with sepsis-bundled care and that HF patients may be among the largest benefactors of additional fluid (5, 6). The question of utility remains—how will the greatest number of patients benefit? To give all patients with sepsis-induced hypoperfection 30by3, or to give providers latitude to determine the appropriateness of early fluid administration? Existing literature, and our finding for decreased ICU length of stay, could support a more indiscriminate strategy but is presented through a retrospective lens and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the HF patient without sepsis, but given 30by3 for presumed sepsis, may have a different outcome, which was not evaluated in this analysis.

Paradoxically, the shorter duration of ICU stay amongst HF patients receiving 30by3 was only observed for reduced EF. One study has shown increased mortality for septic patients with patients with preserved EF but does not evaluate this effect with regards to fluid resuscitation. This is theorized to occur as patients with preserved EF may be more sensitive to changes in intravascular volume compared to patients with reduced EF (9). Conversely, patients with chronic systolic dysfunction with reduced EF may have relatively greater left-ventricular end-diastolic volumes; therefore, a loss of effective intravascular volume through sepsis-induced vasodilation and capillary leak may be problematic and adequate fluid resuscitation to augment effective contractility, per the Frank-Starling principle, may be paramount. Additionally, myocardial depression often resolves along with sepsis recovery, and EF in sepsis may inversely align with peripheral vascular resistance. Therefore, a hyperdynamic EF may reflect poor prognosis (10). However, we did not observe any change in left-ventricular function in the subset of patients with an echo obtained before or during their hospitalization for sepsis (Supplementary Table S2). Finally, how HFrEF prior to the onset of sepsis predicts outcomes based on fluid resuscitation remains unknown and requires further investigation.

In conclusion, HF patients with severe sepsis and septic shock are subject to significant deviations from guideline-recommended fluid resuscitation and appears to be driven by provider perception of volume “overload”. Administration of 30by3 in this cohort of HF patients demonstrated decreased ICU length of stay, but without influence on mortality. Impact of fluid resuscitation may differ depending on specific HF phenotypes and additional research into these specific populations is necessary. Under-resuscitation of patients with HF may have deleterious consequences and remains a blind spot within the sepsis literature.
