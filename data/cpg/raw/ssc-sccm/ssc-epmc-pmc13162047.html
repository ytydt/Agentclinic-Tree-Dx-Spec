Vasoactive Medications and the Microcirculation in Septic Shock: A Scoping Review

Abstract

Objective:

To summarize knowledge and identify gaps in evidence regarding the effects of vasoactive medications on microvascular perfusion in septic shock.

Data sources:

We conducted a comprehensive search of MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials.

Study Selection:

Studies included adult patients with septic shock who received vasoactive therapy with corresponding microcirculatory measurements between 1946 and May 7, 2025.

Data Extraction:

Data extraction was performed in Covidence by two independent reviewers. Data items included study design, patient population, vasoactive medication, macrohemodynamics, and microcirculation measurements and techniques.

Data Synthesis:

33 studies were included evaluating several vasoactive medications and microvascular measurement techniques. Despite heterogeneous study design, studies that recruited patients within 24 hours tended to report more significant microcirculatory changes. Microcirculatory changes and cardiac index only aligned in 39% of cases, highlighting a disconnect between global hemodynamics and microvascular perfusion. Several vasoactives, including norepinephrine, vasopressin, terlipressin, and levosimendan demonstrated improvements in peripheral/sublingual microcirculation. Despite limitations in directly monitoring gastric microcirculation, dobutamine may enhance perfusion, however, further studies are needed to support dobutamine and other vasoactive agents’ impact on clinical outcomes.

Conclusions:

Despite growing interest in perfusion-guided care, significant challenges remain in understanding how vasoactive agents affect the microcirculation. Clarifying these effects through standardized research and point-of-care perfusion monitoring remains a challenge in advancing outcome-driven resuscitation strategies.

Keywords: Microcirculation, hemodynamic coherence, septic shock, vasopressors, vasodilators

Introduction

Despite significant advances in management, sepsis remains the leading cause of inpatient deaths at approximately 34%.1 Optimizing global hemodynamic parameters such as mean arterial pressure (MAP) and cardiac output (CO) are the cornerstones of septic shock management. Restoration of global or macro-hemodynamic parameters, however, does not necessarily correlate with improved end-organ perfusion.2–4 This loss of autoregulation that dissociates the microcirculation from macrohemodynamics is referred to as “hemodynamic incoherence.”2

Septic shock is characterized by endothelial inflammation and capillary injury leading to systemic vasodilation and shock. The microcirculation—comprising of terminal arterioles, capillaries, and venules generally less than 20–200μm in diameter—determines organ perfusion and tissue oxygenation at the cellular level.5 Lymphatics are also an essential regulator of perfusion, especially in intraabdominal organs, but rarely studied in critical illness.6

Microcirculation is assessed using a variety of imaging modalities. Sublingual microscopy was first developed using orthogonal polarization spectral (OPS) imaging, followed by side-stream dark field (SDF), and incident dark field (IDF) illumination.7,8 Laser doppler flowmetry (LDF) measures doppler shifts of moving red blood cells, typically in the forearm or gastric mucosa.9 Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) estimates tissue oxygenation of cutaneous tissues, generally in the hand or forearm.10 Gastric tonometry measures the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in the stomach lining.11 A comprehensive guide to these and several other markers are discussed in several reviews.12–14

Over the past several decades, efforts to understand microcirculatory effects of vasoactive medications have remained insufficient to guide clinical therapy. We performed a scoping review to analyze the methodologies used, identify gaps in knowledge, and guide future clinical research in this field.

Methods

2.1. Protocol and Registration

This review’s protocol and search strategy are registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/vygz8/). Ethics and Institutional Review Board approval were not required. This review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (Supplement 1).15 We included a scoping review methodology based on the heterogeneity in microcirculation measurement techniques and study design.

2.2. Information Sources, Search Strategy, and Eligibility Criteria

MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials were queried on May 4, 2024, and updated on May 7, 2025. A comprehensive search strategy was implemented by our librarian (NN) and included various combinations of search terms involving microcirculation measurement techniques, vasoactive medications, and septic shock (Supplement 2). We included experimental and observational studies of adult patients with septic shock who received vasoactive medications and underwent microcirculatory hemodynamic measurements.

2.3. Selection of sources and evidence

Studies were selected if they reported microcirculatory measurements before and after vasoactive medication initiation or dose change. Case series, case reports, animal studies, pediatric studies, conference abstracts, meta-analyses, and review articles were excluded. We excluded studies evaluating agents without primary vasoactive properties, such as vitamin C and methylene blue, and agents not contemporarily used in septic shock management, such as prostacyclin. Indirect measurements of metabolism that did not directly quantify microcirculation, such as global lactate/pyruvate levels, oxygen delivery (DO2)/ oxygen consumption(VO2), and indocyanine green plasma disappearance rate, were excluded.13,16 Capillary refill time (CRT) was incorporated due to its growing use in clinical research and correlation with microvascular skin flow.17,18

2.4. Data charting process and data items

Covidence software (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) was used for screening. Two investigators independently screened all abstracts and conducted full text reviews and data extraction. A third reviewer resolved any conflicts. Articles that were identified by the authors outside of the initial screen (e.g. by citation searching) were added to the screening pool.

2.5. Synthesis of results

Formal synthesis was not performed given the scoping review design. We compared study design, macrohemodynamics, and microcirculation measurements for each vasoactive medication. In studies that studied multiple medications, we extracted available data for each medication. For studies with serial measurements, data was extracted at the time point with the largest difference in microcirculation measurements. Measurements beyond six hours were generally excluded if earlier measurements were available to avoid confounders associated with routine care.

All reported microcirculatory measurements were extracted for each methodology. The exception was gastric tonometry, which was primarily evaluated by the difference in gastric and arterial carbon dioxide (PCO2 gap), especially those ≥20 mmHg, rather than intramucosal pH and other less reliable measurements of gastric perfusion.11,13,16,19–21

2.6. Statistical Analysis

Differences in qualitative variables involving study design were compared using two-tailed Fisher’s exact tests.

Results

3.1. Characteristics of Sources of Evidence

The electronic search resulted in 1,802 records, of which 1,393 were screened, 60 underwent full-text review, and 33 were included. (Figure 1).

3.2. Study Design and Patient Characteristics

Of the included studies, 11 (33%) were randomized controlled trials, and 22 (67%) were non-randomized experimental studies (Supplement 3). Only two studies utilized a placebo group.22,23 The median reported mortality was 56%, derived from heterogeneous reporting of hospital mortality, ICU mortality, 30-day mortality, and 90-day mortality. 1991 ACCP/SCCM Consensus criteria was most commonly used (45%), while only two studies (6%) used Sepsis-3 criteria (Supplement 3).

Adjuvant medications, usually NE and/or dopamine, were frequently administered in 64% of studies at fixed doses during the study period. Almost all studies (94%) administered intravenous fluids prior to and/or during the study, with various fluid resuscitation goals including central venous pressure > 8, pulse pressure variation < 13%, and pulmonary artery occlusion pressure (PAOP) > 12 or > 18 (Supplement 3).

Recruitment times varied from <24 hours (27%) to 1–3.5 days (24%) after admission or initiation of shock management. Most studies (45%) did not report their recruitment times (Supplement 3).

Studies that recruited patients <24 hours were more than twice as likely to report significant microcirculatory changes compared to those that recruited >24 hours (67% vs 27%, p = 0.347). After vasoactives were initiated, the microcirculation was re-measured after various intervals, ranging from within 1 hour (33%), to after 1–2 hours (21%), and after 4–24 hours (21%). Studies that collected measurements after 1–2 hours were more likely to report significant microcirculatory changes compared to measurements taken <1 hour (44% vs 18%, p=0.158). Five studies with serial measurements found microcirculation measurements to peak from 1 to 6 hours, while two studies noted a return to baseline by 24 hours.24–28

3.3. Microcirculatory Measurement Techniques & Trends Over the Years

Across the 33 studies included in this scoping review, 8 different microvascular techniques were utilized, as summarized in Figure 2. Nine studies (27%) used more than one microcirculation technique. The period from 2006 to 2013 represented a peak in research activity with up to 4 microcirculatory techniques reported per year. Several methodologies (gastric tonometry, LDF, and OPS) declined in use while SDF became the most common technique selected by investigators in recent years.

3.4. Microcirculation and Cardiac Index

Most studies (85%) reported cardiac indices (CI). Microcirculation and CI trended the same way (both significantly improved or unchanged) in only 39% of measurements (Supplement 4). Otherwise, microcirculation improved without a change in CI (32%) and vice versa (29%). Studies that recruited patients <24 hours had more than a twofold higher concordance between cardiac indices and microcirculation measurements compared to patients with recruitment times >24 hours (67% vs 25%, p=0.153).

3.5. Microcirculatory Effect by Measurement Technique – (Supplement 5)

Gastric Tonometry

Eighteen studies (55%) used gastric tonometry. Eleven used air tonometry and seven used saline tonometry. Proton pump inhibitors/histamine antagonists were administered in 56%, discontinued in 17%, and unreported in the remaining. Tube feeds were usually noted to be held, although ranging from 1 hour to 8 hours prior to study. (Supplement 6).

Sublingual Microscopy

Thirteen studies (39%) utilized sublingual microscopy - twelve using SDF and one using OPS. Most reported measurements were microvascular flow index (MFI) (86%), perfused vessel density (PVD) (79%), and proportion of perfused vessels (PPV) (79%). When measured, PVD had the highest incidence of improvement with intervention (73%), followed by PPV (64%), and MFI (58%) (Supplement 7).

Laser Doppler Flowmetry

Seven studies used LDF (21%), five to measure gastric perfusion and two to measure perfusion in the forearm. In the four studies that used both gastric tonometry and gastric LDF, microcirculation assessment changed in the same direction in all studies but one.26

Near Infrared Spectroscopy

Three studies utilized NIRS with a vascular occlusion test (VOT). To perform the vascular occlusion test, Georger et al inflated a cuff to 220mmHg until StO2(%) reached 40% of baseline (occlusion time ~4.5 min) while Thooft et al performed a 3 minute VOT, inflating the cuff to 50mmHg above systolic pressure.29,30 The optimal method is debated in the literature.31,32

Capillary Refill Time

Two studies examined CRT as a marker of microcirculatory change. Hernandez et al found no change in CRT when comparing dobutamine to placebo.23 Fage et al found that increasing norepinephrine dose did not change CRT when baseline CRT was normal.33 When baseline CRT was prolonged and MAP improved ≥15%, changes in CRT were variable, indicating a dissociation between the two.

Norepinephrine

Georger et al was the only study to investigate the direct impact of norepinephrine (NE) on the microcirculation to target a MAP of 65 mmHg, without increasing the MAP goal. NE was associated with an improvement in cardiac index (CI) and microcirculation assessed by NIRS methodology in early septic shock (<6 hours).29

Vasopressin

Five studies evaluated vasopressin demonstrating some improvement in sublingual microcirculation and impaired gastric perfusion. Two studies found that the addition of vasopressin to NE reduced NE doses and improved microcirculation measured via SDF.22,27 When compared to a placebo group, however, the change in microcirculation was not different.22 Regarding gastric perfusion, three studies found that adding/replacing NE with vasopressin worsened PCO2 gap measured via gastric tonometry.25,34,35

Terlipressin

Three studies assessed terlipressin. One study showed improvement in sublingual perfusion via SDF after terlipressin bolus36, however, terlipressin infusion in another study did not improve SDF markers more than in placebo group22. Another studied patients in hyperdynamic shock and found terlipressin to improve gastric perfusion via LDF.26

Phenylephrine

Two studies investigated the effects of phenylephrine on gastric perfusion. One found that phenylephrine was associated with slightly worse gastric perfusion compared to norepinephrine37, while the other reported comparable effects between the two agents - in the setting of lower average dose of phenylephrine in the latter study38.

Dobutamine

Eleven studies investigated dobutamine, most of which suggested an improvement in gastric perfusion, in patients with a mean baseline CI ≥ 2.5 L/min/m2. Six studies found dobutamine improved gastric perfusion measured via gastric tonometry24,27,28,39,40 and/or LDF41 while two did not detect improvement23,42. Increased gastric perfusion was observed in three studies despite no change in CI.27,39,41 Two studies found dobutamine improved sublingual microcirculation40,43 while three studies did not detect a difference23,44,45. The only study to utilize a placebo group did not detect differences using multiple microcirculation measurement modalities.23

Epinephrine

Six studies evaluated the effect of epinephrine on gastric perfusion with inconsistent results. One study found that epinephrine improved gastric perfusion compared to NE41, while two studies did not detect a difference between the two vasopressors42,46. Relative to the combination of NE-dobutamine, epinephrine was observed to increase gastric perfusion in one study47, decrease gastric perfusion in another41, and a third did not detect a difference42. One study found NE-dopexamine improved gastric perfusion compared to epinephrine.48 In patients in hyperdynamic states, epinephrine was associated with worsened gastric perfusion compared to dopamine28 and a reduction in gastric perfusion to cardiac output ratio.48

Levosimendan

Two studies assessed levosimendan with improvements in microcirculation. The first was in patients with septic cardiomyopathy treated with NE-dobutamine.49 Dobutamine was switched to levosimendan in half of the patients, with an improvement in left ventricular function and gastric perfusion. A randomized controlled trial also found that levosimendan improved SDF markers compared to dobutamine.44

3.8. Increasing MAP goal

Eight studies showed variable results when testing whether a higher MAP resulted in recruitment of the microcirculation. Four studies used NE to increase MAP from 65 to 85–90 mmHg, and although CI increased, no differences were detected in gastric tonometry, sublingual microscopy, LDF of the forearm, or CRT.33,50–52 Another two studies found improvements using SDF and NIRS30, and hyperspectral imaging of the forearm53. Two studies evaluated whether increasing MAP was beneficial in patients with pre-existing hypertension. One found an improvement in SDF markers54, while the other did not55. (Supplement 8)

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes current evidence on the microcirculatory effects of vasoactive medications in septic shock, serving as both a comprehensive reference and a foundation for identifying gaps and priorities for future research. (Figure 3).

Studies enrolling patients in earlier septic shock (within 24 hours) more often reported significant microcirculatory changes with vasoactive use, and greater alignment with changes in cardiac index. Despite not reaching statistical significance, these trends align with the concept of “hemodynamic coherence,” suggesting that optimizing macrohemodynamics with vasoactive agents may be most effective in early stages of septic shock, with diminishing benefit as coherence declines. The transition from hemodynamic coherence to incoherence is difficult to detect without continuous monitoring of organ perfusion or microcirculation at the bedside. Serum lactate is suboptimal to measure dynamic changes in perfusion.56 Two studies with longitudinal measurements noted improvements in microcirculation that peaked by 6 hours and decreased again by 24 hours, possibly due to clinical progression, but not excluding a loss of microcirculatory response to vasoactives consistent with hemodynamic incoherence. While we refer to early shock as <24 hours based on outcome differences in included studies, in clinical practice, patients present with significant variability. The development of point-of-care microcirculatory monitoring may enable more physiologically guided, individualized resuscitation strategies.

Vasoactive agents had variable and context dependent effects on the microcirculation. Norepinephrine, the recommended first-line vasopressor in septic shock, improved cutaneous microcirculation in initial resuscitation. Although a known peripheral vasoconstrictor, NE did not impair microcirculation even in higher MAP goals, however its specific effects on gut/gastric microcirculation were not directly assessed. As a control against phenylephrine and epinephrine, norepinephrine had similar effects on gastric perfusion. Adding vasopressin to norepinephrine appeared to improve peripheral/sublingual microcirculation by reducing overall norepinephrine dose in vasopressin responders, through V2 receptor-mediated vasodilation56, or via an unknown synergistic effect. Nascente et al highlighted the differences between vasopressin responders and non-responders as assessed by microcirculation perfusion, with responders at baseline being on higher doses of NE and without a reduction in CI compared to non-responders.57 Further studies differentiating vasopressin responders vs non-responders may be able to phenotype patients with better microcirculatory profiles. Vasopressin was noted to worsen gastric perfusion, supporting its use in bleeding complications of cirrhosis. Terlipressin bolus was also associated with decreased NE doses and improved sublingual perfusion. However, in one study of hyperdynamic septic shock, terlipressin improved gastric perfusion, likely a context-dependent benefit of splanchnic vasoconstriction in situations where cardiac compensation is insufficient to perfuse gastric circulation. Contrasting gastric perfusion results for vasopressin and terlipressin reflect the challenges in clinically studying gastric microcirculation and reinforce the need for control groups and macrohemodynamic context in study design.

Dobutamine was the most studied vasoactive agent, with some evidence to suggest dobutamine may overcome some of the vasoconstrictive effects of catecholamines on gastric perfusion. All studies were conducted in patients with normal cardiac indices using dobutamine solely as an adjunct to recruit the microcirculation, which has been debated in the literature.58–62 Further studies are needed to evaluate its role in septic shock as the studies in our review did not address clinical outcomes or effects of tachyphylaxis. Epinephrine, on the other hand, yielded conflicting results with some studies showing improved gastric perfusion compared to NE and NE-dobutamine, while others showed no difference. Epinephrine may worsen gastric perfusion in hyperdynamic states where a further increase in CI is insufficient to compensate for end-organ perfusion. Levosimendan demonstrated more consistent improvements in both myocardial function and microcirculation, especially in patients with septic cardiomyopathy.

To this date, despite decades of research, there remains insufficient evidence to justify raising the MAP goal above the standard threshold of 65 mmHg.63–65 Discussion regarding the benefit of higher MAP goals in certain complications of shock, such as AKI, delirium, etc., remain ambiguous but continue to be investigated in trials such as OVATION-65.66 The continued efforts in investigating higher MAP goals reinforces the need for microcirculation research that focuses on clinical outcomes in addition to improvements in microcirculation.

Limitations & Future Directions

Our study provides a qualitative assessment of effects on the microcirculation; however, there are several limitations to acknowledge. Clinical studies varied greatly by sepsis definitions, recruitment times, study design, and microcirculation techniques. Most studies were small, under-powered, and non-randomized. Concomitant fluid administration and adjuvant medications also confound microcirculation measurements.

Microcirculatory assessments vary in methodology and limitations. Sublingual microscopy via handheld video microscopy (HVM) has evolved considerably in recent years, with ongoing improvements in imaging resolution and machine learning-based analytics.13,67,68 Among validated metrics, perfused vessel density (PVD) was most consistently improved in our review, in alignment with evidence that support PVD as a comprehensive microcirculatory variable that incorporates both flow and vessel density.8 Despite these advances, sublingual microscopy poorly correlates with intraabdominal microcirculation as demonstrated in clinical and animal studies, possibly due to local villi ischemia that persists despite preserved sublingual flow in septic shock69,70.

Measuring intraabdominal microcirculation more directly with techniques such as laser doppler flowmetry or gastric tonometry remains a major challenge. These two imaging modalities have largely been abandoned due to technical limitations, weakening the strength of their findings.11,19–21 For example, LDF is highly susceptible to probe movement and changes in gastric fluids due to H2 blockers and enteral nutrition. Gastric tonometry is likely the least reliable method, collecting very localized markers of relative ischemia and dependent on tonometry type. Most studies in this review utilized saline tonometry, which is known to be less reliable than air tonometry.71 Overall, the relevance of measuring microcirculation in distant sites (e.g., skin and sublingual) as proxies for vital organ microcirculation (e.g., gut, brain, kidney, liver) warrants further validation.

Ultimately, while numerous studies report changes in microcirculatory parameters, the clinical relevance of these measurements remains unclear. Trials such as ANDROMEDA-SHOCK have demonstrated the potential value of perfusion-targeted resuscitation strategies.72,73 In contrast, the DAMIS trial, which integrated SDF measurements into patient’s therapy plans, did not improve mortality or reduce fluid administration.74 The role of microcirculation or peripheral perfusion monitoring in the management septic shock is an evolving area of research.75,76

Based on our review, we recommend the following aims for future clinical research of microcirculation-guided vasoactive support

  1. Establish standardized methodologies, including the use of control groups, multi-site microcirculation assessment, and serial measurements over time

  2. Differentiate early versus late sepsis phenotypes in study design by limiting recruitment times or accounting for the influence of hemodynamic coherence on microvascular response to vasoactive agents

  3. Investigate drug-specific effects on microvascular function by leveraging emerging biomarkers in septic shock and differentiating between vasoactive responders vs non-responders

Conclusion

As septic shock management moves toward individualized, perfusion-guided strategies, it is increasingly important to understand how vasoactive medications affect the microcirculation. This scoping review highlights the potential of several vasoactive therapies to improve regional perfusion; however, the clinical relevance of these effects remains uncertain. The lack of standardized methodologies including the homogenization of early and late sepsis phenotypes in current studies limits interpretation. Future studies should focus on standardized methodologies and outcome-driven strategies to determine whether microcirculatory-guided resuscitation can improve outcomes.

Key Point.

Question:

How do vasoactive medications affect microcirculatory perfusion in septic shock?

Findings:

A scoping review of the literature identified 33 studies using a variety of microcirculation measurement techniques to evaluate several common vasoactive medications in septic shock.

Meaning:

To advance perfusion-guided management of septic shock, a clear understanding of how vasoactive agents affect the microcirculation calls for standardization of research methods, bedside perfusion monitoring, and the integration of hemodynamic coherence into study design.
