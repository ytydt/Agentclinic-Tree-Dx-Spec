Abstract

Objective:

Quantify hypotension burden using high-resolution continuous arterial blood pressure (ABP) data and determine its association with outcome after pediatric cardiac arrest.

Design:

Retrospective observational study.

Setting:

Academic PICU.

Patients:

Children ≤18 years old admitted with in- or out-of-hospital cardiac arrest who had invasive ABP monitoring during post-cardiac arrest care.

Interventions:

None

Measurements:

High-resolution continuous ABP was analyzed up to 24 hours following return of circulation (ROC). Hypotension burden was time-normalized integral area between mean arterial pressure (MAP) and 5th percentile MAP-for-age. Primary outcome was unfavorable neurologic status (PCPC ≥3 with change from baseline) at hospital discharge. Mann-Whitney tests compared hypotension burden, duration, and magnitude between favorable and unfavorable patients. Multivariable logistic regression determined association of unfavorable outcome with hypotension burden, duration, and magnitude at various percentile thresholds from the 5th through 50th percentile-for-age.

Measurements and Main Results:

Of 140 patients (median age 53[IQR 11,146] months, 61% male); 63% had unfavorable outcome. Monitoring duration was 21[7,24] hours. Using a MAP threshold at 5th percentile-for-age, median hypotension burden was 0.01[0,0.11]mmHg-hours per hour, greater for patients with unfavorable compared to favorable outcome (0[0,0.02] vs 0.02[0,0.27]mmHg-hours per hour, p<0.001). Hypotension duration and magnitude were greater for unfavorable compared to favorable patients (0.03[0,0.77] vs 0.71[0,5.01]%, p=0.003; and 0.16[0,1.99] vs 2[0,4.02]mmHg; p=0.001). On logistic regression, a one-point increase in hypotension burden below 5th percentile-for-age (equivalent to 1 mmHg-hour of burden per hour of recording) was associated with increased odds of unfavorable outcome (aOR 14.8, 95%CI 1.1–200, p=0.040). At MAP thresholds of 10–50th percentiles-for-age, MAP burden below threshold was greater in unfavorable compared to favorable patients in a dose-dependent manner.

Conclusions:

High-resolution continuous ABP data can be utilized to quantify hypotension burden after pediatric cardiac arrest. Burden, duration, and magnitude of hypotension are associated with unfavorable neurologic outcome.

Keywords: hypotension, cardiac arrest, pediatric ICU, arterial line, lactate

Introduction

Cardiac arrest is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in pediatrics, with approximately 20,000 arrests occurring per year in the United States.1–5 Rates of survival to discharge vary from 10–60% depending on whether the event was an in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) or out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). Brain injury is the primary driver of morbidity following return of circulation (ROC) and up to 60% of children who survive cardiac arrest sustain at least moderate brain injury.6–8 Brain injury is due to a combination of primary hypoxic-ischemic injury that occurs during the arrest itself and secondary brain injury that occurs in the hours to days following ROC.9

Post-arrest hypotension is common and contributes to secondary brain injury through cerebral hypoperfusion.(10, 11) Hypotension, in turn, is caused by a combination of myocardial dysfunction, inflammatory response, and vasoplegia.12–15 Multiple studies have observed an association between hypotension in the first 24 hours after ROC with poor clinical outcomes.16–23 As a result, American Heart Association (AHA) Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) guidelines recommend maintaining systolic blood pressure (SBP) >5th percentile-for-age (Class I, LOE C-LD).24 Studies informing these guidelines largely relied on manually abstracted blood pressure data from medical records and binary classification of hypotension based on the lowest blood pressure value within a given timeframe post-ROC.14, 16–19 Due to the methodological limitations of these studies, optimal blood pressure targets to prevent secondary brain injury are unknown.

We aimed to address this knowledge gap by developing a quantitative method of defining the burden of hypotension, incorporating both magnitude and duration of mean arterial pressure (MAP) below pre-defined thresholds.25 We examined the association between hypotension burden below the 5th percentile-for-age in the first 24 hours after ROC and unfavorable neurologic outcome. Secondarily, we aimed to determine whether the association between hypotension burden and unfavorable outcome varied as the hypotension threshold was increased incrementally from the 5th to 50th percentile, and whether the association was modified by post-ROC lactate level, a surrogate marker of cardiac arrest severity.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective observational study of patients ≤18 years old from November 2018 until May 2023 who experienced an IHCA or OHCA, received post-cardiac arrest care in the PICU with an invasive arterial line, and had data captured and stored using a Moberg Component Neuromonitoring System, a system for the capture and storage of high-resolution, multimodality physiologic data (CNS, Moberg Research, Ambler, PA, USA). Arterial lines were placed at the discretion of the clinical team. Post-cardiac arrest blood pressure targets were guided by an institutional pathway, with specific interventions at the discretion of the medical team.26 For patients who experienced multiple cardiac arrests within the study period, only the patient’s index arrest was included. Patients were excluded if they had poor-quality ABP waveforms defined as >50% noise/artifact removed from raw waveform via unbiased filtering, as per below.

Patient demographics, clinical data related to the cardiac arrest, and outcome were abstracted from an institutional cardiac arrest database. The study was approved by the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) Institutional Review Board with a waiver of consent (IRB #19–016118, initial approval 4/17/19, Acute Brain Injury Neuromonitoring). Procedures were followed in accordance with the CHOP Research Institute and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Hypotension burden quantification

For each cardiac arrest event, arterial blood pressure (ABP) waveform data, sampled at 100Hz, were extracted. We abstracted all evaluable ABP data from the first 24 hours post-ROC. MAP was calculated from the raw ABP waveform using a rolling 5-second window. We removed artifact using an unbiased variable-window multi-order median filtering algorithm (Figure 1). For each patient, we then calculated:

  1. Hypotension burden (mmHg-hours per hour): Area between the patient’s MAP and the 5th percentile-for-age for MAP, during time when MAP was below the 5th percentile-for-age (with units of mmHg-hours). This was calculated using np.trapz(), a function included in Python’s public NumPy package. This quantity was then normalized by recording duration, resulting in normalized units of mmHg-hours per hour. This measurement incorporates both duration and magnitude of hypotension. For example, supposing a one-hour total recording duration, a one-point increase in hypotension burden could result from MAP below threshold by one mmHg for one hour (the entire recording duration), or any multiplicative equivalent (e.g., MAP two mmHg below threshold for thirty minutes, or MAP ten mmHg below threshold for six minutes).

  2. Hypotension duration (%): Percentage of total recording duration that the patient’s MAP was below the 5th percentile-for-age.(24)

  3. Hypotension magnitude (mmHg): Median difference between the patient’s MAP and 5th percentile-for-age, during time when patient’s MAP was below the 5th percentile-for-age.

The same calculations were performed using MAP thresholds at the 10th, 20th, 30th, 40th, and 50th percentiles for age.

Patient outcome

Clinical outcome was based on Pediatric Cerebral Performance Category (PCPC) score, a 6-point scale of global neurobehavioral function: (1) normal; (2) mild disability; (3) moderate disability; (4) severe disability; (5) coma or vegetative state; (6) death.27–31 Pre-arrest and hospital discharge PCPC scores were assigned by trained raters via medical record review and discussion with medical providers. PCPC determination occurred at hospital discharge or 30 days post-arrest, whichever occurred first.(32) Unfavorable neurologic outcome was defined as death or a change in PCPC ≥1 from pre-arrest that resulted in PCPC score of 3, 4, or 5.33

Statistical analysis

Demographic and cardiac arrest characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical and medians and IQRs for continuous variables. Comparisons between outcome groups were performed with Mann-Whitney tests for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables.

We compared hypotension burden, duration, and magnitude below the 5th percentile-for-age between neurologic outcome groups using Mann-Whitney tests. We performed multivariable logistic regression to determine the association between burden, duration, and magnitude of hypotension below the 5th percentile-for-age and unfavorable outcome, accounting for age, sex, and arrest location (IHCA vs OHCA). Analyses were repeated using MAP thresholds at the 10th, 20th, 30th, 40th, and 50th percentiles for age. We conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding patients who 1) were ultimately declared dead by neurologic criteria since MAP may be elevated secondary to refractory intracranial hypertension, and 2) had pre-arrest PCPC >1 since patients with chronic neurologic conditions may require different blood pressures to limit secondary brain injury after cardiac arrest.

Secondarily, we hypothesized that the relationship between hypotension burden and outcomes could vary according to arrest severity. To test this, we stratified patients based on initial post-arrest lactate as a surrogate for severity of initial arrest. Patients were grouped into terciles based on initial post-ROC lactate, and we repeated the multivariable regression analysis for each tercile.

Finally, we sought to characterize the impact of varying durations and magnitudes of hypotension on outcome. For each patient, we calculated the durations and magnitudes of all distinct episodes of hypotension below the percentile threshold. For each distinct episode of hypotension (characterized by a duration of x minutes with a MAP nadir y mmHg below threshold), we calculated f(x,y), the probability of unfavorable outcome across all patients of equal or greater severity. This was normalized by the probability of unfavorable outcome in patients experiencing zero hypotension burden, giving the relative risk of unfavorable outcome for a dose of hypotension at least x minutes long at y mmHg below threshold. These data were plotted as two-dimensional heatmaps.

All data processing, including filtering, integral computation, and statistical analysis, were performed using Python 3 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE).

Results

Population

Of 145 patients meeting inclusion criteria, five patients (3%) were excluded due to poor-quality ABP waveforms. The remaining 140 patients were analyzed (61% male, median age 4.4 [IQR: 0.9,12.2] years). 61% (75/140) had an OHCA, and 63% (88/140) had an unfavorable outcome (Table 1). Median time from ROC to availability of ABP data for analysis was 65 [0,103] minutes, with no difference between outcome groups (p=0.436). A median of 21 [7,24] hours of data per patient was analyzed. The median MAP percentile was the 60th [40th,90th] percentile for age, with no difference between unfavorable and favorable outcome groups (60th [35th,90th] vs 60th [50th,80th], p=0.921). There were no significant differences in sex, age, race, ethnicity, arrest duration, or arrest etiology between favorable and unfavorable outcome groups. Compared to patients with favorable outcome, patients with unfavorable outcome had longer CPR duration, higher initial lactate, and lower initial pH after ROC (Table 1 and Supplemental Table 1). Median vasoactive inotropic score34 was significantly greater for patients with unfavorable compared to favorable outcomes at 6 hours post-ROC (7.75 [0,20.1] vs 0 [0,6]; p=0.012), and at 24 hours post-ROC (4 [0,17.8] vs 0 [0,7.3]; p=0.040).

Hypotension Burden

Median hypotension burden below the 5th percentile-for-age was 0.01 [0,0.11] mmHg-hours per hour. Hypotension burden was greater for patients with unfavorable compared to favorable outcome (0.02 [0,0.27] vs 0 [0,0.02] mmHg-hours per hour; p<0.001, Table 2). Hypotension duration and magnitude were also greater for patients with unfavorable compared to favorable outcome (0.03[0,0.77] vs 0.71[0,5.01]%; p=0.003 and 0.16[0,1.99] vs 2[0,4.02]mmHg; p=0.001). Using a multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for age, sex, and arrest location, a one-point increase in hypotension burden was associated with 14.8-fold increased odds of unfavorable outcome (95% CI 1.1–200; p=0.042, Figure 2). Hypotension duration, but not hypotension magnitude, was significantly associated with increased odds of unfavorable outcome (duration: aOR 1.09 per 1% increase in hypotension duration, 95% CI 1.00–1.25; p=0.023 and magnitude: aOR 1.07 per 1 mmHg increase in hypotension magnitude, 95% CI 0.99–1.15; p=0.086).

Hypotension burden stratified by post-ROC lactate

Patients were stratified into terciles based on initial post-ROC lactate. Tercile cutoffs were <4.2 mmol/L for the first tercile (low lactate), 4.2–8.13 mmol/L for the middle tercile (intermediate lactate), and >8.13 mmol/L for the third tercile (high lactate). There were no differences in MAP percentile for age by lactate tercile (low: 70th [40th,90th]; intermediate: 60th [40th,80th]; high: 60th [30th,90th], p=0.875). Among patients with high lactate, greater burden of MAP below threshold was associated with increased odds of unfavorable outcome for MAP thresholds of the 5th, 10th, 20th, 30th, and 40th percentiles-for-age (Figure 3 and Supplemental Table 4). Among patients with low and intermediate lactate, greater hypotension burden was not associated with increased odds of unfavorable neurologic outcome.

3D visualization of duration and magnitude contributions to hypotension burden

Distinct episodes of hypotension with longer durations and larger magnitudes below a given percentile were associated with unfavorable outcome most strongly at the 5th percentile-for-age MAP threshold and persisted at all percentile thresholds (Figure 4). For example, at the 5th percentile-for-age, >5 minutes of hypotension was associated with a 20–30% increased risk of unfavorable outcome, which increased independently as duration or magnitude of hypotension increased. As percentile threshold increased, the duration and magnitude of hypotension required to achieve comparable relative risks increased.

Discussion

In this single-center, retrospective study, we used high-resolution continuous ABP data from arterial catheters to quantitatively define hypotension burden in the first 24 hours after pediatric cardiac arrest. We found that at the 5th percentile-for-age, hypotension burden, duration, and magnitude were all associated with unfavorable outcome, even after adjusting for age, sex, and arrest location. Burden of MAP below threshold was associated in a dose-dependent manner with unfavorable neurologic outcome at thresholds up to the 50th percentile-for-age. Finally, hypotension burden was most strongly associated with neurologic outcome in patients with the most severe arrest injury based on high serum lactate immediately after ROC, suggesting the potential benefits of targeting a higher MAP during post-cardiac arrest care are especially important with these most vulnerable patients.

Previous studies examining the association of post-arrest hypotension with patient outcomes have abstracted blood pressure measurements obtained from medical records and dichotomized them as above or below an age-adjusted threshold.16–23, 34 This approach fails to account for the magnitude, duration, or frequency of individual hypotension episodes. Furthermore, earlier data utilized blood pressure measurements obtained non-invasively; such measurements can be inaccurate, especially when patients are hypotensive.35–37 To our knowledge, this is the first pediatric study to analyze post-arrest outcomes as a function of high-resolution blood pressure data acquired from arterial lines, the recommended modality for hemodynamic assessment based on AHA post-arrest care guidelines.24 Our data acquisition strategy is advantageous because it allows for capturing transient hypotension that may have eluded the sparser, non-uniform, and bias-prone sampling of prior observational studies.

Our primary finding is a significant difference in median hypotension burden between patients with unfavorable vs. favorable neurologic outcome at the 5th percentile-for-age. It is highly clinically significant that only 0.02 mmHg-hours of hypotension per hour of recording time separates patients with unfavorable from favorable neurologic outcome. To illustrate, this corresponds to a patient having a MAP that is 5.8 mmHg below the 5th percentile-for-age for 5 minutes of a 24-hour recording period, or a MAP that is 28.8 mmHg below the 5th percentile MAP for age for only 1 minute of a 24-hour period. This provides the first strong evidence for the hypothesis that brief declines in blood pressure below the 5th percentile-for-age may have a clinically significant impact on outcome. Additionally, these brief episodes can compound such that the total cumulative amount of hypotension has a more profound effect on outcome. During these brief episodes, providers may opt to observe the patient to see if blood pressure normalizes spontaneously, whereas prompt intervention may reduce secondary brain injury and improve outcome. Clinical detection of these brief episodes and prompt treatment to improve outcomes depend on ensuring patients have arterial lines to measure blood pressure continuously and possess adequate training of hypotension recognition and timely treatment.

Interestingly, although both magnitude and duration are important contributors to hypotension burden, magnitude appears to have a greater impact on outcome. One explanation for this may be that in response to a change in MAP, several minutes are required to re-equilibrate cerebral blood flow. If blood pressure is labile during post-arrest care, autoregulation may not be able to maintain adequate cerebral blood flow to meet the brain’s metabolic demands.38

Hypotension burden was associated with neurologic outcome for patients with high lactate (>8.2mmol/L), but not intermediate or low lactate following ROC. Initial lactate level after ROC is a well-established predictor of neurologic outcome due to accumulation during the peri-arrest no-flow state.38–42 Thus, higher initial serum lactate concentrations may reflect a more severe primary hypoxic-ischemic brain injury. This may, in turn, result in vulnerability to secondary neurologic insult through cerebral hypoperfusion. Our data support the “two-hit” model previously proposed to explain post-arrest hypoxic-ischemic brain injury9 and provide evidence that the severity of the initial insult significantly impacts susceptibility to further injury. This is an important consideration for clinicians, who may benefit from incorporating arrest severity into their calculus when intervening on their patient’s hypotension.

Taken together, these results suggest that the blood pressure threshold for secondary neurologic injury from hypotension may be lower than current clinical practice recommendations, and that further optimization of post-arrest hemodynamics may confer additional benefit. Given that small amounts of hypotension below the 5th percentile are associated with unfavorable outcome, 5th percentile MAP may be an inappropriately low target. Emerging research suggests that higher targets may be more appropriate.18, 34 A recent secondary analysis of the ICU-RESUS trial found that SBP >10th and DBP > 50th percentile-for-age was associated with increased survival, which is concordant with our finding that increased hypotension burden below MAP thresholds from the 5th to 50th percentile are associated with unfavorable outcome.43

Our heatmap analysis suggests that episodes of hypotension greater than 5 minutes in length are associated with an increased risk of unfavorable outcome, which increases further as the hypotension increases in severity, either by duration or magnitude. This exploratory analysis reinforces the findings of our primary analysis in a more clinically accessible way, from which clinicians may intuit the individual contribution of a hypotensive episode to their patient’s outcome. Additionally, we found that patients with unfavorable outcomes not only had greater hypotension burdens but also had greater vasoactive ionotropic scores, indicating that clinicians administered vasopressors in patients with hypotension. We are unable to determine from these data whether giving more vasopressor medication to achieve higher blood pressure targets would improve neurologic outcomes.

Further prospective interventional studies are required to determine whether targeting MAP greater than the 5th percentile-for-age improves outcomes. Balancing metrics for these studies must include potential adverse effects of therapies required to achieve those higher thresholds. For example, high-dose vasopressor therapy may exacerbate tissue ischemia, cause arrhythmogenicity, and increased oxygen demand.44 A recent meta-analysis of adult studies found no difference in outcome between targeting high MAP and standard MAP in post-arrest care, though this study was designed and powered to only detect treatment effects of greater than 25%, considerably higher than the odds ratios seen in our results.45 It is likely that there is no one-size-fits-all approach to post-arrest hemodynamic measurement. The optimal blood pressure target after arrest likely depends on many factors, including arrest severity and patient physiology. Identification of personalized physiologic post-arrest targets is an important area of ongoing research.46–48

Limitations

Patients who did not require invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring were excluded for this study, potentially limiting generalizability. However, these patients typically have less severe cardiac arrests and blood pressure optimization may have less of an impact on outcome. While some patients had arterial lines in place at the time of arrest, other patients required placement after the arrest or after transport to the ICU. Thus, our analysis was unable to capture the hypotension burden between ROC and initiation of invasive blood pressure monitoring, a crucial window that may impact outcomes.18, 19, 22 Due to meticulous, pathway driven post-arrest care49, the prevalence of hypotension below 5th percentile in our cohort was below 1% of all recorded time, partially accounting for our wide confidence intervals at this threshold. Nonetheless, we were able to demonstrate significant findings even with such a small signal, reinforcing the association between hypotension below this threshold and unfavorable outcomes. The lack of demonstrable association of hypotension burden with unfavorable outcome in the low and mid lactate terciles may in part be due to inadequate power caused by three-way stratification. This was a retrospective analysis, so it is unknown whether the association between hypotensive burden and outcome is a reflection of severity of illness after cardiac arrest, or a modifiable contributor to secondary brain injury and outcome. Modifiable interventions on blood pressure, such as volume administration or vasopressor therapy, may additionally affect the association between hypotension burden and neurologic outcome. This study was limited to one academic center, which limits generalizability to other hospital populations.

Conclusions

Greater burden of MAP below thresholds from 5th to 50th percentile-for-age within 24 hours of ROC was associated with increased risk for unfavorable neurologic outcome following pediatric cardiac arrest. Optimal blood pressure targets to minimize secondary brain injury may vary based on arrest severity. Further research is needed to determine whether reducing burden of hypotension reduces secondary brain injury and improves neurologic outcome after cardiac arrest.

Key Points.

Question:

Is burden of hypotension measured from high-resolution arterial line data associated with unfavorable neurologic outcome following pediatric cardiac arrest?

Findings:

Hypotension burden, duration, and magnitude less than 5th percentile for age are associated with unfavorable neurologic outcome. Burden of mean arterial pressure less than up to 50th percentile for age is associated with increased odds of unfavorable neurologic outcome in a dose dependent manner.

Meaning:

Extremely small doses of hypotension burden below 5th percentile for age are associated with unfavorable neurologic outcome, which reinforces the need for close hemodynamic monitoring, blood pressure targets to prevent hypotension, and quick correction of hypotension when it occurs.
