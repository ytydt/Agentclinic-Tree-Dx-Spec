Clinical relevance and methodological approach for the assessment of drug–drug interactions in cancer patients: a position statement from the Italian Association of Medical Oncology (AIOM) and the Italian Society of Pharmacology (SIF)
Background
Drug–drug interactions (DDIs) are a critical challenge in managing cancer patients receiving polytherapy, often due to comorbid conditions. DDIs can arise through pharmacokinetic mechanisms, affecting drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion, or through pharmacodynamic interactions, altering drug target binding or leading to overlapping toxicities. These interactions can compromise treatment efficacy, exacerbate adverse events, and increase patient morbidity and mortality.
Methods
Effective DDI management requires a multidisciplinary approach that integrates oncologists and pharmacologists. Key factors influencing DDIs include the pharmacological properties of drugs, patient-specific clinical and genetic characteristics, and the timing of therapy exposure. Identifying and mitigating DDIs also relies on patients' adherence to treatment and their early reporting of adverse events.
Results
This article proposes a structured framework for DDI assessment in oncology, emphasizing actionable strategies tailored to the complexities of cancer care. The framework incorporates the evaluation of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions, identification of high-risk drug combinations, and the use of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) and pharmacogenetics to individualize treatment. Specific recommendations for managing QTc-prolonging interactions, CYP3A4-mediated metabolism, and P-glycoprotein (P-gp)-related transport issues are highlighted.
Conclusions
By fostering collaboration among health care professionals and leveraging advanced tools, the proposed approach aims to optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing DDI risks. The manuscript underscores the importance of tailored strategies in oncology, advocating for the integration of pharmacological insights into clinical practice to enhance patient safety and treatment efficacy.

Introduction
Polypharmacy frequently leads to drug–drug interactions (DDIs) in cancer patients, with an increase in incidence and severity with age and comorbidities. DDIs are particularly prevalent in older adults due to multimorbidity, necessitating multiple pharmacological treatments. Approximately 90% of elderly patients take more than five medications daily. In cancer treatment, the risk of DDIs is increased by the concurrent use of supportive medications such as antiemetics, anticonvulsants, analgesics, and corticosteroids.
Pharmacological DDIs can be categorized into two main types: pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD). PK DDIs occur when one drug affects the absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion of another drug. On the other hand, PD DDIs result from interactions at the drug target level, leading to additive, synergistic, or antagonistic effects, or determining an overlapping of adverse events (AEs). Both PK and PD DDIs can compromise drug efficacy and safety, potentially leading to reduced therapeutic effect or unexpected AEs. Such reactions can be severe and impose a significant burden on the health care system through increased hospitalizations, morbidity, and even mortality. Notably, ∼4% of cancer treatment-related deaths are attributed to serious AEs, with DDIs playing a significant role.
The high prevalence of polypharmacy in cancer patients introduces a unique set of challenges for oncologists and pharmacologists. Managing these patients is complicated by the intricate and often underestimated interplay of multiple medications, which frequently leads to DDIs. These interactions pose a significant challenge for clinicians, potentially compromising treatment efficacy and patient safety. Therefore, an integrated, multidisciplinary approach involving oncologists and pharmacologists is pivotal to address the complexity of DDIs in cancer patients, ensuring optimal therapeutic outcomes while minimizing risks.

Frequencies of DDIs in clinical practice
Approximately two-thirds of patients receiving pharmacological treatment for malignant diseases are at risk of DDIs, with exposure rates nearing 90% in certain populations or clinical settings. Severe DDIs account for ∼2% of hospital admissions and may contribute to 4% mortality. Despite their prevalence, most DDIs are not clinically relevant. Clinicians often experience ‘alert fatigue’ due to negligible warnings, leading to reduced responsiveness.
Retrospective analyses indicate that 16%-41% of cancer patients experience clinically relevant DDIs, with variability depending on study design (e.g. cohort versus cross-sectional studies) and clinical setting (intensive versus non-intensive care units). Emergency departments report higher DDI prevalence due to drugs with narrow therapeutic indices and organ impairments. A review of 1510 DDIs classified 56% as moderate, 11% requiring therapeutic modifications, and 2.6% necessitating avoidance of the drug combination.
The introduction of new oral anticancer agents has increased the complexity of DDI management due to variability in gastrointestinal absorption. A Korean study of over 5600 DDI cases found that targeted molecular therapies accounted for 63% of interactions, compared with cytotoxic agents (21.2%) and hormonal therapies (19.3%).
DDIs can also bias clinical trials, affecting the safety and efficacy of experimental drugs. In a retrospective analysis of a National Clinical Trials Network oncology trial, protocol-based screening identified DDIs in 10.2% of patients, but only 3.1% were clinically significant. Therefore, rigorous DDI surveillance is crucial to mitigate risks and improve outcomes in real-world practice.

Examples of common DDIs affecting various PK processes are reported in Tables 1 and 2. Many PK DDIs impact the absorption phase. Most orally administered drugs dissolve optimally at a gastric pH between 2.5 and 3. Treatments that alter gastric pH, such as antacids (e.g. proton pump inhibitors) or anticholinergics, can reduce the solubility, bioavailability, and efficacy of oral antitumor agents, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). To mitigate these DDIs, TKIs should be administered at specific times relative to acid-suppressive agents, depending on the drug and its duration of action.
Drug absorption can also be impaired by complex formation with metal ion in the digestive tract. For instance, tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, and penicillamine bind metal ions, forming poorly absorbed complexes. Concurrent use of antacids or iron supplements can significantly reduce the absorption of antibiotics. Several anticancer drugs are similarly affected by changes in pH, including palbociclib, cabozantinib, and pazopanib.
Drugs that alter gastric motility can also affect drug absorption. Agents that accelerate gastric emptying (e.g. metoclopramide, cisapride) reduce contact time with the gastric mucosa, decreasing absorption of drugs absorbed in the stomach. Conversely, drugs that slow gastric emptying (e.g. anticholinergics, narcotics, antacids) can enhance absorption in the stomach and reduce absorption in the small intestine.
Food intake can also influence the absorption of oral anticancer drugs. For example, elacestrant, palbociclib, olaparib, and cabozantinib show increased Cmax (maximum plasma concentration) and area under the curve (AUC) when administered with food, enhancing their bioavailability.

DDIs and drug metabolism
Alterations in drug metabolism due to DDIs are also common in clinical practice. Cytochrome P-450 (CYP) enzymes, primarily the CYP1, CYP2, and CYP3 families, handle about 70%-80% of drug phase I metabolism (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105119). DDIs affecting drug metabolism may also involve other enzymes (e.g. dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase, carboxylesterase) or transporters [e.g. P-glycoprotein (P-gp), ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters].
The interaction with CYP enzymes can occur through reversible inhibition, mechanism-based inactivation, or induction. Reversible inhibition includes competitive inhibition, where the inhibitor and substrate compete for the enzyme’s active site; noncompetitive inhibition, where the inhibitor binds to an allosteric site independent of substrate binding; and uncompetitive inhibition, where an inhibitor binds only to the enzyme–substrate complex. Mechanism-based inactivation forms a reactive species that irreversibly inactivates the enzyme, with activity restored only through de novo synthesis. Induction involves the up-regulation of enzyme synthesis through receptor-mediated mechanisms, increasing transcription, translation, and protein expression, thereby raising enzyme abundance in tissues.
Concomitant use of strong CYP inhibitors or inducers is generally not recommended. For example, co-administering CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g. clarithromycin, itraconazole, ketoconazole) with CYP3A4-metabolized drugs like CDK4/6 inhibitors can elevate plasma levels and increase the risk of AEs, such as QTc prolongation. In such cases, if avoidance is not possible, a dosage reduction of CDK4/6 inhibitors is advised. Conversely, CYP inducers (e.g. rifampicin) may reduce drug exposure and, potentially, efficacy, requiring a gradual dosage increase for drugs such as dasatinib.
Clinically relevant examples and targeted recommendations can be found in Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105119.

DDIs and drug transportation
Drug transporters also play a crucial role in DDIs. These proteins, expressed in organs like the intestine, liver, kidney, and brain, facilitate drug absorption, distribution, and excretion. Commonly implicated transporters include P-gp, breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), organic anion-transporting polypeptides (OATP1B1 and OATP1B3), organic anion transporters (OAT1 and OAT3), organic cation transporter 2 (OCT2), and multidrug and toxin extrusion proteins (MATE1 and MATE2K). Interference including induction or inhibition of transporters by other drugs can lead to altered drug distribution and clearance, affecting therapeutic outcomes and increasing the risk of adverse effects. Potential DDIs involving the ABC and solute carrier (SLC) transporter families also require consideration. For example, P-gp induction (e.g. rifampicin) and inhibition (e.g. quinidine) can lead to reduced therapeutic efficacy or increased adverse effects, especially for substrates with a narrow TI.
A pharmacological interaction that often raises concerns involves the interaction between direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and anticancer drugs. DOACs have different characteristics. Dabigatran etexilate is a P-gp substrate and is not metabolized by CYP3A4. Apixaban, an Xa factor inhibitor, is both a P-gp and CYP3A4 substrate, with ∼25% of its metabolism involving CYP3A4. Edoxaban, also an Xa factor inhibitor, is a P-gp substrate, but only a minimal amount (<4%) is metabolized by CYP3A4. Rivaroxaban, another Xa factor inhibitor, is both a P-gp and CYP3A4 substrate, with about 18% of its metabolism dependent on CYP3A4. Therefore, rivaroxaban and apixaban are more likely to experience significant DDIs with CYP3A4 inducers or inhibitors compared with dabigatran etexilate and edoxaban, which may require dose adjustments or careful monitoring, especially when combined with medications that affect P-gp or CYP3A4 activity (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105119).

DDIs and drug excretion
vDDIs affecting drug excretion often arise from changes in renal function induced by certain medications. Diuretics, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can reduce the glomerular filtration rate, decrease drug clearance, and increase plasma concentration. Additionally, competition for tubular transporters can further impact drug excretion. For instance, NSAIDs can inhibit methotrexate excretion by competing for organic anion transporters. Even minor reductions in renal elimination can significantly increase drug toxicity, especially in elderly patients with reduced glomerular filtration rates (GFRs).

PD DDIs
PD explores the relationship between the drug and its target, as well as the resulting biological effect. PD DDIs are categorized as additive, synergistic, and antagonistic. Additive DDIs occur when the combined effect of two co-administered drugs combined is greater than the effect of each drug when administered alone, but not greater than the sum of their individual effects. For example, co-administering clopidogrel and ibrutinib increases the risk of bleeding due to their combined impact on platelet function. Synergistic DDIs produce a combined effect greater than the sum of the individual effects. This is often used therapeutically, such as combining nivolumab and ipilimumab in metastatic melanoma to improve progression-free survival. Similarly, folinic acid enhances the effect of 5-fluorouracil by inhibiting thymidylate synthase, leading to greater cytotoxicity. Antagonistic DDIs occur when one drug reduces or negates another’s effect. For example, concomitant use of antibiotics that affect the flora of the gastrointestinal tract might interfere with the enterohepatic circulation of regorafenib and might decrease regorafenib absorption.
An important example of PD DDI is related to the concomitant administration of drugs that prolong QT interval, resulting in an additive or potentiating effect. Cancer patients are particularly susceptible to QTc prolongation due to the combined effects of anticancer therapies with QTc-prolonging potential and electrolyte imbalances caused by gastrointestinal AEs. Drug-induced QTc prolongation is clinically significant as it can lead to torsade de pointes (TdP), a potentially fatal polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. The absence of detailed information on QTc prolongation and TdP risk in drug labels complicates the implementation of appropriate safety measures during anticancer treatment.
Anticancer agents associated with a moderate to high risk of QTc prolongation include many traditional drugs, such as fluoropyrimidines, TKIs (likely due to their interaction with hERG K+ channels), including cabozantinib, imatinib, osimertinib, pazopanib, ribociclib, as well other agents, including abiraterone and rucaparib. Additionally, drugs frequently used by cancer patients, such as antiemetics (e.g. ondansetron, domperidone), antibiotics, antifungals, and some antidepressants, pose a moderate risk of QTc prolongation and potential DDIs.
Reduced efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) can occur when co-administered with antibiotics, corticosteroids, or proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). ICIs rely on restoring T-cell responses, which can be impaired by disruptions in gut microbiota balance or immune suppression. Recent data show that antibiotic use alters the gut microbiota, impacting ICI efficacy. Similarly, corticosteroids can suppress immune responses, and PPIs may affect gastric pH and microbiome, further influencing ICI efficacy. Concomitant use of these medications has been shown to modulate the tumor microenvironment, potentially affecting ICI outcomes.

The victim and the perpetrator

In the evaluation of DDIs, the terms ‘victim’ and ‘perpetrator’ drugs are commonly used. Perpetrators typically include inhibitors or inducers that significantly affect the activity of enzymes or transporters involved in the distribution, metabolism, or elimination of one or more therapeutic agents, referred to as the victims. The potency of a perpetrator is measured by changes in the AUC of the victim drug. Specifically, an increase in AUC by ≥5-fold, 2- to 5-fold, or 1.25- to 2-fold suggests a strong, moderate, or weak inhibition respectively, while a reduction in AUC by ≥80%, ≥50% to <80% and ≥20% to <50%, respectively, suggests a strong, moderate or weak induction (Figure 1).
DDIs involving protein inhibition occur when enzyme or transporter activity is reduced by a perpetrator, leading to clinically relevant interactions. The clinical effects of enzyme inhibition are typically observed more quickly (within a few days) compared to enzyme induction, with the duration of the effect depending on the perpetrator’s half-life. Reversible inhibition can occur via different mechanisms: (i) rapidly via competitive inhibition, where the perpetrator and victim compete for the same enzyme active site (e.g. azole antifungal agents on CYP3A4); (ii) slowly, via the formation of an intermediate complex (e.g. erythromycin on CYP3A4), and (iii) long-lasting via non-competitive inhibition, where the perpetrator binds to an allosteric site, altering enzyme activity (e.g. omeprazole on CYP2C19).
For an accurate interpretation of the clinical relevance of metabolic DDIs, it is crucial to consider all elimination pathways involved in drug clearance. The quality of the evidence supporting DDI predictions must also be considered. For drugs highly bound to plasma proteins, caution is necessary when interpreting in vitro CYP-450 induction or inhibition data, as the unbound fraction of the drug may be too low to affect the target enzyme’s activity. A metabolically relevant interaction is indicated when the 90% confidence intervals for the mean ratios of the AUC and/or the Cmax of the victim drug, in the presence of the perpetrator, fall outside the 80%-125% acceptance range for single-agent administration (90%-110% for drugs with a narrow TI).

Therapeutic index
Understanding the TI is essential for interpreting and preventing DDIs. In preclinical studies using animal models, the TI is defined as the ratio between the median lethal dose (LD50), which causes lethality in 50% of the test population, and the median effective dose (ED50), which produces the desired therapeutic effect in 50% of the animals. In clinical settings, the median toxic dose (TD50) is used instead of LD50, representing the dose that produces a specified toxic effect in 50% of the population.
DDIs involving drugs with a wide TI are generally safer, as the dose required to produce toxicity or lethality is significantly higher than the therapeutic dose. Conversely, drugs with narrow TIs are more likely to be involved in a clinically significant DDI when used in combination therapy. The related concept of the therapeutic window refers to the dosage range that provides efficacy without toxicity, making drugs with a narrow therapeutic window suitable candidates for therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM).
In oncology, drugs with a narrow therapeutic window are often deemed acceptable due to the life-threatening nature of cancer and limited therapeutic alternatives. However, when such drugs transition from the use in advanced-stage disease to earlier stages, such as adjuvant therapy, safety standards become more stringent.
For many cytotoxic agents, metabolites are responsible for both efficacy and toxicity, making parent drug concentrations unreliable for predicting TI due to variability in metabolism and genetic factors.
The diversity of oncology treatments, including chemotherapy, targeted therapies, hormonal agents, monoclonal antibodies, and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), adds complexity to DDI assessments. Each drug class has unique characteristics, necessitating an individualized approach that extends beyond the standard TI definition.

Covariates
A covariate is any individual-specific variable that may explain PK and/or PD inter-individual variability. Several covariates influencing drug PK, and consequently impacting DDIs, include anthropometric parameters (such as age, sex, ethnicity, weight, and body surface area), liver and kidney function, and pharmacogenetic parameters (genetic polymorphisms of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporter proteins).
The management of patients with cancer is inherently complex due to the wide range of therapeutic agents used to treat the disease, mitigate treatment-related AEs, and maintain the quality of life. The metabolism of each drug is influenced by multiple factors including genetics, age, liver and kidney function, diet, and DDIs.

Impact of liver and kidney function
Liver and renal functions are critical for drug metabolism and elimination, significantly influencing the pharmacokinetics of many agents. Numerous anticancer drugs are extensively metabolized in the liver into active or inactive metabolites, while renal clearance plays a key role in the elimination of these drugs and their metabolites. Impairments of either organ can lead to altered drug levels, increasing the risk of toxicity or therapeutic failure. Age-related organ impairment often complicate the management of elderly patients with cancer. This highlights the need for tailored dosing strategies in patients with compromised liver or kidney function.

Sex-related differences in pharmacokinetics
Sex-related differences in drug PK can also influence drug efficacy and safety. These differences manifest in drug absorption, distribution, and excretion. For example, women typically exhibit slower gastric motility and intestinal transit, which can affect the absorption and timing of orally administered drugs. Additionally, women generally have a higher body fat percentage and lower body water content, which can influence the volume of distribution of lipophilic drugs. With age, these sex-dependent differences may become more pronounced due to disproportionate increase in body fat among women. Renal drug excretion also differs by sex, with men generally exhibiting higher renal blood flow and GFRs than women. As a result, women tend to have slower clearance of renally excreted drugs, such as methotrexate.

Aging
Aging affects PK and PD due to age-related changes in organ function, comorbidities, and polypharmacy. These factors can alter drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, requiring careful consideration in older populations.

Pharmacogenetics
Genetics plays a crucial role in individual drug metabolism and response. Pharmacogenetics focuses on genetic variations affecting drug response, while pharmacogenomics explores the broader interplay between genetic factors and drug efficacy or toxicity. Understanding genetic polymorphisms affecting drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporter proteins is essential for identifying vulnerable patients, such as poor metabolizers or ultra-rapid metabolizer, and optimizing treatment.
Pre-emptive pharmacogenetic testing can identify patients at risk of AEs. Testing is supported by guidelines and regulatory agencies labeling for several drugs, providing recommendations for dosage adjustments based on genetic profiles. For example, azathioprine and mercaptopurine metabolism is influenced by TPMT and NUDT15 variants. Poor metabolizers of azathioprine should avoid the drug, while mercaptopurine dosage should be reduced to 10% of the standard dose. For belinostat, patients with UGT1A1∗28/∗28 (poor metabolizers) may experience higher systemic concentrations and increased AE risks, and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidelines recommend reducing the starting dose to 750 mg/m2 in poor metabolizers. Similarly, metabolism of capecitabine and fluorouracil is impaired by DPYD loss-of-function mutations, increasing the risk of severe, life-threatening toxicities. The FDA recommends withholding or discontinuing therapy in these patients, as no safe dosage has been established for poor metabolizers. Gefitinib, a CYP2D6 substrate, requires caution in poor metabolizers, as they may experience higher systemic concentrations and increased AEs. Erdafitinib, metabolized by CYP2C9, also requires monitoring in poor metabolizers due to increased systemic concentrations and a higher risk of AEs.
Plasma levels of belzutifan, metabolized by CYP2C19 and UGT2B17, may be higher with increased risk of AEs in poor metabolizers. Similarly, sacituzumab govitecan, if administered to patients with UGT1A1∗28/∗28 genotype (poor metabolizers), may be associated with an increased risk of neutropenia. The FDA recommends monitoring these patients, if the genotype is known, for AEs and adjusting treatment as needed.
For irinotecan, mutations in UGT1A1 increase the risk of neutropenia and severe diarrhea, with the FDA advising dosage adjustments and close monitoring for these patients.
Pharmacogenetic testing provides valuable information to guide clinical decision making. However, it should be considered as a supportive tool alongside clinical pharmacology assessment, ensuring that genetic data are used to optimize therapy and reduce adverse effects without replacing clinical judgment.

How to manage DDIs

TDM
Pharmacological laboratories play a crucial role in understanding and managing DDIs. TDM, which involves measuring the drug concentrations in body fluids using chromatographic or immunoassay techniques, holds particular significance in oncology, where the precise assessment of drug levels is crucial for ensuring treatment efficacy and safety.
When an anticancer drug acts as a perpetrator, and the victim cannot be changed, measuring variations in the victim’s plasma concentration helps maintain drug levels within the therapeutic range, allowing for timely adjustments to minimize toxicity risks. Drugs such as ceritinib, idelalisib, ribociclib, and tucatinib can inhibit CYP enzymes, posing toxicity risks when combined with CYP3A4 substrates with a narrow TI. For a drug to be suitable for TDM, specific criteria must be met, including (i) a narrow TI, (ii) a significant PK variability, (iii) evidence of a relationship between plasma concentrations and treatment outcomes, (iv) an established target concentration range, and (v) the availability of a validated and cost-effective drug assay. Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105119, summarizes some commonly used drugs in clinical practice that meet these criteria.
TDM typically measures the steady-state minimum concentrations (Cmin,ss or Ctrough) after four to five half-lives, requiring accurate information about the patient’s last dose intake and close coordination between the patient and clinician team. When executed correctly, TDM provides valuable data for tailoring drug doses based on individual PK parameters.
TDM can also prevent anticancer drugs from becoming victims of interactions. If the perpetrator drug cannot be changed, strict monitoring and dose adjustments may be needed. However, evidence remains insufficient to recommend routine TDM for most oncology drugs.

Pharmacogenetic analysis
Pharmacogenetic variants of drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters play a pivotal role in influencing treatment outcomes and toxicity. Remarkably, it has been reported that 95% of the population carries genetic alterations, that may warrant therapy adjustments based on international guidelines. While there is strong evidence for drug–gene interactions such as DPYD variants for fluoropyrimidines and UGT1A1 for irinotecan, data for other interactions remain limited due to the lack of prospective studies. The identification of genetic variants that alter enzymatic or transporter functionality can provide insight into unexpected AEs or treatment failure. Assessing variant alleles offers two significant advantages: (i) it explains drug exposure variability, which may also be corroborated by plasma concentrations identified through TDM; (ii) it creates an opportunity to pre-emptively address the risks associated with DDIs, as this evaluation can be conducted before treatment.
Pharmacogenetic assessments can identify patients with a genotype-predicted phenotype from ‘poor’ to ‘ultra-rapid’ metabolizers, allowing for personalized drug dosing strategies. For example, in a scenario where a DDI involves a perpetrator that inhibits drug metabolism and the patient is a poor metabolizer due to a genetic deficiency, the risk of a clinically relevant interaction strongly increases.
Schematic representation of four possible drug–drug–gene interaction (DDGI) scenarios involving drug metabolizing enzymes. Each panel illustrates the balance between genetic metabolic capacity (poor versus ultrarapid metabolizer) and the effect of a co-administered drug-metabolizing enzyme’s inhibitor or inducer. Top panels show additive effects: (left) reduced drug exposure in an ultrarapid metabolizer taking an inducer; (right) increased drug exposure in a poor metabolizer taking an inhibitor. Bottom panels show compensatory effects: (left) normalization of drug levels in an ultrarapid metabolizer taking an inhibitor; (right) partial restoration of metabolism in a poor metabolizer taking an inducer.
In some cases, a phenomenon known as ‘pheno-conversion’ may occur. This happens when the effects of perpetrators that inhibit or induce drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters (e.g. CYP enzymes or P-gp) counteract the genetic phenotype (Figure 2, Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105119). For instance, an enzyme inhibitor may not lead to an expected clinically significant DDI if the patient is an ultra-rapid metabolizer, as the inhibitory effect is offset by the patient’s baseline metabolic capacity. Examples of strong inducers include certain antiepileptics (carbamazepine, phenobarbital, phenytoin) and inhibitors include antifungals (posaconazole, voriconazole, ketoconazole), antivirals (ritonavir, telaprevir), and antibiotics (clarithromycin, erythromycin).

How to avoid and monitor risk for drug–drug interactions in oncology
Despite advances in medical knowledge and technology, DDIs often remain underestimated in clinical practice. The complex interplay between various medications, food and supplements, can lead to potential synergistic or antagonistic effects that often go unrecognized.

Patient education and empowerment
A key strategy for preventing DDIs lies in patient education. Raising awareness among patients about the risks associated with polypharmacy and non-adherence is essential. Patients play a crucial role in early detection and prevention of inappropriate treatments. Providing patients, families, and caregivers with appropriate tools and resources empowers them to take an active role in their health care. Encouraging open communication about all medications, including over-the-counter drugs and complementary therapies, is particularly important for patients with multiple conditions and complex treatment regimens.

Multidisciplinary teams of clinical pharmacologists and oncologists
Oncologists, as frontline health care providers in comprehensive cancer care, have a pivotal role in identifying and avoiding DDIs to ensure patient safety and treatment efficacy. Collecting data on DDIs from both clinical trials and real-world practice enhances understanding and supports the development of collaborative platforms involving institutions and pharmaceutical companies. Training physicians within multidisciplinary teams, where pharmacologists are integral members, further facilitates proactive DDI management. Effective communication among health care providers is fundamental in identifying and mitigating DDIs. The integration of a collaborative approach between oncologists and pharmacologists in clinical practice offers a proactive method for predicting and managing DDIs. These multiprofessional teams streamline DDI evaluations, allowing for professional assessments during patient visits.

Future perspectives
Future advancements are likely to incorporate artificial intelligence (AI) approaches to rapidly and concurrently evaluate a wide range of variables, such as age, liver and renal function, ethnicity, and concomitant medications. These AI-driven predictive models hold the potential for improving DDI detection. However, it is important to note that machine learning models rely on pre-supplied data, which may limit their accuracy in predicting new or rare interactions. Therefore, any AI-generated information should be validated within a multiprofessional assessment to enhance treatment success rates and reduce the risk of DDI-related AEs.

How to structure a DDI assessment report
Proposal of a work plan for the a priori evaluation of DDIs. DDI, drug–drug interaction; PD, pharmacodynamic; PK, pharmacokinetic.
A DDI assessment report should serve as a practical document to support clinicians in making informed decisions. The report should evaluate the clinical relevance and the probability risk of potential DDIs, both before they occur (a priori) and after they have occurred (ex post) (Figure 3). The clinical pharmacologist is the professional figure qualified and board-certified responsible for writing and legally signing the DDI evaluation report.
To prepare a reliable DDI report, the evaluation request from the oncologist should include comprehensive patient information, such as medical history, current treatments, organ function status, and smoking behavior.

Pre-treatment DDI assessment
In the pre-treatment phase, the DDI report should provide a summary of the PK and PD characteristics of the drugs taken by the patient, followed by an evaluation of their interactions, considering metabolic pathways, absorption changes, and the TI. The report should assess the quality of evidence (preclinical, clinical, or post-marketing), prescribing notes, and the therapeutic plan (dose, timing, and administration route). Key covariates like renal and hepatic function, sex, and pharmacogenetics should be included oer considered, especially in cases of moderate enzyme induction or inhibition. The report should propose mitigation measures and evaluate the risk-to-benefit ratio for continuing the current treatment. After review by a multidisciplinary team, the report should classify the DDI risk (high, medium, low) and the clinical relevance of potential AEs (e.g. life-threatening, serious, or minor). It should provide case-specific recommendations (contraindications, alternative therapies, dose adjustments, monitoring, pharmacogenetic testing, or TDM) with supporting references.

Post-event DDI assessment
In the post-event evaluation, the DDI report should detail the adverse event, concomitant treatments, and the event’s time course, noting if it occurred during initial treatment or ongoing therapy. The report should indicate if the event was observed during challenge, dechallenge, or rechallenge phases.
The Drug Interaction Probability Scale (DIPS) can help assess the likelihood of a causal relationship between the DDI and the adverse event. The DIPS consists of 10 questions answered with a ‘Yes’, ‘No’, or ‘Unknown/NA’, similar to the Naranjo ADR Probability Scale (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105119). Based on the score, the relationship is classified as highly probable (>8 points), probable (5-8), possible (2-4), or doubtful (<2). If a high or probable correlation is found, the clinical team should consider actions like drug discontinuation or a therapeutic switch.

Conclusions
DDIs are a significant yet often underestimated challenge in cancer care. The complexity of oncology treatments—including chemotherapy, biologics, and medications for comorbidities—creates a high risk for DDIs. These interactions can compromise efficacy, increase AEs, and disrupt drug metabolism, posing serious health risks.
A multidisciplinary approach is essential, combining clinical and pharmacological expertise. Pharmacologists play a key role in evaluating DDIs, interpreting PK and PD data, and recommending mitigation strategies. Their input helps clinicians navigate complex regimens and make informed decisions. Consulting prescribing information, databases, and scientific literature is crucial for identifying risks and developing management plans.
Regular follow-up of cases helps assess the DDI management team’s effectiveness in predicting and mitigating risks. Table 2 summarizes practical recommendations from the Italian Association of Medical Oncology and the Italian Society of Pharmacology for managing DDIs in oncology.
