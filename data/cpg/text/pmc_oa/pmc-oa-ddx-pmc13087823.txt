IAEA Coordinated Research Project on “A generic approach for the development of genetic sexing strains for Sterile Insect Technique applications”— an overview
Abstract
The sterile insect technique (SIT) plays an important role in environmentally sustainable pest management. Its effectiveness hinges on specialized genetic tools called genetic sexing strains (GSSs), which enable the production and release of sterile male insects while excluding females. A collaborative initiative by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), designated as D44003 “A generic approach for the development of genetic sexing strains for Sterile Insect Technique applications,” aimed to establish a universal framework for developing GSSs applicable to various pest species. This overview summarizes findings from 17 research studies, including laboratory models and field pests. The studies focused on enhancing genetic markers, genome editing, understanding sex determination, creating temperature‐sensitive lethal systems, refining radiation protocols, and improving strain components. Collectively, these efforts demonstrate that a versatile, species‐independent approach is both feasible and practical in real‐world pest control efforts. This progress paves the way for the broader adoption of the SIT, promising a significant advance in integrated pest management.

Introduction
Climate change and international trade are continuously changing how insect pests and disease vectors spread and affect our environment, agriculture, ecosystems, and public health (Hendrichs et al.,; Papadopoulos et al.,; Acosta et al.,). Although chemical insecticides are still commonly used, their heavy reliance raises serious concerns, including environmental pollution, health risks to people, and the rapid increase in resistance among pests (Pu & Chung,).
The Sterile Insect Technique (SIT) offers a promising, ecofriendly and sustainable alternative that targets species‐specifically. Many large‐scale programs have proven that SIT can reduce or even eradicate pest populations (Dyck et al.,; Hendrichs et al.,). The results are especially strong when only sterile males are released, a strategy that has been effective with species like the Mediterranean fruit fly (McInnis et al.,; Hendrichs et al.,; Rendón et al.,). In mosquitoes, releasing only males is also crucial because female mosquitoes are responsible for spreading diseases (Papathanos et al.,; Lutrat et al.,).
Ensuring that only males are released depends on specialized genetic systems called Genetic Sexing Strains (GSSs). Classic strains like VIENNA 7 and VIENNA 8 for the Mediterranean fruit fly have been fundamental in SIT efforts (Augustinos et al.,; Franz et al.,). However, these strains depend on specific genetic mutations that are hard to apply to other pests.
In response, the Coordinated Research Project (CRP) entitled “Generic approach for the development of genetic sexing strains for sterile insect technique (SIT) applications” of the Insect Pest Control Section of the Joint FAO/IAEA Centre of Nuclear Techniques in Food and Agriculture was initiated to develop a generic (neoclassical), modular framework for GSS development that is applicable across various pest species. This 5‐year project (2019–2024) included 24 research groups from 19 countries. This special issue synthesizes results from 17 studies that developed various tools and methods, including marker systems, genetic approaches for sex separation, genome resources, irradiation optimization, and molecular engineering of strain components, to enhance SIT and increase its applicability.

Marker systems: from classical to minimalist designs
The white pupae (wp) phenotype remains a highly reliable visual marker in the development of genetic sexing systems (GSS). Using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, researchers have successfully created wp mutants in Bactrocera dorsalis, B. correcta, and B. oleae, with complementation experiments confirming that wild‐type alleles can rescue the phenotype. This confirmed that the marker is reliable and effective for sexing purposes (Ioannidou et al.,). In Bactrocera zonata, an additional trait, white‐eye, has been successfully targeted, offering another marker that could be useful in the future (Nazarov et al.,).
Interest has also grown in developing more compact genetic constructs. In C. capitata, a minimal wp allele, comprising an intronless coding sequence and a short promoter, has shown effective function and efficient integration into sex chromosomes. This design supports strategies involving efficient transformation and integration into sex chromosomes, supporting modern, neo‐classical GSS developments (Prates et al.,).
Beyond pigmentation markers, transcriptome analyses of the Caribbean fruit fly Anastrepha suspensa have identified testes‐specific genes, such as wampa and Prosα6T, which are expressed exclusively in males throughout development. These genes hold promise as male‐specific promoters and as targets for fertility control methods in future GSS approaches (Handler et al.,).

Temperature‐sensitive lethality systems
Classical strains of medflies have long relied on temperature‐sensitive lethality as a sexing mechanism. Now, with advances in synthetic biology, researchers are expanding these methods to other species. Current approaches include destabilizing essential proteins, using intein‐mediated protein splicing, and implementing N‐degron‐based degradation, as discussed by Leung et al.. Additionally, the tsl locus in medflies has recently been identified as the highly conserved LysRS gene and used to engineer a temperature‐sensitive LysRS mutant and rescue in medflies, highlighting the usefulness of traditional mutations for future GSS developments (Aumann et al.,).
Progress in managing other pest species is promising. For instance, in the Queensland fruit fly, Bactrocera tryoni, a targeted mutation in the shibire gene (shibirets4 ) caused conditional paralysis and lethality at temperatures above 30°C. This demonstrates that tsl‐based strategies can be effectively applied beyond model organisms (Okada et al.,).

Engineering sex‐determination pathways
Manipulating sex‐determination cascades provides a direct route to sex separation. In Drosophila suzukii, the transformer gene (Dstra) was isolated and characterized, and tetracycline‐regulated expression in XY individuals produced feminized pseudo‐females with altered morphology, disrupted behavior, and abnormal reproductive organs (Yan et al.,). A related study in Bactrocera carambolae cloned tra, tra‐2, and dsx. RNA interference against tra in XX individuals yielded intersex or masculinized phenotypes, and dsx knockdown altered external sexual characteristics. These results show that manipulation of the sex‐determination pathway is feasible even in species without extensive genetic tools (Laohakieat et al.,).
In medfly, Dicer‐substrate siRNAs targeting tra and tra‐2 caused masculinization of XX embryos and reduced gene expression in ovaries following adult injections, pointing to a transient, non‐transgenic route to sex conversion (Volpe et al.,).
In Lepidoptera, mechanisms differ but offer comparable opportunities. Most species rely on interaction between the W‐linked Feminizer (Fem) and the Z‐linked Masculinizer (Masc). Fem‐derived piRNAs silence Masc and direct female development. Males develop in the absence of Fem. Notable exceptions such as Bicyclus anynana and Samia cynthia depend on zygosity or Z : A ratios, expanding options for sex‐based interventions in moth pests (Marec et al.,).

Modular and neoclassical strategies
One major outcome of the CRP is a modular strategy for GSS development that can be applied widely. Combining visible and temperature‐sensitive markers, such as wp and LysRS, with precise genome editing enables the construction of strains guided by practical criteria, including transformation efficiency, recombination stability, and regulatory feasibility (Petrucci et al.,). Compact endogenous elements, such as the minimal wp allele, improve integration, reduce sequence complexity, and favor stable placement on sex chromosomes—features that suit neoclassical designs (Prates et al.,).

Genomics as a foundation
Modern GSS development depends on robust genome resources. For Anastrepha fraterculus sp. 1, chromosome‐level assemblies for both sexes, including annotated sex chromosomes, now support marker discovery and the identification of sites for Y‐linked rescue constructs (Rivarola et al.,). In B. zonata, a chromosome‐level genome with Y‐linked scaffolds provides similar guidance for GSS design (Nazarov et al.,).
Where reference genomes are not yet available, computational pipelines can assist. R‐CQ and KAMY detect sex‐linked sequences from read depth and k‐mer signatures. KAMY performed well even without a polished reference genome and therefore extends these methods to non‐model tephritids (Rallis et al.,).

Male fertility disruption
Irradiation has long been used to sterilize males for SIT, but additional pathways are under study. In Anastrepha suspensa, the testes‐specific expression of wampa and Prosα6T across developmental stages suggests that these genes are candidates for conditional sterility systems that might help maintain mating performance (Handler et al.,).

Classical strains and irradiation optimization
Classical genetics continues to deliver operational strains. In A. obliqua, naturally occurring pupal‐color variants were used to develop new GSSs. Two lines (GSS‐22 and GSS‐354) were created through radiation‐ and EMS‐induced chromosomal translocations, with GSS‐22 showing greater stability and better suitability for mass rearing (Cárdenas‐Enríquez et al.,). Work on irradiation timing remains important: in C. capitata, males treated 6–24 h post‐eclosion induced higher sterility in wild females than pupae treated earlier, while retaining mating competitiveness. Small adjustments to standard procedures can therefore improve outcomes (Ramírez‐Santos et al.,).

Molecular tools and regulatory considerations
Recent advances in molecular biology continue to reshape options for SIT. Reviews describe progress in female‐specific lethality, precision‐guided SIT, and self‐limiting gene drives, with a clear focus on biosafety and reversibility (Davydova et al.,). Conditional expression systems, such as Tet‐on/off, Gal4/UAS, QF/QUAS, and light‐inducible platforms, offer additional control. Selection or counterselection schemes provide non‐conditional alternatives (Hempel & Tu,).
Several concepts have moved from theory to practice. In D. suzukii, tetracycline‐regulated Dstra constructs built with piggyBac include intronic regulatory elements and fluorescent markers that simplify screening and enable controlled sex conversion (Yan et al.,). Synthetic biology components, including destabilizing domains, transcriptional repressors, and intein‐based switches, provide refined regulation of selectable markers and may raise fewer biosafety concerns than gene drives (Leung et al.,).

Cross‐species framework
The CRP shows that genetic sexing can be approached through a generic, modular framework for developing GSS across diverse pest species. Combining compact visible markers, tsl systems, or regulators of sex determination, such as tra and dsx, allows the construction of strains that are transferable across species and regulatory‐compliant. Focusing on minimal constructs, genome engineering with small modifications and endogenous genes only, along with conditional expression, improves scalability for SIT programs (Ioannidou et al.,; Petrucci et al.,; Prates et al.,).

Future perspectives
Priorities include expanding the set of selectable markers and sexing systems, improving strain fitness and stability through targeted editing, and integrating automation in rearing and sorting through the use of robotics and machine learning. These steps will increase throughput and consistency in production facilities. A follow‐up CRP on “Advanced approaches for the development of genetic sexing strains for SIT applications” will build on the foundations laid here.

Conclusion
The IAEA CRP project “A generic approach for the development of genetic sexing strains for SIT applications” demonstrates that GSS development can be standardized across species. Advances in genomics, synthetic biology, genome editing, and classical genetics together provide a flexible, regulator‐ready base for expanding SIT to additional insect species such as invasive pests. The studies summarized here indicate a clear path toward efficient and environmentally sound control strategies for a wider range of species.
