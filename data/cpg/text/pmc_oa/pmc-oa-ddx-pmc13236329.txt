Evaluation of cystic neoplasms using EUS, MRI, and CT scan and treatment strategy
Background:
Cystic neoplasms, and pancreatic cystic lesions (PCLs) in general, are increasingly a practice dilemma in diagnosis with their increasing incidental discovery and indeterminate risk of malignancy. Characterization is needed to maximize patient outcomes, balancing the risks of overtreatment against the risks of missed malignancy. In this review, endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and computed tomography (CT) diagnostic performance, prognostic information, and clinical utility have been extensively reviewed in the imaging and treatment of cystic neoplasms, and most importantly pancreatic neoplasms.
Methods
We performed narrative review synthesis and comparative analysis of MRI, EUS, and CT technology, including technical principles, diagnostic accuracy, safety profiles, and therapeutic decision-making roles. Focus was given to guideline-directed management approaches, risk stratification factors like mural nodules, cyst size, and main pancreatic duct dilatation, and advancing technologies such as AI-enhanced imaging and biopsy.
Results:
EUS was better for mural nodule and small cystic lesion detection, particularly in combination with fine-needle aspiration (FNA). MRI offered better non-invasive morphologic description of cyst shape and communication with the duct, and CT was an acceptable first detection device, but less specific to identify mucinous vs non-mucinous lesions. Multimodal imaging approaches, i.e., combination of EUS and MRI/ MR cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), optimized diagnostic accuracy and informed individualized treatment plans. Guideline-based analysis identified variability among large societies (AGA, IAP, and ESGE) in surgical thresholds and surveillance guidelines and underscored the need to use individualized, evidence-based decision-making.
Conclusion:
There is each of a unique niche in the diagnosis and management of cystic neoplasm for MRI, EUS, and CT. A multimodal imaging plan customized to lesion and patient considerations provides maximum diagnostic and treatment planning. The use of new technologies such as AI-assisted image analysis and advanced biopsy could potentially enhance risk stratification and decrease nonessential treatment. Prospective validation of the new technologies as well as standardization of imaging-guided management algorithms will be needed to better optimize outcomes.

Introduction

HIGHLIGHTS
Imaging guides safe and accurate care for pancreatic cystic neoplasms.
EUS with FNA is highly sensitive for small lesions and mural nodules.
MRI clearly shows cyst shape and duct links without being invasive.
CT is useful for first detection but less precise for cyst type.
AI, radiomics, and new biopsy tools may boost future diagnosis.
Sac-like tumors known as cystic neoplasms grow inside the tissues of many organs. The most often occurring organs that produce cystic neoplasms are the ovaries, liver, and pancreas. Thanks in significant part to imaging advancements and the general population aging, pancreatic cystic lesions (PCLs) are being seen increasingly[]. Given their rising frequency – between 4 and 14% in the general population – characterizing and risk stratifying these cysts becomes crucial to guide treatment[]. Both benign lesions like pseudocysts and epithelial pancreatic cysts and malignant lesions are included in cystic pancreatic lesions. Ten percent of cystic lesions in the pancreas are rare, cystic neoplasms[]. Although cystic pancreatic neoplasms might mimic pancreatic pseudocysts, a definitive diagnosis is usually made possible by a comprehensive clinical history or association with test data (amylase or lipase). A primary cystic neoplasm has to be taken into account when the diagnosis of pancreatitis can be excluded or is clinically doubtful. These tumors have long been categorized as either cystadenomas or cystadenocarcinomas of the serous (microcystic) and mucinous (macrocystic) subtypes. Mucinous cyst neoplasms (MCN), intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMN), and solid pseudo papillary neoplasms (SPN) comprise cystic neoplasms in the pancreas. About 5 to 10% of the population suffers from cystic disorders of the liver[]. Though basic cysts are most prevalent, biliary cystic tumors (BCTs), more especially biliary cystadenoma (BCA) and cystadenocarcinoma (BCAC), can develop in a subset of people[]. Cystic neoplasms are classified according to the location of the lesion, cyst size, serous or mucinous character of the contents, and most-dedifferentiated epithelial alteration that is obvious at pathology[]. Furthermore, practically any pancreatic tumor, including the most frequent type, adenocarcinoma, can show as a cystic mass. Current guidelines for cyst shape evaluation call for endoscopic ultrasonic (EUS), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and computed tomography (CT) scans. EUS brings fine needle aspiration (FNA) of cyst fluid for cytology and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)[]. Improving current criteria and correctly estimating related risks depends on a better knowledge of investigative traits leading to malignancy. Current recommendations for assessing PCLs are based on less-than-ideal diagnostic methods, so either surgical over-treatment or missed early cancer detection is produced. Early separation of primary cystic neoplasms from adenocarcinomas and other pancreatic malignancies is crucial since asymptomatic microcystic adenomas do not call for surgical excision, while macrocystic cystadenomas or cystadenocarcinomas demand surgery. The Resection of PCLs should be pretty selective since pancreatic surgery usually has a 20–40% morbidity rate and an estimated 2% death rate[]. Thus, evaluating cysts and introducing appropriately timed treatments depend on research and using safe and effective diagnostic techniques with great precision. This article adheres to the TITAN 2025 Guidelines on AI use in research and reporting[].

Endoscopic ultrasound

Technique overview
Mini probes are passed through either specialist echoendoscopes or conventional endoscopes during EUS. Clinically, two basic forms of echoendoscopes are radial and curved arrays[]. Radial imaging generates a picture in a plane perpendicular to the direction of the echoendoscope employing a rotating 360° transducer. This image obtained is more generally interpretable than the images generated by curved arrays and is thus similar to that of cross-sectional computed tomography (CT). Apart from the radial echoendoscope, an echoendoscope using an electronic transverse array transducer produces a picture in the same axis but roughly 270°. Placed at the tip of an oblique viewing endoscope, linear devices produce sector images in a plane parallel to the direction of echoendoscope insertion. Although this latter kind of imaging is less understandable to the novice, using linear devices has the significant benefit of allowing action. For instance, since one can watch the whole length of the needle’s insertion, FNA and biopsy are typically done using a linear instrument. Furthermore, characteristics like pulse Doppler and color Doppler provide many linear echoendoscopes and the new electronic transverse array echoendoscopes[].
Standard echoendoscopes run on ultrasonic frequencies between 5 and 12 MHz[]. Although it has poor resolution, it permits tissue imaging up to 5–6 cm from the transducer. The creation of catheter ultrasonic probes – miniprobes – passed through the operative channel of conventional endoscopes marked a breakthrough in EUS. These miniprobes permit great resolution of structures within 1–2 cm of the transducer by using frequencies between 7.5 and 20 MHz[]. Examining the layers of the gastrointestinal wall is especially benefited by miniprobes. Using miniprobes also lets the operator utilize a conventional endoscope, which is simpler than the stiffer echoendoscopes. Imaging the biliary tree and the pancreas increasingly uses intraductal ultrasound (IDUS) using high-frequency catheter probes[].

Diagnostic accuracy and role in fine-needle aspiration
While EUS has raised the accuracy of local tumor staging, accurate differentiation of benign from malignant lesions remained challenging. This issue has been slightly addressed with the use of EUS-guided FNA (EUS-FNA). This technique has substantially enhanced EUS’s diagnostic capability and significantly increased the list of EUS indications. The several systems covered in this review emphasize the application of FNA. Usually employing linear array echoendoscopes, EUS-FNA is carried out since a real-time view of the entire needle length is possible. Furthermore, linear-array systems offer pulse and color Doppler, which helps to ensure FNA’s safety since one can check blood flow and prevent vessel damage[]. Although technically more difficult, carrying a greater complication risk, and is not advised, FNA is feasible with radial array systems[]. FNA is commonly performed using needles ranging in size from 22 to 25 gauge; a 19-gauge needle is available for certain indications. FNA guided by EUS utilizing a linear array has a complication rate of less than 1%[]. Including eighteen studies and a total of fourteen thousand patients, a meta-analysis of EUS-FNA for PCL diagnosis revealed a pooled sensitivity and specificity of cytological diagnostic ability for discriminating mucinous from non-mucinous cysts as 54% (95% CI: 49–59) and 93% (95% CI: 90–95), respectively[]. Using cyst fluid acquired by EUS-FNA, sensitivity for identifying mucinous cyst and malignant transformation was not especially strong. EUS-guided through-the-needle microforce biopsy has lately been shown to increase the pathology yields and exhibit more significant diagnostic potential than EUS-FNA[]. Further research of this method is necessary since diagnostic yields could be limited, given the possible patchy distribution of particular pathological features for differential diagnosis on the cyst wall. Using EUS-FNA for PCL analysis imposes various technical and safety constraints. If the PCL is small or if the cyst fluid is particularly viscous, it can be challenging to get enough of either. FNA itself runs some risk as well from side effects. A meta-analysis of 40 articles evaluating morbidity and mortality associated with EUS-FNA for PCL showed overall morbidity of 2.66% (95% CI: 1.84–3.62) and associated mortality of 0.19% (95% CI: 0.09–0.32) and also showed common post-procedure adverse events including pancreatitis (0.92%; 95% CI: 0.63–1.28), hemorrhage (0.69%; 95% CI: 0.42–1.02), pain (0.49%; 95% CI: 0.27-0.79), infection (0.44%; 95% CI: 0.27–0.66), desaturation (0.23%; 95% CI: 0.12–0.38), and perforation (0.21%; 95% CI: 0.11–0.36)[]. Although most trials omitted long-term follow-up data, the overall adverse event rate was low. Clinicians should be careful when sending a probable needle-tract when EUS-FNA is used to diagnose malignant PCL since cyst fluid may readily leak through the needle tract into the peritoneal cavity[].

Magnetic resonance imaging

Technique overview
For approximately four decades, PCL examinations have used MRI[]. Small initial studies showing the value of MRI in PCLs conducted with low-field magnet strength of 0.35 Tesla (T, unit of measurement for magnet strength) were done[]. Clinically, 1.5 and 3 T are the most often used magnet strengths nowadays. Though at the expense of more susceptibility to picture deterioration by metal and air, these systems have a better signal-to-noise ratio and higher spatial resolution imaging[]. Originally, 2D procedures were used, and more recently, 3D sequences have allowed near-isotropic image capture and the best image resolution. MR cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) sequences were added to MRI 30 years ago[]. Further developments also result in a reduction in scan time acquisition and the introduction of new sequences capable of supplying further information [e.g., diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)][]. According to a recent comment from the Society of Abdominal Radiology, MRIs with at least 1.5 T magnets fitted with phased-array coils should be used for PCL evaluation[]. Although MRI techniques vary between institutions, a conventional MRI protocol for the first assessment of PCLs should include: (1) T1-weighted images: useful in assessing fat or blood content and in assessing presence of enhancement when compared to the postcontrast images; (2) T1-weighted postcontrast image: obtained after gadolinium is administered, shows low/delayed enhancement with adenocarcinoma and generally early enhancement with neuroendocrine tumor; (3) T2-weighted images: obtained in assessing fluid containing structures; (4) MRCP: mostly relies on T2-weighted sequence and suppresses non-fluid signals from surrounding parenchyma to enhance ductal structure; (5) DWI: increasingly employed in PCLs, as it helps recognizing small mural nodules and early malignancy[].

MRCP
A highly T2-weighted MR sequence, MRCP shows the fluid contained pancreatic duct in a noninvasive way, therefore avoiding possible difficulties related to ERCP. MRCP is helpful for the diagnosis and characterization of cystic pancreatic masses as well as for the identification of abnormal ductal architecture and complications in the context of pancreatitis. Moreover, pancreatic adeno carcinoma can also be found and staged with the inclusion of gadolinium-enhanced sequences to MRCP as well as traditional T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences. With an emphasis on its application for directing treatment choices, this paper discusses MRCP regarding the evaluation of prevalent pancreatic disorders[].
With its natural higher soft tissue contrast and lower invasiveness – that is, absence of any radiation hazard and no need for an intravenous contrast agent – MRCP has several advantages over multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) for evaluating pancreatic cystic lesions. MRCP has been extensively applied to diagnose and assess the anatomic relationship of pancreatic cystic lesions. However, it may not be very useful for the representation of solid lesions or parenchymal lesions[].
Several studies have found MRCP equivalent to ERCP in diagnosing extrahepatic biliary and pancreatic ductal anomalies[]. MRCP has superseded ERCP for various indications at several institutions. Several studies have found that, in assessing IPMT, MRCP is more sensitive and efficient than ERCP[] . Although MRCP does not provide clear pathologic data to determine if a lesion is malignant, characteristics that point to malignancy have been noted. Usually, the main-duct type of IPMT reveals a less-favorable histology[]. The diagnosis of malignancy[] is aided by the sight of thick walls and mural nodules; detecting nodules in the cystic lesion is better performed with MRCP than ERCP. While ERCP-related morbidity and death are not minor, with possible complications including pancreatitis (3.9%), bleeding (1%), perforation (1%), sepsis (0.5%), and even death in up to 0.5% of patients, MRCP is noninvasive[]. Furthermore, hanging over us is the possibility of sedation-related problems. Using no ionizing radiation, MRCP is less dependent on the operator’s ability than ERCP and is less costly. MRCP (apart from occasional brief fasting at some centers) requires no preparation or exogenous contrast. Moreover, MRCP depicts the ductal diameter more precisely than ERCP; thus, contrast injection during ERCP could cause biliary ductal caliber to vary as much as 6 mm[], giving the wrong impression of ductal dilatation. While ERCP failure rates span 3 to 10%, MRC has a high success rate[]. ERCP failure could be the result of poor operator competence. Regarding duodenal and gastric blockage, periampullary diverticula, and in patients who have had an operational choledochoenteric or pancreaticoenteric anastomosis, ERCP may be technically unfeasible and fail in up to 20% of instances[]. MRCP lets one see a duct outside of an obstructive lesion. Together with conventional T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences, MRCP also enables complementary imaging. Thus, in most patients – especially in the 40 to 70% of those undergoing ERCP with normal findings[]. Considered the test of choice in all patients with failed or partial ERCP, MRCP seems to be the first choice for those suffering from pancreatic illness[]. MRCP has two key restrictions. First of all, MRCP presents no therapeutic choices at diagnosis, unlike ERCP. Such decisions include biopsy, endoscopic lithotomy, brush cytology, sphincterotomy, stricture dilatation, stent implantation, and pancreatic juice collecting. MRCP proponents counter that MRCP offers direction for these alternative therapies. Second, in cases when the exact delineation of the pancreatic side branches is required, the increased degree of spatial resolution attainable by ERCP could be of great relevance. Among the recently recommended less-invasive pancreatic surgeries – segmental pancreatic resections, cyst enucleation – management of the pancreatic duct and its ductules is essential to prevent postoperative pancreatic leaks causing fistula formation, abscess, and bleeding. Usually, ERCP, rather than MRCP, allows one to reach a more exact characterization of the pancreatic side branches[] The development of secretin-enhanced MRCP (sMRCP) could assist in offsetting the poor spatial resolution of traditional MRCP. MRCP still needs more technological improvements, though, if it is to reach a degree of spatial resolution matching ERCP’s[].

Advantages and limitations
With imaging alone, correct diagnosis of particular cyst types and determination of the degree of neoplasia remains a serious difficulty, notwithstanding developments in MRI technology. This results from overlapping, non-specific MRI results from the several PCLs, inter-observer variation in imaging interpretation, and difference in the type of MRI scanner employed. Another drawback of MRI is that its 30 to 45-minute test time exceeds that of other imaging modalities and could cause motion artifacts. These elements, together with the enclosed nature of the scanning equipment, scanning noise, and lying flat posture, help to cause claustrophobia, which affects 1–10% of all patients having MRI[]. Sometimes, open MRI scanners or anesthesia help to reduce claustrophobia. Nevertheless, despite these treatments, ~1% of patients need another imaging modality since claustro fear prevents them from using MRI[]. Patients with metallic implants – such as a pacemaker, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, or joint replacement – have various possible contraindications given the use of magnetic fields that need to be carefully reviewed before MRI[]. Recent technological advancements have produced MRI-compatible devices and helped to lower the use of ferromagnetic material inside many metallic implants. MRI for PCL evaluation should finally be postponed until medical optimization in very unwell patients, cannot lie flat, or cannot hold their breath.

Computed tomography

Technique overview
Spectral CT imaging rests on variations in the fundamental makeup of structures[]. Examining two separate energy spectra allows one to distinguish objects with a similar density but differing elemental composition depending on photon absorption. For example, this material-specific imaging enables selective visualization and iodine measurement delivered intravenously. However, only materials with a significant photoelectric effect – calcium, iodine, barium, and xenon – can be distinguished from other bodily tissues, including fat, with a modest photoelectric effect. The two usually utilized energy spectra feature a peak between 140 and 150 kVp (high energy spectrum) and between 70 and 100 kVp (low energy spectrum)[]. Two classes can be distinguished among all presently in-use clinical CT systems with spectral imaging: systems based on emission and detector counts[]. Emission-based systems exploit X-ray photons with varying energy spectra. Two separate X-ray tubes, one tube producing low energy and the other having high energy, or a single X-ray tube rapidly alternating between low and high energies, can be used here. A split beam with two distinct filters, e.g., tin and gold, during a 360° rotation will allow filtration into low- and high-energy spectra, generating various energy spectra with just one X-ray source[]. The basis of detector-based systems is their capacity to separate energies employing signal separation between the low-energy X-ray photons and the high-energy photons. A photon-counting or dual-layer energy-integrating detector with varying photon re-gassing in each layer – e.g., a yttrium-based scintillator – allows one to achieve this separation. The most often used and readily available clinical spectrum CT systems are in Europe[].

Role in initial detection and characterization of cystic neoplasms
With the evolution of 1-second and subsecond helical CTs, the principal imaging study for a spectrum of clinical symptoms and presentations has evolved as well. Given its growing use, it is not surprising that unintentional, serendipitous, or iatrogenic consequences will arise[]. CT is still the preferred imaging modality for both the initial detection and characterization of cystic pancreatic lesions, particularly the macrocytic ones, despite the progress of MRI in abdominal imaging. Therefore, it is important to find the helpful CT characteristics for differentiating benign cysts from premalignant and malignant cysts to eliminate non-indicated surgery, which carries the risk of removing pancreatic tissue and significant morbidity[].
Many elements contribute to the improvement of CT evaluation of pancreatic cystic lesions, including adjustment of contrast agent injection time and multiplanar imaging capture, hence enhancing detection. Curved planar reformations, volume rendering, maximum intensity projections, and minimum intensity projections are a few of recent MDCT techniques for pancreatic evaluation that have shown especially helpful in the assessment of pancreatic pathology[].
For patients with IPMN, a CT scan can show one or more cystic dilatations in the pancreas (branch type) or diffuse or segmental dilatation of the MPD (main duct type), either with or without a polypoidal lesion. Although duct dilatation is appreciated even in non-contrast CT scans, water distending the duodenal lumen will help to identify the projecting papilla. But if a cystic mass is communicated to the pancreatic duct, IPMN is established[].
With correct specific diagnosis in just 39% and accurate prediction of malignant potential in 61%, the newest CT equipment and procedures paired with skilled interpretation permitted. This is in line with the 25–60% range for CT diagnostics accuracy for cystic neoplasms recorded in the literature[]. Small unilocular lesions observed more often in asymptomatic patients are more likely to be treated nonoperatively, reducing the diagnostic accuracy of CT. Although there was only fair agreement on each case across radiologists (Kappa 0.28, 95% CI: 0.01–0.55, P = 0.05), accuracy rates were 60.4 and 62.5% for the two radiologists in the research by Xie et al (2020). Other research has documented poor inter-observer agreement on the genesis of cystic pancreatic lesions[]. Similar diagnostic accuracy rates for MRI and CT have also been found in recent investigations characterizing cystic pancreatic tumors as benign or malignant[].

Advantages and limitations
It is abundantly evident that CT offers little help in distinguishing serous from mucinous ne tumors. Reviewers were given this information and removed pseudocysts and other pancreatic pathologies readily confused with serous and mucinous neoplasms; nonetheless, they struggled greatly to differentiate between the two groups. Most serous neoplasms still require auxiliary tests such as biopsies to reach a precise diagnosis since the radiographic appearance of serous cystadenomas varies and overlaps in CT features with mucinous neoplasms calls for. This reveals notable variation in the imaging features of serous cystadenomas and mucinous neoplasms of the pancreas; hence, CT proves to be an insensitive technique for distinguishing these malignancies[].

Accuracy of diagnosis and prognosis based on imaging modalities
Imaging modalities’ influence on diagnosis and prognosis accuracy Cystic neoplasms can be malignant and show themselves in several ways[]. They provide a difficult diagnostic problem. Thus, appropriate patient care depends on the differentiation between benign and malignant cystic lesions[]. Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate three of the most well-known imaging modalities – EUS, MRI, and CT – in terms of their diagnostic accuracy and prognosis so that clinical decisions can be based on them[]. Researchers have repeatedly investigated the sensitivity and specificity of EUS, MRI, and CT in diagnosing cystic neoplasms[]. A study by Kim et al found that MRI and EUS were both equally good at correctly identifying lesions as cystic or solid. MRI had a sensitivity of 100% and an EUS specificity of 90.5%. The specificities ranged from 82.8 to 96.6%[]. According to another study, EUS was also better than CT and MRI in precisely identifying a cyst as a tumor. Even though MRI was more specific than EUS (86 vs 75%)[], A systematic study showed that MRI and CT were equally good at telling the difference between benign and malignant tumors (76 and 80%, respectively)[]. Finding a suitable treatment plan depends on differentiating benign from malignant cystic neoplasms. In identifying malignant pancreatic cystic lesions, MRI and CT have shown equivalent accuracy; MRI is more specific than EUS[]. EUS, especially in conjunction with FNA, has shown great sensitivity in identifying pancreatic cystic lesions; its specificity may be less than that of CT[]. Therefore, although EUS is useful for identifying malignancy, its specificity could be less than that of CT. Imaging modalities have prognostic significance since they allow one to find characteristics predictive of malignancy, thereby affecting patient outcomes[]. EUS performs well in identifying internal septations, mural nodules, solid masses, vascular invasion, and lymphatic metastases, which are signs of malignancy or aggressiveness in cystic pancreatic diseases. Assessing characteristics related to malignancy can direct treatment options, including cyst size, wall thickness, and the existence of solid components, which MRI and CT help to evaluate[]. Cystic neoplasms are associated with a higher risk of cancer due to certain imaging characteristics. On imaging, mucinous cystic neoplasms (MCNs) can show as a single, big cyst with many locules, a thick outer wall, septations, and enhancing intramural nodules[]. Malignancy has been mainly linked to mural nodules, wall augmentation, and calcifications. Risk categorization and management planning depend on the identification of these elements. Multiple imaging modalities assess cystic neoplasms and improve diagnosis accuracy[]. EUS, combined with MRI or CT, can reveal tissue type and form, helping you distinguish benign from malignant tumors. EUS and CT demonstrated comparable sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value, positive predictive value, and accuracy for all cyst types and sites, suggesting they can be used together[]. MRI/MRCP is also better than CT for finding internal septations, mural nodules, and many cysts, as well as for the connection to the central pancreatic duct. Thus, a multimodal imaging technique can offer a more accurate diagnosis and guide therapy plans[]. Correct diagnosis and prognosis for cystic neoplasms depend on efficient imaging techniques. Every EUS, MRI, and CT offers particular benefits and drawbacks for identifying and describing these lesions. Doctors must be aware of the accuracy of each imaging modality in cancer diagnosis and prediction as well as which imaging characteristics are connected with cancer if they are to make the best clinical decisions and assist patients to have the best potential results.

Impact of imaging on treatment planning

Surgical vs non-surgical management
Imaging findings are essential to the resection or non-resection stratification of intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs). Consistent with European evidence-based guidelines, resection is recommended for IPMNs with high-risk stigmata such as obstructive jaundice, mural nodule enhancement ≥5 mm, and MPD dilation ≥10 mm. Relative indications are cyst size ≥40 mm, MPD dilation 5–9.9 mm, cyst growth rate ≥5 mm/year, elevated serum CA 19-9 (>37 U/ml), and new-onset diabetes mellitus[].
The Fukuoka guidelines similarly recommend surgical resection for patients with main-duct IPMNs, MCNs, and branch-duct IPMNs (BD-IPMNs) exhibiting high-risk stigmata. Worrisome features, such as cysts ≥3 cm, thickened or enhancing cyst walls, non-enhancing mural nodules, and MPD dilation between 5–9 mm, warrant further evaluation with EUS and possible surgical consideration[]. On the other hand, the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) guidelines are more conservative in that they would only suggest resection by surgery in the event of a solid component with dilated pancreatic duct or in the presence of suspicious or proven cytology for malignancy[].
MCNs are most frequently found in women and localize to the pancreatic body or tail[]. Symptomatic MCNs, those with a size of ≥4 cm, or with high-risk features such as mural nodules or suspicious cytology are generally treated with surgical excision[]. MCNs <4 cm with bland features have been shown to have an extremely low risk for malignancy, so that more conservative treatment is feasible in some cases[]. Griffin et al reported that 10-year disease-specific survival was 79.6% with invasive carcinoma in resected MCN patients, highlighting the good prognosis even when malignancy is present[].
In summary, imaging modalities are central to the management of PCNs, providing critical information that influences treatment pathways, from surgical resection to surveillance and emerging non-surgical therapies.

Monitoring and surveillance of cystic neoplasms
MRCP and EUS are the preferred imaging modalities for surveillance due to their superior soft-tissue contrast and ability to visualize the changes over time[]. MRI/MRCP is non-invasive with good delineation of cyst morphology and ductal communication, while EUS provides high-resolution images and the ease of FNA for cyst fluid analysis. CT is utilized less due to radiation and lower sensitivity in visualizing certain cyst characteristics[].
Fukuoka Guidelines: In non-high-risk stigmata BD-IPMNs, surveillance intervals are determined by cyst size. Cysts <1 cm: MRI or CT every 6 months for 1 year and then every 2 years, if stable; 1–2 cm: yearly imaging for 2 years, then every 2 years; 2–3 cm: EUS within 3–6 months and then alternating MRI and EUS yearly; >3 cm: alternating MRI and EUS every 3–6 months.
American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Guidelines: Recommend MRI during the initial year, then 2 yearly for cysts <3 cm with no features concerning. Use EUS when there is change in cyst feature or the cyst >3 cm.
European Evidence-Based Guidelines: Suggest MRI or EUS at 6 months for cysts <1 cm, and every 2 years if stable; cysts 1–2 cm, annual imaging for 2 years, then every 2 years; cysts >2 cm, EUS at 3–6 months, then alternate MRI and EUS annually.
Surveillance methods are tailored in relation to features of cyst, patient status, and risk categorization. Some guidelines provide guidance[]:
EUS with FNA allows cyst fluid analysis, including CEA levels, amylase, cytology, and molecular markers, to assist in differentiating mucinous from non-mucinous cysts and risk estimation of malignancy. Surveillance needs to be individualized based on patient age, comorbidities, surgical risk, and patient preference. Shared decision-making is essential, especially when balancing the risks of surgery against the risk of malignant transformation. Good follow-up and compliance with surveillance protocols are critical to early malignancy detection and outcome enhancement.

Resection based on imaging characteristics
The existence of enhancing mural nodules (EMNs) within a cystic lesion is a definitive marker for malignancy. It has been reported that EMNs with a diameter greater than 5 mm are prognosticators of a high risk of malignancy in main-duct (MD) and mixed-type (MT) IPMNs. For instance, one study recognized EMNs >5 mm as an independent malignancy risk factor in MD- and MT-IPMNs with sensitivity of 93.5% and specificity of 52.6%[]. Furthermore, EUS has also been reported to be more sensitive than CT or MRI in the identification of mural nodules[]. EUS detected the enhancement of mural nodules in 6 among 82 patients that were not visible on CT, of which two were malignant[].
Cyst size is also a significant consideration when assessing malignancy risk. A multicenter series of 180 patients with BD-IPMNs without increased mural nodules on CT found that cyst diameter ≥30 mm was an independent malignancy risk factor with an odds ratio of 8.6 (P = 0.001)[]. MPD dilation is a typical imaging characteristic that has been well-documented to be associated with increased malignancy risk. The European evidence-based guidelines recommend surgical resection in IPMNs with MPD dilation ≥10 mm, in consideration of it as a high-risk stigma[]. Another study also revealed that an MPD diameter ≥5 mm was an independent risk factor for malignancy of “pure” BD-IPMNs without more mural nodules on CT, with an odds ratio of 4.1 (P = 0.01)[].
Dilation of the MPD is a well-documented imaging feature of increased risk for malignancy. The European evidence-based guidelines recommend surgical resection in the presence of IPMNs with ≥10 mm dilation of the MPD, and consider it as a high-risk stigma[]. Also, in one study it was shown that an MPD diameter ≥5 mm was an independent predictor of malignancy risk in “pure” BD-IPMNs without increased mural nodules on CT, with odds ratio 4.1 (P = 0.01)[].
The combination of imaging features, i.e., the presence of enhancing mural nodules and MPD dilation, strongly increases the risk of malignancy in pancreatic cystic neoplasms. In one study by Zhang et al, it was found that both mural nodules and MPD size are independent risk factors for the identification of malignant IPMNs[]. The combined model using MPD and mural nodule had enhanced predictive performance (AUC = 0.803) than either feature alone (AUC = 0.619 for mural nodule, 0.746 for MPD diameter)[]. This information supports the usefulness of evaluating a number of imaging features in combination to enhance predictive accuracy for malignancy in pancreatic cystic neoplasms.
Imaging characteristics like mural nodule enhancement, cyst size, and main pancreatic duct dilation are relevant in surgical planning for pancreatic cystic neoplasms. These characteristics, individually and collectively, are useful for risk stratification and management planning. Utilization of high-resolution imaging modalities like EUS and MRI enhances the detection and assessment of these characteristics, and clinical decisions are better informed.

Non-surgical management strategies and imaging
In cases where surgery is not emergently required, imaging allows for non-surgical treatment. EUS-guided FNA allows for the study of cyst fluid, which is useful in risk stratification. EUS-guided ablative therapies, such as ethanol lavage with paclitaxel injection, have also been studied for the treatment of selected cystic lesions, but their effectiveness needs more data[].

Imaging modality for optimal decision-making
EUS is high-resolution imaging, and it can closely assess cystic lesions, septations, and mural nodules. EUS was superior to CT and MRI, particularly for lesions with diameters less than 3 cm[]. EUS detected 80% of small lesions, whereas CT and MRI detected 26.7 and 53.8%, respectively. In detection of large lesions (≥3 cm), EUS was superior to MRI (80 vs 57.5%)[].
MRI, with or without MRCP, is not invasive and more superior in detailing cyst morphology and communication with the ducts. MRI is as appropriate for determining involvement of the duct but lesser in diagnostic accuracy compared to EUS, especially of small cysts[]. CT is, however, less appropriate in the case of small cystic lesion and between mucinous vs non-mucinous varieties. Diagnosis quality is suboptimal compared to EUS and MRI, especially of lesions <3 cm in diameter. Preoperative imaging is most valuable in the decision to obtain appropriate management. Studies have established that EUS alone or in combination with FNA significantly enhances PCN diagnosis accuracy compared to CT and MRI. In a study of 108 patients, the accuracy of EUS diagnosis was 81.6%, which was higher than that of CT (54.7%) and MRI (56.3%)[]. Increased accuracy will distinguish between malignant and benign lesions, thereby directing surgical treatment and reducing unwarranted resections.

Cost-effectiveness and accessibility
Cost and accessibility are factors in the selection of imaging modalities. While CT is most accessible and least costly, with lower diagnostic accuracy, it may lead to additional testing or inappropriate treatment. MRI offers more contrast of soft tissue but at higher cost and with lower availability in certain environments. EUS, though more costly and requires special training, offers better information, particularly when used in conjunction with FNA. A cost-effectiveness analysis concluded that the addition of contrast-enhanced EUS to the diagnostic workup in PCNs is cost-effective in that it increases diagnostic accuracy and reduces unnecessary procedures[].
Combining imaging modalities may enhance diagnostic sensitivity and treatment planning. For instance, integration of the application of EUS with MRI has the potential to provide complementary information – MRI offers a broad view of pancreatic anatomy, whereas EUS allows precise assessment of cystic lesions and facilitates FNA for cytologic analysis. This multimodal imaging has the potential to better characterize PCNs and guide management. In conclusion, although each modality has its limitations and advantages, EUS is particularly notable for its enhanced diagnostic accuracy, particularly for small cystic lesions. MRI is a useful non-invasive method for assessing ductal anatomy, and CT is still useful for upfront evaluation. A multimodal imaging approach, specific to the patient’s own clinical context, can maximize treatment planning and results in the management of pancreatic cystic neoplasms.

Current guidelines and recommendations
Multiple global organizations, such as the AGA, International Association of Pancreatology (IAP), and European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE), have issued guidelines regarding cystic neoplasms (CNs) management. Of these, IPMNs have been studied most. IAP Fukuoka recommendations consist of surgery for resection of IPMNs with documented high-risk features of malignancy such as obstructive jaundice, mural nodule ≥5 mm in diameter, or dilation of MPD ≥10 mm[]. Cyst diameter ≥3 cm with suspicious features, non-enhancement of the mural nodule, or MPD dilation 5–9 mm requires further investigation by EUS[]. AGA is conservative and recommends follow-up of cysts <3 cm without high-risk features, and MRI at 1 and 2 years for 5 years[]. ESGE advises, however, lifelong follow-up in asymptomatic patients and surgical excision for cysts ≥40 mm or suspicious findings[]. These are guidelines that are a consensus regarding the significance of mural nodule and ductal dilatation but differ with respect to surveillance duration and surgery threshold.
Mucinous cystic neoplasms (MCNs) are female predominant and commonly present in the body or tail of the pancreas. They are always recommended for surgical resection in all guidelines due to their malignant potential. Resection is recommended by the IAP and ESGE in MCNs greater than 40 mm or MCNs with symptoms or mural nodules[]. However, asymptomatic small MCNs without high-risk features can be monitored under AGA, but resection is indicated[]. Serous cystic neoplasms or SCNs, being of benign nature, are managed conservatively in the absence of symptoms, >4 cm, or when diagnosis is uncertain. SCNs are very rarely operated on and serial imaging can follow to assess growth[]. Solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPNs) are rare with malignant potential and therefore surgical removal is strongly advised regardless of symptomatology or lesion size. Biennial follow-up imaging for a minimum of 5 years postoperatively is recommended to diagnose recurrence[].
The newer imaging modalities brought the diagnostic potential of high-resolution modalities like MRI, MRCP, and EUS to the fore of initial evaluation and risk stratification. EUS, particularly with FNA, enhances the diagnostic yield by cytological and biochemical examination such as CEA and molecular markers[]. Enhanced EUS also enhances sensitivity for mural nodule identification and ductal disease. Besides this, analysis of cyst fluid on a molecular basis is currently in the early stages of being explored as an excellent way to further individualize risk stratification but is limited by cost and availability. Multimodal imaging with MRI and EUS are being increasingly recommended as a measure of trying to raise diagnostic certainty and guide treatment planning. While there may be some variance of guidelines, most concur that the patient should be managed on an individual basis with respect to malignancy risk to surgical morbidity, patient comorbidities, and life expectancy.

Future perspectives
The ongoing development of imaging technologies should improve the diagnosis and treatment of cystic neoplasms. One of the most intriguing advances is high-resolution micro-ultrasound, which offers higher spatial resolution than conventional EUS[] Contrast-enhanced ultrasonic waves (CEUS) also make it possible to tell the difference between benign and malignant cystic lesions by providing real-time vascular imaging. By making tissue contrast better and being able to see small changes in cyst content[] Spectral and dual-energy CT (DECT) are becoming more useful tools for better characterizing cystic lesions. Also, more advanced MRI techniques like MR elastography and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) are better at identifying lesions and figuring out who is at risk[]. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) transform medical imaging by automatically detecting and classifying cystic neoplasms[]. AI-assisted radiomics can get quantitative information from imaging data that humans cannot see. This leads to higher diagnostic accuracy[]. Deep learning models that have been trained on huge amounts of data have shown promise in predicting cancer in pancreatic cystic neoplasms. This can help doctors decide whether to monitor the tumors or perform surgery[]. Using AI algorithms along with computer-aided diagnosis systems also helps radiologists interpret imaging results more accurately and consistently. Imaging developments open the path for customized treatment plans catered to the particular patient[]. Through-the-needle microforceps biopsy during EUS is one of the imaging-guided liquid biopsy methods under development for better molecular profiling of cystic lesions[]. Imaging biomarkers and genetic and proteomic analysis are likely to be used together in future studies to better group patients, which will improve treatment outcomes and cut down on procedures that are not needed. Theranostic imaging is also becoming more popular. This type of imaging can both diagnose and treat diseases. It is especially useful for guiding minimally invasive treatments for cystic neoplasms[] Combining several imaging modalities is expected to improve the accuracy of cystic neoplasm evaluation. Hybrid imaging techniques like PET/MRI and PET/CT give us better information about anatomy and metabolism, which helps in identifying the difference between benign and malignant cysts. Furthermore, the project is projected to improve preoperative planning and procedure direction through 3D imaging reconstruction and virtual endoscopy, thus improving patient outcomes[].

Conclusion
Cystic neoplasms present a significant diagnostic and therapeutic difficulty. EUS, MRI, and CT taken together have greatly enhanced the risk stratification, identification, and characterization of these lesions.
