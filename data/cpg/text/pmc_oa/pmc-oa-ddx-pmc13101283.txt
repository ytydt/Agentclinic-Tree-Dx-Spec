The economic imperative of artificial intelligence in maternal and neonatal health: a review of evaluation benefits, frameworks, challenges, future perspectives, and limitations
Integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into maternal and neonatal health (MNH) offers significant opportunities for enhancing patient care through advanced predictive modeling, early disease diagnosis, and ongoing monitoring of conditions such as preeclampsia or gestational diabetes. However, significant challenges in economic valuation persist, including data scarcity, complexity, and the nascent stage of AI implementation in clinical practice. There has been no consolidated empirical proof directly justifying widescale AI application in MNH so far, despite its potentially significant economic benefits and direct cost savings. This review demonstrates that AI systems can mitigate adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and enhance the operational efficiency of organizations. As the full economic potential has yet to be understood and quantified, this review examines several existing economic evaluation frameworks: Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA), Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA), Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA), and Budget Impact Analysis (BIA). A crucial gap exists between rapid technological advancements and robust economic evaluations, further compounded by a lack of standardized reporting frameworks that hinder the synthesis of available evidence. In addition, the review addresses key challenges, including how they affect the healthcare workforce and the economic impact of systemic errors and security breaches, and then discusses the clinical and liability risks posed by “black box” models. Furthermore, the frequent updates essential for the clinical efficacy and safety of AI tools in MNH are often tied to subscription-based models, creating significant financial strain, particularly in low and middle-income-countries (LMICs). To bridge this crucial research gap and the absence of uniform reporting, this paper proposes the AI-MNH economic evaluation lifecycle and a tailored CHEERS checklist. This multi-phase framework is designed to guide comprehensive, long-term economic evaluations and the adoption of a consolidated, standardized approach to support evidence-based policymaking and sustainable resource allocation.

Introduction
The integration of AI into MNH offers transformative potential that can save lives by identifying and tracking serious conditions much earlier. To achieve Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1, which aims to reduce the maternal mortality rate, the strategic integration of AI to address maternal health disparities is essential for accelerating progress toward this global objective, particularly in LMICs. These applications leverage machine learning (ML) algorithms, natural language processing (NLP), and computer vision to enable preventive analytics, optimize administrative workflows, and enhance diagnostic accuracy. For example, ML models are trained on large datasets that include patient records along with vital signs and lab results have the potential to predict the risk of adverse MNH conditions such as preeclampsia. The systematic review conducted by Liu et al. (2024) demonstrates that ML models for preeclampsia prediction achieve high diagnostic accuracy through their AUC and sensitivity results; yet significant methodological heterogeneity remains a challenge for standardized economic appraisal across conditions such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, or preterm birth. The risk factors, patient progress, and clinical documentation requirements in healthcare settings can be automated through the application of NLP technology, which reduces administrative duties for healthcare workers. Lastly, computer vision algorithms utilize ultrasound images to identify fetal abnormalities while determining fetal growth and detecting potential placental complications achieving results comparable to clinician-based assessment. Expanding beyond imaging, the research of Francis et al. (2024) proves that ML technologies can analyze intrapartum cardiotocography (CTG) data to detect fetal oxygen deficiency, which helps reduce unnecessary emergency cesarean sections and their related surgical expenses.
Despite rapid technological advancement, AI development has not yet reached the economic assessment standard that organizations need for its effective implementation despite the fast advancement of technology. This review argues that while established economic evaluation frameworks Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA), Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA), Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA), and Budget Impact Analysis (BIA) provide a theoretical foundation, their application to dynamic AI interventions in MNH is underdeveloped and often insufficient. This methodological deficiency limits understanding of the true economic value of AI technologies in the MNH field. Therefore, this review critically synthesizes the nascent literature on the economic evaluation of AI in MNH to facilitate evidence-based policymaking and sustainable resource allocation, a need echoed in recent systematic reviews.
The unique contribution of this review is the proposal of the AI-MNH Economic Evaluation Lifecycle. This novel, multi-phase framework is designed to provide the methodological consistency and long-term justification necessary for evidence-based policy and sustainable resource allocation in this rapidly evolving field. By moving beyond a single-point-in-time analysis, the proposed Lifecycle addresses the dynamic, iterative nature of AI and the need for continuous economic justification, thereby transforming the assessment of AI from a descriptive exercise into a critical, evidence-based process.

Objectives & methodology of this review
The objectives of this review were to:
Critically synthesize the current evidence base regarding the potential economic benefits and costs of AI applications in MNH.
Analyze the limitations of traditional health economic evaluation frameworks (CEA, CUA, CBA, BIA) when applied to dynamic AI technologies, with reference to the overview of the evaluation benefits of these four methods provided in Supplementary Table 1.
Propose and detail the AI-MNH Economic Evaluation Lifecycle as a novel, multi-phase framework for long-term, comprehensive economic assessment.
To achieve these objectives, the methodology was structured to provide a transparent and rigorous approach to synthesizing the nascent literature on the economic evaluation of AI in MNH. As a policy-oriented narrative review, the core theme was identifying conceptual frameworks, empirical evidence gaps, and strategic recommendations rather than a quantitative systematic review.

Search strategy and scope
The initial search across PubMed, Scopus, IEEEXplore, and Google Scholar yielded a total of 184 records. After removing duplicates, 52 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 8 primary economic evaluation studies were identified for the evidence map, supported by 59 clinical and policy papers. The search strategy utilizes Boolean operators (AND, OR) to connect keywords from three main conceptual areas.
Technological Intervention: The Terms „Artificial Intelligence (AI)”, „Machine Learning”, „Deep Learning”, „Natural Language Processing (NLP)” and „Computer Vision” were used.
Relevant Terms: Terms employed included ‘Maternal Health’, ‘Neonatal Health’, ‘Obstetrics’, ‘Perinatal Care’ and ‘MNH’.
Economic Evaluation: For this purpose, terminology such as “Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA),” “Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA),” “Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA),” “Budget Impact Analysis (BIA),” and “Economic Evaluation” were employed.
The search strategy targeted studies published between 2016 and early 2026, emphasizing research from 2022 onward. The search was supplemented by a grey literature review of reports from international organizations and policy documents from high-income countries to capture strategic and implementation challenges.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The literature selection process involved a multi-stage screening of titles, abstracts, and full-text articles. Inclusion Criteria: Articles (studies or reviews) that were published in English and discussed the application of AI technologies specifically within the MNH domain or addressed the economic evaluation, cost, value, or implementation challenges of AI in MNH. Exclusion Criteria: Articles (studies or reviews) that were published in a language other than English or that discussed the application of AI technologies or digital transformation outside the MNH field were excluded. In addition, articles that were duplicates or abstracts for which the full text was inaccessible were excluded.

Data extraction and synthesis
The final synthesis employed a narrative analysis approach. This qualitative approach was adopted because the economic evaluation of AI in MNH is a nascent field, lacking the volume of homogeneous empirical data required for a quantitative systematic review. Data extraction focused on identifying: (1) existing economic evaluation frameworks applied to AI in MNH (2), reported direct and indirect potential economic benefits and costs, and (3) methodological barriers to robust economic valuation. The synthesis aimed to provide a conceptual and contextual overview that identified the strategic trajectory, detailed the current state of economic evidence, and presented a comprehensive analysis of opportunities and challenges. It is worth noting that, while eight primary studies were identified, only three [Chatzikou et al. Ginsberg et al., and Morrison et al., met the criteria for formal quality appraisal using the CHEERS 2022 checklist. The remaining five studies were classified as predictive modeling evidence and were excluded from the checklist as they focus on technical validation rather than providing complete economic evaluations (e.g., ICER or cost-utility metrics) (Supplementary Table 2).

Benefits of AI in MNH: a dual perspective
Strategic AI applications in healthcare facilitate direct cost savings and economic benefits alongside enhanced patient care. Such benefits are delivered by different applications, which improve diagnostic accuracy, operational efficiency, and preventive care, thus fostering a sustainable and effective healthcare ecosystem. In this section, will investigate the major avenues through which AI presents direct opportunities for cost savings.

Reducing adverse drug reactions and associated treatment costs
AI-based systems offer a significant opportunity to reduce the incidence of ADRs within MNH settings. A growing body of research indicates that AI technology in both pharmacovigilance and personalized medicine can bring benefits to the MNH field. Liu et al. (2022) and Li et al. (2024) present their research on AI-based pharmacovigilance systems and their optimized therapies, which can enhance drug safety in resource-poor MNH environments. Beyond pharmacological safety, AI demonstrates its ability to predict urgent obstetric emergencies, which are critical in resource-limited settings. Shah et al. (2024) developed ML algorithms for the specific purpose of forecasting postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) in a Kenyan population. These targeted AI tools demonstrate their capacity to help organizations efficiently manage resources in LMICs, which experience high maternal mortality rates from hemorrhages. Rueden et al. (2021) established a solid research framework that utilizes deep learning (DL) to forecast ADRs during drug discovery, creating systems that match the specific biological characteristics of pregnant women. Such predictive ability allows clinicians to choose personalized treatment regimens according to a strong scientific rationale, potentially prevents adverse events that would otherwise require additional medical intervention, longer hospital stays, and exorbitant treatment costs. The consequent reduction of ADRs creates large savings in costs for drugs, emergency care, and complication management, all of which lead to a more cost-efficient and safer healthcare system.

Optimize resource allocation and operational efficiency
Healthcare institutions utilize AI-driven technologies to boost their operational performance while optimizing resource distribution, which results in major financial savings. The hospital can use predictive analytics to forecast patient demand and bed usage patterns and required medical services, which helps them allocate resources more effectively. The MNH initiative enables NICU discharge planning to improve which results in reduced NICU length of stay (LOS) through effective discharge planning. The technology also helps reduce emergency C-section procedures by enhancing risk assessment accuracy and minimizing unnecessary ultrasound examinations through AI-powered image quality assessment. These operational efficiencies lead to decreased waste while increasing staff and facility utilization resulting in major financial benefits for healthcare providers and their patients through lower copayments and reduced work absences. AI-based systems achieve this by handling repetitive administrative functions, include appointment scheduling and medical coding, as well as managing supply chain logistics for medical equipment and pharmaceuticals.

Impact on the healthcare workforce: a dual-edged sword
One key area of concern was the potential impact on the healthcare workforce. A scoping review by Kranz et al. (2023), documented the “dual effect” of AI on professional roles, especially for midwives and nurses. While AI could reduce administrative load and strengthen decision support, leading to increased efficiency, the potential for deskilling and the necessity for new educational frameworks represent significant, often unquantified, indirect economic costs.

Improve patient outcomes and quality of life
AI technologies support healthcare providers in delivering precise diagnostic assessments while developing customized treatment strategies that permit ongoing patient observation, ultimately improving health outcomes while decreasing chronic health complications. AI-powered diagnostic tools use advanced detection methods to identify disease progression signs that standard diagnostic techniques would overlook. This enables healthcare teams to take immediate action keeping patients in good health and maintains their overall well-being. AI systems create personalized care through their ability to analyze extensive patient information, helping to identify the best treatment options that improve patient outcomes while decreasing unnecessary medical expenses. A recent study protocol by Gali et al. featured the development of an “MNH eCohort,” a longitudinal mixed-mode survey that uses AI to track the quality of care from a woman’s first antenatal visit through the postpartum period. This study proposed filling data gaps by focusing on previously under-measured dimensions of MNH care quality, such as continuity of care and the patient’s user experience. The data generated from this eCohort may inform policymakers and healthcare systems on strategies to improve adherence to care standards, ultimately leading to better health outcomes and a higher quality of life for mothers and newborns.

Economic evaluation frameworks for AI in MNH
To achieve the potential benefits of AI as discussed earlier and to ensure responsible resource allocation, Robust health economic evaluations must accompany the clinical adoption of AI applications. While four frameworks provide the foundational methodology in medicine to facilitate decision-making, their direct transferability to AI in MNH is conditioned by unique domain-specific factors. MNH is characterized by a high baseline risk involving a dual patient focus (mother and neonate) and a critical, short time horizon (gestation and early infancy). This necessitates complex cost structures, where direct intervention costs must be weighed against catastrophic acute care costs (e.g., NICU stay) and long-term costs of mortality and morbidity (e.g., maternal T2DM, childhood disability). Therefore, the subsequent discussion emphasizes the necessary adaptation of these general principles to account for MNH’s high-stakes, dual-benefit economic profile, as later detailed in our proposed Lifecycle framework (Table 1).

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) of AI interventions
The potential synergy between CEA and AI applications was identified as useful for clinical outcomes, particularly where AI models can detect pregnant individuals at risk of complications. Building upon foundational insights into the economic value of traditional MNH interventions for example, Kangaroo Mother Care could be enhanced by integrating AI with wearable devices. A promising approach that may offer greater accuracy than traditional methods. Community-based newborn care packages with AI applications can greatly improve the speed, accuracy, and contextuality of WHO-CHOICE analyses. Methods based on AI could process and synthesize large and widely dispersed datasets. The central challenge is to empirically demonstrate that AI integration provides a superior Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) compared to optimized, non-AI traditional methods. Literature often fails to provide this critical economic comparison, a gap the proposed framework was designed to close.

Cost-utility analysis (CUA) of AI interventions
CUA in MNH addresses a recognized gap, as researchers have long called for comprehensive “value-for-money” assessments to appropriately guide resource allocation (Banke et al. 2017). More recently, studies such as Ginsberg et al. (2024) provide concrete examples, demonstrating how AI, specifically DL, can be integrated into CUA to evaluate interventions like the prenatal diagnosis of congenital cardiac diseases. These emerging applications, which utilize Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) as a key metric, clearly illustrate a significant transition in the field from general calls for economic evaluation to the publication of specific, technology-driven case studies.

Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) of AI interventions
Unlike other forms of evaluation, CBA converts all costs and health effects directly into financial terms. CBAs can assign monetary value to a wide range of benefits, including avoided productivity loss resulting from maternal morbidity or infant disability, and reduced costs for social services. Although there are currently few completed studies calculating benefit-cost ratio (BCR) specifically for AI interventions in MNH, the existing literature provides a robust methodological framework for conducting such analyses. For instance, a study from the HosmartAI project documented that AI-powered tools led to significant cost savings by reducing the severity of prematurity.

Budget impact analysis (BIA) of AI interventions
BIA measures the net financial impact on a given budget incurred by the technologies in the immediate to mid-term, usually one to five years. Direct Budget Impact Analyses (BIAs) for AI in the diagnosis and treatment of very low birth weight (VLBW) infants are still emerging, primarily due to the novelty of widespread AI implementation and the necessity for real-world budgetary data, but the predictive power of AI in this domain is well established and will form the basis of future analyses.

Challenges in economic evaluation and implementation of AI in MNH
AI introduces unique indirect costs. While human errors are usually localized, AI flaws can spread across an entire system, magnifying their impact.

Systemic and algorithmic failures and security breaches
Fundamental errors in an AI diagnostic tool could consistently misinterpret specific fetal ultrasound images, leading to delayed diagnoses, inappropriate treatments, and adverse outcomes for a significant patient cohort. The resultant costs are multi-faceted, encompassing re-diagnosis, corrective medical interventions, compensation for harm, and substantial reputational damage to the healthcare provider. Similarly, AI systems in healthcare are attractive targets for cyber-attacks. A security breach could compromise extensive volumes of sensitive patient data, disrupt critical healthcare services (e.g., disabling AI-powered monitoring during labor), or enable the malicious manipulation of AI algorithms to deliver incorrect care. The recovery from such breaches is inherently costly, involving forensic investigations, system restoration, patient notification expenses, and substantial legal ramifications. A recent review of AI-based security strategies for neonatal care highlighted the vulnerability of these systems to cyber-attacks, such as unauthorized access and data leakage, which could lead to diagnostic errors and compromise the well-being of susceptible newborns. The review highlights that AI systems in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) and other MNH settings are susceptible to computational errors in diagnoses and treatment plans. Given the interconnected nature of the modern healthcare system, such a breach in one corner of the network could halt patient care across multiple facilities. These costs, often overlooked in initial economic projections, are crucial for a comprehensive economic evaluation of AI systems in maternal and neonatal health.

Explainability and ethical challenges
The ‘black box’ nature of many AI models, combined with ethical dilemmas, imposes a significant and frequently overlooked economic burden. When a model’s decision-making process is not interpretable, it complicates efforts to identify the root cause of errors and assign liability, which can lead to prolonged investigations and diminished clinician trust. Furthermore, unaddressed issues like algorithmic bias can cause disproportionate health outcomes for certain demographic groups, increasing healthcare disparities and exposing developers to legal liability. Economically, these factors can translate to higher re-diagnosis rates, longer treatment times, and a reduction in public trust, all of which ultimately diminish the return on investment (ROI) for advanced AI technology. Therefore, ensuring sustainable AI integration requires addressing these ethical issues through a strong regulatory framework and transparent development processes, recognizing them as not just moral but also critical economic priorities.

Lack of standardized reporting
There is a need for more standardized reporting in health economic evaluations of AI, especially considering the unique aspects of MNH outcomes. A study by Yaseen et al. (2024) on the subject of AI for MNH echoed these concerns, noting that while AI can improve diagnostics and outcomes, its successful implementation may be hindered by ethical considerations, including algorithmic bias and the imperative for human oversight to maintain the centrality of the patient-clinician relationship. These real-world examples demonstrate that the true cost of AI in this domain involves a complex interplay of workforce considerations, ethical implications, and systemic readiness factors that are frequently overlooked in initial cost-benefit analyses.

Implementation; scalability and financial barriers
The review mentions infrastructural and developmental issues and upfront costs. However, a larger discussion is required regarding the economic challenges of implementation and long-term scalability across various healthcare settings. This includes training and upskilling healthcare workers to understand AI insights. Furthermore, the economic burden of integrating AI systems into existing, often fragmented, healthcare workflows and ensuring their interoperability with legacy systems is frequently underestimated. The Qatar model demonstrates that a centralized National Electronic Health Record (EHR) system acts as a critical ‘digital bedrock,’ allowing for the real-time data retrieval and cross-verification necessary to overcome these fragmentation barriers and support evidence-based AI deployment. Long-term sustainability comprises the ongoing costs related to data governance, model retraining, and continuous monitoring for performance degradation or bias. To ensure long-term ethical and economic sustainability, initiatives like the Qatar Genome Program (QGP) illustrate the importance of developing region-specific genomic databases; this ensures that AI models remain accurate for diverse populations, thereby reducing the long-term financial risks of algorithmic bias and diagnostic errors.
A more thorough exploration of these multifaceted implementation and scalability costs would provide a more realistic economic perspective on the widespread adoption of AI in MNH, especially emphasizing the unique challenges faced by LMICs, where such considerations are paramount for sustainable impact. Beyond these operational costs, significant financial barriers impede the widespread adoption of AI tools. The high costs associated with implementation, ongoing maintenance, and continuous updates pose a formidable challenge. As highlighted by recent research, the financial burden of AI adoption extends beyond the software itself to include necessary hardware, cloud computing services, and the specialized IT personnel required for implementation and management. This poses a particular financial strain in cases when the ROI is not immediately obvious or when there is no support from reimbursement structures. The scarcity of empirical evidence is not simply an oversight; rather, it is the result of serious methodological and systemic barriers that must be conscientiously examined. Future directions: an economic roadmap for AI in MNH
Building on the preceding analysis of economic evaluation frameworks, future directions for advancing AI in maternal and neonatal health must prioritize systemic readiness and long-term sustainability. The existing literature is rich with research that discusses challenges such as data heterogeneity, scarcity, and bias and presents solutions such as Federated Learning (FL) and XAI. However, there is a critical need for a consolidated framework that effectively links these technological solutions to economic realities.

Framework for future empirical studies on AI in MNH
To address the critical gap between technological advancements in AI for MNH and robust economic evaluations, a prescriptive framework is essential. Future research should move beyond descriptive reviews by adopting rigorous methodologies, such as quasi-experimental designs or randomized controlled trials (RCTs), to accurately isolate and quantify the economic impact of AI interventions. Secondly, process mapping and micro-costing should be employed. Detailed process mapping of the care pathway before and after AI integration can help identify specific points where AI influences costs and outcomes. Micro-costing, which involves itemizing and valuing all resources consumed by an intervention, can provide a granular understanding of the economic changes attributable to AI. Finally, hybrid models-developing hybrid economic models that combine elements of decision trees, Markov models, and discrete event simulation can allow for the dynamic modeling of complex care pathways and the explicit incorporation of AI’s probabilistic nature and its interaction with other system components.

Advancing evaluation and reporting standards
The existing body of economic evaluations of AI in healthcare, while valuable, often places primary emphasis on short-term cost savings and efficiency gains. To achieve a more holistic understanding of AI’s economic value and broader societal impacts, there is an imperative need for rigorous longitudinal analyses. This objective can be effectively addressed through the strategic adoption of standardized economic evaluation frameworks and clear reporting guidelines. The absence of clear reporting guidelines for the increasingly complex and interdisciplinary nature of AI interventions can significantly hinder the comparability, transparency, and generalizability of economic evaluation studies. A recent systematic literature review found that most studies evaluating AI utilized traditional methods to assess the impact on quality of life and present the cost per QALY. However, the most recent 2024 advancements in maternal risk prediction, including models for preeclampsia, fetal hypoxia, and PPH, primarily report technical predictive metrics such as AUC rather than formal economic outcomes. This reinforces the necessity of the proposed AI-MNH Lifecycle to bridge the gap between high-performing algorithms and evidence-based economic justification. Conversely, existing health economic reporting standards, such as the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS), offer a promising solution for achieving the necessary standardization. Specifically, key elements such as analytic perspective, methods for handling uncertainty (e.g., PSA), and the time horizon for cost capture are frequently omitted. To guide researchers in the field and ensure data transferability, this paper proposes an MNH-AI-specific CHEERS checklist (Supplementary Table 3), which tailors CHEERS requirements to the unique challenges of AI implementation costs and long-term maternal-neonatal outcomes.

Balancing performance with interpretability
Explainable AI (XAI) could be a potential solution to address the “black box” nature (as discussed in Sect.  “Explainability and ethical challenges”) by designing models that are not only accurate but also provide transparent and clear explanations for their outputs. This allows clinicians to comprehend and critically evaluate the reasoning behind an AI’s recommendations, thereby upholding the integrity of clinical judgment. While economic evaluations specifically focused on XAI in MNH are still emerging, recent studies have begun to address this need:
Postpartum Depression Prediction: Saqib et al. (2024) demonstrated the use of XAI to predict postpartum depression (PPD). By employing techniques like SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), LIME, and Anchor, the researchers provided transparent and comprehensible explanations for the model’s predictions. This enables healthcare providers to identify at-risk individuals earlier and implement more effective interventions.
IoMT and Risk Factor Explanation: Another study presented a comprehensive framework that integrates Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) architecture with an XAI-based DL model. This system provides continuous monitoring of a pregnant woman’s vital signs and offers interpretable explanations for identified risk factors. The transparent insights generated by this approach empower healthcare providers to make informed decisions and deploy timely interventions.

Addressing the research gap: a lifecycle framework for economic evaluation
Policymakers must establish clear regulatory pathways and suitable reimbursement mechanisms to encourage innovation while balancing safety and equity concerns. Hence, the current review proposes a framework for “AI-MNH Economic Evaluation Lifecycle,” a multi-phase approach to conducting comprehensive economic evaluations of AI interventions in MNH that is outlined in Fig. 1, while Table 3 provides a more detailed breakdown, demonstrating how each phase addresses the challenges identified. The Lifecycle aligns with the standard Health Technology Assessment (HTA).

Conclusion
While current literature highlights the potential benefits of AI in MNH, there remains a limited exploration of direct and indirect economic costs, implementation challenges, and ethical ramifications. This review, therefore, is a direct effort to address these critical research gaps by identifying and analyzing unaddressed economic burdens. These include the financial impact of AI bias, the costs of upskilling the MNH workforce, and the legal uncertainties of “black box” models. A comprehensive evaluation must also consider significant indirect costs and challenges, such as the economic repercussions of systemic errors, security breaches, and liability risks associated with AI models.
Therefore, a concerted effort is required to address economic, ethical, and practical considerations. Several key priorities in this regard are proposed in the review: first, conducting rigorous, long-term economic evaluations accounting for both short and long-term consequences; second, adopting standardized reporting guidelines for economic evaluations, such as the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) as proposed in Supplementary Tables 3, to improve future research comparability and quality; and third, promoting international cooperation so that economic evaluations can take into account different healthcare settings and resource limitations. To move past these challenges, a consolidated and prescriptive framework is essential. The proposed “AI-MNH Economic Evaluation Lifecycle” serves as this comprehensive roadmap, guiding future research from foundational design to transparent reporting. Ultimately, the success of AI in MNH depends on the collective ability to comprehend, assess, and optimize its economic impact, ensuring that technological advancements translate into equitable, sustainable, and high-quality care for all.
Limitations of the Review.
While this review provides a strategic roadmap for the economic evaluation of AI in MNH, several limitations must be acknowledged:
Scarcity of Empirical Data: A primary limitation is the nascent state of the field; while 184 records were initially screened, only eight primary economic evaluation studies were identified, and only three of these met the criteria for formal quality appraisal using the CHEERS checklist.
Emphasis on Technical Over Economic Metrics: A significant portion of current literature focuses on technical predictive performance (e.g., AUC, sensitivity) rather than providing complete economic metrics such as (ICER) or (CUA) results.
Methodological Heterogeneity: There is substantial variability in feature selection and study designs across the literature, which hinders the ability to conduct a quantitative meta-analysis or synthesize evidence into a single standardized economic appraisal.
Geographic and Contextual Gaps: Much of the current research is centered on high-income healthcare settings, meaning the specific financial, infrastructural, and regulatory barriers unique to Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) may not be fully represented in the existing evidence base.
Theoretical Constraints of Frameworks: As noted, traditional frameworks like CBA and CEA carry inherent limitations, such as the ethical complexities of assigning monetary value to health outcomes in maternal care and the difficulty of comparing disparate outcomes across the mother-neonate dyad.
Nature of the Review: As a policy-oriented narrative review, this study prioritizes identifying conceptual frameworks and strategic gaps over the exhaustive data synthesis typical of a quantitative systematic review.
