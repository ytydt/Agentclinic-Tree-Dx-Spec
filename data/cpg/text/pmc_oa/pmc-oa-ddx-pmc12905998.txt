Application of the Laennec approach in laparoscopic repeat liver resection
Laparoscopic repeat liver resection (LRLR) is widely recognized as an effective therapeutic option for recurrent liver disease but is challenged by extensive adhesions and altered anatomical structures resulting from previous surgeries. The Laennec approach, which leverages the unique intrahepatic membranous structure of Laennec's capsule, has emerged as a promising surgical strategy to address these technical issues. This literature review comprehensively summarizes the anatomical basis, technical evolution, clinical applications, advantages, limitations, and prospects of the Laennec approach in LRLR. By systematically organizing evidence from clinical studies, case reports, and anatomical research, this review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the value of the Laennec approach in overcoming the complexities of LRLR and to provide insights for its standardized application and further research.

Introduction

Current status and challenges of laparoscopic repeat liver resection
Laparoscopic repeat liver resection (LRLR) has become a cornerstone treatment for recurrent primary liver cancer, metastatic liver cancer, and certain recurrent benign liver diseases. Compared with traditional open surgery, LRLR retains the advantages of minimally invasive techniques, such as reduced abdominal wall trauma, shorter operative time, less intraoperative blood loss, and accelerated postoperative recovery, while achieving comparable long-term survival outcomes. However, the technical complexity and surgical risks of LRLR are significantly higher than those of primary laparoscopic liver resection, primarily owing to two core challenges: extensive postoperative adhesions and altered anatomical structures.
Surgical procedures often lead to dense adhesions in the abdominal cavity, particularly in the hepatic hilum and perihepatic regions. Perihilar adhesions caused by prior inflow control maneuvers (e.g., vascular clamping or the Pringle maneuver) can obscure key structures such as the Glissonian pedicles, increasing the risk of iatrogenic vascular or intestinal injury during dissection and significantly prolonging the operative time. Perihepatic adhesions originating from previous parenchymal transection surfaces further complicate surgical exposure and liver mobilization. In addition, previous surgeries may alter the liver morphology and vascular anatomy. Combined with inherent anatomical variations, these factors make it extremely difficult to identify target pedicles, control inflow, and assess future liver remnant function.
The evolution of precise minimally invasive anatomical liver resection has promoted the shift from traditional total hepatic inflow occlusion (Pringle maneuver) to selective regional inflow control. However, in cases with severe hepatic hilar fibrosis and adhesions, it is difficult to achieve clear exposure and meticulous dissection of the target pedicles using conventional surgical methods. The tedious process of adhesiolysis not only increases operative time but also increases the risk of uncontrolled bleeding and iatrogenic injury, which limits the safety and efficacy of LRLR. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a surgical strategy that can bypass adhesions and distorted anatomical regions, directly access stable intrahepatic anatomical planes, and improve the safety and feasibility of LRLR.

Overview of the laennec approach
The Laennec approach is based on the anatomical characteristics of Laennec's capsule, which is a distinct fibrous membranous structure covering the liver parenchyma. Unlike the traditional “pedicle-first” approach or adhesiolysis-focused strategies, the Laennec approach establishes a safe surgical pathway using the natural avascular space between Laennec's capsule and the underlying liver parenchyma, as well as the overlying intra- and extrahepatic vasculature. This approach enables surgeons to circumvent dense adhesions in the hepatic hilum, directly access target pedicles, achieve regional inflow control, and manage vascular variations through a standardized anatomical plane.
The Laennec approach has been validated in primary laparoscopic liver resection and has advantages in simplifying hilar dissection, reducing intraoperative blood loss, and improving the safety of anatomical resection. In recent years, with the deepening of anatomical understanding and the accumulation of clinical experience, the Laennec approach has been gradually applied to LRLR and shows potential value in addressing the core challenges of adhesions and anatomical variations. This review systematically collated and analyzed research on the Laennec approach in LRLR to comprehensively evaluate its technical characteristics, clinical outcomes, and application prospects.

Anatomical basis and technical evolution of the laennec approach

Anatomical characteristics of Laennec's capsule
In 1802, René Laennec first proposed the existence of a fibrous membranous structure distinct from Glisson's capsule that covers the liver parenchyma. However, the existence of Laennec's capsule remained controversial until it was confirmed through cadaveric dissection and histological studies in 2008. Subsequent morphological studies using Azan-Mallory stain further verified that Laennec's capsule is a continuous, independent fibrous membrane that envelops the entire liver parenchyma, Glissonian system, and hepatic veins, forming a natural avascular plane between itself and surrounding tissues.
Laennec's capsule is 20–50 ​μm thick, and its continuity and integrity provide a stable anatomical reference for surgical dissection. However, Laennec's capsule may be thinner or even absent near the terminal branches of hepatic veins, requiring careful blunt dissection and intraoperative hemostasis. This unique anatomical structure forms the foundation of the Laennec approach: surgeons can avoid injuring the liver parenchyma and vascular structures by following this natural avascular plane.

Technical evolution from the Gate Theory to the Pan-Gate Theory
The development of the Laennec approach is closely linked to the evolution of anatomical liver resection theories. In 2017, Sugioka et al. proposed using Laennec's capsule as a natural interface to simplify hilar dissection. In 2018, researchers further clarified the role of Laennec's capsule as a core anatomical landmark throughout the entire process of anatomical liver resection, including perihepatic mobilization, pedicle isolation, hepatic vein management, and retrohepatic dissection, and formally named this systematic anatomical pathway the Laennec approach.
The initial application of the Laennec approach was guided by the traditional Gate Theory, which identifies fixed anatomical “gates” (e.g., hepatic hilum or intersegmental fissures) as entry points to access Laennec's space. However, this theory has limitations in addressing congenital or acquired anatomical variations, such as displacement of the “gate” leading to the misidentification of target pedicles. To overcome this shortcoming, Yang et al. proposed the Pan-Gate Theory in 2025 as an expansion and refinement of the Gate Theory. The Pan-Gate Theory suggests that surgeons should abandon the idea of fixed anatomical entry points and instead dynamically identify the optimal surgical entry point along the “red-yellow” line on the liver surface with reference to the target pedicle. This flexible strategy allows surgeons to select the most accessible entry point based on individual patient anatomy, greatly improving adaptability to anatomical variations and enhancing the applicability of the Laennec approach in complex LRLR.

Clinical outcomes and advantages of the laennec approach in LRLR

Clinical evidence
Clinical evidence supporting the application of the Laennec approach in LRLR mainly comes from case reports, single-center retrospective studies, and a small number of multicenter cohort studies. A multicenter prospective cohort study showed that the prevalence of prior abdominal surgery was significantly higher in the Laennec approach group than in the conventional approach group. However, despite the more complex baseline conditions (more severe adhesions), the conversion rate and overall complication rate were comparable in the Laennec approach group and the conventional approach group, confirming the safety and feasibility of this approach in cases with complex adhesions.
Case reports have further verified the value of the Laennec approach in extreme scenarios. Burlaka et al. reported a case of colorectal cancer liver metastasis occurring 10 years after colectomy, with extensive adhesions in the hepatic hilum. The Laennec approach was used to successfully perform en bloc adhesiolysis and central hepatectomy (S4/5/8 ​+ ​middle hepatic vein), with intraoperative blood loss of only 350 ​mL (no transfusion required), discharge on postoperative day 5, and no recurrence during 18 months of follow-up. Zhang et al. applied the Laennec approach in a patient with chronic cholecystitis with Calot's triangle adhesions; the cystic plate was dissected along the space between Laennec's capsule and the gallbladder bed, resulting in less than 50 ​mL of blood loss and no bile leakage.
Our single-center preliminary study evaluated 21 patients who underwent LRLR using the Laennec approach from 2022 to June 20, 24. Among them, 20 cases were completed laparoscopically, while one was converted to open surgery due to extreme intraoperative difficulty (Iwate difficulty score: 7 points). The mean operative time was 126.8 ​± ​38.1 ​min, mean inflow occlusion time was 31.9 ​± ​17.6 ​min, and mean intraoperative blood loss was 66.8 ​mL (80% of cases had ≤55 ​mL of blood loss). The mean postoperative hospital stay was 6.2 ​± ​1.6 days, with no major complications or 90-day mortality. All cases achieved R0 resection and there was no recurrence in 1 year of follow-up. (The unpublished data from our center involved in this article have been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (NO.NFEC-2017-119))
Collectively, these clinical data provide compelling evidence supporting the safety, feasibility, and efficacy of the Laennec approach in LRLR. The consistent demonstration of reduced intraoperative blood loss, shorter hospital stays, and favorable oncological outcomes, even in the setting of complex adhesions and anatomical variations, highlights the potential of this approach to address the key challenges of LRLR.

Core advantages

Effective adhesion bypass and management
The main advantage of the Laennec approach over conventional Laparoscopic liver resection techniques is its ability to avoid tedious and high-risk adhesiolysis. By entering the avascular Laennec's space, which is not affected by previous surgeries, surgeons can bypass dense perihilar and perihepatic adhesions, treating the adhesive region as an “integral entity” without layer-by-layer dissection. This not only shortens the operative time but also significantly reduces the risk of vascular or intestinal injury associated with adhesiolysis. In clinical practice, this translates to a more efficient surgical procedure with fewer intraoperative complications, particularly in patients with a history of multiple abdominal surgeries or severe intra-abdominal adhesions.

Precise regional inflow control
Using Laennec's capsule as a stable anatomical landmark, the Laennec approach enables the skeletonization and mobilization of target pedicles, simplifying the implementation of regional inflow occlusion. The clear ischemic demarcation line that forms after inflow occlusion allows surgeons to directly verify the accuracy of target pedicle occlusion and ensure that the devascularized area covers the tumor boundaries. This is particularly valuable in performing LRLR in patients with altered anatomical structures, overcoming the difficulties associated with localization in distorted vascular territories when using conventional methods. This precise control not only minimizes intraoperative blood loss but also ensures that the resection margins are adequate, reducing the risk of local recurrence.

Enhanced safety in managing anatomical variations
Laennec's capsule envelops the entire Glissonian system and hepatic veins and forms an anatomical space that provides a consistent pathway for managing vascular variations. For pedicle variations (e.g., variant caudate lobe pedicle originating from the right pedicle), dissecting along Laennec's space can safely isolate the target pedicle while protecting adjacent major vessels. The Laennec approach enables the safe transection of hepatic vein variations (e.g., anomalous tributaries of segment VII draining into the right hepatic vein) to ensure R0 resection. In cases of hilar tumors invading the middle hepatic vein, the two-layered structure of Laennec's capsule can provide support for tumor dissection and reinforce the venous wall, avoiding intraoperative venous tearing. This enhanced ability to manage anatomical variations safely is a critical advantage in LRLR, where prior operations may have further complicated inherent anatomical variations, increasing the risk of intraoperative complications with conventional approaches.

Preservation of the liver parenchymal integrity
Compared with the conventional “pedicle-first” approach, which may cause parenchymal injury during pedicle dissection, the Laennec approach operates within the avascular plane of Laennec's capsule and thus minimizes iatrogenic injury to the liver parenchyma. This is particularly important in LRLR, as it is crucial to preserve residual liver function to reduce postoperative liver failure and improve prognosis. In patients with underlying liver disease or reduced liver reserve, preserving as much functional liver parenchyma as possible is essential for postoperative recovery. The Laennec approach's ability to achieve anatomical resection with minimal parenchymal injury makes it an ideal choice for these high-risk patients, as it balances the need for complete tumor resection with the preservation of liver function.

Limitations and challenges

Technical learning curve and surgeon expertise requirements
Laennec's capsule is extremely thin (20–50 ​μm), making its identification and dissection highly dependent on the surgeon's experience in liver surgery, knowledge of membranous anatomy, and laparoscopic skills. Thus, there is a steep learning curve in the initial application stage; inexperienced surgeons may find it difficult to accurately locate Laennec's space, leading to accidental entry into the liver parenchyma or vascular injury. Additionally, Laennec's capsule may be thin or absent near the terminal branches of hepatic veins, requiring more delicate surgical techniques to avoid uncontrolled bleeding. To overcome this limitation, the surgeon must receive training on hepatic anatomy concerning Laennec's capsule and laparoscopic techniques.

Limitations of existing evidence
Evidence supporting the use of the Laennec approach in LRLR is mostly derived from case reports and single-center retrospective studies with small sample sizes. Although a multicenter cohort study has been published, it lacks direct comparisons with conventional approaches. The quality of evidence is limited by selection bias and confounding factors, and the long-term oncological outcomes (e.g., recurrence-free survival and overall survival) of the Laennec approach in LRLR have not been fully verified. Furthermore, the anatomical basis of the Laennec approach in LRLR is largely extrapolated from primary liver resection research. Therefore, the specific anatomical changes in Laennec's capsule after previous surgeries (e.g., fibrosis or adhesion to surrounding tissues) have not been fully elucidated. To address these limitations, there is a pressing need for high-quality, prospective, multicenter comparative studies with large sample sizes to provide more robust evidence regarding the efficacy, safety, and long-term outcomes of the Laennec approach in LRLR.

Case selection restrictions
The Laennec approach is currently not recommended in certain complex cases. For example, one research team listed major vascular and biliary variations as exclusion criteria for laparoscopic left hemihepatectomy using the Laennec approach. This indicates that the adaptability of the Laennec approach to address extremely complex anatomical variations needs verification through the accumulation of clinical experience. Additionally, the feasibility of the Laennec approach may be affected by the integrity of Laennec's capsule, which may be compromised in patients with extensive liver fibrosis or cirrhosis. In such cases, the Laennec approach may not be feasible, and alternative surgical strategies may need to be considered. Furthermore, the size and location of the tumor may also impose limitations on the application of the Laennec approach. For example, very large tumors or tumors located in close proximity to major vascular structures may require modifications to the standard Laennec approach or may be better managed with conventional surgical techniques.

Dependence on auxiliary technologies
The successful implementation of the Laennec approach is dependent on auxiliary technologies such as preoperative 3D reconstruction, intraoperative ultrasound, and ICG fluorescence navigation. However, these technologies are not available in all medical institutions, limiting the widespread adoption of the Laennec approach, especially in resource-constrained settings. To address this limitation, efforts should be made to improve the accessibility of these technologies and to develop alternative methods for identifying Laennec's capsule and target pedicles that do not rely on advanced imaging modalities.

Prospects and research directions

High-quality clinical studies
Well-designed, prospective, multicenter, comparative studies with large sample sizes are needed to verify the advantages of the Laennec approach over conventional methods in terms of intraoperative blood loss, conversion rate, complication rate, postoperative recovery, and long-term oncological outcomes. In particular, propensity score-matched or randomized controlled trial designs reduce selection bias and provide higher-level evidence for clinical decision-making. Additionally, these studies should include comprehensive subgroup analyses to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the Laennec approach in different patient populations, such as those with varying degrees of liver function, tumor size and location, and number of prior abdominal surgeries. This will help identify the patients who are most likely to benefit from the Laennec approach and those for whom alternative strategies may be more appropriate.

Standardization of surgical procedures and training programs
It is crucial to establish standardized surgical procedures for the Laennec approach in LRLR to reduce technical variations and improve surgical consistency. This includes defining standardized entry points, dissection techniques, inflow control methods, and management strategies for special situations (e.g., adhesions involving major vessels, extreme anatomical variations). Standardization will not only improve the reproducibility of the approach but also enhance patient safety by ensuring that surgeons adhere to best practices. Additionally, the development of systematic training programs is essential to address the steep learning curve associated with the Laennec approach. These training programs should include anatomical workshops to enhance understanding of Laennec's capsule and its relationship to surrounding structures, virtual reality simulations to allow surgeons to practice the technique in a safe environment, and mentored surgical training with experienced surgeons who specialize in the Laennec approach.

Optimization of auxiliary technologies
Auxiliary technologies must be optimized to improve the accuracy and accessibility of the Laennec approach. Examples of these optimizations include developing more accurate preoperative 3D reconstruction software to simulate Laennec's space and adhesion distribution; improving intraoperative ultrasound resolution to enhance real-time visualization of Laennec's capsule; and exploring the application of artificial intelligence to identify anatomical landmarks to assist in precise dissection. Furthermore, efforts should be made to make these auxiliary technologies more affordable and accessible, particularly in resource-constrained settings, to ensure that the benefits of the Laennec approach are available to a wider patient population.

Basic and translational research
There is a need to strengthen basic research into the usefulness of the Laennec approach in LRLR by investigating the histological changes in Laennec's capsule after previous surgeries and exploring its biomechanical properties to provide a theoretical basis for optimizing surgical techniques. Additionally, translational research should focus on developing surgical instruments tailored to the Laennec approach (e.g., delicate dissecting forceps for thin membrane dissection) to improve surgical efficacy and safety. Translational research should also evaluate the potential of combining the Laennec approach with other innovative technologies, such as robot-assisted surgery, which offers enhanced dexterity and visualization, or microwave ablation, which can be used to treat small satellite lesions. These combinations may further expand the clinical applicability of the Laennec approach and improve patient outcomes.

Expansion of the application of the laennec approach
There is a need for investigation into the application of the Laennec approach in more complex LRLR scenarios, such as re-resection after multiple liver surgeries, LRLR for large tumors (≥5 ​cm), and LRLR in patients with cirrhosis or severe liver fibrosis. Additionally, the combination of the Laennec approach with other innovative techniques (e.g., robot-assisted surgery, microwave ablation) should be evaluated to further expand its clinical applicability.

Discussion
The integration of the Laennec approach into LRLR marks a paradigm shift in addressing the technical bottlenecks of recurrent liver disease treatment, with its core innovation rooted in the strategic utilization of anatomical naturalness. Unlike conventional surgical strategies that confront adhesions and anatomical distortions through forced dissection or compromise, the Laennec approach leverages the avascular plane of Laennec's capsule to “navigate around” rather than “break through” complex pathological anatomy, which aligns with the modern minimally invasive surgical concept of maximizing tissue preservation while minimizing iatrogenic injury. This anatomical advantage not only resolves the practical dilemma of difficult adhesiolysis and unclear pedicle identification in LRLR but also redefines the safety threshold for complex repeat liver resections, especially for patients with a history of multiple abdominal surgeries or severe hilar fibrosis.
From the perspective of clinical evidence translation, the current data on the Laennec approach in LRLR presents a “promising but preliminary” landscape. Although single-center studies and case reports have consistently demonstrated its advantages in reducing intraoperative blood loss, shortening hospital stays, and achieving R0 resection, the lack of large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and head-to-head comparisons with conventional approaches limits the generalization of these findings. Notably, the anatomical basis of the Laennec approach in LRLR remains extrapolated from primary liver resection research, and the potential changes in Laennec's capsule (such as fibrosis, thinning, or adhesion to surrounding tissues) after previous surgeries have not been fully clarified through histological or radiological studies. This knowledge gap raises critical questions: whether the integrity of Laennec's capsule varies among patients with different degrees of liver fibrosis or surgical histories, and how such variations affect the safety and effectiveness of the approach. Answering these questions requires cross-disciplinary collaboration between anatomical research and clinical practice to establish a more precise patient selection criteria.
In terms of clinical positioning, the Laennec approach should not be regarded as a universal replacement for conventional LRLR techniques but rather as a complementary strategy tailored to specific high-risk scenarios. For example, it shows obvious advantages in patients with dense hilar adhesions or complex vascular variations, but its feasibility and safety in patients with advanced cirrhosis, extensive liver fibrosis, or very large tumors (≥5 ​cm) still require further verification. Additionally, the long-term oncological outcomes of the Laennec approach, such as recurrence-free survival and overall survival, need to be confirmed by long-term follow-up data from multicenter studies. It is worth noting that the application of the Laennec approach also depends on the availability of medical resources. In resource-constrained settings where preoperative 3D reconstruction and intraoperative fluorescence navigation are not accessible, exploring simplified surgical pathways or alternative imaging guidance methods may be an important direction to expand the clinical applicability of the approach.
Looking forward, the development of the Laennec approach in LRLR lies in the integration of basic research, clinical innovation, and technological progress. Basic research should focus on clarifying the histological and biomechanical changes of Laennec's capsule under different pathological conditions, providing a theoretical basis for optimizing surgical techniques. Clinical research needs to prioritize large-scale, prospective, comparative studies to verify the long-term efficacy and safety of the approach, and establish evidence-based clinical practice guidelines. Technological innovation should focus on the development of specialized surgical instruments and intelligent navigation systems to reduce technical dependence and learning curves. Ultimately, the goal of the Laennec approach is not only to improve the technical success rate of LRLR but also to enhance the prognosis and quality of life of patients with recurrent liver diseases, making minimally invasive precision surgery more accessible to a broader patient population.

Conclusion
The Laennec approach is an innovative and valuable surgical strategy for LRLR, effectively addressing intra-abdominal adhesions and anatomical variations via the natural avascular plane of Laennec's capsule. Enhanced by the Pan-Gate Theory, it enables personalized, safe, and precise surgery, with clinical evidence supporting reduced intraoperative blood loss, minimized iatrogenic injury, and favorable short-term outcomes—particularly in high-risk patients with complex backgrounds. As a significant advancement in minimally invasive liver surgery, it redefines the safety and feasibility of LRLR for recurrent liver diseases. With continued technical refinement, high-level evidence accumulation, and expanded clinical accessibility, it is poised to become a cornerstone strategy for complex repeat liver resections, ultimately improving global patient outcomes.
