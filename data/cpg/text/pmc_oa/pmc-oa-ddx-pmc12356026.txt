Ultrasound-assisted extraction with deep eutectic solvents for valorization of agro-food waste: A sustainable and eco-friendly approach
Graphical abstract
The growing need for innovative, sustainable, and eco-friendly solutions worldwide has intensified efforts to valorize agro-food waste, which is generated in vast quantities worldwide and is rich in valuable substances with diverse industrial applications. Conventional extraction methods using organic solvents have long been employed for this purpose, but their significant drawbacks highlight the need for alternative approaches. This review explores the application of non-thermal ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) with deep eutectic solvents (DESs) for extracting valuable substances from agro-food waste. It critically examines recent innovations in the UAE with DESs, including mechanisms, applications to agro-food waste, limitations, and aspects of safety, sustainability, and scalability. The key findings indicate that UAE with DESs outperforms conventional and other novel extraction techniques for agro-food waste valorization. This approach offers significant benefits, such as enhanced extraction efficiency, high-quality extracts, reduced processing time and energy consumption, biodegradability, low toxicity, and the reusability and tunability of DESs, all of which align with eco-friendly practices. While identifying a single best DES is challenging due to their varying extraction efficiencies with different waste materials, but optimal ultrasonication parameters commonly reported in the literature include 300–400 W power, 30 min extraction time, 40 °C temperature, and a solid-to-solvent ratio (SSR) of 1:30 g/mL to achieve high extraction efficiency. Future studies should investigate a wider range of plant and animal-based waste, optimize ultrasonic parameters and DES compositions, explore combinative extraction methods, enhance compound purification and solvent recovery, and assess industrial scalability through TEA, LCA, safety testing, and regulatory alignment.

Introduction
Owing to increasing environmental concerns, the focus on sustainability and eco-friendly approaches across industries worldwide is growing.. Agro-food waste, in particular, is gaining attention as a valuable resource in the transition to a circular bioeconomy, due to growing global challenges, such as climate change, resource scarcity, environmental degradation, and the demand for sustainable economic systems. In 2023, the FAO reported that around 30 % or one-third of global food production is lost or wasted every year. Improper disposal of this waste presents significant environmental and economic challenges. Therefore, there is a pressing need for innovative waste management strategies aimed at halving agro-food waste along the supply chain, as outlined in the 2030 Agenda and Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 12.3). Initiatives such as the European Circular Economy Action Plan support these efforts by focusing on waste minimization, prevention, and the valorization to reduce resource depletion and promote sustainability.
Agro-food waste can be utilized in several ways, creating value through processes known as waste valorization. Waste valorization is the process of converting discarded materials into value-added food products, extracting valuable substances from them, developing packaging materials, producing bioenergy, composting, and creating animal feed. One of the most valuable ways of valorizing the immense amount of waste is the extraction of valuable substances from it, such as bioactive compounds (BC), nutrients, polymers, pigments, and other usable materials. These waste derived components have a lot of applications in different industries, especially agriculture, food, pharmaceutical and cosmetics. Various extraction techniques are employed based on the target material and the specific compound being extracted. The conventional extraction methods primarily rely on the solvent's ability, heat, and agitation for effective extraction. These methods have many drawbacks, but despite these limitations, they are still employed for extracting valuable substances from diverse sources.
Technological advancements have paved the way for eco-friendly, sustainable, green-label, safe, and cost-effective extraction methods as viable alternatives to conventional methods. Among these, one of the best method is ultrasound assisted extraction (UAE) using deep eutectic solvents (DESs), which aligns with the principles of an environmental friendly and sustainable extraction method with several advantages, including reduced extraction time, decreased solvent requirements, extraction of compounds in a purer form, high yields, and lower energy consumption. This technology utilizes ultrasound (US) waves with frequencies higher than those audible to humans (>20 kHz). When these waves are transmitted through a liquid medium, they generate compressions and rarefactions that create significant disturbance in the form of cavitation bubbles. The growth and collapse of bubbles are related to chemical and physical effects resulting into cellular disruption and enhancement of mass transfer. DESs being the most efficient and eco-friendly proved to be best for extraction purposes because their melting point is generally much lower than that of their individual components, owing to the formation of intramolecular hydrogen bonds. The advantages that make them promising include moderate processing conditions, biodegradability, cost-effectiveness, and less toxicity.
Although previous studies have investigated the use of UAE with DESs for extracting valuable substances from various natural sources, to the best of our knowledge, none has comprehensively assessed the potential of this technique specifically for the valorization of agro-food waste. We gathered a total of 75 recent articles published in last 5 years related to UAE with DESs for the recovery of valuable substances from agro-food waste. These articles were sourced from Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar. This review provides a comprehensive analysis of studies related to UAE with DESs for the extraction of valuable substances from agro-food waste. It explores the nature of DESs, including their properties, preparation methods, and the key factors influencing their extraction performance. The review highlights recent innovations in extraction techniques for agro-food waste valorization, with a specific focus on the mechanisms and applications of UAE with DESs, as well as the limitations of this approach. Furthermore, the safety, sustainability, and scalability of this method are critically evaluated. Future perspectives are outlined to guide further research in this emerging area. The insights presented have significant implications for the agriculture, food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetics industries, promoting the adoption of green labeling principles and advancing sustainable, cost-effective waste valorization strategies within a circular economy framework.

Deep eutectic solvents (DESs)
DESs, first introduced by Abbott in 2003, are a class of green solvents formed by mixing hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) and hydrogen bond donors (HBDs), resulting in a eutectic mixture with a significantly lower melting point than its individual components. They are used in extraction, catalysis, biorefining, biofuel production, nanomaterials synthesis, metal recovery, polymer recycling, and pharmaceutical applications. These solvents can be classified into four distinct types based on their components and applications, each offering a diverse range of chemical and physical properties. DESs are increasingly recognized for their diverse properties, making them highly suitable for a broad range of scientific and industrial applications. One notable benefit is their cost-effectiveness, as they are formulated from readily available and inexpensive components. Additionally, DESs provide significant safety benefits due to their low-toxic nature, offering a safer alternative to conventional volatile organic solvents, which can be harmful to human health and the environment. The physical properties of DESs significantly influence their applications, encompassing melting point, density, viscosity, conductivity, surface tension, refractive index, polarity, pH and hydrophobicity/hydrophilicity. DESs have lower melting points due to strong hydrogen bonding, enhancing safety and usability. They are typically denser than water and exhibit tunable viscosity, conductivity, surface tension, and polarity based on their HBD:HBA ratio and temperature. DESs are non-flammable, biodegradable, thermally and mechanically stable, and environmentally friendly with low volatility. Their high solvating capacity and ability to act as both solvent and catalyst make them versatile for various applications. DESs align with green chemistry by offering a safer, sustainable, and eco-friendly alternative to volatile, and toxic conventional solvents. Their low toxicity, biodegradability, thermal stability, and renewability reduce environmental risks and support waste minimization. This shift enhances safety, efficiency, and environmental responsibility across applications.

Preparation methods of deep eutectic solvents (DESs)
The preparation of DESs encompasses various techniques/methods, each designed to enhance the solvent's properties and suitability for specific applications. Understanding these preparation methods is essential for selecting the optimal approach to achieve a clear, homogeneous, and effective solvent. First method is mortar and pestle method that involves manually grinding solid components until a liquid phase forms, suitable for small-scale, and heat-free DESs preparation. Mixing with magnetic stirrer without heating involves stirring components at room temperature until a clear DESs forms, suitable for systems not requiring thermal activation.. Mixing with a magnetic stirrer along with heating is a widely used method that involves stirring and controlled heating to dissolve components and form a uniform DESs, especially when room temperature mixing is insufficient. This is the most commonly used method for preparing DESs, as evidenced by its application in nearly all studies. Freezing method involves freezing a premixed blend of components to promote DESs formation, ideal for systems benefiting from solidification or low-temperature processing. Vacuum evaporation method uses added water for easier mixing of viscous components, followed by rotary evaporation and desiccation to obtain a consistent, water-free DESs.

Factors affecting the extraction efficiency of deep eutectic solvents (DESs)

Viscosity
Viscosity is a critical factor in the DESs based extraction, particularly in the UAE, where high viscosity hampers cavitation, acoustic wave propagation, and mass transfer efficiency. This is due to strong hydrogen bonding, van der Waals, and electrostatic interactions between eutectic components. In the UAE, high-viscosity DESs resist cavitation bubble collapse and reduce microstreaming and shear forces, limiting cell disruption and bioactive release. They also form smaller, less active bubbles with reduced pressure differentials, prolonging extraction time. To address this, strategies like adding water (5–30 %) are employed to lower viscosity, although >50 % can disrupt DESs structure and performance. Temperature increase (e.g., 40–60 °C) further reduces viscosity by enhancing molecular mobility and weakening intermolecular forces. Osamede Airouyuwa et al. observed that sugar-based natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES) showed decreased viscosity with temperature rise due to expanded molar volumes and increased kinetic energy. Thus, low-viscosity DESs are generally preferred for efficient UAE. However, exceptions exist, in some cases, high-viscosity DESs yield better extraction, such as for flavonoids from citrus peels, indicating that viscosity, while crucial, is not the sole determinant of extraction efficiency.

Temperature
Temperature plays a pivotal role in DESs-based extractions, as it reduces the viscosity (by expansion in molar volumes) and enhances molecular mobility, resulting in accelerated extraction. However, temperatures exceeding 60 °C can cause the degradation of thermally sensitive target compounds, increase energy costs, and reduce yield. The different studies have shown that the optimal temperature range for DESs extraction generally falls between 25 °C and 60 °C. Rashid et al. reported that increasing the temperature from 20 to 40℃ enhanced the extraction efficiency of TPC and TFC from apple pomace by UAE with DESs. However, it decreased when the temperature increased further from 40 to 70 ℃.

Extraction time
The extraction time has a considerable impact on both the efficiency and the cost of the process. DESs based extractions generally should not be too long or too short and it mainly take between 20 min to 2 h, which is notably faster compared to conventional solvents. Energy-assisted techniques such as US and microwave can further reduce extraction times. In UAE, increasing extraction time enhances cell permeation, facilitating the release of the targeted substance. However, excessively prolonged cavitation can lead to the degradation of them. But the yield increases with time up to a certain limit, after which it starts to decline. This phenomenon can be explained using Fick’s second law of diffusion, which states that equilibrium between the matrix solid and the extractant is reached after a certain period. Therefore, optimizing this critical parameter is essential for maximizing extraction efficiency.

Sample-to-solvent ratio (SSR)
SSR significantly affects extraction yield and efficiency in UAE with DESs, as it determines solvent availability and system homogeneity. A low SSR (more sample, less solvent) increases viscosity and reduces cavitation and mass transfer, while a high SSR (less sample, more solvent) may cause dilution and inefficient energy distribution. Optimal SSR balances cavitation, solvent penetration, and mass transfer. Airouyuwa et al. showed that an increase of SSR from 1:20 to 1:30 g/mL increased the extraction efficiency but further increase did not show any effect on increase in extraction of BC. Similarly Rashid et al. reported maximum phenolic compound extraction from apple pomace at 1:30 g/mL. These findings suggest that an SSR around 1:30 g/mL is ideal for maximizing extraction without compromising efficiency.

pH, polarity and solubility
pH plays a crucial role in extraction as it influences the interaction between target solutes and solvents. The pH of the solvent significantly impacts extraction efficiency, for instance acidic DESs are more effective for extracting alkaloids, while amine-based DESs are preferable for flavonoids. Additionally, pH determines the chemical form in which target compounds are extracted, further affecting their solubility and yield. In Elgharbawy et al. research, UAE was employed with DESs for pectin extraction from pomelo peel and they confirmed the role of pH in extraction efficiency while extracting polysaccharides. DESs are gaining widespread recognition in current research due to their broad solubility range. BC exhibit significantly higher solubility, often several thousand times more in DESs compared to distilled water. Extraction efficiency is also influenced by the polarity of the solvent, with organic acid-based DESs being the most polar, followed by amide-based and then sugar-based DESs. Additionally, the polarity and efficiency of DESs can be further enhanced by adding water, which reduces viscosity and improves mass transfer.

Innovations in extraction methods and solvents for agro-food waste valorisation
Agro-food waste (crop residues, fruits and vegetables waste, dairy and meat processing waste, aquatic waste, oilseed and grain by-products) is rich in various valuable substances (proteins, lipids, organic acids, BCs, carbohydrates, fibers, vitamins, minerals, pigments, and polymers) making it a promising resource for sustainable utilization. These substances can be extracted from waste using variety of techniques. Solvent based conventional extraction methods are most widely used methods to extract these valuable substances from agro-food waste. These methods rely on the ability of specific solvents to dissolve and isolate target compounds efficiently. These solvents, primarily composed of hydrogen and carbon, are effective at dissolving a wide range of substances and often contain functional groups such as ketones, alcohols, and esters. Common examples include ethyl acetate, methanol, chloroform, toluene, hexane, acetone, benzene, dichloromethane, and petroleum ether.
The comparison of DESs with conventional organic solvents.
Conventional extraction techniques that rely on these conventional solvents, such as maceration, digestion, infusion, percolation, decoction, hydrodistillation, and Soxhlet extraction, have been extensively used in agro-food waste valorisation. In a comprehensive review by More et al., the authors have explained in detail about the conventional extraction methods using organic solvents for food waste valorisation. They stated that these techniques offer only a few advantages, such as low capital investment and simple procedures, but have numerous limitations (except water and some hydroalcoholic mixtures) pose significant issues such as high environmental impact, flammability, unstable nature, high-cost, limited selectivity, unpleasant smell, and often toxic nature (particularly halogenated or petroleum-based solvents) (Fig. 1). However, these properties represent general trends and are not absolute. The behavior of both conventional solvents and DESs can vary significantly depending on their specific chemical composition, the target compounds, extraction method, and processing conditions.
With advancements in technology, several new extraction methods have been introduced, including microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), which employs microwave radiation, ohmic heating where electric current is utilized, infrared extraction using infrared radiation, pressurized hot water extraction involving water at elevated temperatures and pressure, and induction heating extraction, which generates heat through electromagnetic induction. While these techniques can improve extraction yields, they also pose challenges such as thermal degradation of heat-sensitive compounds, limited penetration, high solvent usage, scalability issues, lack of specificity, and high energy consumption.
In recent years, innovative non thermal techniques have emerged for extracting valuable substances from agro-food waste, including UAE, enzyme-assisted extraction, pressurized liquid extraction, pulsed electric field extraction, subcritical water extraction, high voltage electrical discharge extraction, high pressure-assisted extraction, and supercritical fluid extraction. These non-thermal methods offer notable benefits, such as less solvent usage, protecting heat-sensitive compounds and enhancing extraction efficiency. In the article by Galanakis. the author compared traditional and novel extraction methods for recovering valuable substances from food waste. They outlined the step-by-step innovation process in extraction techniques, emphasizing the role of organic solvents in enhancing recovery efficiency across both approaches. However, despite these advancements, the organic solvents employed in conventional and novel processes still pose significant challenges.
In recent years, the increasing climate concern has spurred the search for greener alternatives to these solvents. As a response to the detrimental effects of conventional organic solvents, green solvents are those with enhanced biodegradability, reduced health risks, and minimal environmental impact, have been proposed as sustainable solutions. The most recent innovation, DESs represents a significant leap forward. These solvents are environmental friendly, biodegradable, cost-effective, and highly customizable for specific applications. Collectively, these advancements mark a major transition towards more sustainable and efficient extraction methodologies. The increasing number of studies utilizing DESs as extraction media is evident in recent years. For instance, in a study by Del-Castillo-Llamosas et al. the authors prepared a mixture of avocado seeds and DESs (ChCl:Glycerol) at a SSR of 1:15 g/mL. This mixture was subjected to an orbital shaker at a temperature range of 40–60 °C with a residence time of 60–80 min. The results demonstrated an enhancement in the extraction of phenolic compounds and carbohydrates, showcasing efficacy of DESs in improving the recovery process. Similarly, in another study by Silva et al., it was observed that the total phenolic content (TPC) and antioxidant activity of both orange by-products and spent coffee grounds extracts obtained using DESs composed of Mannitol:Citric acid were significantly high. The extraction was performed at 80 °C, with 10 % water content, SSR of 1:10 (w/w), and a mixture ratio of 1:15 mg (orange by-product to spent coffee grounds). This further highlights the effectiveness of DESs in enhancing the recovery of BC from agro-food waste. Like these research works, hundreds of articles have been published in recent years on the extraction of valuable substances from agro-food waste using DESs without the aid of additional extraction technologies. These studies have consistently demonstrated the effectiveness of DESs in enhancing extraction efficiency compared to conventional organic solvents, primarily due to their unique physicochemical properties.
The application of advanced extraction technologies further enhance the extraction efficiency, such as when MAE has been combined with DESs, as demonstrated in the review by Tapia-Quirós et al.. The authors explained that this combination significantly boosts TPC recovery from waste, offering faster processing times, higher yields, and broad applicability in industries such as food, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals as compared to using organic solvents. Mavai et al. have also provided a concise overview of some non-thermal novel techniques in combination with DESs for extraction of BC from food waste for several applications and highlighted there many advantages.

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE)

Mechanism
US is applied in various processes of food industry, including extraction, homogenization, degassing, cell disruption and lysis, emulsification, crystallization, meat tenderization, cleaning and sanitation, defoaming, and fermentation. Among these, UAE has gained prominence due to its higher efficiency compared to conventional techniques. US refers to sound waves with frequencies between 20 kHz and 10 MHz beyond the human audible range. These waves are generated by converting mechanical vibrations from a high-frequency electrical field, with piezoelectric and magnetostrictive transducers playing a key role in transforming electrical energy into acoustic energy. The mode of US application such as bath, probe (horn), plate transducers, or flow-through reactor also affects cavitation uniformity and intensity. Horn-type systems often offer higher energy density but limited scalability, whereas flow reactors enable continuous processing and better reproducibility for industrial applications. During US treatment, pressure fluctuations at the vapor–air interface generate cavitation bubbles, which rapidly collapse under alternating stress. This violent collapse produces localized shock waves, extreme temperatures (up to 5000 K), and pressures (hundreds of atmospheres), leading to the to the generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and hydrogen radicals (H•) in aqueous or hydroxylated systems however, in non-aqueous or less polar systems, other radical species may be formed depending on solvent composition and dissolved gases. These chemical and physical effects including shear stress, microjets, and turbulence enhance mass transfer and promote chemical reactions..
Specifically, during asymmetrical bubble collapse near solid surfaces, high-speed microjets (200–700 m/s) are formed, causing particle fragmentation and cell wall disruption. This facilitates the efficient release of intracellular compounds into the surrounding solvent. Additionally, US can act as a pre-treatment by generating pores and inducing micro-turbulence, further improving extraction efficiency. Furthermore, US enhances extraction efficiency also through non-cavitation effects such as acoustic streaming, microstreaming, and pressure gradients, which significantly enhance solvent penetration, mixing, and diffusion. These effects are particularly important in viscous systems like DESs. The entire UAE mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 3. The effectiveness of UAE is influenced by several key factors, such as US frequency, power intensity, extraction duration, temperature, solvent selection, and SSR. Lower frequencies lead to more intense cavitation, whereas higher frequencies create milder conditions. Frequencies between 20 and 50 kHz are considered optimal, as multiple processes, including cavitation, shear stress, and fragmentation, work synergistically to maximize yield and extraction efficiency. Increased power intensity enhances cavitation activity, improving extraction efficiency but potentially damaging heat-sensitive compounds.
Shorter extraction times are necessary to prevent the degradation of compounds, and maintaining a temperature range of 20–50 °C allows the process to utilize localized heat from cavitation without significantly elevating the overall temperature, thus preserving thermolabile compounds. The solvent used for extraction play most important role in the whole process of UAE so selection of an optimal solvent and an appropriate SSR are also critical for effective extraction. A variety of solvents are typically employed for extraction, depending on the specific materials from which the extraction is being performed. In studies related to the application of DESs for extraction, a variety of solvent combinations are prepared and screened to identify the most effective solvent. The screening process typically involves evaluating several parameters, such as the viscosity, density, polarity, solubility, and pH of the solvent to ensure its suitability for the extraction process. Additionally, Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) is employed to identify functional groups and monitor chemical interactions between the solvent.

Applications
Distribution of 75 studies employing UAE with DESs for the recovery of valuable substances from various agro-food waste sources.
UAE using DESs as an extraction medium is a most effective strategy of recent times for extracting a variety of valuable substances from agro-food waste. The application of UAE with DESs across various agro-food waste sources is illustrated in the pie chart (Fig. 2), which summarizes data from 75 studies and highlights the most prolific fields of research, along with the relative frequency with which each type of waste has been investigated for the extraction. of valuable substances using this innovative approach. The US waves create cavitation bubbles within the DESs, resulting in localized high pressure and temperature that disrupt the cellular structures of waste materials which facilitates the release of targeted substance from it. DESs are recognized as green effective solvents with unique properties that make them effective in breaking down plant matrices and solubilize compounds while preserving the integrity of heat-sensitive constituents. The cavitation process further enhances the solvent penetration and mass transfer, leading to higher extraction yields. This sustainable and eco-friendly approach not only shortens extraction time but also ensures that the extracted compounds retain their advantageous properties, positioning it as an innovative method for optimizing the utilization of agro-food waste. However, although US improves extraction yields, repeated use of DESs under US conditions may affect their stability. Some studies have reported changes in viscosity, pH, and even minor degradation products after multiple cycles. This highlights the need for further evaluation of long-term recyclability and potential formation of byproducts, especially when considering food and nutraceutical applications.
The schematic representation of whole process of UAE with DESs of valuable substances from agro-food waste.
UAE with DESs involves selecting agro-food waste, which is first dried and ground to reduce particle size and increase surface area for improved interaction with the extraction medium. The sample is mixed with the solvent in a specific ratio and placed in the extraction vessel of either a bath-type or probe-type ultrasonicator. US is then applied at predetermined power, frequency, amplitude, temperature, and time. After extraction, the mixture is filtered or centrifuged to separate the solid residues from the liquid phase. The targeted substance is then isolated from the DESs phase and purified for further use (Fig. 3). Waste seeds are by-products of food processing industries, often discarded. These seeds are rich in BC making them valuable for various industrial applications. UAE with DESs has emerged as an efficient method for recovering these compounds due to enhanced cell wall disruption, improved solubilization, and low environmental impact. Table 1 illustrates the significant works related to UAE with DESs from various agro-food waste seeds.
In a study, Airouyuwa et al. employed UAE with carboxylic acid-based DESs for extraction of BC from date seeds. The authors found that the maximum extraction of total phenolic content (TPC) (∼160 mg GAE/g) and total flavonoids content (TFC) (∼90 mg QE/g) occurred with ChCl: Malic acid (MA) and ChCl:Ascorbic acid (AA) at 60 °C for 40 and 30 min, respectively. In another study Airouyuwa et al. extracted TPC and TFC of date seeds using UAE with seven different sugar-based DESs (70 %). The highest yield of TPC obtained was ∼ 125 mg GAE/g, and TFC ∼ 80 mg QE/g, using ChCl:Lactic acid (LA), which had a very low viscosity of 10 cP at 60℃. This yield was significantly higher compared to TPC (∼40 mg GAE/g) and TFC (15–20 mg QE/g) extraction using 80 % ethanol and methanol. To further evaluate the efficiency of innovative approach, binary and ternary hybrid technologies with alcohol based DESs were used for extraction of BC of date seed in another study of Airouyuwa et al.. The study employed three extraction techniques, homogenization-assisted extraction (SSR 1:30 g/mL, 10,000 rpm for 5 min), MAE (SSR 1:30 g/mL, power 600 W, temperature 50 °C for 10 min), and UAE (SSR 1:30 g/mL, amplitude 80 %, temperature 50 °C for 50 min), applied individually, in binary combinations, and in a ternary approach using five different alcohol-based DESs. The highest TPC yield was achieved with ChCl:Ethylene Glycol in homogenization-assisted extraction (∼132 mg GAE/g), MAE (∼160 mg GAE/g), and UAE (∼125 mg GAE/g). However, the highest yield of ∼ 275 mg GAE/g was obtained through US-microwave assisted extraction, demonstrating an extraction efficiency of 52 %. This indicates that a single technique may not achieve complete extraction due to the tough date seed matrix and outer coat. However, slight degradation of sensitive compounds was noted, suggesting that while hybridization improves yield, optimization is needed to avoid thermal losses. As compared to these studies Ghafoor et al. reported yields of only ∼2.74 mg QE/g TPC and ∼1.23 mg QE/g TFC using supercritical fluid extraction, Ranasinghe et al., achieved a little bit higher yields of ∼24 mg GAE/g TPC and 14 mg QE/g TFC using MAE with water as the solvent and even in a study where UAE was conducted using water as the solvent, the extraction yielded only 21.63 mg GAE/g of TPC and 15.39 mg GAE/g of TFC. Collectively, these results emphasize the superior efficiency of UAE with DESs especially with low viscosity alcohol or acid based solvents, in recovering phenolics and flavonoids from tough matrices like date seeds.
UAE with DESs has demonstrated strong potential for extracting BC from various waste seeds beyond date seeds. For instance, a study by Sun et al. extracted TPC from grape seed powder (a winemaking byproduct) using this technique. Using 14 different solvents, the study optimized UAE conditions at a power of 240–400 W, an extraction time of 10–50 min, a temperature range of 30–70 °C, and SSR of 40–80 g/L. The highest TPC yield (∼112–125 mg GAE/g) was obtained at 20 min, 50 °C, 320 W, and an SSR of 50 g/L, demonstrating the efficiency of UAE-DESs extraction. Using optimum UAE conditions with different DESs, Alrugaibah et al. extracted 41 mg/g and 3.90 mg/g of phenolic acids, flavonols and flavan-3-ols from muscadine grape skin and seeds. Gavarić et al. investigated rosehip seed waste (comprising 30–35 % of the fruit mass) after defatting via supercritical fluid extraction. UAE using lactic acid, glucose, and water based DESs achieved yields of ∼22  mg/g quinic acid and ∼5 mg/g catechin, highlighting the method’s effectiveness even on previously treated matrices. For protein recovery, Sharma et al. explored the use of UAE with ChCl:glycerol on Sacha inchi seed meal. Among four tested DESs, ChCl:glycerol under UAE (40  W, 20  kHz, 50 % amplitude, 90–120 °C, 20  min) resulted in the highest protein yield (∼75 %), substantially outperforming UAE alone (29.21 %) and DES alone (58 %). The enhanced performance of the combined system is likely due to improved cell wall disruption and superior solubilization of macromolecules. These examples consistently demonstrate that UAE with DESs systems can outperform other extraction methods, especially when targeting phenolic and protein compounds from complex, underutilized seed residues. Moreover, solvent viscosity, matrix pretreatment (e.g., defatting), and extraction intensity appear to influence recovery outcomes.

Peels and pomace
Fruit and vegetable peels and pomace are major by-products of the food industry, often discarded as waste. Much attention has been given to them in recent years because, they are rich in BC, dietary fiber, and essential nutrients, making them valuable for various applications. Table 1 summarizes the studies performed on UAE with DESs from peals and pomace based substances. In our own research, Chaudhary et al. compared various extraction methods for recovery of valuable substances from orange peel and demonstrated that UAE using ChCl:Lactic Acid (LA) yielded the highest recovery of TPC (36.51 mg GAE/g), TFC (28.66 mg QE/g), antioxidant activity (DPPH inhibition: 74.4 %), and minor components such as carotenoids, proteins, and lipids. These results significantly outperformed UAE with ethanol, heating-assisted extraction with ChCl:LA, and Soxhlet extraction with ethanol, highlighting the enhanced solubilization and cell disruption achieved with UAE-DES systems. In our another research, Chaudhary et al. again got the similar results related to extraction yield of valuable components from orange peel using UAE with DESs. The analogous findings have also been demonstrated by Lanjekar et al. who extracted TPC and TFC from mango peel using UAE with DESs. The extraction was performed at power levels ranging from 20-100 W for 30 min and was compared with conventional batch extraction and Soxhlet extraction using ethanol as the solvent. The highest yield of TPC (∼70 mg GAE/g) and TFC (∼17 mg QE/g) was obtained using UAE with LA:Glucose DESs, outperforming batch extraction (∼50 mg GAE/g of TPC and ∼10 mg QE/g of TFC at 60 min) and Soxhlet extraction (∼56 mg GAE/g of TPC and ∼12 mg QE/g of TFC at 300 min). The study attributed this improvement to the high acoustic density during UAE, which promotes cavitation and solvent penetration, and to factors like lower viscosity of DESs, increased surface area from reduced particle size, and higher SSR. Similarly, the study Plaza et al. demonstrated the efficiency of UAE in recovering high amounts of non-extractable TPC from mangosteen peel residues. Using ChCl:LA as a solvent, the study recovered 37,638 mg of epicatechin/100 g of non-extractable phenolics, while the highest antioxidant activity (66 %) was obtained using ChCl:Ferulic Acid at an amplitude of 60 % for 15 min. This extraction process targeted the residues left after conventional methanol-based extraction, highlighting the significant presence of phenolics that remain in waste materials. In comparison, conventional extraction yielded only 1,017 mg of epicatechin/100 g and exhibited just 5 % antioxidant activity, emphasizing the inefficiency of traditional methods in fully utilizing BC. These findings underscore the importance of employing advanced extraction techniques to maximize resource efficiency while maintaining sustainability.
In a similar way enhanced BC extraction via UAE has been reported in the study by Rashid et al.. The authors extracted TPC and TFC from apple pomace using UAE with different DESs and water content. ChCl:Glycerol, ChCl:LA, and ChCl:Citric Acid produced higher yields (∼5.6, 5.1, and 4.6  mg GAE/g TPC, respectively) than 70 % ethanol (4.5 mg GAE/g), with optimal extraction achieved at 30 °C, 40  min, and 30 % water concentration. However, beyond this point, solvent dilution or cavitation disruption reduced efficiency, emphasizing the need for parameter optimization. As compared to these findings Egüés et al. obtained the low yield of TPC (∼3.60 mg GAE/g) and TFC (∼2.95 mg CE/g) using UAE with deionized water. So the overall results of increasing extraction efficiency agreed with some other studies where authors used the somewhat similar UAE parameters along with DESs such as Della Posta et al. extracted ∼7.30 g GAE/100 g from avocado peel using Urea:Betaine as compared to ∼5.50 g GAE/100 g obtained by ethanol, Elgharbawy et al. extracted 96 % yield of pectin from pomelo peel using UAE with ChCl:Malonic acid DESs, and Deniz et al. obtained TPC (∼8 mg/g) and TFC (∼18 mg/g) by UAE with Glucose:Fructose from apple pomace. However the study by Marinaccio et al. showed that UAE with volatile DESs (Thymol:Menthol) resulted in 484 mg/g yield of lycopene as compared to 735 mg/g obtained by hexane. The authors reported the reason of high solubility of lycopene in hexane organic solvent that increased the yield.
In addition to the previously mentioned studies, research has also explored the combined effect of UAE and MAE with DESs for extracting valuable substance from agro-food waste. For instance, in the study by Panić et al., the authors reported the extraction of 1.77 mg/g of anthocyanins from grape pomace using a combined UAE-MAE approach with ChCl:Proline:Malic acid. This yield was significantly higher than that obtained with UAE (0.4 mg/g) or MAE (0.6 mg/g) alone. Similarly, the study Stramarkou et al. demonstrated enhanced TPC extraction from apricot and peach waste (kernel and pulp) using a combined UAE-MAE with DESs approach. Moreover, the study Riyamol & Jeevitha provided similar findings, highlighting the effectiveness of UAE and MAE in pectin extraction from onion peel when combined with DESs. These studies reinforce that combining DESs with innovative extraction technologies such as UAE and MAE not only improves extraction efficiency but also enhances the recovery of otherwise inaccessible BC, contributing to a more circular and sustainable food processing system.

Leaves generated as agro-food waste come from crop harvesting, food processing, and pruning activities. As summarized in Table 1, several studies have explored the enhanced recovery of valuable compounds from various leaf matrices using UAE-DESs approaches. For instance Ünlü investigated the extraction of BC from olive leaf waste using UAE under controlled conditions (140  W, 37  kHz, 60  min, 55–75 °C) and 11 DESs. The highest yields were achieved using a ChCl:Fructose:Water mixture (5:2:5 M ratio) at 75 °C, recovering 187.31 mg GAE/g of TPC, 12.75 mg APE/g of TFC, and 853.46 mg/kg of oleuropein. In contrast, ethanol-based UAE yielded only 80 mg GAE/g, and thermo-shaker extraction with ChCl:Acetic acid (1:2) produced 34 mg GAE/g. Even pulsed electric field extraction, which improved cell permeability, yielded only 25 mg GAE/g TPC, underscoring the comparatively higher efficiency of UAE-DES systems. The underlying mechanism involves acoustic cavitation, which disrupts plant cell walls through shear forces generated by rapid bubble collapse, enhancing porosity and solvent penetration. When paired with DESs, which offer tunable polarity and hydrogen bonding capabilities, the extraction of polar and non-polar compounds was significantly improved (Fig. 4).
Supporting evidence comes from Zurob et al., who extracted hydroxytyrosol from olive leaves using UAE with various DESs. The highest yield (87 ppm) was obtained using CA:Glycine:Water, compared to only 20–30 ppm with ethanol or water. The study highlighted the importance of DESs polarity and molar ratios in optimizing extraction efficiency. Wu et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of UAE-DES in extracting BCs from Moringa leaves. Using L-Proline:Glycerol DES under optimized UAE conditions (144 W, 15 min, 40 °C, SSR 1:80 g/mL), the researchers obtained 45 mg GAE/g TPC, 49 mg RE/g TFC, and 585 μmol Trolox equivalents/g antioxidant activity. These values were significantly higher than those from maceration or stirring extraction, which yielded only ∼15–16 mg GAE/g TPC and ∼30 mg RE/g TFC, with markedly lower antioxidant capacity (103–152 μmol TE/g).
In addition to phenolic compounds, studies have also explored the use of UAE-DESs for lignin removal and other plant metabolites. Zhang et al. achieved 47 % lignin recovery from oil palm fronds using NaOH in ChCl:Urea DESs under UAE, compared to 39 % without US. In exactly similar study by Ong et al. using the same US conditions with same DESs (ChCl:Urea) just without NaOH obtained 36 % lignin from oil palm fronds which was more than double of 14 % obtained using DESs only. Comparable findings have been demonstrated on UAE (Using optimal parameters as described above) with DESs by Santos-Martín et al. who obtained 135 GAE/g of TPC by employing DESs as compared to 86 GAE/g by methanol from blueberry leaves, Cheng et al. extracted 2.7 mg/g of flavonoids from peanut leave and stem using ChCl:AA out of 22 DESs prepared for extraction, Cao et al. observed yield of 22 mg GAE/g of TPC from broccoli leaves by ChCl:1,2 propylenglycol showing 63 % higher extraction efficiency as compared to ethanol based extraction, and Christou et al. obtained 24 mg GAE/g of TPC and 7 mg CAE/g of TFC from taro leaves by different DESs. These findings collectively affirm that UAE with DESs provides a versatile and highly efficient platform for extracting diverse BC from various leafy agro-wastes. This approach not only boosts recovery yields but also aligns with sustainability goals through reduced reliance on organic solvents and enhanced valorization of biomass.

Others agro-food waste
Besides seeds, peel, pomace and leaves other agro-food waste materials have also been explored by this novel extraction method (Table 1). Cereal brans (Wheat, rice, oat, barley and corn) are the outer layers of cereal grains that are removed during milling. They are rich in dietary fiber, essential fatty acids, proteins, vitamins (especially B vitamins), minerals (such as iron, magnesium, and zinc), and BC like polyphenols and phytochemicals. In research by Thakur et al. the researchers investigated the extraction of anthocyanins from black rice bran using UAE (Amplitude 10–55 %, temperatures ranging from 30 to 90 °C for durations of 5 to 30 min, employing SSR of 5 to 60 g/mL) with five different DESs. Among these, the LA:Fructose was found to be the most effective for highest anthocyanin extraction (109.68 mg/100 g). The optimized extraction parameters included an amplitude of 21.31 %, an extraction time of 11.25 min, SSR of 18.75 mg/mL and a water content of 12.63 %. The improved yield is attributed to the high shear strain generated by US waves, which disrupt particle structures and enhance mass transfer from rice bran. Additionally, the collapse of cavitation bubbles induces macro-turbulence, facilitating high-velocity collisions among particles and improving diffusion of anthocyanins within the bran. Cherif et al. also researched UAE coupled with thermal treatment with DESs for extraction of phenolics from wheat bran. At optimum conditions of UAE (Power 120 W, frequency 100 kHz, temperature 50 ℃ and time 5–40 min) and subsequent thermal treatment (temperature 50 ℃, time 150 min) with acidic tertiary (Glycerol:CA:Glycine) DESs, highest yield of TPC 94.62 mg FAE/g as compared to only thermal treatment (57.12 mg FAE/g) showing the extraction efficiency of (39 %).
In a research conducted by Duarte et al. researchers obtained 280 mg/g of TPC from maritime pine residues at optimum UAE conditions with LA:FA, and Deng et al. found the highest yield of saponins (144 mg/g) from Gleditsia sinensis Lam. pods husk by UAE with ChCl:LA which was higher than 133 mg/g obtained using UAE with 80 % ethanol. In a separate investigation Roy et al. focused on the recovery of astaxanthin from shrimp wastes for biodegradable packaging using UAE employed at a power of 750 W at a frequency of 20 kHz, with an amplitude ranging from 20 % to 80 % for durations of 15 to 60 min, and SSR of 1:10 with DESs. The researchers revealed that UAE at an amplitude of 60 % for 30 min with the (ChCl:LA) DESs increased the astaxanthin content to 69 mg/g. This enhancement in extraction was attributed to the low viscosity of ChCl:LA, which improved surface tension and solvent penetration, facilitating efficient cavitation. A highly similar study, Denga et al. , reported an astaxanthin yield of 68 mg/g from shrimp waste using UAE (175 W, 30 min, 40 ℃, SSR 1:10 g/mL) with Lysine:CA DESs containing 30 % water. This yield was nearly five times higher than that obtained using acetone.

Limitations and challenges
Despite the novelty, efficiency and sustainability, this extraction method has certain limitations. The research works discussed in previous sections have documented these limitations and challenges. While UAE is the most efficient technology, it also has various limitations that need to be properly tackled to maximize efficiency. The extraction efficiency using UAE can be highly dependent on various parameters, including power, frequency, time, temperature, choice of solvent, SSR, and particle size. Excessive US power or prolonged sonication can generate localized hotspots and shear forces that lead to thermal degradation or structural breakdown of target molecules. The cavitation phenomenon in UAE generates free radicals, which have both positive and negative effects, as they can enhance the extraction process, but excessive production decreases efficiency. The free radicals can react with agro-food waste dissolved in solvent to form secondary free radicals and also start lipid oxidation that can affect the extracts. Too high or too low extraction time can cause undesirable effects on yield. Moreover, the temperature during UAE can enhance mass transfer but extraction yield of some sensitive substances decreased drastically when the temperature exceeded 50 °C, likely due to their degradation at high temperature. So, increasing the temperature beyond the optimal limit to enhance yield can have detrimental effects on the extraction process. The choice of solvent affects solubility, viscosity, and cavitation efficiency. An optimized SSR ensures effective mass transfer without oversaturation or dilution. Smaller particles enhance the surface area for extraction, but too fine size may hinder filtration and handling.
The effectiveness of DESs can vary based on the specific combination of components used and their interactions with the target compounds, leading to unpredictable results in some cases. This arises from differences in hydrogen bonding and solubility interactions between the solvent components and target compounds. To address this, selecting an optimal composition through systematic screening, adjusting HBD to HBA ratios, and fine-tuning extraction conditions can enhance efficiency and reproducibility. When UAE is combined with DESs, the high viscosity and complex hydrogen-bonding networks of these solvents can dampen cavitation intensity, reducing US effectiveness and requiring optimization of parameters specifically for DESs based systems. High viscosity of DESs required water addition, as excessive viscosity hindered extraction efficiency. But increasing water content beyond 30 % resulted in a decline in extraction yield due to disruption of hydrogen bonding between solvent and decrease in its polarity. To balance the viscosity and hydrogen bonding disruption in DESs for optimal extraction efficiency, a controlled water content (typically 20–30 %) is recommended. Instead of exceeding this limit, adjusting the molar ratio of HBD and HBA can help reduce viscosity while maintaining strong hydrogen bonding because the extraction efficiency is contingent on the precise molar ratios of HBA and HBD. An inappropriate SSR may result in decreased extraction efficiency. If the SSR exceeds a specific threshold, it can hinder effective extraction. To optimize the SSR in UAE with DESs, it is essential to determine an ideal ratio (e.g., 1:10 to 1:30 g/mL) to prevent saturation or excessive dilution. Enhancing solvent diffusion through stirring, adjusting sonication power, or mild heating can improve mass transfer efficiency. Additionally, using multiple sequential extractions with fresh solvent and optimizing particle size can further enhance extraction yield while avoiding clumping or hindered diffusion.
While high extraction yields are often reported, it is important to note that recovery of target valuable substances from DESs presents significant challenges that can limit their practical applications in the chemical, pharmaceutical, and agri-food sectors. Unlike conventional solvents, from which target compounds can be easily separated by simple evaporation or lyophilization, the non-volatile nature, negligible vapor pressure, high water miscibility and high viscosity of DESs complicate the separation process. This makes it difficult to isolate and purify high-value substances from the extraction mixture. In many applications, the presence of residual DESs is not acceptable, particularly when purity, regulatory standards, or formulation stability are critical.
To address these challenges various researchers have proposed different strategies. For instance, centrifugal separation that involves centrifugation to separate extract from the extraction mixture, followed by drying to remove any residual moisture. This approach is widely used in various studies due to its simplicity and efficiency in isolating compounds. Centrifugal separation, though simple, may not fully separate all soluble components or remove residual DESs effectively. In a back-extraction process, an organic solvent is first added to the DESs containing extraction mixture to extract the target compounds, followed by phase separation. The remaining DESs phase is then diluted with water and subjected to a second extraction using another solvent, thereby enhancing recovery efficiency through selective partitioning. Back-extraction relies heavily on the use of organic solvents, which undermines the green nature of DESs and introduces additional environmental and safety concerns. In a general chromatographic purification, the extraction mixture is first diluted and filtered, then subjected to techniques like HPLC using a reverse-phase column to separate target compounds. The desired fractions are collected based on retention times and can be further concentrated or purified if needed.
On the other hand, macroporous resin chromatography using an AB-8 column involves passing the extract through a non-polar resin that selectively adsorbs target compounds. These compounds are then eluted using suitable solvents, enabling efficient purification based on polarity and molecular size. Chromatographic methods like HPLC and resin-based purification can be highly selective, but they are often expensive, time-consuming, and require complex instrumentation, making them less feasible for large-scale operations. Vacuum evaporation is a method in which extraction mixture is subjected to reduced pressure and gentle heating. This facilitates the removal of solvents or volatiles, concentrating and isolating the desired extract without degrading thermolabile compounds. Another method involves solvent-based purification, where solid residues are washed with acetone–water mixtures, followed by vacuum filtration and drying. The combined filtrates are then evaporated to remove the solvents, and the target compound is precipitated. To purify further, the precipitate is washed with an ethanol–water mixture and then dried for storage. Vacuum evaporation and solvent-based purification risk degradation of heat-sensitive compounds and involve multiple steps, increasing energy consumption and processing time. In resin adsorption method, to purify target compounds from an extraction mixture, the compounds are adsorbed onto the resin and then desorbed using a solvent mixture, typically involving an ethanol solution with a mild acid, to isolate the purified substance. In one study, researchers used a method that combines purification techniques for the isolation of target compounds extracted with DESs. This approach involved ethanol precipitation to isolate the compounds, followed by further purification using solid-phase extraction columns and cut-off filtration for size-based separation. Resin adsorption and ethanol precipitation, while useful, may suffer from low specificity and incomplete recovery of certain bioactives. Additionally, many of these methods generate significant waste or require secondary purification steps, reducing overall sustainability and scalability.
Additionally, the recovery and reuse of DESs after extraction is a complex but crucial aspect for improving process sustainability. Unlike volatile organic solvents, DESs are difficult to recycle due to their strong hydrogen bonding networks, high viscosity, water miscibility, and thermal stability. These properties hinder the separation and purification of both the target compounds and the solvents themselves, thereby increasing process complexity and cost. Several strategies have been developed to enhance DESs recyclability. Anti-solvent precipitation is one of the most commonly used methods, where the addition of water, ethanol, or acetone breaks the hydrogen bonds in DESs and causes the target compounds to precipitate, allowing the remaining solvent to be recovered by evaporation. Liquid-liquid extraction involves mixing the DESs phase with an immiscible organic solvent such as ethyl acetate to extract target compounds, followed by simple decantation-based phase separation for solvent recovery. Solid-liquid extraction using macroporous resins enables the adsorption of target compounds, while the DESs can be recovered through water washing and subsequent evaporation. Short-path distillation offers another route by enabling thermal separation at low pressure and short residence time, although its application in DESs recycling is still limited and under research. Supercritical fluid extraction, particularly using CO2, is a promising green method that allows for the simultaneous recovery of target compounds and regeneration of the DESs with minimal post-processing. Membrane-based processes such as ultrafiltration and electrodialysis provide selective separation and high-purity recovery of DESs, especially when co-solvents are used to reduce viscosity and improve membrane performance. Rotary evaporation is used to gently remove water from the DESs under reduced pressure, allowing the concentrated DESs to be recovered and reused in subsequent extraction cycles without significant degradation. So, when they are properly recovered, these DESs demonstrate good reusability and can be effectively used for multiple extraction cycles.
Despite their potential, the recovery methods for DESs face notable limitations. Anti-solvent precipitation may lead to incomplete separation and dilution of DESs, reducing their effectiveness in reuse. Liquid-liquid extraction often requires the use of volatile organic solvents, which compromises the green nature of the process. Solid-liquid extraction with resins can suffer from limited adsorption capacity and high material cost. Short-path distillation is energy-intensive and not yet widely optimized for high-viscosity DESs. Supercritical CO2 extraction, while green, demands expensive equipment and precise control conditions. Membrane-based techniques like ultrafiltration and electrodialysis face challenges due to membrane fouling, high viscosity of DESs, and limited scalability. Rotary evaporation is suitable for water removal but may not fully recover DESs from complex mixtures, and repeated cycles could lead to degradation or loss of solvent efficacy.
The transition of UAE with DESs from laboratory-scale to industrial applications faces challenges due to differences in equipment design, process scalability, extraction efficiency and high cost of equipment. Non-uniform distribution of US energy in larger volumes, leading to uneven cavitation zones and inconsistent extraction results. Industrial-scale US reactors also face difficulties in maintaining precise control over temperature, power density, and solvent dynamics, impacting reproducibility and process efficiency. Additionally, higher energy consumption, cost constraints, and regulatory considerations can further impact the feasibility of large-scale implementation. Although UAE with DESs is highly effective on a laboratory scale, scaling up for industrial applications of agro-food waste valorisation presents significant challenges. Overcoming these obstacles requires careful optimization and evaluation of the overall feasibility of large-scale implementation. Additionally, integrating artificial intelligence is essential to further enhance the efficiency of UAE with DESs, aligning with the increasing demand for advanced technologies in the valorization of agro-food waste. Therefore, future research should prioritize not only enhancing extraction yields but also advancing efficient, sustainable strategies for the purification of target compounds and the recovery and reuse of DESs. This integrated approach is essential to fully unlock the potential of UAE with DESs systems for scalable industrial applications.

Safety, sustainability, and scalability
The integration of UAE with DESs represents an innovative and sustainable approach for extracting valuable substances from agro-food waste, aligning with global efforts toward environmental sustainability. The application of DESs in the food industry is regulated by organizations such as the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and the Codex Alimentarius Commission, which establish safety standards by evaluating toxicity, residue levels, and the potential migration of these solvents into products. Ensuring the safe use of DESs in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetics industries requires careful selection of components, thorough characterization of the prepared solvents, and comprehensive safety assessments of both the solvents and the extracted compounds. Although numerous studies have suggested that DESs exhibit low toxicity, their vast number of possible component combinations necessitates further evaluation. Without thoroughly assessing all potential solvent formulations, it is difficult to categorically deem DESs entirely non-toxic. Various in vitro and in vivo studies indicate that toxicity is influenced by several factors, including the tested cell or organism, solvent composition, viscosity, molar ratio, synergistic interactions, organic acid content, and pH levels. Therefore, extensive toxicity studies are essential before their widespread application.
DESs are considered green solvents due to their biodegradability, low toxicity, and customizable properties, which help to minimize the environmental impact of chemical extraction processes. Their ability to replace conventional organic solvents many of which are hazardous and non-renewable makes them a crucial component of eco-friendly extraction technologies. US further enhances sustainability by reducing energy consumption and processing time while maximizing extraction efficiency of the process. The cavitation effect generated by US waves disrupts cell structures, facilitating the release of valuable compounds with minimal resource input. The combined use of UAE and DESs not only adds value to agro-food waste but also supports a circular economy and UN’s 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development Goals by transforming underutilized by-products into high-value ingredients for industries such as food, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics. However, consumer acceptance of this modern extraction technique depends on ensuring its safety for both human health and the environment.
Only two successful studies have so far explored the scale-up of UAE with DESs. In the first study, Panić et al. extracted polyphenols from grape pomace using a betaine:glucose (1:1 M ratio). A specific amount of dry grape pomace was used, and the extraction system included a Hielscher flow cell with an US processor operating at a frequency of 20 kHz. The system featured a probe connected to a booster with amplification settings and was operated at varying amplitude levels without any external cooling. The highest polyphenol yield (21.6 mg GAE/g) was achieved at an amplitude of 28.13 %, an extraction time of 13.21 min, and SSR of 0.04. In another study, Fu et al. extracted phenolic compounds from Carya cathayensis Sarg. peels using a probe-type US system in both batch and continuous modes with solvent volumes of 150 mL, 450 mL, and 3000 mL. The setup included a 15 mm diameter probe, and solvent circulation was maintained through a peristaltic pump connected to a 5 L solvent container. The pump operated at a flow rate of 80 mL/min to ensure a continuous solvent loop between the container and the reaction vessel. The maximum yield achieved was 59.27 mg GAE/g, which was approximately six times higher than that obtained using conventional solvents. According to Cauduro et al., only 14 studies have been published so far in the past five years focusing overall on UAE of various compounds from diverse natural sources. In their work, ethanol and water were primarily used as solvents across different application modes, including flow-through, batch, and continuous systems. The UAE reactors employed included flow cells with flow mode, US probes operated in batch mode, and probes configured for flow mode applications.
Overall the successful application of UAE at larger volumes in these studies suggests promising scalability. And based on the configurations and outcomes reported by Panić et al. and Fu et al., it can be reasonably speculated that UAE with DESs also holds strong potential for scale-up. However, the scalability of this novel, cost-effective, and sustainable extraction method is a critical aspect that requires further attention, with government bodies playing a crucial role in facilitating its widespread implementation. It is well recognized that scaling up US applications presents significant challenges. This is primarily due to inherent system limitations, including the limited durability of transducers under high-power loads and the difficulty in achieving uniform energy distribution within the extraction reactors. Additionally, a major obstacle in the field of sonochemistry is the lack of standardized reporting of experimental parameters, which hinders reproducibility and comparison across studies. But relying on conventional extraction methods, which have a significant environmental footprint, is no longer a viable option, especially given the rapidly changing climate that poses severe threats to humanity. This review, along with other related studies, highlights the potential of employing sustainable and efficient extraction techniques on a large scale for the valorization of agro-food waste. With continued research advancements, the current limitations of this method can be addressed, making it a cost-effective alternative for extracting valuable compounds. Widespread adoption of these sustainable practices can contribute to mitigating climate change, preserving the planet for future generations, restoring biodiversity, and fostering resilient industries with positive environmental impacts. Additionally, embracing these innovative extraction techniques supports the broader goal of achieving global food security by ensuring zero tolerance for food waste and hunger.
No study to date has been conducted on techno-economic assessment (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) for this innovative approach, making such evaluations a pressing necessity to support its practical and industrial adoption. TEA helps determine the cost-effectiveness of the process by analysing capital investment, operational costs, and profitability compared to conventional extraction methods. Meanwhile, LCA assesses the environmental impact, including energy consumption, carbon footprint, and solvent biodegradability, ensuring that UAE with DESs aligns with green chemistry principles.

Conclusion and future perspectives
The present review underscores the strong potential of UAE with DESs for the valorization of agro-food waste. Compared to conventional and other emerging extraction techniques, UAE with DESs offer notable advantages, including higher extraction yields, reduced energy and solvent consumption, shorter processing times, and enhanced selectivity while aligning well with green chemistry principles. However, it is evident that no universal set of optimal extraction parameters or DESs composition can be applied across all waste types, as efficacy is highly dependent on the specific nature of the biomass and the target compounds. While general trends suggest that conditions such as a US power of 300–400 W, extraction time of ∼30 min, and a temperature of ∼40 °C are effective in many cases, these values should be viewed as preliminary guidelines rather than definitive recommendations. More work is needed to refine these parameters through systematic optimization studies using design of experiments (DoE) or artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted modeling approaches. Crucially, the review identifies several priority areas for future research to enable the industrial adoption of UAE-DESs systems. There is a pressing need to translate lab-scale success into pilot and industrial scale operations. Studies should focus on reactor design (e.g., flow-through and continuous systems), energy efficiency, solvent recyclability, and scalability assessments under realistic operating conditions. Comparative TEA and LCA must be applied to evaluate the economic and environmental performance of UAE with DESs in comparison with conventional and other green extraction methods. Despite being labeled as green, not all DESs are inherently non-toxic. Future work must assess the cytotoxicity, biodegradability, and residual solvent effects of DESs, particularly for food, pharmaceutical, or cosmetic applications. Research should focus on diverse agro-industrial waste streams (e.g., animal-based, fiber-rich, or protein-rich wastes) and other bioactives like lipids, proteins, pectins, and pigments. Combining UAE with other non-thermal methods (e.g., pulsed electric fields, microwaves, cold plasma) or enzymatic pretreatments may further enhance efficiency and reduce solvent demand, leading to synergistic extraction effects. Establishing reporting standards for sonication parameters, solvent composition, and extraction outcomes will be key for advancing the field and enabling meaningful comparisons across studies. Ultimately, successful scale-up and commercial adoption of UAE with DESs will require interdisciplinary collaboration between chemists, engineers, toxicologists, and policy-makers. Regulatory frameworks and certification standards must evolve in tandem to support and incentivize sustainable extraction practices. If these challenges are met, UAE with DESs could represent a transformative advancement in circular bioeconomy strategies for agro-food waste valorization.
