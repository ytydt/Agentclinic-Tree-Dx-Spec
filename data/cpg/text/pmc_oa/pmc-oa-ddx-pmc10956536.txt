Evaluation of perineal wound healing and pain outcomes after low‐angle mediolateral episiotomy in women undergoing vaginal childbirth: A systematic review and meta‐analysis
Abstract
The efficacy of episiotomy, particularly the angle of incision in mediolateral episiotomies, remains a significant area of inquiry in obstetrics. This meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the impact of low‐angle mediolateral episiotomy on perineal wound healing and pain outcomes in women undergoing vaginal childbirth. Adhering to PRISMA guidelines, a systematic review was conducted using the PICO framework. Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, focusing on randomised controlled trials (RCTs) involving low‐angle mediolateral episiotomies. Comprehensive literature searches were performed across major electronic databases including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and Cochrane Library. Data extraction and quality assessments were meticulously carried out by independent reviewers, employing the Cochrane Collaboration's risk of bias tool. A total of 1246 articles were initially identified, with 8 articles meeting the strict inclusion criteria for the final analysis. The meta‐analysis revealed significant heterogeneity among studies regarding postoperative pain (p < 0.0001, I 2 = 77.5%), and employed a random‐effects model. Results showed that low‐angle episiotomies significantly reduced postoperative pain (OR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.17–0.42, p < 0.001), and increased first‐degree healing rates (OR = 2.95, 95% CI: 2.20–3.96, p < 0.001) compared to traditional angles. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of these findings, and no significant publication bias was detected. The analysis suggests that low‐angle episiotomies can potentially reduce postoperative perineal pain and enhance wound healing. However, the limited number and varying quality of the included studies warrant cautious interpretation of these results. Further well‐designed studies are needed to corroborate these findings and guide clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION
Episiotomy, a strategic surgical incision made in the perineum during the second stage of labour, stands as a pivotal obstetric intervention. Its primary intent is to prevent the occurrence of severe perineal tears, which can lead to long‐term complications such as urinary and faecal incontinence, pain during intercourse and psychological trauma. Additionally, episiotomy is employed to facilitate the birthing process, particularly in cases of fetal distress or when there is a need for operative vaginal deliveries, such as with forceps or vacuum assistance. The practice of episiotomy, widely adopted in the mid‐20th century, was initially perceived as a preventative measure against more severe spontaneous tears and subsequent pelvic floor disorders. However, its routine use has been questioned in recent decades, leading to a more judicious and individualised approach. Contemporary research emphasises the importance of evaluating maternal factors such as tissue elasticity, foetal size, and the progress of labour before deciding on an episiotomy.
Despite its benefits, the procedure is not without its controversies. The efficacy and long‐term consequences of episiotomy, particularly in relation to the angle and technique of the incision, are subjects of ongoing debate in the medical community. A prevailing view suggests that incisions closer to the anus, implying a smaller angle, are associated with an increased risk of obstetric anal sphincter injuries (OASIS). Literature references indicate a higher probability of OASIS with smaller angled episiotomies. Conversely, several studies argue that angles less than 40° or greater than 60° increase the risk of OASIS. These disparities in viewpoints underscore the lack of a universally accepted standard for episiotomy angles and their implications for maternal health. Pain management and wound healing are closely linked to the severity of perineal trauma. The process of wound healing in the perineal region post‐episiotomy is complex and multifactorial, influenced by the technique of incision, individual patient factors, and postpartum care. The method of low‐angle mediolateral episiotomy, pioneered by researchers in China based on clinical experience, advocates for an incision angle of 15°–30°, starting from the posterior fourchette and extending medially. This approach also recommends a shorter incision length of 2–3 cm. This technique aims to minimize the risk of severe perineal trauma while promoting more efficient pain control and wound healing.
The primary objective of this systematic review and meta‐analysis is to directly address the existing controversies and knowledge gaps regarding the angle and technique of mediolateral episiotomies, by evaluating the effectiveness of low‐angle (15°–30°) mediolateral episiotomies in improving perineal wound healing and reducing postpartum pain among women undergoing vaginal childbirth. This research seeks to synthesise current evidence to offer clear, evidence‐based insights into the optimal episiotomy practices, aiming to enhance maternal outcomes and inform clinical decision making.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy
During the systematic review process and subsequent reporting of our results, the search strategy was meticulously designed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. This approach was anchored by the PICO framework, focusing on the Patient group of women undergoing vaginal childbirth, the Intervention of low‐angle mediolateral episiotomy, a Comparison with other episiotomy techniques or no episiotomy, and the Outcomes related to perineal wound healing and pain postpartum. The literature search was conducted on 19 September 2023, spanning across four major electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library. To ensure a thorough and inclusive search, no time or language restrictions were imposed. The chosen keywords, ‘vaginal childbirth’, ‘low‐angle mediolateral episiotomy’, ‘obstetric anal sphincter injuries’, ‘episiotomy techniques’, and ‘maternal health outcomes’, were strategically employed to capture a wide range of studies relevant to our research question. This keyword selection was integral to covering the extensive scope defined by our PICO framework. Additionally, to augment the electronic database search, the reference lists of identified relevant articles were manually screened. This step was crucial to uncover any additional records that might have been overlooked in the electronic search, ensuring the comprehensiveness of our meta‐analysis.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria
Study Design: We exclusively included Randomised Controlled Trials (RCTs).
Participants: Studies involving women who underwent vaginal childbirth were included. There were no restrictions based on age, parity or gestational age.
Intervention: The primary focus was on low‐angle mediolateral episiotomy. Studies comparing this intervention with other episiotomy techniques, or no episiotomy were included.
Outcomes: Studies that reported on perineal wound healing and pain outcomes postpartum were considered. This included but was not limited to, the rate of wound healing, incidence of perineal pain and severity of pain.
Language: Articles published in any language were included.

Exclusion criteria
Study Design: We excluded case reports, editorials, reviews and animal studies, as they did not provide the level of evidence required for a meta‐analysis.
Participants: Studies focusing on women who did not undergo vaginal childbirth, such as those who had caesarean sections, were excluded.
Outcomes: Studies that did not specifically report on wound healing or pain outcomes related to episiotomy were omitted.
Incomplete Data: Studies with incomplete data, which could not be resolved through author contact, were also excluded.
Quality: Studies that failed to meet a minimum quality threshold, as determined by a predefined quality assessment tool, were not considered for inclusion.

Data extraction
Two independent evaluators were responsible for the literature screening and data extraction, with a system in place for cross‐checking each other's work to maintain consistency and objectivity. In instances where discrepancies arose during the data extraction process, the evaluators engaged in detailed discussions to reconcile any differences. If a consensus could not be reached, a third‐party reviewer was consulted to provide an unbiased resolution. The specific data extracted from each study included crucial information such as the author(s) of the study, the publication year and the number of cases examined. Additionally, detailed information regarding the intervention measures, such as the type of episiotomy performed, suturing methods used, and the length of the incision, were meticulously recorded. This comprehensive approach was critical for evaluating the effectiveness and outcomes of the low‐angle mediolateral episiotomy. In cases where relevant data was not available or explicitly mentioned in the published reports, we reached out directly to the original study investigators via email.

Quality assessment
In assessing the quality of studies for our meta‐analysis, we implemented the Cochrane Collaboration's risk of bias tool. This involved two independent reviewers evaluating key aspects such as random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, completeness of outcome data, selective reporting and other biases. Each domain was rated as having a low, unclear or high risk of bias. Discrepancies between the reviewers' assessments were addressed through discussion, and when necessary, a third‐party reviewer was consulted to achieve consensus. This thorough quality assessment process was integral to ensuring the credibility and reliability of our meta‐analytical findings.

Statistical analyses
In the statistical analyses of our meta‐analysis, we first assessed the heterogeneity between studies. This was done using chi‐square statistics, and the degree of heterogeneity was quantified by the I 2 value. A fixed‐effect model was employed to compute the combined effect size when the I 2 value was less than 50%, and the corresponding p‐value was more than or equal to 0.10, indicating no significant heterogeneity. Conversely, significant heterogeneity was suggested when the I 2 value was equal to or more than 50%, or the corresponding p‐value was less than 0.10, leading to the use of a random‐effects model to calculate the combined effect size. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of our results and assess the potential impact of individual studies on the overall effect size. This analysis involved sequentially omitting each study from the meta‐analysis and recalculating the overall effect size to identify and eliminate potential causes of heterogeneity. To detect any potential publication bias, we analysed the symmetry of the funnel plot; a balanced distribution of data points around the funnel plot's apex would indicate a reduced likelihood of results being influenced by publication bias. Furthermore, Egger's linear regression test was utilised as a quantitative method to assess the presence of publication bias. All statistical tests were two‐sided, with a p‐value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. The data were analysed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), ensuring a comprehensive and precise statistical evaluation.

RESULTS

Search results and study selection
During the initial phase of our systematic review and meta‐analysis, a comprehensive search across multiple electronic databases yielded 1246 potentially relevant articles. An algorithm was then utilised to eliminate duplicates, ensuring each study was unique. Subsequently, a rigorous screening of titles and abstracts was conducted according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, focusing on study methodology, participant demographics, measured clinical outcomes and research quality. This screening process resulted in the identification of 36 articles for detailed evaluation. Each of these articles underwent an independent full‐text review by multiple investigators to ensure a thorough and unbiased assessment. Of these, 28 articles were excluded due to various reasons: review articles (9), sequentially published works (5), articles with insufficient data for analysis (11), and clinical trials without control groups (3). Ultimately, 8 articles satisfied all stringent criteria set forth in our research protocol and were included in the final meta‐analysis (Figure 1).

Study characteristics
The meta‐analysis encompassed a diverse range of studies, each contributing unique insights into the impact of episiotomy techniques on perineal wound healing and pain outcomes. These studies, conducted between 2010 and 2017, varied in their sample sizes, ranging from as few as 44 to as many as 300 participants in the treatment and control groups. The interventions compared in these studies primarily focused on different angles of mediolateral episiotomy, ranging from 15° to 60°. A notable variety in incision lengths was observed across the studies, with some reporting lengths ranging from 2 to 5 cm. However, a few studies did not specify this detail. The foetal weight in the studied cases also showed variation, with some studies including infants weighing between 2500 and 4100 g. Regarding suturing methods, the studies demonstrated a mix of techniques. These included interrupted suturing for vaginal or perineal wounds, continuous intradermal suturing for the skin, and layer‐by‐layer suturing with absorbable thread. Some studies specified suturing for specific tissues such as subcutaneous tissue and muscle. Overall, this collection of studies provided a comprehensive examination of various episiotomy techniques and their outcomes, reflecting a range of clinical practices and patient demographics (Table 1).

Results of quality assessment
The risk of bias assessment across the included studies in the meta‐analysis was conducted using the Cochrane Collaboration's risk of bias tool, as depicted in the provided figure. The evaluation indicated that most studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in the domains of random sequence generation, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. Some concerns were noted in the domains of allocation concealment and blinding of participants and personnel, where several studies exhibited unclear or high risk of bias. The risk of bias for other potential sources varied among the studies. Overall, the majority of the studies showed a robust methodological quality, though some aspects, particularly blinding and allocation concealment, require cautious interpretation of the results due to the potential for bias (Figure 2).
Diagram representing the quality evaluation of included studies utilising the Cochrane Collaboration's tool criteria.

Impact of low‐angle episiotomy angle on postoperative wound pain
The meta‐analysis comprehensively evaluated the effect of different episiotomy angles on postoperative wound pain. Data from eight studies that recorded this outcome were included. There was a considerable heterogeneity observed among the studies (p < 0.0001, I 2 = 77.5%), which necessitated the use of a random‐effects model for the meta‐analysis. The results from the pooled data analysis revealed that the incidence of postoperative pain associated with low‐angle episiotomy was significantly lower than that experienced with traditional episiotomy angles. The difference was statistically significant, with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.27, and a 95% confidence interval (CI) ranging from 0.17 to 0.42 (p < 0.001). This finding suggests that the angle at which an episiotomy is performed could be a critical determinant of postoperative pain outcomes. The substantial heterogeneity indicates variability in study designs, populations or episiotomy techniques, which underscores the necessity for individualised clinical decision making (Figure 3).
Forest plot demonstrating the impact of low‐angle episiotomy on postoperative wound pain.

Influence of low‐angle episiotomy on postoperative wound healing
This section of the meta‐analysis focused on assessing the influence of the episiotomy angle on the rates of first‐degree wound healing. The analysis incorporated findings from seven studies addressing this specific outcome. The homogeneity test results showed no significant heterogeneity among the included studies (p = 0.138, I 2 = 31.6%), indicating that a fixed‐effect model (Mantel–Haenszel method) was appropriate for the meta‐analysis. The synthesised results indicated that the group receiving low‐angle episiotomies had a significantly higher rate of first‐degree healing compared to the group with conventional episiotomy angles. The statistical significance of this finding was underscored by an OR of 2.95 and a 95% CI spanning from 2.20 to 3.96 (p < 0.001). The absence of notable heterogeneity suggests a consistent effect across different studies, enhancing the reliability of the conclusion that lower episiotomy angles can be beneficial for optimal wound healing. These findings are suggestive of a potential clinical advantage of adopting low‐angle episiotomies in practice, highlighting the importance of technique in surgical outcomes and patient recovery (Figure 4).

Sensitivity analysis of meta‐analytic findings on postoperative wound pain related to low‐angle episiotomy
In the face of significant heterogeneity among the incorporated studies, sensitivity analysis was imperative to verify the robustness of the aggregated findings. The process involved the exclusion of each study in turn, followed by a recalculation of the pooled effect sizes. The results of this comprehensive sensitivity analysis confirmed that the overall effect sizes remained consistent and were not overly dependent on any individual study. This reinforced the dependability of the meta‐analytical conclusions. The consistent nature of the outcomes, despite the iterative removal of studies, substantiates the strength of the primary results and lends additional credibility to the meta‐analysis's overall conclusions (Figure 5).
Sensitivity analysis graph of meta‐analytic findings concerning postoperative wound pain associated with low‐angle episiotomy.

Assessment of publication bias in meta‐analysis
To evaluate potential publication bias within the meta‐analysis, funnel plot analyses were performed on the collection of included studies. The symmetry observed in these plots suggested an absence of significant bias (Figure 6). Furthermore, the application of Egger's linear regression test to the compiled data yielded no evidence of publication bias across various variables. These findings collectively affirm the robustness and credibility of the meta‐analysis outcomes.

DISCUSSION
The episiotomy, particularly the mediolateral approach, has long been a topic of considerable debate in the field of obstetrics. This surgical intervention, despite its initial design to minimize severe perineal trauma during childbirth, has been scrutinised for its potential drawbacks, including pain and healing complications. Mediolateral episiotomy is conventionally performed at varying angles, and the question of which angle optimises healing and minimises pain is pivotal for enhancing maternal care. Our meta‐analysis suggests that a lower angle, deviating from the traditional 40° to 60° range, is significantly associated with reduced postoperative pain and improved wound healing. This finding is particularly relevant as it may influence postpartum recovery, not only in terms of physical healing but also regarding the overall quality of life and psychological well‐being. The discussion of these results must take into account the complex interplay between anatomical, physiological and technical factors that influence episiotomy outcomes. Anatomically, a low‐angle incision may be less likely to encroach upon the highly innervated and vascularized perineal area, thereby reducing acute and chronic pain while also minimising blood loss. Physiologically, such an incision might respect the natural tissue planes and integrity of the pelvic floor musculature, potentially leading to fewer complications and a more straightforward healing process. From a technical standpoint, the execution of a low‐angle mediolateral episiotomy requires meticulous surgical skill to ensure that the benefits of the angle are not offset by an increased risk of unintended extension of the incision. The precision of the incision, coupled with appropriate postpartum care, is essential to facilitate the potential advantages of this approach.
The results of this meta‐analysis elucidate the significant influence of low‐angle episiotomy on postoperative wound pain and healing, with implications for clinical practices in obstetrics. The finding that lower episiotomy angles are associated with reduced postoperative pain, as indicated by the odds ratio of 0.27, prompts a closer examination of the potential mechanisms behind this observation. Similarly, the improved first‐degree healing rates linked with low‐angle episiotomy point to a more favourable tissue response, meriting an analysis of the underlying biological and clinical processes. The relationship between episiotomy angle and postoperative pain can be partially explained by the anatomical distribution and density of perineal innervation. A more acute angle may result in less disruption of nerve endings within the perineal area, leading to decreased nociceptive signalling and, consequently, reduced pain perception. Furthermore, the smaller incision size inherent to low‐angle episiotomies likely contributes to the lower pain scores observed postoperatively. Less tissue trauma typically correlates with reduced inflammatory response and pain, a principle well‐documented across surgical disciplines. Moreover, the angle of the incision in relation to the perineal musculature and fascia might also play a role. Low‐angle incisions potentially preserve the integrity of the perineal body to a greater extent, which might mitigate the extent of muscular and fascial disruption and allow for more straightforward and less painful healing processes. This could account for the observed enhanced rates of first‐degree healing reflecting a less complicated reparative phase.
The high degree of heterogeneity detected in the studies examining postoperative wound pain suggests variable factors influencing this outcome, including differences in pain threshold among individuals, variations in suturing techniques, and inconsistencies in postpartum care protocols. This heterogeneity necessitates a cautious approach when interpreting the data, despite the robustness of the overall pooled effect. It underscores the importance of considering individual patient factors when planning episiotomy procedures. The absence of notable heterogeneity in the analysis of first‐degree wound healing rates implies a more consistent relationship between the episiotomy angle and healing outcomes. This consistency adds weight to the argument for low‐angle episiotomies as a means of optimising wound healing. The integrity of the epithelial and connective tissue structures at lower incision angles may facilitate more efficient tissue approximation and repair, leading to superior healing outcomes. This is consistent with the principles of wound care, where minimally invasive techniques are often associated with better healing trajectories. The sensitivity analysis conducted as part of this meta‐analysis reinforces the reliability of these findings, indicating that no single study disproportionately influenced the results. This robustness supports the potential for low‐angle episiotomy to be a best practice, aiming to minimise patient discomfort and enhance the healing process.
Clinically, these findings suggest that when episiotomy is deemed necessary, the employment of a lower angle could be beneficial for the patient's postoperative experience and recovery. It is essential, however, to balance this with the need for adequate exposure and the prevention of more severe perineal trauma. Therefore, while the low‐angle episiotomy presents advantages, its application must be individualised, considering factors such as foetal size, maternal anatomy, and the dynamics of labour. Future research should aim to further elucidate the mechanisms behind these clinical observations, perhaps through detailed anatomical studies or trials employing standardised pain assessment tools. Additionally, investigations into the optimal angle for episiotomy that consider various maternal and foetal characteristics are warranted to refine guidelines and ensure that episiotomy practices continue to evolve based on evidence that maximises benefit and minimises harm.
To facilitate the translation of our findings into clinical practice, it is imperative to acknowledge the challenges associated with modifying established surgical techniques. Implementing low‐angle episiotomy requires comprehensive training to ensure accuracy in the angle and length of incision, which may differ significantly from traditional practices. To address these challenges, we recommend the development of standardised training modules and guidelines, incorporating simulation‐based training and visual aids to enhance understanding and skill acquisition. Furthermore, interdisciplinary collaboration between obstetricians, midwives, and nursing staff is crucial to integrate these practices effectively into routine childbirth care. The adaptation of low‐angle episiotomy techniques should also be supported by ongoing research and feedback mechanisms to monitor outcomes and patient satisfaction, ensuring that the practice continues to align with the best available evidence. By systematically addressing these challenges, the benefits of low‐angle episiotomies can be more readily realised in clinical settings, ultimately improving maternal health outcomes.
While this meta‐analysis provides valuable insights, it is not without limitations. The included studies exhibited considerable heterogeneity, which may have affected the pooled outcome measures. Additionally, variations in episiotomy techniques and postoperative care protocols across different settings could have influenced the results. The majority of studies did not account for potential confounding factors such as the extent of perineal trauma or the use of analgesia, which could significantly impact pain outcomes. Furthermore, the subjective nature of pain reporting and the lack of standardised assessment tools for wound healing present challenges in interpreting the findings.

CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, the findings of this meta‐analysis suggest that low‐angle episiotomies may reduce the incidence of short‐term postpartum perineal pain and enhance wound healing rates. However, given the limited number and variable quality of the studies included, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution. Further research with more rigorous methodologies is necessary to validate these findings and to establish definitive clinical guidelines for episiotomy practices.
