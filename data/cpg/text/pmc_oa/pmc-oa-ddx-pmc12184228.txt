Cardiac Conduction Disorders Due to Acquired or Genetic Causes in Young Adults: A Review of the Current Literature
Cardiac conduction disorders can manifest in young adults in isolated forms, associated with myocardial diseases or as part of a multiorgan disorder. Underlying causes of cardiac conduction disorders may be genetically determined or acquired. Cardiac conduction disorder in young adults is a complex and often underestimated and underrecognized disease that may need of a multidisciplinary team for the diagnosis, treatment, and long‐term management of these patients. Therefore, it is crucial to raise clinicians' awareness of this condition. In this review, we provide a comprehensive update on the cause, diagnosis, and treatment of young adults with cardiac conduction disorders, also suggesting potential strategies to improve the current clinical management of these patients.

Cardiac conduction disorders (CCDs) encompass a wide range of clinical conditions characterized by cardiac conduction abnormalities, including both acquired and hereditary forms, which can occur either alone or in association with structural heart abnormalities. The Heart Rhythm Society, the European Heart Rhythm Association, and the Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society unanimously define CCDs in young adults, defined as those aged <50 years, as the presence of unexplained progressive conduction abnormalities in structurally normal hearts and in the absence of skeletal myopathies. However, the clinical profile and prognosis of younger adults with CCDs aged <50 years has been poorly investigated. Available epidemiological data reported that CCDs in young adults represent about 13% of all conduction disorders. However, this estimate likely underestimates the magnitude of the problem. Additionally, the incidence, causes, and outcome of a larger part of CCDs remains of unknown origin and labeled as idiopathic. Doubtless, advances in genetic screening over the past 2 decades have aimed to define the prevalence and clinical characteristics of these patients. Nonetheless, their clinical management and diagnosis often remain a challenge due to the need for early diagnosis, effective and personalized treatments, and long‐term follow‐up. In this review, we provide a comprehensive update on the epidemiology, cause, diagnosis, and management of young adults with CCDs also focusing on the current unmet needs of these patients and suggesting potential strategies to resolve these gaps in our knowledge.

EPIDEMIOLOGY
Currently, data on the occurrence of CCDs in young adults are limited. Although CCDs are often perceived as rare conditions, recent trends in migration are reshaping this landscape. Individuals from regions where CCDs are more prevalent are increasingly relocating to nonendemic areas, potentially leading to a rise in the number of cases that health care providers may encounter. This influx of migrants presents both challenges and opportunities for care teams. It highlights the necessity for health care professionals to maintain a heightened index of suspicion, particularly among those who may not be familiar with CCDs. Many clinicians in nonendemic regions may not recognize the signs and symptoms associated with these disorders, making increased awareness crucial for timely diagnosis and effective management. Furthermore, certain populations possess genetic predispositions that elevate their risk for these disorders. When individuals from these genetically at‐risk groups migrate, they introduce these variations into new environments, potentially resulting in previously uncommon cases of CCD. This genetic diversity can complicate clinical presentations, necessitating a more nuanced approach to diagnosis and management in these diverse patient populations.
Existing literature indicates that the primary causes of CCD in this age group differ significantly from those typically observed in patients >50 years. A nationwide Danish study identified the leading causes of atrioventricular blocks in young adults as follows: complications from cardiac surgery (15.3%), congenital atrioventricular block (9.0%), cardioinhibitory reflex (5.0%), congenital heart disease (4.2%), complications from radiofrequency ablation (3.4%), and cardiomyopathies (3.0%). Notably, ischemic heart disease accounted for only 1.4% of cases, making it a less common cause of CCD. Moreover, cardiac amyloidosis, along with other infiltrative disorders such as cardiac sarcoidosis, represents a relevant yet currently underestimated cause of CCDs in young adults. Atrioventricular blocks are commonly associated with light‐chain amyloidosis and variant transthyretin amyloidosis in younger patients presenting with these conduction abnormalities. However, the cause remained unknown in nearly half of the patients. These estimates may underestimate the true prevalence of CCDs in this demographic, as previous studies have often involved unselected populations and retrospective designs. Additionally, intraventricular conduction abnormalities—such as right bundle‐branch block (RBBB), left bundle‐branch block (LBBB), fascicular block, or combinations thereof—may be detected on routine ECG in young adults. These abnormalities can occur as isolated findings or may be associated with underlying cardiomyopathy, systemic diseases or congenital heart disease. CAUSES

Acquired Forms of CCD
Acquired forms of CCDs arise from various pathogenetic mechanisms, frequently involving infiltration or fibrosis within the conduction system. In young patients, the spectrum of acquired causes leading to CCDs generally include ischemic heart disease, myocardial infectious disease, autoimmune disorders, infiltrative diseases, and infections.

Myocardial Infectious Diseases: Myocarditis
Myocardial infectious diseases, particularly viral myocarditis, are uncommon yet significant contributors to CCDs. Advanced atrioventricular blocks often represent the initial and sole symptom. The European Study of the Epidemiology and Treatment of Inflammatory Heart Disease found that 18% of patients suspected of acute myocarditis exhibited arrhythmic symptoms, including ventricular arrhythmias and complete atrioventricular block. Similarly, an analysis of 31 760 patients from the Nationwide Inpatient Sample Database revealed incidences of nonadvanced and advanced atrioventricular blocks during acute myocarditis in up to 1.7% and 1.1%, respectively. Conversely, in patients with fulminant myocarditis, advanced atrioventricular blocks were present in about 7% of patients.

Myocardial Infectious Diseases: Lyme Carditis
Advanced atrioventricular blocks often serve as the primary manifestation of Lyme carditis occurring in approximately 80% to 90% of patients with cardiac involvement, primarily due to the spirochetes' affinity for cardiac tissues and the subsequent autoimmune responses that trigger significant inflammation. However, in these patients, atrioventricular blocks are typically transient; available data have estimated that only 0.03 patients per 100 000 population require a pacemaker implantation.

Myocardial Infectious Diseases: Chagas Disease
Chagas disease is a chronic parasitic infection caused by the protozoan Trypanosoma cruzi, endemic in Latin America but increasingly concerning in nonendemic regions due to population movements. The disease progresses through various stages, often resulting in cardiac conduction disturbances. Common electrocardiographic abnormalities include RBBB and left anterior hemiblock, affecting over half of patients. Furthermore, various degrees of atrioventricular blocks reflecting the atrioventricular node dysfunction, may be observed in these patients. Conversely, LBBB and left posterior fascicle hemiblock are less frequently described.

Autoimmune Disease
CCDs are significant manifestations of cardiac involvement in various autoimmune diseases. For example, in rheumatoid arthritis, infiltration of the atrioventricular node can lead to RBBB in approximately 35% of patients. Moreover, atrioventricular blocks, despite rarely being observed, are typically complete when they do occur. Similarly, systemic lupus erythematosus can result in conduction defects due to small vessel vasculitis and the infiltration of fibrous or granulation tissue, potentially impairing the function of the sinus or atrioventricular nodes in up to 70% of patients.
In this regard, it is important to focus on fetal and congenital heart block secondary to the exposure to maternal anti–Sjögren's‐syndrome‐related antigen A autoantibodies transplacentally passaged via neonatal FcRn (FC receptor). Congenital heart block has significant morbidity and mortality (17.5%) both before and after birth. In utero, the peak onset of fetal bradycardia is usually between 18‐ and 24‐weeks' gestation. The degree of heart block may vary from first‐degree to third‐degree block, but most cases diagnosed in utero present with at least second‐degree or more advanced block. The overall mortality rate remains high, particularly in fetuses diagnosed with hydrops, and it is approximately between 14% and 34%. In the cohort of newborns with atrioventricular conduction impairments, 93% have a complete atrioventricular block, 5% a first‐degree block, and 2% a second‐degree block. According with current literature, two thirds of these patients will have a pacemaker before reaching adulthood. Moreover, it is widely shown that there is a high incidence of cardiomyopathy‐related heart failure (around 10%), death, or transplantation despite successful pacemaker implantation, during childhood and adolescence. In terms of prenatal treatment, studies in the past decade show increase of ventricular rate, lower myocardial inflammation, and even (at least temporary) reversion from second‐degree block after transplacental corticosteroid and intravenous gamma‐globulin administration in 25% of cases.

Cardiac Sarcoidosis
In cardiac sarcoidosis, atrioventricular blocks of different degrees are the most common clinical manifestation. Previous estimates reported that about 30% of patients aged <60 years with unexplained atrioventricular block have an undiagnosed cardiac sarcoidosis. Treatment with corticosteroids may reverse atrioventricular blocks; however, due to the unpredictable nature of conduction disorder reversibility, permanent pacing should be considered even in cases of transient block.

Myocardial Infarction
Myocardial infarction is a common cause of conduction disturbances, primarily due to ischemic effects on various parts of the conduction system and potential imbalances in the autonomic nervous system. Typically, sinoatrial and atrioventricular blocks are associated with inferior myocardial infarctions, whereas RBBBs or anterior fascicular hemiblocks are more prevalent in anterior myocardial infarctions. The new onset of LBBB may indicate the presence of multivessel disease.

Iatrogenic CCDs
Iatrogenic factors are significant contributors to CCDs, arising from drugs, immunological treatments, and complications from cardiac surgeries or procedures. Notably, immune checkpoint inhibitors, designed to enhance T‐cell responses against cancer, have improved survival rates for patients with advanced malignancies over the past decade. However, their increasing use has led to various immune‐related adverse events, including cardiovascular issues such as myocarditis, pericardial disease, vasculitis, and arrhythmias, with reported cases of bradyarrhythmia and atrioventricular block. Additionally, permanent pacemaker implantation due to bradyarrhythmias and atrioventricular conduction defects occurs in 1% to 3% of patients after coronary artery bypass grafting, 5% after mitral valve replacement, and up to 12% after aortic valve replacement.
CCDs following either surgical or transcatheter procedures in young patients often result from interventions to correct congenital heart defects. They depend on the surgical technique and the extent of the intervention, the patient's underlying anatomy, the age at the time of surgery, and the duration of the cardiopulmonary bypass.
In young patients <40 years of age undergoing atrial septal defects closure both surgically or via transcatheter procedures, the risk of advanced conduction disease is low. On the other hand, nowadays 3% of patients undergoing surgical ventricular septal defect closure present complete atrioventricular block at long‐term follow‐up. A natural history study showed occurrence rates for pacemaker implantation of 9.8 per 10 000 patient‐years. Advancements in catheter techniques and devices are leading us into the era of percutaneous closure of ventricular septal defects; with such technique complete heart block has been observed as a temporary complication in 1.07% to 1.9% of patients. There was also transient BBB in 2.8%, which tends to regress.
As is well known, patients who underwent surgical repair of tetralogy of Fallot with transannular patch and infundibulotomy present right after the procedure with complete RBBB as consequence of the surgical myocardial fibrosis at the level of the infundibulum for right ventricular outflow obstruction release. Unfortunately, this CCD, along with other characteristic feature (such as severe pulmonary regurgitation, increased right ventricular pressure, right ventricular overload, slow conducting zones) reflects high burden for ventricular arrhythmia at long‐term follow‐up in this cohort.

Nonischemic Dilated Cardiomyopathy
CCDs are also common in patients with nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) significantly affecting their long‐term clinical outcomes. In these patients, structural remodeling of the heart, marked by ventricular dilation and impaired contractility, disrupts normal electrical conduction pathways, leading to arrhythmias such as BBBs and atrioventricular blocks. These disturbances can worsen heart failure symptoms and increase the risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD). The underlying mechanisms for these conduction abnormalities typically involve myocardial fibrosis and alterations in the cardiac electrical system, resulting in delayed conduction and impaired synchronization of ventricular contraction.

Iron‐Overload Cardiomyopathy
CCDs may be also diagnosed in young patients with iron overload cardiomyopathy, which can result from conditions like hereditary hemochromatosis or repeated blood transfusions. Excess iron accumulation in the myocardium leads to structural and electrical abnormalities that disrupt normal conduction pathways. The severity of CCD typically correlates with the extent of iron deposition, generally leading to first‐ and second‐degree type I atrioventricular blocks. Additionally, iron presence in the conduction system can cause nodal disease, and its heterogeneous distribution within cardiac tissue can result in nonuniform conduction velocities, increasing the risk of arrhythmias. Thalassemia cardiomyopathy is a significant manifestation of iron overload resulting from repeated blood transfusions, which are commonly used to manage thalassemia. These iron‐overload cardiomyopathy, share several pathophysiological mechanisms related to iron deposition in the myocardium, which can lead to similar clinical outcomes, such as restrictive cardiomyopathy or DCM. Patients with beta‐thalassemia major, who rely on repeated blood transfusions for survival, exhibit a high incidence of arrhythmias, with a median age of 26 years at presentation. Among these patients, CCDs are common, showing histochemical evidence of fibrosis in the sinoatrial node, atrioventricular node, and His bundle in those not treated with chelation therapy. In recent years, iron chelation therapy has become the standard of care, leading to a significant reduction in the incidence of heart block.

Left Ventricular Noncompaction
In left ventricular noncompaction, the disorganized architecture of the myocardium is often associated with CCD, which can significantly affect long‐term outcomes. An embryonic arrest in the development of the left ventricular myocardium results in a thin, hypocontractile ventricular wall with extensive hypertrabeculated areas. As the disease progresses, myocardial fibrosis may develop, further impairing both cardiac contractility and conduction. Early identification and appropriate management are crucial for preventing the consequences of heart failure and arrhythmic complications.

Congenitally Corrected Transposition of Great Arteries
Congenitally corrected transposition of great arteries is a complex cardiac disorder with a low prevalence (about 1:33 000 life births), characterized specifically by atrioventricular and ventricular‐arterial discordance, allowing proper direction of pulmonary venous return to the aorta through a systemic morphological right ventricle and systemic venous return streaming to the pulmonary circulation through a pulmonary morphological left ventricle. Other structural anomalies may be present, including ventricular septal defects, either subvalvular or valvular pulmonary stenosis, and abnormalities of the systemic tricuspid valve with coexisting accessory pathways. The intrinsic disruption of usual anatomic relation between atrial situs and ventricular looping (namely atrial septum and ventricular septum) is associated with displacement of the atrioventricular node and the atrioventricular conduction bundle making this lesion specifically prone to time‐dependent atrioventricular conduction disorder and block. Conduction disease in this cohort may appear in any moment of life, from fetal life to adulthood, and patients can be free of symptoms for a long time before presenting any degree of atrioventricular block. Only a few of them show complete atrioventricular block initially (around 10%), whereas one third of patients has either first‐ or second‐degree atrioventricular block, instead. The incidence of complete atrioventricular block in older children and adults with congenitally corrected transposition of great arteries is 30% to 38%, instead of at the follow‐up. Furthermore, apart from the tendency of atrioventricular block progression with age and development of fibrosis of the abnormally long atrioventricular conduction bundle, the incidence of complete atrioventricular block is higher in patients undergoing arterial switch operation, rather than with Rastelli procedure. Given these data, permanent pacing therapy is often required in these patients and regarding the timing of the indication, it is usually tailored on an individual basis, depending on the fragility of the conduction system, and in some cases it may be prudent to implant prophylactic epicardial pacing leads in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, even if the patients are not in imminent need of pacing, to prevent the need of another sternotomy. Nevertheless, midterm univentricular pacing has been shown to deteriorate the systemic right ventricular dyssynchrony and therefore systolic function, and to worsen the tricuspid regurgitation. The upgrade to biventricular and nowadays conduction system pacing has demonstrated to improve significantly right ventricular fractional area change and New York Heart Association functional class. Moreover, there are data suggesting that patients undergoing primary biventricular implantation for heart block do not develop ventricular dysfunction during short‐term follow‐up.

Familial Forms
Familial forms of CCDs may occur as isolated conditions or in association with structural heart disease or metabolic disorders.

Isolated Forms
Mutations in genes encoding ion channels are the primary cause of familial CCDs, with the SCN5A (sodium voltage‐gated channel alpha subunit 5) and the TRPM4 (transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M member 4) being the most implicated. The SCN5A gene encodes the cardiac sodium channel NaV1.5, which mediates the inward sodium current in the heart. Mutations in SCN5A can lead to a wide range of phenotypes, primarily inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, with specific molecular defects influencing the resulting phenotype. Gain‐of‐function mutations typically cause increased inward sodium current, manifesting as long‐QT syndrome Type 3 or early‐onset arrhythmic forms. In contrast, loss‐of‐function mutations are generally associated with familial CCD, Brugada, and sick sinus syndrome. Notably, the same SCN5A mutation can produce different phenotypes within a family. Moreover, the TRPM4 gene, encoding a Ca2+‐activated channel found in pacemaker cells, Purkinje fibers, and both atrial and ventricular tissues, has been linked to CCDs.

CCDs Associated With Cardiomyopathies
A common form of CCD in young adults is associated with DCM due to mutations in the lamin A/C (LMNA) gene, which encodes the lamin A/C protein essential for nuclear envelope stability. Variants in the LMNA gene are found in approximately 6% of these patients, increasing up to 10% in familial cases and up to 33% in those with both DCM and CCDs. In young adults, early conduction defects and arrhythmias often present before left ventricular dysfunction and dilation occur, typically manifesting symptoms in the third or fourth decade of life.

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Conduction abnormalities are frequently observed in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, including LBBB and RBBB. Moreover, especially when associated with specific genotypes, conduction delays may progress to advanced or complete atrioventricular blocks, which may occur in up to 15% of patients, necessitating pacemaker implantation. Additionally, conduction abnormalities are frequent sequelae to septal reduction therapies due to the anatomic location of the bundle branches.

Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy
The term “arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy” is used to describe a group of diseases characterized by pathological changes primarily due to fibrofatty replacement of myocardial tissue that disrupt normal electrical conduction and can lead to life‐threatening arrhythmias. The overall arrhythmia burden in arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy is significant, and although bradyarrhythmias may not be the primary concern, they can still occur in this patient population. Conduction abnormalities often manifest as intraventricular conduction delays, reflecting alterations in the affected ventricle's conduction system. However, atrioventricular blocks have been described in 10% of the population with arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy.

CCDs Associated With Metabolic and Neuromuscolar Disorders
Other forms of CCD arise from metabolic disorders that can mimic cardiomyopathies. One such condition is Anderson–Fabry disease, an X‐linked lysosomal disorder caused by alpha‐galactosidase deficiency, leading to the accumulation of globotriaosylceramide and globotriaosylsphingosine in various organs, including the heart. Fibrosis and conduction tissue involvement contribute to ventricular arrhythmias and conduction disturbances, with a short PR interval as an early finding and more advanced atrioventricular block occurring later. In a study of young patients with Anderson–Fabry disease, 6% required devices for bradyarrhythmias. PRKAG2 glycogen storage cardiomyopathy is another rare inherited disorder caused by mutations in the PRKAG2 gene, leading to pseudohypertrophy of the ventricles and progressive conduction impairment, often necessitating pacemaker implantation. Neuromuscular diseases, such as myotonic dystrophy types 1 and 2, Emery–Dreifuss muscular dystrophy, and limb‐girdle muscular dystrophy type 1B, may also present with cardiac manifestations, including conduction disease and arrhythmias. Emery–Dreifuss muscular dystrophy is particularly associated with progressive conduction abnormalities that can lead to atrioventricular block and SCD. Approximately 80% of patients with myotonic dystrophy type 1 experience cardiac issues, with life‐threatening complications like atrioventricular or intraventricular conduction defects. In contrast, myotonic dystrophy type 2 has less severe cardiac involvement, involving about 10% to 20%, typically limited to first‐degree atrioventricular block and BBB. Mitochondrial myopathies, such as Kearns–Sayre syndrome, affect about 50% of patients with cardiac complications, most commonly presenting as conduction disease that may progress to complete atrioventricular block and SCD.

CCD Associated With Congenital Heart Disease
Holt–Oram syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant inherited disease (prevalence between 0.7 and 1 per 100 000 births), determined by mutation in T‐box transcription factor 5 gene (chromosome 12), a member of T‐box transcription family, which is primarily known for its role in cardiac septation, in pattering of cardiac conduction system and forelimb development. In fact, patients with such mutation characteristically present defects of either cardiac interatrial or interventricular septum, abnormalities of upper forelimb. Rhythm disturbances are found in 30% of cases. Arrhythmias are more frequent with missense variants rather than other variants, and may present several conduction disorders, such as sinus bradycardia, various degrees atrioventricular block, and early onset of atrial fibrillation/flutter, even in the absence of overt structural heart disease.
The gene NKX2‐5 is located in chromosome 5 and is an essential homeobox transcription factor for embryologic cardiac development. It was the first gene associated with congenital heart disease in humans, and nowadays its relation with atrial septal defects (the signature phenotype of the disease), conduction disorders, complex congenital heart disease, and different phenotypes of DCMs is well demonstrated. Most patients present with early onset of various degrees of atrioventricular block, characteristically not related with atrial defects or surgery. Atrial fibrillation/flutter is documented in about one third of patients, and a not negligible portion of this cohort experiences ventricular arrhythmias and SCD, more commonly associated with cardiomyopathy. Given the increased risk of SCD in the cohort, it is a good clinical practice to screen familial atrial septal defects for NKX2‐5 mutation to better evaluate the risk of life‐threatening arrhythmias and to assess the indication of implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator implantation.

DIAGNOSTIC WORKUP
Young patients exhibiting signs or symptoms of CCDs may have concealed heart or undiagnosed extracardiac diseases. A comprehensive diagnostic workup for those requiring pacemaker at an early age could help identify these underlying conditions, stratifying risk for adverse outcomes and guiding therapeutic strategies. In young adults, CCDs can present asymptomatically or with a range of symptoms, including syncope, dizziness, fatigue, heart failure, and, in rare cases, SCD. Accurate diagnosis involves correlating symptoms with rhythm disturbances through detailed history‐taking, physical exams, and noninvasive tests like 12‐lead ECGs, 24‐hour Holter monitoring, exercise testing, and implantable loop recorders. A holistic assessment is essential to identify extracardiac conditions that may contribute to CCDs, such as myotonic dystrophies and Kearns–Sayre syndrome.
Electrophysiological studies are not frequently used in these cases. Indeed, the interpretation of a 12‐lead ECG and a 24‐hour Holter monitor is often sufficient to identify and classify conduction disturbances. However, in certain scenarios, electrophysiological studies may be essential. One such case may be borderline atrioventricular conduction disturbances in patients with ambiguous or unclear symptoms where intracardiac recordings can further differentiate between supra‐Hisian, intra‐Hisian, and infra‐Hisian blocks.
Furthermore, situations involving patients with intraventricular conduction delays and unexplained syncope may benefit from electrophysiological studies for diagnostic clarification. The diagnostic panel should also include an initial evaluation with echocardiography and, especially in cases suspected of structural cardiomyopathy, further assessment with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. For instance, all young patients with BBBs should be evaluated for underlying structural heart disease. A comprehensive evaluation using these tests is often sufficient for case assessment and guiding subsequent management strategies.

ECG
Various degrees of atrioventricular blocks can be the initial and only sign of disease. In general, an accurate ECG interpretation is crucial in diagnosing CCDs, as certain ECG signs are pathognomonic and can help identify the underlying cause of conduction disorders. For example, in cardiac sarcoidosis, the ECG typically shows a RBBB or LBBB, QRS fragmentation, increased T‐wave amplitude in lead augmented vector right, and T‐wave inversion. Conversely, approximately one third of patients with DCM exhibit LBBB, which may precede structural heart changes and indicates a poor prognosis. Furthermore, early conduction abnormalities such as sinus bradycardia, sinus node dysfunction, and atrioventricular block are common in DCM linked to LMNA gene variants (Figure 3).

CARDIOVASCULAR IMAGING
Cardiovascular imaging, particularly echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging are essential for evaluating young patients with CCDs. Echocardiography serves as the first‐line imaging tool, revealing cardiac structure and function, and identifying subtle abnormalities like left ventricular hypertrophy or valvular dysfunction. CMR can assess myocardial fibrosis related to genetic mutations in genes such as SCN5A, TRPM4, and LMNA. However, the presence of LBBB can affect CMR image quality, causing artifacts in T1 mapping due to mechanical dyssynchrony. Manual correction of endocardial and epicardial contours can enhance T1 mapping accuracy. Furthermore, DCM and LBBB often coexist in young patients, complicating diagnosis; indeed, LBBB may lead to DCM or vice versa. In this scenario, CMR can clarify this relationship by showing global myocardial thinning in DCM cases that precede LBBB, whereas preserved lateral wall thickness may indicate LBBB occurring first.
In patients with Fabry disease, CMR can detect early cardiac involvement through native T1 mapping, with low T1 values indicating glycosphingolipid overload. Additionally, CMR often shows late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) affecting midwall or subepicardial regions. Conversely, in cardiac sarcoidosis, CMR may reveal edema during the acute phase as well as LGE patterns involving the interventricular septum.
Dilated hypokinetic phenotypes, resulting from myocarditis, autoimmune diseases, and genetic conditions such as LMNA mutations may present myocardial edema which appears as hyperintensities on T2‐weighted images, sometimes associated with LGE. Data regarding CMR tissue characterization in patients with Lyme disease are scant; however, some previous investigations reported LGE with a subepicardial distribution. In contrast, Chagas cardiomyopathy typically presents as DCM with fibrosis affecting the left ventricle's posterior and apical regions. LMNA‐associated cardiomyopathy generally shows mid‐myocardial fibrosis even in asymptomatic carriers, with LGE linked to ventricular arrhythmias. Finally, dystrophinopathies like Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy often exhibit subepicardial fibrosis resembling viral myocarditis patterns. Decreased postcontrast T1 values in Duchenne muscular dystrophy may inform therapeutic strategies aimed at improving patient outcomes.

GENETIC TESTS
Current European guidelines recommend genetic testing for patients <50 with progressive CCD. Familial CCD without structural heart disease typically results from mutations in cardiac ion channel genes, whereas mutations related to structural heart disease often involve transcription factors, enzymes, or structural proteins. Given the overlap between arrhythmic and structural inherited phenotypes, a comprehensive gene panel that includes both types should be recommended. If a gene mutation is identified in the index case, a genetic counseling is indicated for the proband and his family. Genetic testing should be extended to first‐degree relatives, although asymptomatic children may defer testing due to age‐related factors and incomplete penetrance. In general, regular monitoring of asymptomatic carriers is essential to detect any progression of symptoms or cardiac conduction issues. Despite these recommendations, genetic testing remains underused, leading to potential underdiagnosis of familial CCD, which could increase the risk of heart failure, tachyarrhythmias, or SCD. Notably, mutations involving SCN5A, DES, and LMNA genes are those associated with a higher risk of SCD.

To mitigate the risk of ventricular pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy, some programming strategies, such as managing atrioventricular delay with atrioventricular hysteresis and switching to AAI‐DDD mode in patients with sinus node dysfunction and high‐grade atrioventricular block, are recommended. However, over the past decade, conduction system pacing, has emerged as the preferred option for maintaining physiological left ventricular activation and avoiding the long‐term effects of chronic pacing However, improved diagnostic evaluation can identify patients who may benefit from alternative therapies, such as immunosuppressive treatment for cardiac sarcoidosis or implantable cardioverter ‐defibrillators for those at risk of SCD. In particular, the 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend considering implantable cardioverter ‐defibrillators over pacemakers in LMNA‐related conduction disease and suggest permanent pacing in Kearns–Sayre syndrome due to the risk of complete atrioventricular block. For genetically inherited hypertrophic cardiomyopathy subtypes prone to atrioventricular block, such as Fabry and Danon diseases, implantable cardioverter ‐defibrillatorsor cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator should be considered for symptomatic bradycardia patients with left ventricular ejection fraction ≤35% or those meeting SCD primary prevention criteria.

LONG‐TERM OUTCOMES AND FOLLOW‐UP
Although treatment is often straightforward, identifying the underlying cause can be complex and significantly affects management and long‐term follow‐up. Currently, there is no standardized diagnostic protocol for young patients with CCDs, and the cause remains unidentified in half of the cases. Although older patients with conduction disorders without structural heart disease have survival rates like those observed in the general population, previous investigations reported that patients receiving their first pacemaker before age 50 faced a 3‐ to 4‐fold increased risk of death or hospitalization for heart failure, ventricular tachyarrhythmia, or cardiac arrest. Moreover, this risk was highest within the first 5 years post implantation, particularly for those with persistent atrioventricular block. These findings suggest that young patients without an identified underlying cause require more intensive follow‐up. Research indicates that myocardial biopsies or cardiac imaging can reveal cardiac sarcoidosis in 25% to 34% of young and middle‐aged patients with unexplained atrioventricular block. However, the recommendations regarding the follow‐up of young adults with CCDs are lacking and cardiologists often face challenging situations in “no guidelines land.”

UNMET CLINICAL NEEDS: FILLING THE GAPS
Despite guidelines recommend genetic tests for young patients with CCDs, they remain underused for multifactorial reasons including limited knowledge, resource availability, and costs. Furthermore, many young adults with CCDs initially present with noncardiac symptoms, leading to delayed recognition of cardiac involvement. Therefore, a multidisciplinary approach could improve diagnosis and management of young adults with CCDs, particularly those with multiorgan syndromes. To mitigate the current unmet clinical needs in the diagnosis of young adults with CCDs, the international scientific community should initiate efforts to enhance data quality and quantity regarding these conditions. Further studies are needed to define their epidemiological scope, understand disease progression, and identify long‐term outcome predictors. To this regard, large multicenter studies based on prospective registries may yield more practical insights than randomized controlled trials due to the heterogeneity of these disorders. Additionally, raising awareness among international guideline committees and all specialists who encounter these patients is crucial. Establishing multidisciplinary teams can facilitate better diagnosis and treatment. Identifying “red flags” for early recognition is essential, alongside promoting research into nongenetic markers that aid diagnosis while awaiting genetic test results. Finally, centralized management in specialized centers is vital for implementing tailored diagnostic, therapeutic, and follow‐up pathways in an era focused on precision medicine and gene‐tailored clinical management.

CONCLUSIONS
CCDs in young adults are increasingly prevalent and necessitate a systematic approach for both initial evaluation and long‐term management to enhance the recognition of underlying diseases. A structured diagnostic workup should be implemented to rule out potential secondary causes, and primary forms of CCD must be identified and thoroughly evaluated through genetic analysis and family screening to assess progression risk and establish appropriate follow‐up. Acquired forms should also be promptly diagnosed and treated. Establishing dedicated multidisciplinary teams and raising awareness about CCDs will be essential in reducing the burden of these arrhythmic conditions in young adults.
