Navigating the delicate balance of the transorbital approach
The transorbital approach (TOA) is a significant advance in minimally invasive neurosurgery, eliminating the need for the extensive craniotomy and brain retraction typically associated with traditional methods. By accessing deep-brain and skull base lesions through a small periorbital incision, TOA significantly reduces surgical trauma, accelerates recovery, and offers superior esthetics. The well-equipped instruments and navigation system provide a magnified, meticulous view, proving effective for complex lesions near the orbital-cranial junction. However, the technique is challenged by the “narrow passage” and cannot be applied to all skull base lesions. Lateral orbitotomy, often performed via an upper eyelid crease incision, is a common corridor that requires careful navigation and anatomic knowledge to facilitate surgical dissection and minimize complications. Postoperative complications are usually transient, including periorbital edema, diplopia, and ptosis. Although serious long-term sequelae are rare, careful manipulation of orbital tissue and pupillary monitoring are crucial to prevent permanent nerve injury or visual impairment. TOA is a safe and valuable technique, provided there is careful patient selection and a high level of surgical expertise.

Introduction
The evolution of modern neurosurgery has been profoundly shaped by the pursuit of minimally invasive surgery. While the traditional transcranial approach offers a wide surgical field and stable access, it often necessitates craniotomy and brain retraction, carrying risks of complications and delayed recovery. To overcome these limitations, the transorbital approach (TOA) using neuro-endoscopy has emerged. This introduction will set the stage by highlighting how TOA represents a new paradigm that addresses the shortcomings of conventional methods.
TOA involves accessing deep-brain lesions through a small incision around the eye, navigating through the orbit. One of the primary advantages of this method is minimal invasiveness. By avoiding a large craniotomy, TOA significantly reduces surgical trauma and the risk of neurological damage from brain retraction. This results in faster patient recovery and an enhanced quality of life after surgery. Utilizing a high-definition endoscope, surgeons gain a magnified view of the surgical site, allowing for complete removal of tumors while preserving delicate surrounding blood vessels and nerves. This is particularly effective for complex lesions at the orbital-cranial junction or in the cavernous sinus. This approach leaves minimal facial scarring, which is a significant esthetic advantage for patients.
The efficacy and safety of TOA have been consistently demonstrated for skull base lesions, reinforcing its growing importance in neurosurgery. Despite its many benefits, TOA is not a universal solution. It has certain limitations that must be addressed. The narrow passage of the orbit limits the surgical view, making it challenging to apply the technique to large lesions or those with complex relationships to surrounding structures. Thus, the suitability of TOA is highly dependent on the lesion’s location and size, meaning it cannot be applied to all cases.
These challenges are being addressed through advancements in preoperative imaging, intraoperative navigation systems, and ongoing efforts to enhance surgical teams’ capabilities. The use of endoscopic tools in a confined space demands a high level of expertise and extensive surgical experience. For example, an endoscope equipped with an external sheath connected to a manual or automated irrigation system is useful for maintaining a clear endoscopic view, thereby minimizing the need for frequent endoscope movements in and out of the surgical corridor. An endoscopic holding system can also help maintain a steady endoscopic view without the need for assistants.

Various Corridors to the Skull Base
The type of periorbital incision can be chosen to provide a proper approach angle or the shortest corridor to the target lesion in the skull base. Lateral orbitotomy using an upper eyelid crease incision is a frequent entry point to spheno-orbital meningiomas, cavernous sinus lesions, and trigeminal schwannomas involving Meckel’s cave. In addition, inferior orbitotomy can be used for the infratemporal fossa and pterygopalatine fossa lesions. Otherwise, superior orbitotomy can be performed for lesions in the anterior cranial fossa and middle cranial fossa, while medial orbitotomy can be used for orbital apex and suprasellar lesions. Lateral Orbitotomy with Eyelid Crease Incision
For endoscopic TOA, special instruments and a navigation system should be prepared to address deep-seated lesions. A 0° rod-lens endoscope is introduced to visualize the limited surgical space. An electric robotic holding arm (ENDOFIXexo, AKTORmed, Germany) can be used to hold and guide the endoscope, allowing the surgeon to use the surgical instruments bimanually. While holding the orbital contents with a silastic sheet, the lateral orbital wall, the greater wing of the sphenoid bone, is drilled with a diamond burr or a cutting burr to keep the surgical field efficient. For tumor dissection, fine instruments of scissors, nerve hooks, various retractors, and dissectors need to be prepared, and for tumor debulking, a Coblator or an ultrasonic aspirator is helpful. A neurosurgical navigation system is essential in safely traversing the complex anatomy.
Lateral orbitotomy with eyelid crease incision is a typical surgical procedure for TOA. Corneal protector and lubricant are applied before the surgical procedure to prevent exposure keratopathy. For lateral orbitotomy, an upper eyelid crease incision can provide a relatively bloodless dissection plane between the orbital septum and the orbicularis oculi muscle layer, resulting in an esthetically acceptable scar. After reaching the lateral orbital rim, a periosteal incision is applied with monopolar cautery. With periosteal elevation from the incision site, the lateral canthal tendon is dissected from the lateral orbital tubercle together with the periosteum. It is reattached to the original site during closure of the periosteum after surgery. Periosteal dissection proceeds from the zygomatic bone to the greater wing of the sphenoid bone, which is a critical landmark because it is the target for drilling to access the middle cranial fossa, cavernous sinus, and other deep-seated lesions.
The superior orbital fissure is a crucial gateway at the junction of the lateral orbital wall and the roof. It contains multiple cranial nerves and the superior ophthalmic vein. Surgeons must exercise extreme caution to avoid injuring these nerves and vessels.
The recurrent meningeal artery, a branch of the lacrimal artery, can typically be seen before the dissection reaches the superior orbital fissure [Figure 2]. The structure is an essential surgical landmark during TOA; it should be identified carefully because bleeding from this small artery may obscure the surgical field. Awareness of its potential course and location is crucial for preventing and managing intraoperative hemorrhage. Identifying the recurrent meningeal artery during surgery can help the surgeon confirm entry into the extradural plane of the middle cranial fossa. Its presence within the bone or on the dural surface serves as an anatomical landmark indicating that the dissection has extended beyond the confines of the orbit.

The Meningo-Orbital Band (MOB) is a fibrous connective tissue band between the periorbita and the adjacent dura mater, superior and lateral to the superior orbital fissure [Figure 3]. The MOB serves as a crucial surgical landmark in TOA. Carefully releasing the MOB is a key step in gaining access from the orbit into the intracranial space. Once divided, it creates a pathway to the extradural space of the middle cranial fossa, providing improved visualization around the superior orbital fissure and the anterior clinoid process.
An intraoperative view. For the removal of an orbitocranial schwannoma on the right side, the greater wing of the sphenoid is drilled out, and the temporal lobe of the brain is exposed. At the orbital apex, the meningo-orbital band is seen (arrowhead). Careful coagulation or division of the band helps in achieving hemostasis of the small blood vessels inside the band.
The inferior orbital fissure is located at the junction of the lateral orbital wall and the orbital floor, and contains branches of the maxillary nerves and the inferior ophthalmic vein.
While bony dissection proceeds, orbital retraction should be gentle and not compromise circulation to the orbital tissue. A silicone sheet can be used for gentle and effective orbital retraction [Figures 2 and 3]. It is also helpful when removing cranio-orbital tumors, where orbital fat prolapse can hinder the surgical field after orbital tumor resection. During orbital tissue retraction, frequent pupillary monitoring is mandatory to preserve orbital tissue function.
A lateral orbital bony window can be created to improve exposure, providing greater horizontal and vertical endoscopic movement, which might broaden the indications for TOA. The osteotomy can be performed using the standard lateral wall osteotomy method in the lateral orbitotomy approach, from 5 mm superior to the frontozygomatic suture to the zygomatic arch. The extent can be modified superiorly or inferiorly, depending on the location of the intracranial lesion, facilitating vertical endoscopic movement.

Postoperative Orbital Complications
Postoperative complications from TOA are generally considered minor and often transient, but they can significantly impact a patient’s recovery and quality of life. Complications are typically categorized as early (occurring within a few months) and persistent (occurring in the long term).
Early complications may include periorbital edema and ecchymosis, which are almost universal after TOA due to tissue manipulation. This usually subsides within a few weeks. Diplopia can develop after TOA and is a common temporary complication resulting from the manipulation of extraocular muscles. It typically resolves as the muscles recover their function. Ptosis can occur due to swelling or minor injury to the levator muscle. In most cases, it improves over time.
Persistent complications can be associated with TOA. While rare, some complications may persist beyond the initial recovery period. These are more frequently associated with complex lesions. Although most cases of diplopia or ptosis are transient, some patients may experience long-term or permanent symptoms that may require further intervention. Enophthalmos can be a persistent complication, particularly if the bony orbit is compromised during the procedure and not properly reconstructed, especially in cases involving spheno-orbital meningiomas. In cases with a significant orbital bony defect, orbital wall reconstruction should be considered to avoid this complication. Visual impairment or loss is a very rare but serious complication, primarily caused by direct injury to the optic nerve during surgery or a postoperative retrobulbar hematoma compressing the nerve. Judicious pupillary monitoring is needed while orbital retraction is performed. If pupillary dilation is present, immediately release retraction and observe for normalization of the pupil.
No significant adverse events related to TOA have been reported. No permanent ophthalmological complications were found in 18 cases of spheno-orbital meningioma surgery with TOA. In Austria et al.’s series of 35 patients with intradural, extradural, cavernous sinus and Meckel’s cave, and sphenoid wing meningioma, newly-developed extraocular motility deficits after TOA were found in 38% of the patients, of which 100% recovered within 182 days. Diplopia developed in 25% of the patients, but resolved within 45 days. All postoperative ptosis cases recovered within 100 days. However, 66% of subjects had persistent postoperative V1 or V2 hypoesthesia, preexistent or newly developed. Sensory nerve abnormalities were related to the tumor removal of the schwannoma, which contains the sensory nerves, or to the surgical approach itself.
Although permanent sequelae related to this surgical procedure are very rare, the surgeon must retract and manipulate the orbital tissue to create a safe working channel. This can lead to temporary or, in rare cases, permanent extraocular muscle dysfunction. Manipulation of the orbital tissue can cause temporary or prolonged periorbital swelling, and damage to the nerves can lead to persistent sensory impairment or neuropathic pain in the periorbital region.
In conclusion, while TOA is a valuable and safe technique for many intracranial lesions, a thorough understanding of its potential effects on delicate orbital structures is essential. Careful patient selection and a skilled surgical team are critical for minimizing morbidity and achieving successful long-term outcomes.
