Tree-based ensemble learning models for protein-protein interactions detection: a review and experimental evaluation
Motivation
Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) are central to many biological processes, influencing cellular functions and offering potential therapeutic insights. The increasing availability of genomic and proteomic data has propelled computational approaches, particularly machine learning (ML), to the forefront of PPI prediction, addressing limitations of traditional experimental methods such as scalability and cost. This paper presents an in-depth review of modern ensemble learning models for PPI prediction, specifically focusing on XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM, and Random Forest. Ensemble learning models are particularly noteworthy for their ability to surpass traditional approaches by leveraging the combined strengths of multiple base learners. Unlike previous reviews that often concentrate on theoretical comparisons or a limited selection of methods, this study offers a balanced analysis that includes both empirical findings and experimental evaluations. The review examines recent advancements in the field and assesses these models based on critical factors such as scalability, interpretability, accuracy, and efficiency, providing a structured framework to highlight nuanced differences among the techniques.
Scientific Contribution
The article evaluates several ML techniques for PPI detection, with LightGBM and XGBoost emerging as the most effective methods due to their high accuracy and computational efficiency, particularly for large and complex datasets. Gradient Boosting and Random Forest also demonstrate strong performance, with Gradient Boosting excelling in capturing non-linear relationships and Random Forest offering robust interpretability. LightGBM stands out for its scalability and efficiency, while XGBoost benefits from built-in regularization to reduce overfitting.
Results
Experimental evaluations across three benchmark datasets—DIP, HPRD, and STRING—demonstrated that LightGBM consistently achieved the highest performance among all models, with average accuracy reaching up to 86%, and top sensitivity, specificity, and precision scores. XGBoost followed closely, showing strong generalization and robustness due to its regularization capabilities. Gradient Boosting provided competitive accuracy but lagged slightly in computational efficiency. Random Forest, while the most interpretable, showed comparatively lower sensitivity but maintained solid precision, highlighting its strength in minimizing false positives. Overall, LightGBM and XGBoost outperformed other methods in handling large, complex, and imbalanced PPI datasets. These results affirm the suitability of advanced ensemble learning techniques for enhancing PPI prediction and offer practical guidance on selecting models based on performance priorities and application constraints.

Introduction
Proteins, composed of amino acids, are integral to fundamental biological functions, including DNA replication and molecular transport within organisms. They play a pivotal role in Protein-protein interactions (PPIs), which underpin numerous biological processes. Disruptions in these interactions can result in diseases such as cervical leukemia and tuberculosis. PPIs represent specific interactions between proteins, shaped by forces like electrostatic interactions, and they highlight the intricate molecular complexity within cells. These interactions are essential for forming large molecular assemblies. Moreover, they are critical for various cellular functions, providing valuable insights into cellular mechanics and potential therapeutic applications.]. The study of protein interactions has advanced significantly, leading to the development of PPI networks that exhibit scale-free properties. This field has received significant attention in high-impact journals, including Nature, Science, and the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. With the growing volume of PPI data, efficient management has become crucial, necessitating the development of specialized tools and databases. PPI data is collected using high-throughput screening techniques, computational studies, and literature mining approaches. Integrating these diverse sources of data enables ongoing advancements in research. Repositories such as BIND, DIP, IntAct, and HPRD have been established to organize this data systematically.
With the increasing availability of genomic and proteomic data, computational approaches have become indispensable for predicting PPIs. ML has demonstrated exceptional capability in identifying patterns within complex biological datasets. By integrating diverse biological data, ML techniques offer insights that traditional methods cannot achieve. These advancements have the potential to revolutionize our understanding of biological systems and drive innovations in biomedical research. However, several challenges must be addressed to develop robust ML models, including managing imbalanced datasets, selecting relevant features, optimizing hyperparameters, and validating predictions.
This paper provides a comprehensive review of contemporary ensemble learning models for PPI prediction, focusing on XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM, and Random Forest. Unlike existing reviews, which often emphasize theoretical comparisons or a narrow range of methods, this study integrates both empirical and experimental evaluations to deliver a holistic analysis. The analysis explores state-of-the-art advancements and evaluates these models using key criteria such as scalability, interpretability, accuracy, and efficiency, enabling a structured approach to identifying subtle distinctions among methods.
What sets this paper apart is its dual focus on experimental and empirical assessments, which together provide a nuanced understanding of the strengths and limitations of each model. XGBoost, for instance, is recognized for its computational efficiency and advanced regularization, making it highly effective in noisy biological datasets. Gradient Boosting offers a balance of interpretability and performance, while LightGBM, with its leaf-wise growth and histogram-based algorithms, is uniquely suited for large-scale datasets. Random Forest further enhances prediction reliability with its bagging approach and robustness in handling high-dimensional data. This systematic comparison equips researchers with actionable insights for selecting the most appropriate technique for specific PPI prediction tasks.
The significance of ensemble learning models lies in their ability to outperform traditional methods by combining the strengths of multiple base learners. By addressing limitations in existing reviews and providing a detailed empirical evaluation, this paper establishes itself as a valuable resource in advancing bioinformatics research. It highlights the transformative potential of XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM, and Random Forest in driving innovation in PPI detection, setting a new benchmark for ensemble-based methodologies.

XGBoost for PPI prediction is built on several integral components that contribute to its high predictive performance and efficiency. The foundation of XGBoost lies in gradient boosting, where an ensemble of decision trees is trained sequentially, with each tree aiming to correct the residual errors of its predecessors. This iterative optimization is driven by a regularized objective function that balances model accuracy with complexity, making it particularly effective for avoiding overfitting in complex PPI datasets. XGBoost also employs second-order gradient information (i.e., both the gradient and the Hessian) to make more accurate and stable updates during training. An important component of XGBoost is its sparsity-aware algorithm, which efficiently handles missing or sparse biological features often present in PPI data. Furthermore, XGBoost supports parallel computation and tree pruning strategies, allowing it to scale well for large-scale PPI networks while maintaining strong performance. Gradient Boosting (G-Boosting) for PPI prediction comprises several essential components that contribute to its predictive power and adaptability. At its core, G-Boosting builds an ensemble of weak learners—typically decision trees—sequentially, where each new tree is trained to correct the errors made by the combined ensemble of previous trees. The model optimizes a differentiable loss function, such as logistic loss or exponential loss, through gradient descent, allowing it to effectively handle the binary or multi-class classification challenges present in PPI tasks. One of the key components is its use of learning rate (shrinkage), which controls the contribution of each tree to the final model, enhancing generalization and reducing overfitting. G-Boosting also incorporates subsampling strategies, where random subsets of the data are used to build each tree, further improving robustness and reducing variance. Also, feature importance evaluation within G-Boosting helps identify the most influential biological features driving protein interaction predictions. Figure 2 illustrates this process.

The key components of LightGBM for PPI prediction include the gradient boosting framework, which builds decision trees sequentially to minimize prediction errors. Its leaf-wise tree growth strategy allows deeper trees in high-error regions, capturing complex interactions between protein features more effectively than level-wise methods. LightGBM’s histogram-based algorithm discretizes continuous values into bins, significantly reducing computation time and memory usage when handling large PPI datasets. The feature importance mechanism helps identify biologically relevant attributes, such as sequence-derived or structural features, that contribute most to protein interactions. Additionally, its regularization components (e.g., L1 and L2) help control overfitting, which is crucial in high-dimensional, noisy biological data environments. Rationale for using LightGBM in PPI prediction
LightGBM is well-suited for PPI prediction due to its high computational efficiency and scalability, which are essential when analyzing large-scale omics datasets. It employs a histogram-based learning algorithm and leaf-wise tree growth strategy, enabling faster training with reduced memory usage compared to traditional gradient boosting methods. PPI prediction tasks often involve complex, non-linear relationships between protein features, and LightGBM effectively captures these patterns through its ensemble of decision trees. The model can handle categorical, continuous, and sparse input features, making it adaptable to diverse biological data representations such as amino acid sequences, physicochemical properties, and structural descriptors. LightGBM also includes built-in mechanisms for regularization and overfitting control, which are critical in high-dimensional biomedical applications. Its ability to output feature importance rankings aids in the biological interpretation of the model, helping identify key predictors of protein interactions. Overall, LightGBM offers a powerful, interpretable, and resource-efficient solution for accurate PPI prediction.

The Random Forest algorithm for PPI prediction is composed of several key components that work together to enhance classification accuracy and robustness. At its core, Random Forest builds an ensemble of decision trees, where each tree is trained on a different subset of the protein interaction data using bootstrapped sampling, allowing the model to capture diverse interaction patterns. To further increase variability among the trees, only a random subset of features—such as amino acid composition, domain similarity, or structural properties—is considered at each node split. This feature randomness not only improves generalization but also reduces the risk of overfitting, which is especially important in high-dimensional biological datasets. The final prediction for a protein pair is obtained through majority voting among the trees, which stabilizes predictions and reduces variance. Additionally, Random Forest provides feature importance scores, helping researchers interpret which biological features contribute most to interaction prediction, thus adding a layer of explainability to the model. Overall recommendations
Large datasets/heavy class imbalance: Prefer LightGBM or XGBoost for the best accuracy–throughput trade-off; use class weights or sampling to address imbalance and cap leaf depth in LightGBM to avoid overfitting.

Comparative analysis of execution times
LightGBM: LightGBM’s performance advantage stems from its Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB) and Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) mechanisms, which substantially reduce computational complexity during split finding. On the Tesla V100, these optimizations maximize GPU memory utilization and minimize redundant feature traversal, resulting in consistent, low-latency inference even with large feature sets.
XGBoost: Although XGBoost uses highly parallelized GPU histogram methods, it incurs minor overhead from device quantile building and data structure conversion (e.g., DMatrix creation). These operations slightly reduce its per-sample efficiency compared to LightGBM. Nevertheless, its memory-aware parallelism provides excellent scalability, making it ideal when accuracy or dataset heterogeneity outweighs minimal runtime differences.
G-Boosting: The hybrid DNN + XGBoost pipeline benefits from GPU acceleration but remains limited by two-stage dependency between neural feature extraction and gradient boosting classification. The GPU eliminates most DNN computation cost, yet sequential execution introduces unavoidable latency overhead. This trade-off highlights the balance between model expressiveness and execution efficiency in hybrid designs.
Random Forest: Random Forest achieves competitive latency despite running solely on CPU because of its embarrassingly parallel tree traversal structure. On multi-core CPUs, inference across trees scales almost linearly with thread count. Its deterministic inference cost and memory-bound nature make it efficient for smaller or CPU-only deployments.

Conclusion
Empirical Evaluations: The empirical analysis underscores that LightGBM and XGBoost consistently outperform other techniques across multiple metrics, achieving the highest accuracy (86% and 85%, respectively) and F1-scores (0.85 and 0.84). These methods demonstrate exceptional precision and recall, making them ideal for handling complex PPI data and imbalanced datasets. Gradient Boosting shows competitive performance, effectively capturing non-linear relationships, but it lags slightly in efficiency and computational scalability. Random Forest emerges as a strong alternative for its interpretability, although it incurs increased computational demand for large datasets.
Experimental Evaluations: The experimental evaluations reveal that LightGBM is the top-performing technique, consistently achieving superior sensitivity and specificity while maintaining computational efficiency. XGBoost follows closely, demonstrating robust performance and resilience to noisy data due to its regularization techniques. Gradient Boosting is effective for capturing non-linear relationships, but its slower training times may hinder scalability for large datasets. Random Forest excels in precision, reducing false positives, an essential attribute for critical biological applications.
Key Insights and Recommendations: Overall, LightGBM and XGBoost are identified as the best all-around performers for large-scale, complex datasets due to their ability to handle intricate feature interactions efficiently. Gradient Boosting serves as a strong alternative but requires careful tuning to optimize performance. Random Forest offers interpretability and high precision, making it particularly valuable for applications requiring reduced false positives.
Future Perspectives: To further enhance PPI prediction, future research should focus on integrating biologically motivated features and multi-task learning approaches to better capture the multifaceted nature of protein interactions. Advancements in hyperparameter optimization, distributed computing frameworks, and dynamic learning algorithms are also essential to improve scalability and robustness.
This comprehensive review emphasizes the transformative impact of statistical learning algorithms in advancing PPI prediction, addressing challenges of scalability, cost, and complexity inherent in traditional experimental methods. By proposing a hierarchical taxonomy, this study provides a systematic framework for categorizing and evaluating state-of-the-art techniques. The review highlights the strengths, limitations, and suitability of key algorithms, offering actionable insights for researchers.
