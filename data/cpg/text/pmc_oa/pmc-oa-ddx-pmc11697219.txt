Evaluation of antibiofilm agents for treatment of cystic fibrosis‐related chronic rhinosinusitis
Key points
Treatment of cystic fibrosis‐related chronic rhinosinusitis should target sinonasal biofilms.
NaHCO3 salts with/without xylitol have limited antibiofilm properties, whereas rhDNAse has not.
Phage effectivity varies and depends on the phage and the combination with antibiotics.

INTRODUCTION
Currently, there is no consensus on the treatment of cystic fibrosis (CF)‐related chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS). The difference in pathophysiology between CF (neutrophilic Th1‐inflammation) and non‐CF CRS (predominantly eosinophilic Th2‐inflammation) complicates treatment validation. Increased mucus viscosity and impaired mucociliary clearance lead to bacterial colonization and biofilm formation by pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus. These biofilms lead to disease recalcitrance, poor postoperative outcomes, more outpatient visits and increased antibiotic use.
The goal of this study was to explore the in vitro efficacy of sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3)‐enriched salts with (XNI) and without (SNI) xylitol, rhDNAse, antibiotics and bacteriophages, or combinations thereof, in reducing CF‐CRS‐related biofilms.

Experiments were conducted in a synthetic CF sputum medium (SCFM2) to mimic the infectious microenvironment (Figure 1). In the prevention assay, diluted overnight cultures and antibiofilm agents (SNI, XNI, rhDNAse, bacteriophages, antibiotics, and combinations) were added to a 96‐well plate and incubated at 37°C. After 24 h, biofilms were disrupted, and CFU/mL was determined. In the treatment assay, biofilm aggregates were grown in SCFM2, followed by adding antibiofilm agents after 24 h and further incubation for 24 h at 37°C. CFU/mL was then determined.
Overview of experimental design with isolation and identification of clinical isolates obtained using a sinonasal swab in the first step. Next, biofilms were grown in a pathology‐specific growth medium (synthetic CF sputum medium; SCFM2) and treated with either sodium bicarbonate‐enriched rinsing salts with/without xylitol, rhDNAse, antibiotics, bacteriophages, or combinations thereof in prevention (left) or treatment (right) setting. As an outcome measure, differences in colony forming units/mL were measured.
Three biological replicates were performed per clinical isolate. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1), which employs paired t‐tests, Wilcoxon tests, one‐way ANOVA, or the Friedman test, depending on data distribution. p‐Values <0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS
Ten S. aureus and four P. aeruginosa sinonasal isolates were collected from 13 patients. Half of the S. aureus isolates could be infected with phage ISP, and half of the P. aeruginosa isolates were sensitive to phages PNM and/or 14‐1 (Table 1).

Treatment with SNI, XNI, rhDNAse, or antibiotics
In the prevention assay, SNI and XNI significantly reduced S. aureus (‒0.36 and ‒0.23 log CFU/mL, respectively) and P. aeruginosa (‒0.43 and ‒0.36 log CFU/mL, respectively) counts. Similarly, SNI and XNI significantly reduced S. aureus (‒0.43 and ‒0.30 log CFU/mL) and P. aeruginosa (‒0.51 log CFU/mL for both) counts in the treatment assay. No differences between SNI and XNI could be observed. rhDNAse showed no significant effect.
Sub‐minimally inhibitory concentration (MIC) levels of levofloxacin did not reduce mature S. aureus biofilms, while supra‐MIC levels did (‒0.71 log CFU/mL). Both sub‐MIC and supra‐MIC levels of ceftazidime significantly reduced P. aeruginosa biofilms, compared to control (‒0.53 and ‒0.63 log CFU/mL, respectively).

Treatment with bacteriophages
Compared to control, phage ISP significantly reduced S. aureus counts (‒2.68 log CFU/mL) in the prevention assay. No reduction was observed in mature biofilms, regardless of the phage sensitivity. Higher titers of ISP led to a reduction in CFU/mL from ‒0.57 (multiplicity of infection [MOI] of 1000) to ‒0.81 log CFU/mL (MOI of 10.000).
Phages PNM and 14‐1 significantly reduced P. aeruginosa counts in the prevention assay (‒2.50 and ‒2.70 log CFU/mL, respectively). In treatment assays, both phages reduced mature P. aeruginosa biofilms (‒0.98 and ‒0.76 log CFU/mL, respectively). Increasing MOI did not yield additional reductions.

Combination treatments
Combining S. aureus phage ISP with SNI or XNI showed no additive effect. However, phage 14‐1 combined with XNI showed an additional reduction in mature P. aeruginosa biofilms compared to 14‐1 alone (‒0.70 log CFU/mL). Combining phages with rhDNAse did not yield significantly different results from phages alone. Nevertheless, in both assays, significantly higher S. aureus counts were observed when combining phage ISP/rhDNAse, compared to ISP/SNI and phage ISP/XNI. The same was observed for P. aeruginosa phages PNM (prevention assay) and 14‐1 (treatment assay).
Combining sub‐MIC levofloxacin with phage ISP significantly lowered S. aureus counts compared to control (‒0.63 log CFU/mL), ISP (‒0.50 log CFU/mL) or antibiotics alone (‒0.42 log CFU/mL). Supra‐MIC levofloxacin levels combined with ISP showed no additional reduction. Combining supra‐MIC ceftazidime with phage PNM significantly reduced P. aeruginosa counts compared to control (‒0.87 log CFU/mL), whereas combination of sub‐MIC ceftazidime with PNM did not. Sub‐MIC levels with phage 14‐1 were effective (‒0.81 log CFU/mL, p = 0.015), whereas this was not the case for the combination of ceftazidime at supra‐MIC levels and phage 14‐1. For both phages, phage‒antibiotic combinations were equally effective as ceftazidime monotherapy.

DISCUSSION
CF‐CRS biofilms are associated with severe disease and poor therapeutic response. This study explored the ability of SNI, XNI, rhDNAse, antibiotics, and bacteriophages to inhibit and degrade S. aureus and P. aeruginosa biofilms in a CF‐adapted medium.
SNI and XNI were able to prevent and degrade biofilms, but only to a limited extent. Xylitol has previously shown inhibition of CRS‐related S. aureus and P. aeruginosa biofilms, particularly when combined with other agents such as lactoferrin. No significant difference was found between SNI and XNI, suggesting that NaHCO3 might be the active component. rhDNAse showed no antibiofilm activity, which aligns with previous findings.
Phages PNM and 14‐1 prevented and degraded P. aeruginosa biofilms effectively, while phage ISP was only effective in the prevention of S. aureus‐related biofilms. The degradation range for PNM and 14‐1 was consistent with typical phage‐mediated biofilm degradation. No additional biofilm reduction was observed when phages were combined with SNI, XNI, or rhDNAse. Interestingly, combining ISP with sub‐MIC levels of levofloxacin worked synergistically, while supra‐MIC levels were less effective.
The major limitation of this study was the use of predefined concentrations of antibiofilm agents, limiting dose‒response analysis. While the results are statistically significant, their clinical relevance remains to be confirmed in trials. Further exploration of phage‒antibiotic synergy is necessary to optimize treatment combinations in clinical settings.
