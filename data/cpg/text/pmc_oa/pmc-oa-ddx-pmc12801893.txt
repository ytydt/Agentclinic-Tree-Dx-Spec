Is the superior septal approach a safe alternative to conventional left atriotomy for mitral valve surgery? Evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis
Background
The superior septal approach (SSA) was developed to improve mitral valve exposure during surgery, particularly in patients with complex anatomy. However, concerns regarding prolonged operative times and rhythm disturbances have limited its widespread adoption. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare SSA with conventional left atriotomy (CLA) in terms of perioperative outcomes, complications, rhythm disturbances, and mortality.
Methods
A systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was performed up to August 2025 according to PRISMA guidelines. Outcomes assessed included postoperative cardiac rhythm outcomes (primary outcomes), perioperative metrics (secondary outcomes) -including pacemaker implantation, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and cross-clamp times, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and hospital stay, and complications (safety outcomes). Pooled risk ratios (RR) and mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using a random-effects model.
Results
A total of 12 cohort studies were included. Postoperative sinus rhythm (RR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.71–1.04) and atrial fibrillation (AF) (RR = 1.12, 95% CI 0.83–1.49) did not differ between SSA and CLA; junctional rhythm and complete atrioventricular block (AVB) were also comparable. Within-group (pre–post) analyses showed sinus rhythm increased after both techniques, more with SSA (RR = 1.36) than CLA (RR = 1.20), while AF changes were non-significant. Meta-regression indicated more recent publication year and higher repair (vs. replacement) proportion were associated with greater sinus rhythm benefit favoring SSA. Secondary outcomes were similar overall; pacemaker implantation was borderline higher after SSA and became significant in sensitivity analysis. Safety endpoints were also comparable between approaches.
Conclusion
The SSA provides equivalent perioperative efficiency and clinical safety compared with CLA in mitral valve surgery. Concerns about rhythm disturbances and operative burden are not supported by pooled evidence, suggesting that SSA remains a safe and valuable option, particularly in cases requiring enhanced exposure. Further high-quality prospective studies are warranted to define long-term outcomes and refine patient selection.

Introduction
Since minimally invasive mitral valve surgery is most commonly performed through a right mini-thoracotomy, a right-sided left atriotomy is considered the anatomically optimal approach for direct access to the left atrium. However, in patients with a small left atrium or a counterclockwise-rotated heart, obtaining sufficient exposure through this route can be challenging. Additionally, the placement of an atrial retractor on the left atrial roof may displace the mitral valve deeper into a narrow operative field, thereby complicating technically demanding procedures. Furthermore, in redo-cases with severe adhesions at the site of the former atriotomy, it could be difficult to perform a right-sided left re-atriotomy.
To overcome these challenges, alternative exposure techniques such as transseptal and superior septal atriotomies have been developed. These approaches provide a broader surgical field, thereby facilitating complex valve repair. The superior septal approach (SSA) has been described as providing one of the most favorable exposures. It is advantageous when visualization is compromised and intricate reconstructive maneuvers are required, as it minimizes the need for atrial retraction and allows assessment of the mitral valve without distortion. Despite these advantages, SSA has not been universally adopted because it involves a longer suture line and necessitates division of the sinoatrial nodal artery in most cases.
The comparative evidence between SSA and conventional left atriotomy (CLA) remains limited. Although SSA is thought to provide superior visualization and potentially safer surgical conditions, it has been associated with prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and aortic cross-clamp times. Several studies suggest an increased incidence of rhythm disturbances, including atrioventricular block (AVB), atrial fibrillation (AF), and supraventricular or junctional dysrhythmias following SSA, likely due to disruption of the sinus nodal artery. Conversely, other investigations report no significant difference in arrhythmic outcomes between SSA and CLA. For example, Masuda et al. observed a higher incidence of early postoperative dysrhythmias in the SSA group compared to CLA (22% vs. 4%), but this difference diminished on longer-term follow-up.
Despite three decades of clinical use, the literature remains inconclusive regarding whether the SSA confers increased risks compared to CLA, particularly in terms of rhythm disturbances, complications, and postoperative outcomes. This meta-analysis was therefore conducted to systematically compare SSA and CLA in mitral valve surgery, to clarify their relative impact on perioperative outcomes, postoperative complications, rhythm disturbances, and mortality, and to provide evidence-based guidance for surgical decision-making.

Methods

Search process
The current systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the latest PRISMA guidelines. The protocol was registered on PROSPERO with a registration number CRD420251230396. A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed (MEDLINE), Scopus, Web of Science, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from their inception to August 2025, without language or publication restrictions. Reference lists of all eligible studies and relevant reviews were also manually screened to identify additional records. The search strategy was constructed using a combination of controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to mitral valve surgery and atrial exposure approaches. Boolean operators and truncations were used to maximize sensitivity. The full search string used was: (“mitral valve surgery” OR “mitral valve repair” OR “mitral valve replacement”) AND (“superior septal approach” OR “extended vertical transseptal” OR “superior septal atriotomy”) AND (“left atriotomy” OR “lateral atriotomy” OR “conventional approach” OR “atriotomy technique”). Filters of title and abstract were used in the databases as appropriate.

Eligibility criteria and screening
Screening was conducted independently by two authors. Titles and abstracts were initially assessed, and full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved. Study selection followed a structured PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study Design) to ensure consistent inclusion across all stages of review.
Population (P): Patients undergoing mitral valve surgery (repair or replacement), with or without concomitant cardiac procedures.
Intervention (I): Use of the superior septal approach (SSA) for left atrial exposure.
Comparator (C): The conventional left atriotomy (CLA).
Outcomes (O): Studies were eligible if they reported at least one relevant perioperative or postoperative outcome, including rhythm disturbances (sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, junctional rhythm, atrioventricular block), operative metrics (cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, ICU or hospital stay), or safety endpoints (mortality, stroke, bleeding, renal failure, respiratory complications, or pacemaker implantation).
Study Design (S): Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective cohort studies, and retrospective comparative studies were included. Non-comparative case series, case reports, editorials, and reviews were excluded.
Additional exclusion criteria included:
Studies limited to non-human.
Abstract-only publications, conference proceedings due to insufficient data and inadequate peer-review.
Duplicate datasets or overlapping patient populations (in which case, the most comprehensive or recent report was retained) to avoid duplication of data.
Non-English language publications were included if sufficient methodological and outcome data could be extracted; otherwise, they were excluded after consensus discussion.

Data extraction
Data extraction was performed using a standardized form. Two authors independently extracted study characteristics such as author, year, country, design, and sample size, as well as patient demographics, including mean age, sex distribution, and type of mitral valve procedure performed. Baseline measurements of interest included preoperative rhythm status, namely sinus rhythm, AF, junctional rhythm, and paced rhythm. Outcomes were also extracted and grouped into primary outcomes (postoperative cardiac rhythm), secondary outcomes (perioperative parameters), and safety outcomes. Postoperative cardiac rhythm outcomes encompassed arrhythmias (sinus rhythm, AF, junctional rhythm, and AVB). Perioperative parameters included pacemaker implantation, CPB, aortic cross-clamp time, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and hospital length of stay. Safety outcomes included several complications such as bleeding, pulmonary complications, renal failure, low cardiac output, stroke, and mortality.

Quality assessment
The quality of included studies was appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates observational studies across three domains. The selection domain (up to four points) assessed representativeness of the exposed cohort, appropriateness of the comparison group, ascertainment of exposure, and confirmation that outcomes were not present at baseline. The comparability domain (up to two points) considered whether studies adequately adjusted for important confounders, particularly age, sex, and baseline rhythm status. The outcome domain (up to three points) assessed the objectivity of outcome measurement, adequacy of follow-up, and whether the follow-up period was sufficient for outcomes to occur. Each study could achieve a maximum of nine points, with scores of seven or more considered high quality, four to six moderate quality, and fewer than four low quality. Quality assessments were conducted independently by two authors, with discrepancies resolved by discussion.

Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was conducted for any outcome reported in at least 3 studies using Review Manager software. For dichotomous outcomes, a pooled risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated using Der Simonian–Laird model, while continuous outcomes were analyzed using mean difference (MD) with 95% CI. A random-effects model was used to account for heterogeneity. The degree of heterogeneity was quantified with the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Sensitivity analyses were performed using a leave-one-out approach to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the robustness of the pooled results. Subgroup analysis and meta-regression were performed for the primary outcomes. Moreover, we have conducted sensitivity analysis to evaluate the heterogeneity and the robustness of the results. Finally, publication bias was assessed for outcomes including more than 10 studies.

Results

Searching and screening
The search yielded 678 articles, of which 346 were identified as duplicates and removed. The remaining 332 articles underwent title and abstract screening. Following this, 23 full text articles were assessed for eligibility, resulting in the inclusion of 12 studies in the final systematic review and meta-analysis. (Fig. 1)
PRISMA flow diagram of searching and screening processes

Baseline characteristics
Twelve cohort studies were included in this review, encompassing patient populations from Asia, Europe, and America. The majority of studies compared the SSA with CLA techniques in patients undergoing mitral valve surgery, whether repair or replacement, while others assessed SSA independently. Sample sizes varied widely, ranging from small single-center experiences of fewer than 20 patients to larger observational cohorts with over 100 patients per group, providing a broad but heterogeneous evidence base. The average age of included patients spanned from the mid-30s in earlier surgical series to the mid- to late-60s in more recent cohorts. Overall, age distributions were generally well balanced between the SSA and conventional atriotomy groups. Gender distribution showed marked variability across studies, with male proportions ranging from one-third to nearly three-quarters. In most cohorts, however, the proportion of men did not differ significantly between surgical techniques. All the studies included measured the outcomes at discharge except Misawa et al., who reported the long-term outcomes after follow-up period. Baseline characteristics and summary of the studies are provide in tables S1 and S2.

Primary outcomes
No significant difference was observed between SSA and CLA in the baseline sinus rhythm, AF, and junction rhythm. No heterogeneity was observed among all of them. (Fig. 2)
Comparison between SSA and conventional left atriotomy in the baseline sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, and junction rhythm
Across comparative studies, the SSA and CLA achieved broadly similar postoperative rhythm profiles. Postoperative sinus rhythm did not differ significantly between approaches (RR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.71–1.04; p = 0.11; I²=54%) (Fig. 3). Postoperative AF was likewise comparable (RR = 1.12, 95% CI 0.83–1.49; p = 0.46; I²=79%) (Fig. 4), and postoperative junctional rhythm showed no significant difference (RR = 1.59, 95% CI 0.60–4.23; p = 0.35; I²=59%) (Figure S1). Complete AVB was uncommon with no significant between-group difference (RR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.23–1.42; p = 0.23; I²=0%) (Figure S2). In sensitivity analyses, the postoperative AF comparison remained non-significant with lower heterogeneity (RR = 1.10, 95% CI 0.91–1.33; p = 0.32; I²=33%) after removing Masuda 1996 (due to the inclusion of many patients with AF in the SSA group compared with the conventional group) and Misawa 1999 (due to the reporting of measurement after extended follow-up not at discharge as the rest of the studies) (Figure S3), whereas junctional rhythm favored CLA after excluding Garcia-Villarreal 2023 (RR = 2.15, 95% CI 1.14–4.06; p = 0.02; I²=0%) (Figure S4).

Within-group (pre- vs. post-operative) analyses demonstrated a clear significant improvement in rhythm after the SSA. Specifically, sinus rhythm increased by 36% post-SSA (RR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.10–1.68; I²=66%; p = 0.005) (Figure S5), whereas the rise CLA was more modest at 20% (RR = 1.20, 95% CI 1.03–1.39; I²=24%; p = 0.02) (Figure S6). On the other side, AF showed not significant reduction after SSA (RR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.72–1.12; I²=72%; p = 0.35), indicating a relative 10% reduction from baseline (Figure S7), while AF in the CLA cohort remained essentially unchanged (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.92–1.19; I²=0%; p = 0.48) (Figure S8).

Subgroup and Meta-regression analysis
Subgroup analyses did not detect effect-modification by study design or geography for the between-group endpoints (all subgroup-difference p values > 0.40); isolated within-subgroup signals (e.g., higher post- vs. pre-operative sinus rhythm in prospective SSA series and in European SSA cohorts) did not translate into significant between-group differences (Table S3 and S4).
From the univariate meta-regression in Table S5, only two predictors showed statistically significant associations with the between-group risk ratio (SSA vs. CLA) for postoperative sinus rhythm; none were significant for postoperative AF. Specifically, publication year was positively associated with the RR (B = 0.013, SE = 0.006, p = 0.04; 8 studies), indicating that more recent studies tend to report a larger RR for sinus rhythm when comparing SSA with CLA, this corresponds to a noticeable cumulative increase, moving the point estimate toward the SSA side (RR > 1 favors SSA for this outcome) (Fig. 5). In addition, a higher mitral valve repair proportion was also positively associated with the RR for postoperative sinus rhythm (B = 0.010, SE = 0.002, p = 0.019; 5 studies) (Fig. 6). This means cohorts with a greater share of repairs (vs. replacements) tended to show higher SSA-to-CLA RRs for sinus rhythm. For pre- vs. post-operative analyses, aortic valve replacement proportion was inversely associated with postoperative sinus rhythm in SSA cohorts; other covariates were non-significant (Fig. 7).

Secondary outcomes
Across seven comparative cohorts (SSA n = 531; CLA n = 521), CPB time was numerically longer with the SSA, but not statistically different from CLA (MD = 7.81 min, 95% CI − 3.34 to 18.97; p = 0.17; I²=85%), with wide between-study variability (Figure S9). By removing Ansar 2017 (MD = 11.55 min; p = 0.03), Masuda 1996 (MD = 11.55 min; p = 0.03), or both (MD = 16.06 min; p < 0.0001), the results became significantly favouring CLA group. A similar pattern was observed for aortic cross-clamp time across seven studies (SSA n = 549; CLA n = 515): the pooled MD favored CLA but remained non-significant (MD = 4.56 min, 95% CI − 4.25 to 13.37; p = 0.31; I²=86%), again with substantial heterogeneity (Figure S10).
Recovery endpoints were likewise comparable. ICU stay, synthesized from four studies (SSA n = 292; CLA n = 261), showed no difference (MD = 0.55 days, 95% CI − 0.18 to 1.27; p = 0.14; I²=74%) (Figure S11). Postoperative hospital length of stay also (three studies; SSA n = 250, CLA n = 231) did not differ significantly (MD = 1.68 days, 95% CI − 0.46 to 3.82; p = 0.12; I²=66%) (Figure S12). By removing Démoulin 2024 in ICU and hospital stay outcomes the heterogeneity resolved (I2 = 26%; I2 = 15% respectively) and the results remained not significant. Pacemaker implantation was higher after SSA (five studies; events 20/413 vs. 15/419), but the pooled risk ratio showed borderline significance (RR = 1.85, 95% CI 0.97–3.51; p = 0.06; I²=0%) (Figure S13). By removing Gaudino 1997, the pooled RR significantly favoured CLA group (RR = 2.16; p = 0.03).

Safety outcomes
Across all safety endpoints, no statistically significant differences were detected between SSA and CLA. All-cause mortality was similar (RR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.48–1.83; p = 0.84; I²=3%) (Figure S14). Respiratory complications showed a nonsignificant trend favoring SSA but with substantial heterogeneity (RR = 0.41, 95% CI 0.07–2.29; p = 0.31; I²=65%) (Figure S15). This effect became significant (RR = 0.16, 95% CI 0.04–0.73; p = 0.02; I²=0%) when Démoulin 2024 was removed. Stroke did not differ between groups (RR = 0.32, 95% CI 0.07–1.51; p = 0.15; I²=0%) (Figure S16). Renal failure likewise showed no significant difference (RR = 0.53, 95% CI 0.17–1.68; p = 0.28; I²=0%) (Figure S17). Bleeding was also comparable (RR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.33–1.30; p = 0.23; I²=0%) (Figure S18). Finally, low cardiac output was also similar (RR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.43–2.38; p = 0.97; I²=0%) (Figure S19). Overall, the safety profile of SSA appears comparable to CLA, with no endpoint demonstrating a statistically significant excess risk for either strategy.

Publication bias assessment
We have assessed publication bias for outcomes with ≥ 10 studies included. There was only 2 outcomes including 10 studies or more. Assessment of publication bias for the pre–post SSA rhythm outcomes did not suggest small-study effects. For postoperative sinus rhythm, the funnel plot was broadly symmetric and both Begg’s test (p = 0.44) and Egger’s regression (p = 0.50) were non-significant (Figure S20). For postoperative AF, there was only mild visual asymmetry; Begg’s test (p = 0.64) and Egger’s intercept (p = 0.81) remained non-significant indicating no small-study effect (Figure S21). Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill imputed at most two studies to the right of the mean for AF, shifting the pooled estimate trivially (fixed-effect RR = 0.90→0.91; random-effects = 0.90→0.94) without changing conclusions.

Discussion

Summary of findings
The present meta-analysis provides a comprehensive comparison between the SSA and the CLA in mitral valve surgery, focusing on cardiac rhythm outcomes, perioperative performance, and safety. Overall, the findings demonstrate that both approaches achieve broadly comparable postoperative rhythm profiles and clinical outcomes, with no evidence of inferiority of the SSA relative to the conventional technique.
At baseline, there were no significant differences between the two surgical groups in sinus rhythm, AF, or junctional rhythm, and heterogeneity across the included studies was negligible. Following surgery, the rates of postoperative sinus rhythm, AF, and junctional rhythm remained similar between SSA and CLA, indicating that the type of atrial incision does not substantially influence rhythm restoration or arrhythmic complications. The incidence of complete AVB was low and did not differ between the two techniques, supporting the safety of both approaches in preserving the conduction system. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the consistency of these results, and the exclusion of outlier studies did not materially alter the overall conclusions.
When within-group analyses were conducted to compare preoperative and postoperative rhythms, a meaningful improvement was observed in both groups, though the magnitude of rhythm recovery was greater among patients who underwent the SSA. Sinus rhythm restoration was notably enhanced after SSA, suggesting that this exposure method may facilitate more effective atrial reconstruction and rhythm stabilization. In contrast, patients in the CLA group demonstrated a more modest improvement in sinus rhythm following surgery. AF tended to decrease after the SSA but did not reach statistical significance, while the CLA group showed little change in postoperative AF rates.
Further exploration through subgroup and meta-regression analyses revealed that study design, geographic region, and other methodological factors did not significantly influence the comparative outcomes. However, two predictors appeared to shape postoperative sinus rhythm recovery in SSA cohorts: more recent publication years and a higher proportion of mitral valve repair procedures. These observations suggest that progressive refinements in surgical techniques and the increasing adoption of valve repair rather than replacement have contributed to better rhythm preservation and improved postoperative cardiac performance in contemporary SSA practice.
In terms of operative efficiency and recovery endpoints, both surgical approaches performed similarly. CPB and aortic cross-clamp times were slightly longer with the SSA, yet these differences did not reach statistical significance and were not associated with adverse postoperative outcomes. Likewise, durations of ICU stay and total hospital stay were comparable between the two groups, indicating that the SSA does not prolong recovery or increase postoperative resource utilization.
Safety outcomes demonstrated equivalence between both approaches. Mortality rates, the incidence of stroke, respiratory complications, renal failure, and postoperative bleeding were all similar, with no parameter showing a statistically significant difference. The occurrence of low cardiac output syndrome was also comparable, underscoring that neither approach carries an excess risk for perioperative cardiac dysfunction. Overall, the SSA exhibited a safety profile on par with the CLA.
Finally, assessment of publication bias revealed no evidence of small-study effects, and funnel plots for the main outcomes were symmetrical. Statistical tests confirmed the absence of significant bias, reinforcing the robustness and validity of the pooled analyses.
Taken together, these findings highlight that the SSA provides clinical outcomes, rhythm stability, and safety comparable to those achieved with the CLA. Moreover, contemporary data suggest a gradual improvement in rhythm restoration with the SSA, particularly in centers emphasizing valve repair techniques. This collective evidence supports the SSA as a reliable and effective alternative for mitral valve exposure, offering equivalent safety and rhythm outcomes while allowing for optimal surgical access to the mitral valve apparatus.
The present meta-analysis differs methodologically and anatomically from the earlier systematic review by Harky and colleagues, which compared the transseptal (TS) and CLA approaches for mitral valve surgery. While both studies aimed to evaluate alternative atrial access techniques, the surgical exposure in the current analysis focused specifically on the SSA—a modification that combines an extended atriotomy through the interatrial septum and roof of the left atrium, designed to improve valve visualization while minimizing injury to the conduction tissue. In contrast, the Harky et al. analysis pooled data from studies using both conventional and extended transseptal incisions, which involve traversing the interatrial septum more extensively and often extend toward the right atrial free wall. This key anatomical difference may explain the variation in postoperative rhythm and conduction outcomes observed between the two meta-analyses.
In the study by Harky et al., transseptal access was associated with longer CPB and cross-clamp times, and with significantly higher rates of new-onset AF and permanent pacemaker implantation compared with the CLA. These findings were largely attributed to greater surgical manipulation of the interatrial septum and the adjacent conduction pathways, which could increase the risk of postoperative arrhythmogenicity. Although subgroup analysis limited to isolated mitral valve surgery attenuated some of these differences, the overall results favored the left atrial approach for rhythm preservation and operative efficiency.
In contrast, our analysis demonstrated that the SSA achieved rhythm outcomes and safety profiles equivalent to those of the CLA. Postoperative sinus rhythm, AF, and junctional rhythm were all statistically comparable between SSA and CLA, and the rate of complete AVB was similarly low. Importantly, within-group comparisons revealed that sinus rhythm recovery improved significantly following SSA, suggesting that this approach may actually enhance postoperative rhythm restoration rather than compromise it. Unlike the transseptal approach evaluated by Harky et al., the SSA in our study did not increase pacemaker implantation or arrhythmia incidence, indicating that its septal extension preserves conduction integrity more effectively.
Methodologically, the two analyses also differ in scope and focus. Harky et al. included a heterogeneous mix of isolated and combined procedures, with many transseptal cases performed in conjunction with tricuspid or aortic valve surgery—factors known to prolong bypass times and elevate arrhythmia risk. The current analysis was more refined, emphasizing rhythm outcomes and conducting extensive sensitivity and meta-regression analyses to identify modifiers such as publication year and the proportion of mitral valve repair versus replacement. This focused approach allowed for a more accurate appraisal of rhythm preservation specifically in the context of SSA. The observed positive association between later publication year, higher repair rates, and postoperative sinus rhythm recovery suggests that contemporary refinements in SSA technique and patient selection have further enhanced its safety and efficacy.
Furthermore, the magnitude of conduction-related complications differed substantially between the two studies. Harky et al. reported a consistent increase in pacemaker implantation and postoperative AF across their pooled dataset, findings confirmed by trial-sequential analysis. By contrast, our results demonstrated no excess risk of pacemaker implantation with SSA, and rhythm outcomes remained stable even after sensitivity testing. These differences likely reflect the distinct extent of septal incision and atrial tissue manipulation between the transseptal and SSA. The SSA’s incision trajectory avoids the lower interatrial septum and the atrioventricular nodal region, whereas the classic transseptal approach may traverse or approximate these critical conduction areas.
From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore that while both transseptal and superior septal techniques provide excellent exposure for mitral valve procedures, the SSA appears to achieve a balance between operative visibility and electrical preservation. The absence of increased rhythm disturbances or mortality in our analysis supports its use as a safe alternative to the CLA, particularly in cases requiring enhanced visualization of the mitral apparatus or concomitant valve repair.
The SSA (also called the “extended vertical transseptal” or “combined superior-transseptal” approach) is an atrial incision used during mitral valve surgery. First described by Guiraudon et al. in 1991, it combines a right atriotomy and an extended septal incision into the roof of the left atrium. Early adopters found that SSA affords excellent exposure of the mitral valve, especially in complex cases (small left atrium, prior surgery, or combined procedures). The approach was extensively studied in the 1990 s and 2000 s, as numerous series included in the current systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed its superior exposure compared to CLA. Indeed, SSA was widely taught and adopted at specialized centers for complex mitral valve repairs and reoperations, as the extra exposure permits less tension, thus preserving atrial tissues and preventing damage.
However, SSA necessarily transects atrial tissue carrying the sinus-node artery, raising concerns about rhythm disturbances. Early outcomes reports were mixed as Guiraudon et al.’s initial series (1991) found no inherent conduction problems, but subsequent studies observed high rates of transient junctional rhythm or pacemaker need. By the early 2000 s, studies cautioned that SSA is directly related to the loss of normal sinus rhythm because of the transsection of the sinus node artery. For example, García‐Villarreal et al. warned of sinus node artery transection and recommended caution in patients who are critically dependent on a normal sinus rhythm. Nonetheless, most rhythm disturbances were transient, as in the study by García-Villarreal et al., whereas definitive pacemaker implantation was uncommon, and sinus rhythm usually returned by 3 months.
As observed in the current systematic review and meta-analysis, in the late 2000 s through the 2010 s, published interest in the SSA waned. This may be attributed to several factors, including the following: Rise of minimally invasive techniques: From the mid-2000s onward, mitral surgery increasingly shifted toward minimally invasive approaches (right mini-thoracotomy, endoscopic, and robotic techniques). These techniques typically access the mitral valve via a smaller atriotomy (often a right-sided left atrial incision) or transseptal from the right side without needing a full superior incision. Many centers found that standard right mini-thoracotomy provided good exposure for most degenerative mitral valve cases, especially when the left atrium was not extremely small. Thus, surgeons often preferred less extensive atrial incisions during mini-thoracotomy or robotic cases, reducing the perceived need for a full SSA. Published reviews noted that minimally invasive surgery became the first alternative for mitral disease, and the open SSA was reserved for select complex cases.
The second reason is the emphasis on simpler atriotomy. With advances in anesthesia and instrumentation, many surgeons reconsidered whether the extra exposure of SSA justified its trade-offs. For typical mitral valve pathology (e.g., left atrium and straightforward repair), the traditional left atriotomy behind Sondergaard’s groove sufficed with low risk. The exposure of the mitral valve will be good when using the left atrial approach if the left atrium is enlarged. Because the majority of patients have enlarged left atria, many centers standardized on left atriotomy and only used SSA in reoperations or complex anatomies. Third, the longstanding worry about sinus node dysfunction with SSA remained unresolved. Several studies noted the evidence was contradictory on whether SSA significantly increased permanent heart block. In practice, some surgeons may have been wary after reports of high junctional rhythm rates and, more extended operative closures, and thus preferred to avoid SSA unless clearly needed. Finally, the growing prevalence of catheter-based therapies (e.g., mitral clipping) and earlier surgical referrals changed the case-mix. Surgeons were operating on more elderly or reoperative patients who often had calcified atria or concomitant pathologies, where SSA’s advantages were weighed against faster operations. In busy programs, simplicity and speed gained favor. Education and training also emphasized minimally invasive and robotic skills, with less routine emphasis on older open approaches like SSA. Consequently, publications comparing atrial approaches became sparse in the 2010 s, reflecting diminished routine use.
Together, these trends led to a relative decline in SSA research and adoption by about 2020. Major textbooks and consensus statements during this period gave little attention to SSA beyond noting it as an option for complex cases. In the literature, case series on SSA were replaced by larger series of mini-thoracotomy or robotic cohorts focusing on other endpoints.
Around 2021, the SSA began to re-emerge in literature and clinical use, as observed in the current systematic review and meta-analysis. Several reasons explain this revival, such as: Adaptation to minimally invasive surgery, as innovative surgeons found that SSA could be adapted to minimally invasive (mini thoracotomy) operations, combining the best of both worlds. In 2019 Yamazaki et al. introduced the “drawer-case technique” – essentially SSA performed via a small right thoracotomy. They reported that with SSA (via thoracotomy) one could easily reach the mitral valve annulus without using special instruments. This and later study showed that SSA can be performed safely through minimally invasive approaches, providing excellent exposure in minimally invasive mitral surgery. In fact, Yamazaki et al. in 2025 found no increase in persistent junctional rhythm in SSA patients and considered it as a safe and effective option in right mini-thoracotomy cases. An Indian series focused on ECG changes and noted that although ectopic atrial rhythms are common after SSA (36% at discharge, 17% persistent at 6 months), none of those patients required a pacemaker. In other words, modern cohorts reinforce that the sinus node often recovers and permanent block is rare. Therefore, there is new data that ensures safety of the approach. Moreover, the improved surgical tools, imaging, and cardioplegia techniques may have mitigated some earlier concerns. Modern continuous monitoring picks up conduction changes promptly. Some centers now regularly teach SSA as part of mitral-surgery training, especially in complex mitral valve cases. Expert practitioners have shared techniques (video demonstrations, and step-by-steps) to optimize closure and minimize arrhythmias. For example, Garcia-Villarreal et al. (who cautioned in 2003) later co-authored a 2016 video demonstrating SSA and noted it was a very useful technique for mitral valve surgery. The resurgence also reflects a return to SSA’s traditional niche: very complex operations (e.g. infective endocarditis “Commando” operations, redo valves, and multi-valve work) where maximal exposure is critical. Yamazaki et al. highlighted that in complex scenarios, such as double- or triple-valve operations or mitral valve repair for active infective endocarditis, the SSA provides distinct benefits. These include improved visualization, a broader operative field, and the ability to employ retrograde myocardial protection. Consequently, SSA represents a valuable and practical option in managing such technically demanding cases.
In a specific group of patients whom the sternovertebral (S-V) distance is short, a minimally invasive approach could be technically challenging.
Generally, when the S–V distance is less than 60 mm, adequate exposure of the mitral valve through a right-sided left atriotomy becomes difficult because of the restricted working space available for positioning a left atrial roof retractor. In such situations, surgeons often resort to a median sternotomy as an alternative approach. In the study by Yamazaki et al. (17), 12 patients had an S–V distance of less than 75 mm, of whom 3 had a distance below 60 mm. Surgical intervention through a right mini-thoracotomy is seldom performed in this latter subgroup. The use of the SSA, however, repositions the mitral valve into the right thoracic cavity, away from the most constricted area, thereby overcoming the limitations imposed by a narrow S–V distance. In patients with an extremely narrow distance of less than 60 mm, an additional working space can be gained by elevating the sternum with a closure wire threaded through the sternum. This maneuver allows an increase of approximately 2–3 cm in the S–V distance (17). The excellent exposure obtained through the SSA eliminates this problem with exposing the posteromedial aspect of the mitral valve.
Another common challenge during mitral valve surgery via a right mini thoracotomy is achieving an optimal surgical view. Even when the mitral valve is visible anteriorly, visualization of the posteromedial portion is frequently inadequate. To address this, many surgeons employ retraction sutures placed on the posteromedial atrial wall, while others place a retraction suture directly on the diaphragm and pull it downward. Although effective, this technique carries a risk of liver injury. An alternative is to apply strong caudal retraction of the pericardium adjacent to the diaphragm, which safely depresses the diaphragm itself. This maneuver elongates the axis formed by the superior and inferior venae cavae, thereby extending not only the right atrium but also the posteriorly located left atrium, ultimately improving visualization of the posteromedial mitral valve (17).
The enhanced visualization achieved with the SSA also enables a broader range of concomitant procedures, including aortic valve replacement, Morrow myectomy, ventricular septal defect closure, and left atrial plication. While the addition of these procedures inevitably prolongs aortic cross-clamping time, the SSA facilitates access to the heart’s chambers via the right atrium, making both anterograde and retrograde myocardial protection straightforward. Indeed, Yamazaki et al. (17) reported that combined anterograde and retrograde myocardial protection was significantly more common in the SSA group than in the conventional group [95 (88.0%) vs. 6 (12.8%) patients, respectively]. This approach also offers advantages in myocardial protection and the prevention of air embolism. Nevertheless, three patients in the SSA group experienced coronary sinus injury during insertion of the retrograde cannula. This issue was subsequently resolved by modifying the technique to insert the cannula without the rigid stylet and positioning it shallowly in the coronary sinus ostium (17). From an anatomical perspective, the conventional direct left atriotomy requires only a single incision, whereas both the transseptal and SSA involve the convergence of two incisions. The additional steps needed for creating and subsequently closing two incisions, as opposed to one, naturally prolong operative time. Consequently, conventional direct left atriotomy is generally associated with shorter aortic cross-clamp and CPB times. Furthermore, the transseptal and SSA extend their incisions toward the dome of the left atrium, a structurally delicate region that demands greater surgical precision and additional operative time.
Despite these advantages, the SSA has certain drawbacks, including the requirement for extended incisions traversing both atria and the potential risk of sinus node dysfunction. Although it is generally accepted that the SSA provides superior mitral valve exposure compared with right-sided left atriotomy or the transseptal approach, the incidence of postoperative sinus node dysfunction remains controversial. Because of the anatomical proximity, injury to the sinoatrial node artery is difficult to avoid, and transient sinus node dysfunction may occur. Most patients, however, regain sinus rhythm within approximately one week, and careful postoperative monitoring is usually sufficient. To minimize this risk, the incision can be directed toward the left superior pulmonary vein in order to preserve at least part of the sinoatrial node artery. As a result, persistent junctional rhythm has not been observed, and all patients in sinus rhythm preoperatively recover this rhythm by discharge. Nonetheless, Yamazaki et al. (17) reported two exceptions in patients with preoperative bradycardia, in whom recovery to sinus rhythm was delayed, requiring 14 and 17 days, respectively. Therefore, this approach may be less suitable for patients with marked preoperative bradycardia.

Strengths, limitations and recommendations
This study offers several notable strengths. It synthesizes evidence from a wide range of international cohorts, incorporating data from Asia, Europe, America, which enhances the external validity and generalizability of the findings. Importantly, both perioperative parameters and clinical outcomes were systematically analyzed, providing a comprehensive assessment of safety and efficacy. The inclusion of contemporary studies published between 2021 and 2025 captures the renewed interest in the SSA within minimally invasive practice, complementing earlier data from the 1990 s and 2000s. Furthermore, the robustness of the findings was reinforced by sensitivity analyses, which confirmed the stability of results despite heterogeneity across studies.
Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The majority of included studies were observational in nature, with inherent susceptibility to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Surgical heterogeneity also complicates interpretation in some outcomes. Temporal variability is another challenge, since older studies reflect surgical practices and perioperative care that differ significantly from current standards, thereby limiting their applicability to modern contexts. In addition, incomplete and inconsistent reporting across studies particularly regarding long-term rhythm disturbances, survival, and specific surgical modifications restricted the ability to fully assess late outcomes. Another limitation is the presence of moderate-quality studies according to NOS, however, this tool is subjective and can differ from one person to another. Also, none of the moderate-quality studies included data for the primary outcomes. Finally, the relatively small sample sizes of some SSA cohorts may have limited statistical power, especially in detecting differences in rare complications such as stroke.
Based on these considerations, several recommendations emerge. Clinically, SSA should be considered a safe and effective alternative to conventional atriotomy, particularly in patients with challenging anatomy, reoperative cases, or when enhanced mitral valve exposure is essential. Surgeons adopting SSA should be aware of its technical complexity and potential for conduction disturbances, and training programs should incorporate structured education and simulation-based practice to ensure proficiency. From a research perspective, further well-designed prospective studies, ideally multicenter cohorts or randomized trials, are needed to confirm the comparative safety and efficacy of SSA, especially with respect to long-term rhythm stability and survival. Future investigations should also evaluate the role of SSA in minimally invasive and robotic mitral surgery, where its ability to improve exposure may complement evolving technologies. Standardization of reporting for perioperative complications, arrhythmia outcomes, and follow-up duration will be crucial to improve the comparability of studies and strengthen evidence synthesis. Finally, research should seek to refine patient selection criteria by identifying clinical and anatomical scenarios where SSA offers the greatest benefit over conventional atriotomy.

Conclusion
This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that the SSA provides comparable perioperative and clinical outcomes to CLA in mitral valve surgery. Despite earlier concerns regarding conduction disturbances, contemporary evidence suggests that SSA can be performed safely within minimally invasive contexts, without increasing the risk of major morbidity or mortality. While further high-quality studies are required to define its long-term outcomes and optimal patient selection, SSA remains a valuable surgical option that should be part of the modern cardiac surgeon’s repertoire.
