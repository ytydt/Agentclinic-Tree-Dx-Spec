The evaluation, management, and future of indolent systemic mastocytosis
Indolent systemic mastocytosis (ISM) is a chronic hematologic malignancy that has traditionally been managed primarily by allergists. However, with improved molecular diagnostics such as droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) for KIT D816V on bone marrow aspirate specimens and the availability of targeted therapies against KIT, hematologists are increasingly involved in the care of these patients. This review summarizes current understanding of the evaluation, diagnosis, treatment, and future therapeutic strategies in ISM, with a focus on clinically relevant insights for hematologists. We begin by emphasizing the broad and often debilitating range of ISM symptoms, which frequently include cutaneous, gastrointestinal, and mediator symptoms. The diagnostic workup underscores the importance of molecular testing for KIT mutations and bone marrow evaluation, clarifying the clinical scenarios in which each test should be utilized. Furthermore, management strategies start with symptomatic support and can include targeted KIT inhibition, such as avapritinib, for patients with refractory disease. ISM is a non-advanced subtype of systemic mastocytosis characterized by a wide spectrum of symptoms without evidence of organ damage. While many patients have stable disease, a subset may experience persistent morbidity or disease progression. Future directions include improving early recognition, validating biomarkers for monitoring and therapeutic response, and evaluating investigational therapies aimed at modifying disease course.

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a hematologic malignancy characterized by the pathological proliferation and accumulation of mast cells (MC). Unlike cutaneous mastocytosis (CM), SM involves infiltration of mast cells into extracutaneous tissues. An activating mutation in KIT, canonically KITD816V, is found in more than 90% of patients. There are advanced forms of SM that compromise organ function and, in the case of mast cell leukemia, are associated with an aggressive disease course and poor survival. However the most common form of SM is indolent SM (ISM)—comprising more than 80% of all SM cases —which is a form of non-advanced SM as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) 5th Edition. Differentiating non-advanced SM from advanced SM is of paramount importance and is highlighted in Table 1.
While the epidemiology of ISM has been incompletely elucidated, a recent investigation estimated a prevalence of 10.6 and an estimated mean annual incidence of 0.77 per 100,000. However, given the protean nature of clinical manifestations and lack of full access to highly sensitive testing techniques, this is likely an underestimation. ISM patients typically present younger than those with advanced subtypes of SM, and one study showed Caucasians to have the greatest incidence of ISM.
ISM is a complex, chronic condition that often presents with multisystem symptoms, leading patients to seek care across a range of specialties, including allergy/immunology, dermatology, and gastroenterology. While these specialties play a key role in symptom management, hematologists are essential for establishing a definitive diagnosis through bone marrow biopsy (BM Bx) and the interpretation of molecular findings such as KIT mutations. With the recent expansion of therapeutic options, particularly the development of targeted KIT inhibitors, hematologists are also playing an increasingly central role in the long-term management of ISM.
The shift in clinical involvement and the evolving therapeutic landscape represents an opportune time to provide a comprehensive overview of ISM geared towards hematologists. Here, we detail the initial evaluation, diagnostic workup, and therapeutic management. We end by looking to the horizon with respect to novel therapies and more sensitive technology to detect ISM earlier in its disease course.

Initial evaluation of a patient with suspected ISM
The initial evaluation of a patient with suspected ISM is complicated by the fact that symptoms are nonspecific, vary across multiple systems, and can often be explained by other comorbidities.
Skin findings include flushing, urticaria, dermographism, angioedema, and pruritis. Gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms commonly include cramping abdominal pain, reflux, and vomiting. Other presentations can involve mediator-related symptoms (i.e., recurrent episodes of syncope, anaphylaxis, hypotension, tachycardia) or musculoskeletal pain secondary to osteoporotic fracture. In an investigation of 159 patients with ISM, cutaneous (75%), GI (71%), and mediator-related (69%) symptoms were found to be most prevalent. While these findings are not specific to SM, a constellation of these symptoms should raise concern for ISM. The history should also include any potential triggers of SM including stress, trauma, alcohol, extreme temperatures, and medications.
On physical exam, patients may present with urticaria pigmentosa, or reddish-brown macules and papules. These lesions exhibit a positive Darier’s sign, where rubbing the affected skin triggers mast cell degranulation, resulting in a localized wheal, flare, and/or itching. The urgency of evaluating a patient with CM for potential systemic involvement can be guided by a scoring system that incorporates tryptase levels, bone symptoms, and constitutional or cardiovascular manifestations to help determine whether a BM Bx should be performed promptly or may be safely deferred. Specifically, tryptase levels are scored as follows: < 10 ng/mL = − 1 point, 10–14.9 = 0, 15–19.9 = 1, and ≥ 20 = 3 points, with an additional 1 point each for bone symptoms/osteoporosis and constitutional or cardiovascular symptoms. Although all patients with CM should ultimately be evaluated for SM, lower-risk individuals (i.e. those with a total of 0–1 points) may be able to defer bone marrow biopsy and instead undergo high sensitivity testing for KITD816V, as will be discussed later.
Organomegaly is rare in ISM patients (7.7%), with the most common being hepatomegaly (5.0%), splenomegaly (3.7%), and lymphadenopathy (1.6%). Patients with ISM may occasionally be diagnosed after a vertebral fracture secondary to osteoporosis as an estimated 18–31% of patients with SM have bone involvement. Furthermore, in a Dutch study evaluating 157 consecutive patients diagnosed with ISM, 7.6% of patients had osteoporotic fractures or osteoporosis as a presenting symptom. The following scenarios have been proposed as criteria for SM to be evaluated in the setting of osteoporosis: vertebral osteoporosis in young males < 50 years old, vertebral osteoporosis with comorbid cutaneous symptoms; vertebral osteoporosis refractory to bisphosphonates, vertebral fracture cascade in a subject who is not high risk for fracture. Otherwise, the osteoporosis evaluation for patients with SM is the same screening process proposed for patients without ISM.
In terms of diagnostic tests, more than 90% of patients with SM possess gain-of-function mutations of the gene encoding for the KIT receptor, which allows for increased proliferation and enhanced survival of MCs. While the most common KIT mutation is the KITD816V mutation, noncanonical mutations such as KITD816H, KITD816Y, KITV560G, and KITK509I have also been reported. In a retrospective analysis of 200 patients with ISM, 191 patients (95.5%) had the KITD816V mutation, while 9 patients (4.5%) harbored KITD816H or KITD816Y mutations. Other non-D816 mutations have primarily been described in isolated case reports. MCs release tryptase when activated which makes serum tryptase a relevant marker to help quantify the burden of disease in patients with ISM. As such, laboratory workup should include serum tryptase levels. However, this value must be put into context as patients with hereditary alpha tryptasemia (HαT), which has a prevalence of about 5% in the general population, have increased copies of the TPSAB1 gene that leads to elevated serum tryptase levels at baseline. Importantly, this elevation of serum tryptase was found to be independent of clonal MC burden in patients with SM and HαT; therefore, testing for HαT must be done to contextualize this value. Currently, there is no consensus on the exact normalization of serum tryptase levels based on copy numbers of the α-tryptase–encoding TPSAB1 gene. A recent review proposed the following method to normalize serum tryptase based on various population-based cohort studies: baseline serum tryptase levels increase with the number of α-tryptase–encoding TPSAB1 gene copies, with median (range) values of 4.1 ng/mL (0–10.4) for 0 copies, 13.6 ng/mL (6.5–33.9) for 1 copy, 22.5 ng/mL (10.5–39.5) for 2 copies, 27.3 ng/mL (23.4–40) for 3 copies, and 37.0 ng/mL (25.5–62.7) for 4 copies. This information can be helpful in assessing the true degree of tryptase elevation after tryptase genotyping is performed.
In recent years, digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) has emerged as a sensitive and standardizable means for quantifying KITD816V allele burden and thereby allowing for screening in the peripheral blood for patients with suspected ISM. For reference, the KITD816V variant allele frequency (VAF) in patients with ISM has a median of approximately 0.14% (range: 0.002–46.9%), compared to a median of 2.43% (range: 0.005–46.7) observed in patients with advanced SM (P < 0.001). This assay has a sensitivity of up to 95% for detecting the KITD816V mutation and a lower limit of detection of VAF of 0.02–0.03% in patients with BM Bx-confirmed ISM. This represents a substantial improvement from next-generation sequencing (NGS), which, in the same study, showed a sensitivity of only 28% and lower limit of VAF of 1.9%.
However, as MCs typically do not circulate in the peripheral blood, peripheral blood measurement of KITD816V via ddPCR has its limitation. In a recent study found that 14.7% of patients with ISM had a positive KITD816V result in BM but not in peripheral blood (PB), underscoring the superior sensitivity of BM testing compared to PB (P < 0.0001). This finding reinforces the critical role of the BM Bx to definitively establish an SM diagnosis. In addition, approximately 15% of patients will not harbor an activating KIT mutation, further emphasizing that a negative KITD816V ddPCR from the PB does not exclude the diagnosis of ISM.
Additional tests in the evaluation of ISM include a complete blood count and liver function tests to search for any cytopenias or liver impairment, both of which would be suggestive of advanced SM. Unexplained cytopenias further encourage evaluation with a BM Bx to rule out aggressive SM or SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm, types of advanced SM.

The diagnostic criteria for SM, as defined by the WHO 5th Edition (Table 2), include a major criterion of multifocal dense infiltrates of MCs (≥ 15 MCs in aggregates) in bone marrow biopsies and/or in sections of other extracutaneous organ(s) and minor criteria including atypical mast cell morphology in the biopsy (over 25% of mast cells are abnormal), any KIT mutation in the bone marrow or blood, aberrant expression of CD25, CD2, or CD30 on mast cells, and elevated serum tryptase level (above 20 ng/mL, unless there is a related blood cancer). The diagnosis is made with the confirmation of either 1 major and 1 minor criterion or 3 minor criteria. From there, the distinction between ISM and more advanced subtypes of SM is outlined in Table 1 and further detailed below. PB KIT D816V testing serves as an effective screening tool, particularly when using highly sensitive methods such as ddPCR and duplex sequencing, which demonstrate sensitivities of approximately 85% and 96%, respectively. This approach supports a practical diagnostic algorithm: patients presenting with compatible symptoms or elevated serum tryptase by genotype and a positive PB KIT D816V result should proceed to BM Bx for confirmation of ISM according to WHO criteria (Table 2). However, as illustrated in Fig. 1, a negative PB screen does not rule out ISM. In patients with recurrent or persistent symptoms that are refractory to best supportive care, BM Bx remains warranted even in the absence of a detectable mutation in PB. This is because even highly sensitive PB assays can miss cases of ISM, and BM Bx remains the diagnostic gold standard as well as the most accurate tool to assess disease burden, monitor progression, and evaluate response, especially when peripheral markers are inconclusive or discordant.
“B findings” indicate increased mast cell infiltration or activity without associated organ dysfunction. For example, this may include organomegaly (such as hepatosplenomegaly) without impaired function. In contrast, “C findings” reflect organ dysfunction directly attributable to mast cell infiltration, such as weight loss due to gastrointestinal involvement or pathologic fractures from skeletal involvement.
These distinctions are essential for classifying SM subtypes. Both ISM and smoldering SM (SSM) lack C findings; however, SSM is defined by the presence of two or more B findings, whereas ISM is defined by fewer than two. Aggressive SM (ASM) is characterized by the presence of at least one C finding.
Mast cell leukemia (MCL) is diagnosed when 20% or more of nucleated cells in the bone marrow aspirate are mast cells, regardless of peripheral blood involvement. Lastly, systemic mastocytosis with an associated hematologic neoplasm (SM-AHN) is diagnosed when SM coexists with a separate clonal hematologic malignancy, such as myelodysplastic syndrome, myeloproliferative neoplasm, or acute myeloid leukemia.
Moreover, the REMA score is a validated clinical tool designed to predict the likelihood of clonal mast cell disease in patients with systemic mast cell activation symptoms. It assigns points based on five variables: sex (male + 1, female–1), baseline serum tryptase level less than 15 µg/L (–1) or greater than 25 µg/L (+ 2), presence (–2) or absence (+ 1) of pruritus, hives, or angioedema, and presence (+ 3) of presyncope or syncope. The total score helps stratify patients’ risk for underlying clonal mast cell disease such as SM.

Treatment of patients with ISM

Supportive care
Treatment of ISM begins with symptomatic control. For dermatologic complaints, second-generation H1 antihistamines (e.g. fexofenadine 180 mg daily, cetirizine 10 mg daily, loratadine 10 mg daily) are used as first-line therapy. One recent study of patients with both CM and ISM found daily administration of a second-generation H1 antihistamine significantly improves patients' quality of life (QOL), itching, wheal, flushing, tachycardia, GI symptoms, and headaches. Moreover, as second-line treatment, H2 antihistamines (e.g. famotidine 20 mg or 40 mg daily) and leukotriene antagonists (e.g. montelukast 10 mg daily) may be used. Lastly, for refractory cutaneous symptoms, aspirin or the multidisciplinary management in consultation with dermatology can be beneficial. Specifically, the combination of psoralen plus UV-A photochemotherapy has demonstrated efficacy. Narrow-band UVB phototherapy has been trialed and shown to be effective in providing cutaneous symptom relief as well. In smaller studies, ketotifen (e.g. 2 mg BID) has demonstrated some benefit in alleviating cutaneous, GI, and mediator symptoms in patients with SM. Its dual role as an H1 antihistamine and mast cell stabilizer allows it to serve as a modest adjunctive option, particularly in cases with multi-system involvement. However, ketotifen is only available as a compounded medication in the United States, which can limit its practical use.
Patients with GI distress secondary to ISM should first be trialed on H2-antihistamines (e.g. famotidine 20 mg or 40 mg daily). Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy is recommended as a second-line therapy for GI symptoms. Furthermore, for a patient with GI distress refractory to antihistamines and PPI therapy, sodium cromolyn 200 mg four times daily has been shown to be superior versus placebo for GI symptoms. Importantly, starting at the full dose may induce bloating and abdominal cramps so treatment should be gradually increased based on patient side effects, and it may take a few months to appreciate a noticeable improvement. Prednisone starting a 0.5-1 mg/kg/day can be considered for abdominal pain and discomfort refractory to all the above and for the management of GI malabsorption and ascites. However, a steroid taper should begin as soon as symptom relief is achieved.
As mentioned above, patients with ISM can experience anaphylaxis. In addition to the above, all patients should be counseled to always carry epinephrine autoinjectors. Moreover, a recent report found that a patient who had recurrent anaphylaxis and urticaria pigmentosa refractory to maximal antileukotriene and antihistamine therapy experienced significant improvement of cutaneous symptoms and near resolution of anaphylactic episodes after starting subcutaneous omalizumab injections at a dose of 300 mg every 4 weeks. A larger investigation of 29 patients with ISM taking up to omalizumab 150 mg every week found that 3.4%, 55.2%, and 20.7% of this cohort experienced a complete response (total resolution of symptoms), major response (> 50% reduction of symptoms), and partial response (10–50% reduction of symptoms), respectively. There was a median time to response of 2 months and 29% of the patients experienced side effects including fatigue (5.5%), fever (5.5%), and even mastocytosis “flare-ups” that led to discontinuation of the drug (5.5%). Omalizumab may be considered for patients with either refractory cutaneous symptoms, recurrent anaphylaxis, or both.
Lastly, the treatment of osteoporosis starts with oral bisphosphonates (e.g. alendronate 70 mg once per week). In severe cases or in patients with drug intolerance, IV bisphosphonates (e.g. pamidronate 90 mg every 4 weeks or zoledronate 4 mg every 4 weeks) may be given. For patients presenting with both severe gastrointestinal symptoms and clinically significant osteoporosis, intravenous bisphosphonates are recommended as the initial treatment modality, since gastrointestinal involvement in systemic mastocytosis may impair the absorption of oral bisphosphonates, reducing their efficacy.

KIT-inhibition
KIT inhibitors have been developed that can lead to significant improvement in symptoms and may reduce disease progression, especially in patients with moderate-severe disease that is refractory to supportive care.
One of the earliest therapies to target KIT was midostaurin, a multikinase inhibitor against both the wild type and D816V-mutated KIT. It showed promise in symptom reduction, especially cutaneous symptoms; however, adverse events including nausea (80%), headache (50%), and diarrhea (35%) have limited its use in patients with ISM.
A significant advancement in the treatment of ISM arrived with the development of avapritinib, a selective and potent inhibitor of activated KIT and PDGFRA mutant receptor tyrosine kinases. It has demonstrated a high level of specificity for KIT D816V compared to wild-type KIT. It shows an IC50 for KITD816V phosphorylation of 3.1 nM, while the IC50 values for wild-type KIT proliferation and wild-type KIT phosphorylation are 85.8 nM and 89.5 nM, respectively.
The PIONEER trial examined the effect of avapritinib 25 mg daily versus placebo, both with supportive care adjuvants. The phase 2 portion of the trial examined 212 adult patients who had refractory symptoms to supportive care alone. The primary endpoint of this study was change in TSS score (a combination of ISM symptoms rates from 1–10) over a 14-day average. At 24 weeks, patients treated with avapritinib had a decrease in mean TSS of 15.6 points compared to a decrease of 9.2 points in the placebo group (P = 0.003). In terms of specific symptoms, skin domain symptoms (skin spots, flushing, itching) experienced a mean (SD) decrease of 5.87, compared to a decrease of 2.64 in the placebo arm (P < 0.0001). GI symptoms were also significant improved in the avapritinib group versus placebo. In terms of markers of mast cell burden, 54% of patients treated with avapritinib (compared with 0% of patients in the placebo arm) experienced a ≥ 50% decrease of serum tryptase levels (P < 0.001). In addition, 68% of avapritinib treatment patients (versus 6% with placebo) had a ≥ 50% reduction in KITD816V VAF (P < 0.001). Lastly, mean percentage change in MC burden per BM Bx from baseline to week 24 was -11.3% with avapritinib versus 0.4% with placebo.
The incidence of AEs between the two groups was similar– the incidence of adverse events (AEs) in patients treated with placebo is reflective of the disease burden in patients with uncontrolled ISM. Otherwise, the most common AEs that were more frequently found in patients treated with avapritinib were edema and increases in alkaline phosphatase. However, higher doses of avapritinib may lead to more AEs. This is reflected in the EXPLORER trial in advanced SM, which evaluated avapritinib 200 mg daily (8 times higher than the dose for ISM) and showed that 13% of patients experienced intracranial bleeding, with 8 of the 9 cases (89%) occurring in patients who had severe thrombocytopenia (< 50 × 109/L). This is of particular concern as avapritinib has been shown to cross the blood–brain barrier. Following this finding, the study protocol was amended to exclude patients with severe thrombocytopenia, effectively eliminating intracranial bleeding as a significant concern. In practice, avapritinib 25 mg should be withheld in patients who have a platelet count < 50 × 109/L, a finding which should prompt a BM Bx to evaluate for evolution to advanced SM or a separate hematologic process.
In patients with ISM who are refractory to avapritinib 25 mg, there is mounting evidence that dose escalation to avapritinib 50 mg daily may be efficacious with minimal, if any, additional side effects. In Part 3 of the PIONEER trial, 28 patients dose-escalated to avapritinib 50 mg; compared to those who did not escalate, these patients had significantly higher disease burden, as evidenced by total symptom score (TSS, P = 0.04), serum tryptase levels (P = 0.02), mast cell counts in bone marrow biopsy (P = 0.003), and KITD816V variant allele frequency in peripheral blood (P = 0.006). Early efficacy data is promising with all 21 patients reporting stable-to-improved TSS (18 improved, 3 stable), no discontinuations, and the only TRAE was peripheral edema (n = 4).

Treatment for Wild-Type KIT or other KIT variants
Imatinib, while ineffective against KITD816V, has shown limited benefit in ISM patients with non-D816V mutations such as K509I or wild-type KIT, with occasional clinical responses reported in small studies. In the rare cases of ISM with these non-canonical mutations, imatinib can be considered.

Overall prognosis and progression to advanced systemic mastocytosis
While the course of ISM can be stable for decades and is characterized by a lack of significant organ damage, growing evidence suggests that patients with ISM have worse survival than those with CM. A retrospective cohort study of 897 WHO-defined ISM patients found a median OS of 28.4 years (95% CI: 24.1, 32.7) for ISM, compared to a not reached value for CM (P = 0.024). Notably, 22.7% (n = 5) of the total deaths (n = 22) in patients with ISM were attributable to causes related to progression to advanced SM. Another prognostic study identified age ≥ 60 and alkaline phosphatase ≥ 100 U/L as independent predictors of OS. The latter may reflect clinically silent organ involvement that ultimately impacts survival.
Organomegaly, while rare in ISM, holds prognostic significance, predicting both worse OS and progression-free survival (progression defined as advancement to smoldering or advanced SM). Interestingly, the presence of organomegaly makes the OS and progression free survival similar to that of a patient with smoldering systemic mastocytosis (SSM).
Despite this, progression to more advanced forms of SM is rare in patients with ISM. One of the largest natural history studies of ISM, evaluating 653 patients, found a cumulative progression rate of 4.1% amongst non-advanced SM subtypes, including ISM (4.9%), smoldering SM (9.4%), and bone marrow mastocytosis (1.7%) to more advanced forms of SM, with 2.9% ultimately progressing to advanced SM. Specifically, 1.8%, 1.7%, 1.2%, and 0.1% of patients progressed to SM-AHN, SSM, ASM, and MC sarcoma, respectively.
However, delays in diagnosing SM can skew reported progression rates, as patients with advanced disease may initially be misclassified as having indolent forms at diagnosis, thereby falsely inflating estimates of disease progression. These delays often result from the nonspecific nature of SM symptoms, such as flushing, abdominal pain, and fatigue, that overlap with more common conditions and may not immediately prompt further investigation. Additionally, in retrospective studies, incomplete diagnostic evaluations or limited clinical data may lead to misclassification, where patients with advanced disease are recorded as having indolent SM at diagnosis. A qualitative study of 16 patients reported diagnostic delays of up to 33 years, and a separate case described a patient who experienced 97 episodes over 25 years before receiving a definitive diagnosis.

Future directions

Early detection
Because of the heterogeneity of symptomatology and lack of familiarity with high-sensitivity testing for SM, early detection can be challenging. A recent study found that serum tryptase levels, when interpreted in the context of the patient’s tryptase genotype, represent the most accurate screening test available, with a sensitivity of 84.2% and a specificity of 90.2%.
More sensitive detection of the KIT mutations is also of interest, particularly given the low-level KIT mutations seen in most patients with ISM. A novel technology called duplex sequencing is a highly sensitive diagnostic method that enhances the detection of genetic mutations and was recently employed to analyze patient data from the PIONEER trial (n = 251). The analysis showed that ddPCR of PB samples detected KIT mutations in 85% of the cohort, whereas duplex sequencing identified KIT mutations in 96% of patients, including non-D816V mutations like D816I and D816Y. Further studies are required to see if this screening test can be integrated into the evaluation of a patient with suspected ISM, particularly the decision to pursue BM Bx.

Investigational therapies
Bezuclastinib is an oral, selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) with activity against KITD816V that is being explored in both ISM and advanced SM. It selectively targets mutations in KIT exons 9, 11, 17, and 18, while avoiding activity against related kinases such as PDGFRα, PDGFRβ, and CSF1R which are affected by avapritinib and may lead to some side effects. In a 37-patient cohort of patients with non-advanced SM, those treated with 100 mg bezuclastinib daily experienced a 49.1% reduction in symptoms versus a 21.1% reduction in those treated with placebo at 12 weeks follow-up. Moreover, there was a reduction in MC burden among the group treated with bezuclastinib; specifically, 94.4%, 100%, and 92.3% of patients had at least a 50% reduction of basal serum tryptase, KITD816V VAF, and bone marrow MCs, respectively. TRAEs that affected at least 10% of the cohort included hair color changes, nausea, diarrhea, peripheral edema, GERD, taste changes, neutropenia. Notably, it does not cross the blood–brain barrier as avapritinib does.
Elenestinib (BLU-263) is another investigational KIT inhibitor. Similar to bezuclastinib, elenestinib demonstrates minimal central nervous system penetration and exhibits even greater specificity for KITD816V. In a part 1 study, at 12-week assessment, it was found to reduce MC burden in patients with ISM. Specifically, there was noted to be a reduction in baseline tryptase (-15.4%, -50.9%, -68.4% versus 3.3%) and KITD816V VAF (-37.5%, -70.3%, -77.0% versus -2.5%) in the 25 mg, 50 mg, 100 mg daily versus placebo groups, respectively. It had a favorable safety profile as well with no serious TRAEs or TRAEs that led to drug discontinuation.
Lirentelimab, a monoclonal antibody targeting Siglec-8 to inhibit mast cell activation, was previously investigated as a therapy for ISM but is no longer in active development for this indication. In the high-dose cohort, patients received either 1 mg/kg every 4 weeks for six months or an initial 1 mg/kg dose followed by five doses of 3–10 mg/kg every 4 weeks. Based on the Mastocytosis Symptom Questionnaire (MSQ), patients in this high, multi-dose group experienced a mean 37% improvement of symptoms (95% confidence interval (CI): −62, 12), with notable improvements in skin, GI, and neurologic symptoms that correlated well with both time and escalating dosing. Lastly, these patients experienced a gradual decrease in serum tryptase level (mean maximum reduction: 29%), however there were no clinically meaningful changes in KIT D816V allele burden.
Masitinib is another TKI that has been investigated in the setting of ISM. In a phase III trial, patients with ISM given oral masitinib had an 18.7% response rate compared to 7.4% in the placebo group (P = 0.0076). A response in this study was defined as at least a 75% reduction in either pruritis, flushing, depression, or fatigue. There was also a significant reduction in mean tryptase level, urticaria pigmentosa, and Darier’s sign in the treatment group compared to placebo. However, this study used a more inclusive definition of ISM than the WHO criteria, with 20% of participants having other forms of non-advanced systemic mastocytosis, notably smoldering SM. A confirmatory trial is ongoing (NCT04333108).

Novel therapies on the horizon
While many patients experience relief in symptoms from conservative supportive care measures, there continues to be newer pharmacotherapy aimed at relief for symptoms associated with ISM. TL-895, a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor, is currently being investigated at various dosages for its potential use in patients with ISM (NCT04655118). BTK inhibitors like TL-895 may help mitigate mast cell activation and cytokine responses, both of which play a key role in the inflammatory processes associated with ISM.

Conclusion
ISM is a non-advanced subtype of SM that is often diagnosed by the presence of cutaneous and systemic allergic symptoms without any organ dysfunction. While many patients with ISM have symptoms that can be managed with antimediator treatments, newer therapies, such as the approved KIT inhibitor avapritinib, have demonstrated efficacy in patients with refractory symptoms.
Despite unquestionable benefits to patients, there are several important questions that remain in the current treatment landscape of ISM. These include the optimal dosing and schedule of avapritinib and other KIT inhibitors, whether these therapies can be safely discontinued, and the biomarkers that guide such decisions, particularly since tryptase does not correlate well with symptoms. The true impact of KIT inhibition on disease progression is also not known. Long-term safety concerns also persist, as ISM patients should ideally live a nearly normal life expectancy. Additionally, the role of KIT inhibition in lieu of anti-mediator therapy remains unclear. A better understanding of ISM, its diagnosis, and the incorporation of biomarkers into management is essential for clinicians to identify ISM earlier, provide more targeted symptomatic relief, and address potential disease progression.
