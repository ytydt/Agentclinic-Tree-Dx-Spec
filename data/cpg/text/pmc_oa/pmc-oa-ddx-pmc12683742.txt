Nonmass Lesions on Breast Ultrasound: Radiologic-Pathologic Correlation and a Practical Guide to Diagnostic Approach
Breast lesions that do not meet the criteria for a mass on ultrasound (US), analogous to asymmetry on mammography or nonmass enhancement on MRI, are frequently encountered during diagnostic or screening US. These lesions, referred to as nonmass lesions (NMLs), are discrete areas of altered echotexture compared to surrounding breast tissue, lacking the threedimensionality or conspicuity of a mass. Their subtle nature makes it difficult—particularly for less experienced US operators—to distinguish between benign and malignant NMLs. With increasing clinical recognition, the upcoming sixth edition of the American College of Radiology Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System may include NMLs as a distinct diagnostic category. This article illustrates the sonographic features of NMLs and their pathologic correlations, providing extensive representative examples across benign NMLs, benign NMLs with upgrade potential, and malignant NMLs. In addition, it offers a practical and structured guide for a diagnostic approach to aid clinical management.

INTRODUCTION
Most lesions detected on breast ultrasound (US) are masses, defined by the American College of Radiology Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) as “three-dimensional, space-occupying lesions that are observed on two different planes and can be distinguished from normal anatomic structures”. Advances in US technology have led to increased detection of lesions that do not meet this definition. These are referred to as nonmass lesions (NMLs), defined as areas of altered echotexture lacking three-dimensionality but visible on at least two imaging planes.
Although NMLs are not included in the fifth edition of BI-RADS, descriptors for their components, such as duct changes, architectural distortion, calcifications, and clustered microcysts, are present. In Japan, the concept of NMLs has been described since the 1980s and incorporated into the guidelines of the Japan Society of Ultrasonics in Medicine and the Japan Association of Breast and Thyroid Sonology (JABTS). Since then, most studies on NMLs have been published in Asia. However, no standardized classification system for NMLs exists. The upcoming sixth edition of BI-RADS is expected to include NMLs, reflecting their growing clinical relevance.
Radiologists frequently encounter NMLs during diagnostic US, often in correlation with mammographic asymmetries or suspicious calcifications, or with MRI abnormalities. NMLs range from benign lesions like fibrocystic changes (FCC) to malignant ones such as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC). Sonographic features such as segmental distribution, calcifications, posterior shadowing, and hypervascularity are associated with higher malignancy risk, but their subtle and variable appearance presents diagnostic challenges. Reported malignancy rates of NMLs vary considerably, ranging from 16.1% to 53.8% when detected on diagnostic US performed for symptomatic patients or on targeted US, and from 2.2% to 8.0% when detected on screening US, underscoring the need for careful assessment and multimodality correlation.
This pictorial review summarizes the US findings of NMLs, correlates them with benign and malignant pathology, and proposes a practical guide to support accurate diagnosis and clinical management.

CONCEPT AND DEFINITION OF NONMASS LESION ON US
The distinction between masses and nonmass findings on breast US was first introduced in the 1980s and later incorporated into the JABTS guidelines. Current JABTS guidelines use the term “nonmass abnormalities,” which are classified into five subtypes: ductal abnormalities, hypoechoic areas in the mammary gland, architectural distortion, multiple small cysts, and echogenic foci without a hypoechoic area. Among these, hypoechoic areas are the most common and have served as the basis for defining NMLs in prior studies.
Building on this framework, recent review articles define NMLs on US as discrete areas of altered echotexture compared to surrounding tissue, lacking a defined shape or margins, and not fulfilling the criteria for a mass. For this review, NMLs were specifically defined as areas clearly visible in at least two orthogonal planes. Findings visible in only a single plane were regarded as normal variants, such as heterogeneous glandular tissue, and were excluded from this pictorial review (Fig. 1).

IMAGING FEATURES OF NONMASS LESIONS ON US

Echogenicity
The echogenicity of NMLs on US should be assessed relative to subcutaneous fat. NMLs can appear hypoechoic, isoechoic, hyperechoic, or have mixed echogenicity (Fig. 2). When interspersed with normal parenchyma, NMLs often show mixed, heterogeneous echogenicity, making detection challenging, especially in the presence of a heterogeneous background echotexture.

Distribution
The distribution of NMLs on US is classified as focal, linear, segmental, or regional (Fig. 3), similar to nonmass enhancement (NME) on MRI and calcifications on mammography. “Focal” indicates a small area involving less than one breast quadrant. “Linear” refers to lesions arranged in a line, suggesting a ductal course. “Segmental” describes a triangular area pointing toward the nipple, reflecting a ductal system. “Regiona” involves a larger area, typically exceeding one quadrant in extent.

Associated Features
Associated features of NMLs include calcifications, abnormal duct changes, architectural distortion, posterior shadowing, small cysts, and hypervascularity (Fig. 4). Calcifications appear as echogenic foci with or without shadowing. Abnormal duct changes refer to dilated ducts with irregular calibers or abnormal extensions from the NML. Architectural distortion refers to disrupted tissue planes or thickening/straightening of Cooper’s ligaments around the NML. Posterior shadowing indicates a focal area posterior to the NML that appears darker. Small cysts are defined as two or more thin-walled cysts <1 cm in size, within or around the NML. Hypervascularity indicates increased vascularity within or around the NML on Doppler imaging.

US FINDINGS IN BENIGN AND MALIGNANT NONMASS LESIONS
Regarding echogenicity, one study found that a mixed echoic pattern was significantly more common in malignant NMLs, with a positive predictive value (PPV) of 16.6%; however, another study found no significant correlation. Regarding lesion distribution, multiple studies consistently reported that segmental or linear distributions are associated with malignancy, with PPVs ranging from 22.9%–76.6%. Regarding associated findings, calcifications were consistently associated with malignancy, exhibiting PPVs ranging from 44.3%–100%. Posterior shadowing and abnormal duct changes were frequently associated with malignancy, with PPVs of 21.8%–95.0% and 11.2%–85.1%, respectively. Although architectural distortion showed PPVs of 10.0%–100%, multiple studies found no significant association with malignancy. Small cysts showed PPVs of 4.1%–36.8%, and one study reported their negative association with malignancy. Hypervascularity was generally associated with malignancy, exhibiting PPVs of 17.0%–81.7%.
US is often performed in conjunction with mammography, and mammographic findings can provide important complementary information for the diagnosis of NMLs. Malignant NMLs are frequently associated with suspicious mammographic features such as calcifications or asymmetry, whereas the malignancy rate is low when no suspicious features are present. A recent study further suggested that unnecessary biopsies might be avoided in asymptomatic women with negative mammograms undergoing screening US, when NMLs show combined sonographic features such as non-segmental distribution with an isoechoic pattern or with multiple small cysts. These combinations demonstrated PPVs of only 0.8% (2 of 247) and 1.0% (1 of 98), respectively. Taken together, careful integration of mammographic and sonographic features can improve diagnostic accuracy and help reduce unnecessary biopsies.

PATHOLOGIC FEATURES CORRELATED WITH BENIGN AND MALIGNANT NONMASS LESIONS

Benign Nonmass Lesions

Fibrocystic Change
FCC is the most common benign breast condition, classified as nonproliferative and proliferative types. Nonproliferative FCC includes cysts and apocrine metaplasia. It often appears on US as a focal NML with small anechoic cysts that reflect dilated acini within the terminal duct lobular unit (TDLU) and correspond to clustered microcysts as defined in BI-RADS (Fig. 5). Proliferative FCC includes usual ductal hyperplasia and adenosis []. Adenosis involves small glandular proliferation, and when surrounded by fibrosis that compresses and distorts acini, it is termed “sclerosing adenosis”. On US, fibrotic areas may appear hypoechoic, and compressed acini may cause architectural distortion (Fig. 6).

Stromal Fibrosis
Stromal fibrosis is a benign entity characterized by fibrous stromal proliferation that causes obliteration and atrophy of ducts and lobules, creating a localized fibrotic area with hypoplastic ductal and lobular structures. On US, fibrosis may appear as a hypoechoic area, occasionally accompanied by posterior shadowing or architectural distortion. These features likely result from fibrotic stroma composed of collagenous bundles, which compress and distort acini, occasionally mimicking malignancy (Figs. 7, 8).

Fibroadenomatoid Change
Fibroadenomatoid change is a benign breast lesion, previously termed fibroadenomatoid hyperplasia, sclerosing lobular hyperplasia, or fibroadenomatosis. It resembles a microscopic fibroadenoma, with interlobular stromal sclerosis elongating and compressing the glands, and presents as a diffuse or ill-defined lesion with overlapping features of fibroadenoma and FCC. On US, it may appear as a focal isoechoic or hypoechoic area without clear margins, forming NMLs rather than discrete masses (Fig. 9).

Pseudoangiomatous Stromal Hyperplasia
Pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia is a benign mesenchymal lesion characterized by a complex network of slit-like, anastomosing spaces lined by slender spindle cells within a background of stromal hyperplasia. On mammography, it may appear as developing asymmetry, and is rarely associated with architectural distortion or microcalcifications. Most appear as circumscribed oval hypoechoic masses on US, but when interspersed within normal glandular tissue, they may present as heterogeneous or echogenic areas with central hypoechoic regions, forming focal or regional NML (Fig. 10).

Benign Nonmass Lesions With Upgrade Potential

Papillary Lesions
Papillary lesions feature a fibrovascular stalk surrounded by varying grades of epithelial proliferation, with or without myoepithelial cells. On US, they typically manifest as intraluminal masses within dilated ducts. These may appear as segmental or linear hypoechoic or mixed echoic NMLs with abnormal duct changes and cysts and show hypervascularity due to the fibrovascular core (Fig. 11).

Atypical Ductal Hyperplasia
Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) shows low-grade cytologic and architectural atypia confined to one TDLU and measuring ≤2 mm. It shares identical cellular features with low-grade DCIS but is distinguished by its limited extent. ADH commonly presents as grouped amorphous calcifications on mammography and as NME on MRI; it may also appear as a corresponding NML on US (Fig. 12). However, ADH can also appear as an irregular, hypoechoic, microlobulated mass without posterior features, often mimicking malignancy.

Lobular Carcinoma In Situ
Classic lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) is defined by atypical proliferation of non-cohesive monotonous epithelial cells distending >50% of acini in a TDLU. Variants include pleomorphic and florid LCIS. Classic LCIS lacks distinct clinical or imaging features and is often incidentally detected during biopsies for unrelated lesions. On US, it may present as an NML or appear mass-like when coexisting with fibroadenomas or sclerosing adenosis (Fig. 13). Pleomorphic and florid LCIS frequently demonstrate comedo necrosis and calcifications, facilitating mammographic detection, but may also appear as NMLs with architectural distortions.

Mucocele-Like Lesions
Mucocele-like lesions (MLLs) consist of cysts filled with mucin, often accompanied by focal rupture and stromal extravasation. MLLs are commonly detected incidentally as coarse, heterogeneous calcifications, likely due to calcified mucin. On US, MLLs may appear as well-defined hypoechoic oval or tubular masses with low-level internal echoes, or as complex cystic and solid lesions, with grouped cysts, forming focal or regional NMLs (Fig. 14).

Malignant Nonmass Lesions

Ductal Carcinoma In Situ
DCIS is a malignant clonal proliferation of epithelial cells confined within basement membrane-bound structures, without stromal invasion. On US, DCIS may appear as an NML with echogenic foci representing malignant calcifications (Fig. 15). Ductal abnormalities, such as neoduct formation, duct ectasia, or intraductal echogenic material, may also be seen (Fig. 16). DCIS is commonly identified on mammography as suspicious calcifications or on MRI as NME, and is often detected on US as a corresponding lesion during targeted evaluation rather than screening.

Invasive Ductal Carcinoma
Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) is a malignant epithelial proliferation extending beyond the basement membrane, unlike DCIS. Although IDC primarily presents as a mass, it is the most common type of breast cancer and accounts for 57.3%–62.4% of malignant NMLs detected on breast US. IDCs presenting as NMLs often coexist with DCIS (Fig. 17) more frequently than IDCs presenting as mass-forming IDC, particularly in cases of microinvasion or minimal invasion. On US, these NMLs often appear as segmental lesions with calcifications and posterior acoustic shadowing.

Invasive Lobular Carcinoma
ILC is the second most common histologic type of breast carcinoma, accounting for 10%–15% of invasive cancers. It consists of small, uniform tumor cells with a single-file growth pattern and tends to spread diffusely or along collagen fibers with minimal desmoplastic response. On US, ILC may appear as an NML or a mass, often accompanied by architectural distortion or posterior shadowing. Its growth within periductal stroma without forming a discrete mass likely explains its appearance as an NML on US (Fig. 18). While the desmoplastic reaction is generally less pronounced than in IDC, it may still produce posterior shadowing as an associated US finding of NML.

APPROACH FOR EVALUATING NONMASS LESIONS ON BREAST US
The suggested approach for evaluating NML on US is illustrated in Figure 19. First, determine whether the finding is a true NML or a normal variant, such as heterogeneous or dense glandular tissue. Radiologists should assess whether the altered echotexture is clearly distinguishable in both the initial and orthogonal planes. If the finding is visible in only one plane but poorly defined or absent in the other, it is likely a normal variant. Symmetry may also aid interpretation; a similar finding at the same location in the contralateral breast suggests a physiologic cause.
Once confirmed as an NML, correlation with suspicious findings on other imaging modalities should be evaluated. In such cases, the malignancy rate is higher, and biopsy is recommended. Next, assess the distribution. Linear or segmental NMLs are classified as BI-RADS 4 or 5, with biopsy recommended. For focal or regional NMLs, management depends on associated suspicious findings, such as calcifications, posterior shadowing, abnormal duct changes, architectural distortion, or hypervascularity. If any of these features are present, BI-RADS 4 or 5 applies, and biopsy is recommended. If only interspersed small cysts are observed, BI-RADS 2 may be appropriate. For screening-detected NMLs with focal or regional distribution, a BI-RADS 3 assessment is appropriate if no suspicious features or small cysts are identified; a 6-month follow-up is recommended. NMLs that enlarge or develop suspicious features during follow-up should undergo biopsy. This US interpretation approach supports standardized NML management using literature-based criteria, helping to reduce false positives and unnecessary biopsies while ensuring detection of clinically significant cancers.

CONCLUSION
NMLs on US are defined as discrete areas of altered echotexture compared to surrounding breast tissue, lacking the three-dimensionality of a mass. NMLs on US are described by echogenicity, distribution, and associated features. Among the associated features, the presence of calcifications, abnormal duct changes, posterior shadowing, or correlation with suspicious findings on other imaging modalities suggests a higher likelihood of malignancy. In contrast, NMLs without these features but with multiple small cysts likely indicate benign conditions like FCC. The stepwise, imaging feature–based approach proposed in this study may assist in the diagnostic evaluation of NMLs.

B, C: Transverse (B) and longitudinal (C) targeted US images show a corresponding mixed echoic NML (arrows) with segmental distribution. This NML is associated with multiple echogenic calcifications in dilated ducts (dashed arrows) and posterior shadowing (arrowheads). D: Color Doppler US image shows increased vascularity in the area of the NML. E: Axial T1-weighted postcontrast MR image shows a corresponding nonmass enhancement (arrows) with segmental distribution. US-guided vacuum-assisted biopsy revealed DCIS. F: Histopathology of hematoxylin and eosin-stained section shows high-grade DCIS with large calcifications (red triangles), desmoplastic reaction (scar-like fibrosis, asterisks), and duct dilatations (arrows). NML = nonmass lesion, DCIS = ductal carcinoma in situ, US = ultrasound
A 46-year-old woman with a malignant NML due to IDC with DCIS. A: Right magnification craniocaudal mammographic image shows a focal asymmetry with grouped fine pleomorphic calcifications (arrows). B, C: Transverse (B) and longitudinal (C) targeted US images show a mixed echoic NML (arrows) with focal distribution. This NML is associated with echogenic calcifications (dashed arrows), considered a US correlate for the mammographic abnormality. D: Color Doppler US image shows increased vascularity in the area of the NML. E: Subtracted axial T1-weighted postcontrast MR image shows a corresponding nonmass enhancement (arrows) with focal distribution. US-guided core needle biopsy and subsequent surgery revealed IDC with DCIS. F: Histopathology of hematoxylin and eosin-stained section from surgery shows DCIS (red triangles) with multifocal IDC (arrowheads). Lobular involvement of DCIS (arrows) is observed. NML = nonmass lesion, IDC = invasive ductal carcinoma, DCIS = ductal carcinoma in situ, US = ultrasound
A 51-year-old woman with a malignant NML due to ILC. A: Right craniocaudal mammographic image shows an asymmetry (arrows) at the palpable site. B, C: Transverse (B) and longitudinal (C) targeted US images show a corresponding hypoechoic NML (arrows) with segmental distribution. This NML is associated with posterior shadowing (dashed arrows). D: Subtracted axial T1-weighted postcontrast MR image shows a corresponding nonmass enhancement (arrows) with segmental distribution. US-guided biopsy and subsequent surgery revealed ILC. E: Histopathology of hematoxylin and eosin-stained section from surgery shows ILC (arrows) with a central fibrosclerotic scar-like area (arrowheads), considered a pathologic correlate of posterior shadowing of the NML. NML = nonmass lesion, ILC = invasive lobular carcinoma, US = ultrasound
Diagnostic approach for NML on US. *In diagnostic settings, US NMLs that correlate with clinical abnormalities or significant findings on other imaging modalities are biopsied because the probability of malignancy for these US NMLs is significantly higher than that of screening settings, †Calcifications, posterior shadowing, abnormal duct change, architectural distortion, hypervascularity. In particular, calcifications in NML highly suggest malignancy, ‡BI-RADS 2 assessment is given when “interspersed small cysts” suggestive of fibrocystic changes are seen. NML = nonmass lesion, US = ultrasound, BI-RADS = Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System
