High-density lipoprotein particles are associated with the risk of mortality from all-causes and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) in patients with CVD: a systematic review and meta-analysis study
Background & aims
Increasing data indicate that the concentration of high-density lipoprotein particles (HDL-p) may be a strong indicator of cardiovascular disease. This study aimed to carry out a meta-analysis to examine the link between subclasses of HDL-p and the hazard of mortality in individuals suffering from cardiovascular diseases (CVDs).
Methods
A rigorous systematic search was executed through Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science up to April 2025.
Results
Seven unique cohort studies were deemed eligible for incorporation in this meta-analysis. The overall hazard was derived by applying a random effects model. The results indicated a negative association of total HDL-p (RR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.69–0.78, P < 0.001) and small HDL-p with all-cause mortality (RR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.63–0.75, P < 0.001) for each 5 µmol/l increment. Similarly, each 5 µmol/l large HDL-p was directly related to all-cause mortality (RR: 1.71, 95% CI: 1.14– 2.56; P = 0.009). Moreover, each 5 µmol/l increase in small HDL-p was linked to a 33% reduction in CVD mortality (RR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.50–0.91; P = 0.01).
Conclusion
This study revealed that small and total HDL-p were negatively correlated with all-cause mortality, whereas latge HDL-p was positively related to mortality from all causes. Additionally, small HDL-p had an inverse relationship with CVD mortality in patients with CVD.

Introduction
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) plays a crucial role in both mortality and morbidity worldwide. The total number of prevalent cases increased almost twofold, growing from 271 million in 1990 to 523 million in 2019. Precise predictions are vital for risk evaluation and effective treatment intervention, especially in high-risk individuals, including those with hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, or other cardiometabolic disorders. Hence, finding novel markers that represent many features of the underlying pathophysiology could enhance the evaluation of CVD risk.
Epidemiological research has consistently demonstrated that higher HDL cholesterol (HDL-C) levels are associated with a lower risk of CVD. Nevertheless, the exact function of HDL in the onset of CVD risk remains unclear, and current medications intended to increase HDL-C values do not lead to improved cardiovascular outcomes. Moreover, several investigations have demonstrated a contradictory association between increased levels of HDL-C and an increased risk of any cause and cardiovascular mortality.
One possible explanation for these controversies is that HDL-C, as a marker for HDL-associated risk, does not adequately capture critical aspects of HDL’s atheroprotective features, including cholesterol removal ability and its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antithrombotic impacts. These functional traits are largely associated with smaller HDL particle subtypes, which are not adequately indicated by HDL-C, an agent that primarily represents levels of larger, cholesterol-loaded HDL particles. Therefore, a rise in the level of HDL cholesterol is not necessarily connected to elevated HDL fraction functionality. Furthermore, HDL-C only partially correlates with indicators of reverse cholesterol disposal. Another attributed element is that systemic inflammation can impact the operational properties of HDL subtypes by changing their protein and/or lipid signatures.
Thus, HDL-C has an intrinsic bias toward larger, more cholesterol- and lipid-dense particles and tends to underestimate the smaller, less lipid-filled types. Recent research revealed that the mere serum values of HDL-C may not reliably reflect the atheroprotective effect of HDL. In fact, there is no evidence indicating that the cholesterol within HDL independently exerts antiatherosclerosis effects.
An increasing amount of data emphasizes that high-density lipoprotein particle (HDL-p) concentration may be a stronger prognostic factor for CVD and therapeutic response than HDL-C is. Different subclasses of HDL particles, categorized by their size, have been connected to differing levels of threat for CVD and total mortality in several studies. Studies have indicated that small- and moderate-sized HDL particles contribute to the atheroprotective effects of HDL. Smaller particles typically have a greater proportion of protein to lipid, which may increase their functionality in reverse cholesterol transport (RCT). Furthermore, they contain a high concentration of apolipoprotein AI (ApoA-I), along with enzymes and proteins that play active roles in anti-inflammatory pathways, such as paraoxonase (PON), which reduces oxidative stress. Oxidized low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is a powerful proinflammatory factor that plays a pivotal role in atherosclerosis. Smaller HDL-p can mitigate oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals and inhibiting LDL oxidation.
The smaller size and greater protein content give them a better surface for engaging with cells and other lipoproteins. Hence, they are more effective at promoting cholesterol removal from macrophages. In addition, small HDL-p is associated with better endothelial function because it can enhance nitric oxide production. However, large HDL-p is abundant in high levels of triglycerides and cholesterol esters, making it rich in lipids. Their increased size can result in a more intricate surface structure, which can affect their interactions with other lipoproteins and cells. Additionally, large HDL-p may have limited anti-inflammatory effects in comparison with small HDL-p, as its greater size can impede its ability to penetrate tissues and exhibit protective functions. Although large HDL-p contributes to cholesterol transport, its efficacy in reverse cholesterol transport is generally less than that of small HDL-p. Hence, compared with larger, dense HDL particles, smaller HDL particles are thought to be more efficient at reducing cholesterol, reducing inflammation, demonstrating anti-inflammatory properties, and protecting endothelial cells.
The Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) revealed that heightened levels of HDL particles were connected to a diminished risk of developing coronary heart disease (CHD) when HDL-C levels were taken into account. However, when HDL-p was adjusted for, the inverse relationship between HDL-C and CHD decreased. Similar findings were reported in the JUPITER (Justification for the Use of Statins in Prevention: An Intervention Trial Evaluating Rosuvastatin) study, in which HDL-p levels were conversely related to CVD events, regardless of placebo or medication use. To the best of our knowledge, no systematic review has been performed on the associations between subclasses of HDL-p and the risk of mortality in CVD patients. Although previous research has concentrated solely on HDL-C concentrations, this study systematically reviewed and provided a quantitative assessment (meta-analysis) of HDL-p subtypes and their predictive power for mortality among CVD patients. This approach potentially delivers more nuanced insights than previous studies that have focused primarily on HDL-C levels alone. The hypothesis of this study was that specific particles of HDL-p are associated with varying risks of mortality in CVD populations.

Methods
The present research adhered to the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) report. Moreover, the research protocol was documented in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration ID CRD42024457534.

Search strategy
A thorough and rigorous bibliographic search, without constraints on the date of publication, was executed utilizing electronic databases, such as Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus, up to April 2025 to identify relevant research. The Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms pertaining to “high-density lipoprotein”, “HDL”, “HDL-particle”, and “HDL subclasses” and relevant MeSH terms of “mortality” were incorporated in the query search (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the bibliography of qualified studies, along with the initial 4 pages of Google Scholar, was physically reviewed to determine further possibly related articles. The search had no language or date restrictions. The final output of the study search was examined by two authors to determine eligible studies. Any disputes were settled through discussion with the lead corresponding author (NS).

Study selection
EndNote software (version 20.4.1) was used to handle the obtained documents. After eliminating duplicates, two evaluators individually evaluated the qualifications of the studies that remained using the PECOS framework, which consisted of the following criteria: population: patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) aged > 18 years; exposure: high-density lipoprotein particles (HDL-p); comparator: highest versus lowest concentration of each HDL-p subclass; outcome: CVD and all-cause mortality; and prospective or retrospective cohort studies. If a study was conducted on different populations, the authors considered that study to have distinct effect sizes. Studies were excluded if they were comments, letters, randomized controlled trials, reviews, case reports, cross-sectional and case‒control studies, or work on patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Additionally, studies with inadequate data were also excluded. Two investigators verified the omitted documents, and any inconsistencies were settled via discussion.

Data extraction
To determine eligibility, two investigators independently reviewed the titles, summaries, and full texts of the extracted articles. They then extracted the required data via a standardized evaluation form, which encompassed the following elements: name of the lead author, year of publication, research design, research sample, outcome, participant characteristics, number of individuals with death outcomes, average age, sex, and any adjustments for interference factors. In cases where multiple risk estimates for the outcome were reported, the effect measure from the thoroughly adjusted model was chosen and applied in the meta-analysis. Any inconsistencies during the review process were fixed via consultation and collaboration with the corresponding author (NS).

Quality evaluation of the studies
The quality and methodological standards of the chosen studies were examined via the Newcastle‒Ottawa Scale (NOS). This 8-item checklist encompasses three elements: 1) selection (population representativeness, which can receive up to 4 scores), 2) comparability (managing associated confounders in the research setup or during analytical procedures, with the highest score of 2), and 3) outcomes, with the highest score of 3. The highest score of 9 assigned for each study reflects its top quality. Research studies earning a score above 6 were classified as high-quality, those with grades between 4–6 were regarded as medium-quality, and studies receiving scores of 0–3 were categorized as low-quality. The quality judgment was independently reviewed by two investigators, and any conflicts among the authors were settled by collaboration and consultation with the lead researcher (NS).

Statistical analysis
In all the selected studies, the relative risks (RRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were regarded as the primary effect measures. Presuming that the actual underlying exposure effects differ across studies, the authors chose a random effects model over a fixed effects model. Therefore, a random effects model that accounts for interstudy variability was utilized to determine the combined effect measure. The variability between studies was checked by using Cochrane’s Q test and the I-squared test (I2). Subgroup analyses based on adjustments made for HDL-C, body mass index (BMI), and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) were conducted to identify possible sources of heterogeneity between studies. Furthermore, a meta-regression analysis was performed, incorporating the aforementioned variables along with sample size and follow-up duration. To determine the linear dose‒response association across HDL particle categories, the technique introduced by Greenland and Long Necker and Orsini et al. was employed, requiring the number of deaths and the adjusted effect measure (odds ratio, relative risk, or hazard ratio) for a minimum of 3 HDL particle categories. According to this technique, the middle value of exposure in each classification of HDL particles was regarded as the representative effect measure for each study. In instances where the values of the exposure dose were within a range, the middle value for each category bound was determined by calculating the average of the lowest and highest values. To evaluate whether the overall combined results could be influenced by a particular study, a sensitivity analysis was carried out by sequentially excluding each article from the analysis. Similarly, publication bias assessment was conducted via visual inspection of funnel plots and statistically verified by Egger’s and Begg’s regression tests. If the results indicated significant publication bias, a trim-and-fill method was used to determine the effect of potential unpublished studies on the combined results. Stata, version 14 (StataCorp), was employed to conduct all the statistical analyses. Values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Five studies with six effect measures, including 8,414 participants, examined the connection between serum concentrations of total HDL-p and all-cause mortality. Overall, the meta-analysis indicated that each 5 µmol/l increase in total HDL-p was inversely correlated with all-cause mortality (RR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.69–0.78, P < 0.001), with low interstudy heterogeneity (I2 = 42.1%, P = 0.124) (Fig. 2). A subgroup analysis was carried out based on adjustments for HDL-C, BMI, and the GFR, and the results suggested that the correlation between total HDL-p and mortality was more pronounced in the studies with no adjustment for BMI than in the studies adjusting for BMI (Table 2). The pooled analysis of eight effect sizes, with 253,599 participants, revealed a significant positive correlation between serum values of large HDL-p (each 5 µmol/l increase) and all-cause mortality (RR: 1.71, 95% CI: 1.14–2.56; P = 0.009) (Fig. 3). Considering the I2 = 90.9% and the Cochrane Q test (P < 0.001), the studies had high heterogeneity. In addition, large HDL-p levels were significantly positively associated with death from all causes in the subgroups adjusted for HDL-C and BMI (Table 2). A meta-analysis of four studies with 251,447 subjects did not reveal a significant connection between medium HDL-p and death from all causes (RR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.66–1.39; P = 0.827). High heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 92.1%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Seven studies, including 253,599 participants, evaluated the connection between small HDL-p and all-cause death, and the results revealed that each 5 µmol/l increase in small HDL-p was negatively correlated with the outcome (RR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.63–0.75; P < 0.001). Considering the I2 = 68% and the Cochrane Q test (P = 0.003), heterogeneity between studies was relatively high (Fig. 5). Subgroup analyses adjusted for HDL-C, BMI, and the GFR did not change the overall results (Table 2). The results of the meta-regression indicated that sample size, follow-up duration, participant age, and adjustments for HDL-C, BMI, and GFR had no significant effects on the associations between total HDL-p, large HDL-p, or small HDL-p and all-cause mortality (Table 3).

A combined analysis of three studies involving 2,823 participants revealed no significant connection between large HDL-p and mortality from CVD (risk ratio [RR]: 2.08, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.56–7.80, P = 0.276). There was moderate heterogeneity between studies, but it did not reach statistical significance (I2 = 62.9, P = 0.067) (Fig. 6). In contrast, the combined analysis of three studies with the same 2,823 subjects revealed that each 5 µmol/L increase in small HDL-p was linked to a 33% reduction in CVD mortality (RR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.50–0.91, P = 0.01). The variability among studies was moderate but not statistically significant (I2 = 57.6, p = 0.094) (Fig. 7). Subgroup analysis and meta-regression for the link between serum levels of HDL-p and CVD mortality were not possible due to insufficient effect sizes.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias
The sensitivity analysis regarding the associations of total HDL-p, large HDL-p, medium HDL-p, and small HDL-p with total mortality indicated that the results remained unaffected by the exclusion of any distinct study. The correlation between large particles of HDL and CVD mortality was heavily dependent on the study conducted by Duparc et al.. After excluding this study from the overall analysis, the result shifted to a significant level (RR: 4.79, 95% CI: 1.34–17.08). In addition, excluding those studies conducted by Duparc et al. (RR: 0.33, 95% CI: 0.03–3.04), and Silbernagel et al. (RR: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.04–2.21) changed the results regarding the connection between small HDL-p and CVD mortality. Egger’s test revealed no indications of publication bias for large HDL-p and total mortality (P = 0.224) or for large HDL-p and CVD mortality (P = 0.105). However, Egger’s test indicated publication bias for the associations of total HDL-p (P = 0.027), medium HDL-p (P = 0.006), and small HDL-p with total mortality (P = 0.005), as well as the association of small HDL-p with mortality from CVD (P = 0.033). Nevertheless, there was no indication of publication bias after the trim-and-fill procedure was applied.

Discussion
This study is the first to implement a dose‒response meta-analysis to quantify the correlation between circulating concentrations of HDL particles and mortality in subjects suffering from cardiovascular disease (CVD). The findings indicated that for each 5 µmol/l increase in total HDL-p and small HDL-p, the risk of death from all causes was significantly reduced by 26% and 31%, respectively. In contrast, each 5 µmol/l increase in large HDL-p was related to a 71% increase in the overall risk of death. Only three studies assessed the link between large and small particles of HDL and CVD mortality. Although the results revealed no significant connection between large HDL-p and CVD mortality, a negative relationship was detected between small HDL particles and CVD mortality. These results add to an increasing set of evidence suggesting that HDL-C levels, which represent the cholesterol quantity in HDL particles, offer a rough and occasionally inaccurate evaluation of the role of HDL levels in heart protection. It is becoming increasingly clear that HDL-C is not closely connected to the functionality of HDL. For example, in large-scale randomized clinical trials, the causal cardioprotective properties of HDL-C have not been demonstrated.
Furthermore, in several epidemiologic studies conducted in the general population, extremely elevated concentrations of HDL-C were unexpectedly linked to increased risks of overall and CVD mortality. HDL-p is an alternative metric of HDL that assigns equal importance to all HDL subfractions. A study by McGarrah revealed that HDL-p had a pronounced negative correlation with mortality compared with HDL-C. Furthermore, a distinctive link between HDL subfractions and mortality has been reported, such that small and medium HDL-p levels are more significantly inversely related, whereas large HDL-p levels are not related to mortality. These results align with current research indicating that elevated amounts of HDL particles are linked to a diminished risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) and CHD mortality. Analysis of subgroups adjusted for BMI, HDL-C, and the GFR revealed no significant between-group heterogeneity. For total HDL-p, the association with all-cause death was more pronounced in studies that overlooked BMI adjustment than in those that did. Since only two studies did not control for BMI, drawing a clear conclusion in this regard is challenging. Research indicates that small HDL-p serves as an effective receptor for ATP-binding Cassette Transporter A1 (ABCA1)-induced cholesterol transfer from macrophages. Via this transport process, cholesterol within cells is delivered to the cytoplasmic membrane. The strong ability of small HDL-p to promote cholesterol removal results from their reduced lipid composition and greater fluidity of surface lipids, which can trigger configurational modification in apoA-I, unlike large HDL. Owing to their capacity to deactivate oxidized lipids, small, dense HDL particles might be more effective than large HDL-p particles. The decreased sphingomyelin and free cholesterol content in small, dense HDL-p might result in greater flexibility of the surface lipid monolayer, consequently promoting the assimilation of oxidized lipids from external sources, such as those originating from oxidized LDL. An increase in the enzymatic functions of PON1, lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), and platelet-activating factor acetylhydrolase (PAF-AH) in small HDL-p may account for this effect. The specialized proteome of small HDL-p may also affect its ability to counteract oxidation. Additionally, small, concentrated, protein-enriched HDL-p has been noted to be more potent than large, lipid-heavy HDL-p at suppressing the regulation of vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) in endothelial cells. Aligning with their pronounced antioxidative capabilities. On the other hand, large HDL-p appears to be less potent than small HDL-p in exerting several atheroprotective effects, such as cholesterol transfer, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, and cytoprotective effects on the endothelium. Furthermore, increases in large HDL may indicate impaired HDL catabolism, especially concerning the absorption of HDL by the liver, which is the final stage in reverse cholesterol transport. Similarly, in subjects suffering from coronary artery disease (CAD), HDL-C did not show any association with mortality, which was in agreement with the absence of a relationship between large HDL-p and mortality. HDL-C generally mirrors the cholesterol quantity of large, lipid-dense HDL particles. The subgroup analysis revealed that the association of high levels of large HDL-p with mortality was stronger in those studies that controlled for HDL-C than in those that did not, indicating that other underlying mechanisms may contribute to the observed relationship between large HDL particles and mortality. As previously noted in the results section, the pooled effect sizes of three studies regarding the connection between large HDL-p and mortality from CVD became significant following the removal of the research conducted by Duparc et al.. This might be related to the lower number of participants in the Duparc et al. study than in the other two studies. Additionally, this study did not control for several confounding variables, such as BMI, inflammatory factors, and the GFR, which may influence the results of the sensitivity analysis.
Likewise, excluding the research conducted by Duparc et al. and Silbernagel et al. also changed the results regarding the correlation between small HDL-p and CVD death. These two studies have longer follow-up durations and relatively larger sample sizes, and they account for the substantial weight of the studies considered, resulting in greater power. Therefore, the change in overall results after removing these studies could be related to their greater weight.
The HDL-C measurements denote the overall HDL quantity, which disregards the variability of various HDL-p subfractions. Various tools are currently being used to evaluate the functional heterogeneity of HDL. The gold standard for assessing HDL subclasses is analytical ultracentrifugation. However, this method is time intensive, costly, and deemed unsuitable for routine HDL subclass analyses. In this meta-analysis, all the considered studies used nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, which is a new technique for the examination of lipoprotein types. This technique has not been harmonized, and the projected diameter span of the HDL subset differs depending on the assessment techniques employed. Additionally, no categorization of HDL subtypes measured by NMR has been confirmed for regular use.
Therefore, further research is necessary to clarify the therapeutic and biological importance of these various methods. Additionally, the biochemical and structural links among individual HDL subtypes are not yet fully understood. Notably, small, dense HDL-p has strong atheroprotective effects and shows promise as a new clinical target. Identifying the major biological impacts of HDL subtypes and the clinical significance of anti-atherogenic HDL constituents is pivotal. This will not only help in the creation of more effective cardiovascular risk biomarkers relative to HDL-C but also in the production of new atherosclerosis-preventing medications directed toward specific HDL particle groups. This study offers a valuable perspective on how HDL particles are linked with mortality risk in patients suffering from CVD. These findings may imply innovative methods for therapeutic approaches targeting HDL particle concentrations, potentially resulting in enhanced patient management in a clinical context. This information can assist clinicians in better stratifying risk among patients and in decision-making regarding the treatment of those with CVD, resulting in more personalized treatment strategies. Clinicians can apply these findings to inform patients concerning the significance of HDL particles, enhancing their involvement in lifestyle changes that may promote their cardiovascular health.

Strengths
A thorough and systematic search across online databases, the adoption of a detailed and effective methodology to determine relevant research, the execution of subgroup analysis to detect the source of heterogeneity, and the appraisal of the impact of an individual study on the combined findings represent certain positive aspects of the present research.

Limitations
The present research confronted several challenges that might compromise the overall conclusion. Given that a meta-analysis of observational research was conducted, the effect of potential biases, such as selection bias, should not be overlooked. Importantly, observational data cannot establish causal relationships. Additionally, remaining or unaccounted confounding factors may alter the degree of the relationship between HDL particles and mortality. For example, despite an opposite correlation between CRP and HDL-p being reported by several studies, only one of the included studies controlled for CRP. Since the kidney serves as the central site of HDL catabolism, chronic kidney disease (CKD) intensely alters HDL formation and metabolism. The evidence indicates that advanced stages of CKD may significantly diminish the protective functions of HDL and increase the presence of ineffective or potentially pro-atherogenic types of HDL. This alteration may be attributed to a reduced GFR. Although most of the included studies adjusted for the GFR, none considered CKD as a confounding factor. Furthermore, the number of studies adjusted for lipid-lowering drugs was too small to conduct a subgroup analysis. Therefore, considering that most of the included studies adjusted for various sets of covariates that could lead to different conclusions, caution should be taken when interpreting this study’s findings. Finally, the small number of considered studies, the small population in some of these studies, the heterogeneities in the characteristics of the patients, and the follow-up durations warrant the need for more well-designed prospective studies.

Conclusion
This study revealed that for every 5 µmol/L increase in total and small HDL-p, the risk of all-cause mortality decreased by 26% and 31%, respectively. Furthermore, a 5 µmol/L increase in small HDL-p was associated with a 33% reduction in CVD mortality. In contrast, a 5 µmol/L increase in large HDL-p concentration was linked to a 71% increase in all-cause mortality. These data imply that HDL-p is likely to be a stronger predictor of mortality and therapeutic response than HDL-C is among patients with CVD. However, owing to the limited number of incorporated studies and the variability among them, further prospective research with larger sample sizes and stricter controls for confounding variables is needed to confirm the results. There is also potential for clinical trials to assess interventions aimed at increasing or improving HDL particles and measuring their effects on mortality and morbidity in subjects with CVD.
