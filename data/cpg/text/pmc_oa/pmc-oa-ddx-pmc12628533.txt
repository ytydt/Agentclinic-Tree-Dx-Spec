Preventive interventions on ACL injury reduction based on the landing error scoring system evaluation tool: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Background
Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) injuries pose a significant risk in sports, often leading to long rehabilitation and re-injury. The Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) has been proposed as a means of assessing the risk of potential ACL damage. This study employed Network meta-analysis to assess the effectiveness of various preventive interventions in reducing ACL injury risk among athletes, utilizing LESS evaluation tool to identify the most effective strategies.
Methods
A comprehensive search for related publications in English in databases, such as WOS, Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, and Scopus from the establishment of the databases to February 2025. Relevant content in the literature that passed the screening criteria was extracted, including the sample, first author, intervention, intervention duration, and outcome. We compared the Mean Difference (MD) values across studies and used network meta-analysis based on Bayesian modelling.
Results
A Network meta-analysis of 20 studies (N = 1,356) was conducted. After comparison, assessing the impact of eight training interventions on the LESS scores. Dynamic Taping (MD, -1.87, 95% CrI, -3.29 to -0.46), Neuromuscular Training (MD, -1.04, 95% CrI, -2.01 to -0.10), and Feedback Training (MD, -0.86 95% CrI, -1.54 to -0.20) were the most effective in reducing injury risk. The SUCRA rankings from highest to lowest are as follows: Dynamic Taping (79.0), Neuromuscular Training (53.2), and Feedback Training (43.0).
Conclusion
Network meta-analysis simultaneously integrates evidence from both direct comparisons and indirect comparisons. Evidence from Network meta-analysis based on Bayesian models, Dynamic Taping, Neuromuscular Training and Feedback Training are effective intervention training programs for ACL injury prevention.

Introduction
ACL injuries are becoming more frequent in young athletes who play sports involving leaping, cutting, and rotating. ACL ruptures, in particular, come with a long and costly rehabilitation period and an increased re-injury rate, especially for athletes under the age of 20. Regardless of gender, the consequences of an ACL rupture for athletes are extremely severe. After an ACL injury, only 65% of people return to their preinjury level of sport, despite extensive therapy that frequently takes more than 9 to 12 months. Compared to people without injuries, many people with ACL injuries experience chronic pain, worse knee function, and a lower quality of life. Therefore, given the range of sequelae and high rehabilitation costs associated with ACL injuries as well as the lengthy recovery process, prevention of ACL injuries as well as early screening is essential.
Movement analysis studies have indicated biomechanical risk factors for injuries sustained during jump landings. Although stiffer landing cannot be completely avoided in many parts of the sport, lower extremities injury risk may rise as a result of a stiffer landing method and misdirection of force caused by a limited range of motion of the lower extremity joints (e.g., less knee or hip flexion). Another underlying biomechanical risk factor for jump landing injuries is an increased knee valgus based on simultaneous hip adduction and internal rotation during closed-chain knee flexion with extra ankle eversion. Meanwhile, increased cognitive demands can also lead to an increased risk of ACL injuries including, but not limited to, deficits in euromuscular control, declines in proprioceptive function, and abnormalities in landing postures.
The Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) is a clinical assessment tool often used in research to identify individuals at high risk of sustaining noncontact injuries and to quantify changes in neuromuscular and biomechanical performance subsequent to intervention across sports and performance levels. LESS has also been used to measure outcomes from rehabilitation and residual functional deficits in individuals with a history of injury and following ACL replacement. The current evidence indicates that the overall LESS score has good-to-excellent intra-rater, interrater, and intersession reliabilities. LESS effectively differentiated between groups on a variety of jump-landing biomechanics that have been linked to ACL loading and injury processes in the past, which is the authors’ most significant discovery. In contrast to previous assessment tools, such as Y-balance test, LESS assessment tool is an assessment tool that considers the biomechanics of the overall jump landing phase in a multiplanar movement pattern, whereas ACL injuries are considered to be inherently multifactorial and involve multiple biomechanical risk factors in multiple planes. Therefore, LESS includes a more comprehensive multi-planar biomechanical assessment. Also, none of the analyses have used the same parameter such as LESS, which makes it difficult to compare between the effects of training, therefore, this study selected LESS as the standard for the outcome measures.
Although there were many studies on training to prevent ACL injuries, there is not yet an analysis that summarizes which training approaches is more effective, the Network meta-analysis method innovatively compares the effects of different interventions, simultaneously integrating both direct and indirect evidence. This facilitates the intuitive selection of more effective intervention approaches.
The purpose of this study was to systematically review training interventions designed to reduce biomechanical risk factors for lower extremity joint injuries during jump landing performance based on LESS evaluation tool. Furthermore, relevant effect sizes (ES) were also integrated to explore the most effective interventions to reduce the risk of ACL injury in athletic participants.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses-Network Meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA) criteria were followed in the presentation of our findings. This Network meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO (Number: CRD42024606602).

Search strategy and information sources
We conducted database searches, without filters or date limits, in The Cochrane Library via CENTRAL, SPORTDiscus via Embase, and Embase, PubMed, Web of science. The last search was conducted on February 10, 2025. Google Scholar was used to track citations to ensure that studies were not inadvertently missed. When additional studies were identified that met the inclusion criteria, they were included in the final pool of studies. Because of the small number of relevant studies, we searched studies by keywords “Landing Error Scoring System” and [(“ACL” or “Anterior Cruciate Ligament” or “Anterior Cruciate Ligament Injuries” or “injury” or “wounds” or “biomechanical risk factors” or “noncontact injury mechanism” or “injury factor”) or (“intervention”or “training” or “exercise” or “education” or “neuromuscular training” or “balance training” or “resistance training” or “injury prevention” or “prevention”) or (“RCT” or “random control trails” or “control group”)] and manually screened each article with no restriction on publication date, But it must be published in English and peer-reviewed. Two reviewers (JKZ and ZRZ) independently selected studies for inclusion in the two-step process, in case of uncertainty regarding inclusion, a third reviewer (ZZC) was consulted. First, studies were screened based on title and abstract. In the second stage, full-text studies were reviewed to identify those that met the eligibility criteria. We also searched the ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global website and included literature that met the eligibility criteria. Once a study failed to meet a single inclusion criterion, it was immediately excluded. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.
The final search was undertaken on Endnotes 21.4 software, by two reviewers (JKZ and ZRZ). Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened sequentially for inclusion and exclusion criteria. In case of uncertainty regarding inclusion, a third reviewer (ZZC) was consulted. Reference lists of the articles were hand searched for additional records. The last search was conducted on February 10, 2025.
Two researchers separately performed data extraction (JKZ, ZRZ). The extracted data were compared for all included studies, and disagreement was solved with group communications. The extracted data included the first author′s name, year of publication, basic information about the study population (number, age range, country, and region), LESS measures, type of intervention, and outcome indicators. Mean Difference (MD) values from baseline to post-intervention were the outcomes of interest and were extracted when reported. In cases where these values were not reported, baseline and post-intervention mean (± SD) data were extracted. Mean change was computed by subtracting baseline from the post-intervention value.

Eligibility criteria
Participants (P): Teenager, including young athletes and any youth participating in physical education classes or other sports activities.;
Interventions (I): All exercise or external interventions (e.g., real-time feedback from coaches) aimed at preventing or reducing ACL injuries (e.g., Neuromuscular Training, Augmentative Training, Injury Prevention Programs, etc.);
Comparisons (C): The study contained a control group of similar-age participants performing routine exercise (RE) or sham exercises without a specific focus on modifiable ACL injury risk factors practices.
Outcome (O): Using LESS as main outcome. On the basis of their LESS score, participants were divided into 4 categories, representing excellent (LESS score <4), good (LESS score >4 to ≤5), moderate (LESS score >5 to ≤6), and poor (LESS score >6) jump-landing biomechanics, that means, the higher scoring, the higher injury risk;
Study design (S): All studies that used either a randomized controlled trial or a non-randomized controlled trial.
The inclusion criteria for articles were determined using the PICOS (Participants/Interventions/Comparisons/Outcomes/Study Design) principles, as follows:
Exclusion criteria included: (1) Studies using modified versions of the original LESS (e.g., Real-time LESS or Interactive LESS or Automated Quantification of LESS or LESS with additional items); (2) Studies are not in the English language published were excluded; (3) Studies that did not provide mean and standard deviation or data that could be translated into mean and standard deviation form.

Assessment of quality of include studies
Two authors (JKZ and ZRZ) independently assessed the risk of bias. The Version 2 of the Cochrane Collaboration risk of bias (RoB2) instrument was used to assess bias across seven categories: Random sequence generation (selection bias); Allocation concealment (selection bias); Blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias); Blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias); Incomplete outcome data (attrition bias); Selective reporting (reporting bias); Other bias. As recommended, we rated each item as: (1) “low risk of bias” if it is completely fulfilled quality standards with the least bias; (2) “unclear” if it is plausible that a bias raises some doubt about the results; and (3) “high risk of bias” if it is plausible that a bias seriously weakens confidence in the results. In cases of disagreement, the rest of the group was consulted, and judgment was made based on consensus (JKZ, ZRZ, TL, ZZC) We used CINeMA to evaluate each comparison’s credibility against active control. The Cochrane Comparing Multiple Interventions Methods Group created this web-based tool as a Network meta-analysis adaptation of GRADE. We assessed each comparison for within study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence in accordance with suggested recommendations. As with GRADE, we downgraded the evidence based on concerns in each domain after determining that the comparisons showed high confidence.

Data synthesis

Credibility assessment
Before we conducting the data analysis, we evaluated three assumptions regarding network meta-analysis: transitivity, consistency, and comparability.
Regarding the transitivity assumption in network meta-analysis, we carefully examined the distribution of potential effect modifiers (e.g., patient baseline characteristics, intervention, study design) across the included studies to ensure that they were sufficiently comparable. No major discrepancies that would threaten the assumption of transitivity were identified.
Regarding the consistency hypothesis, we fitted inconsistent and consistent models to compare their Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) values and performed node splitting on key comparisons to validate the consistency hypothesis. If the difference in DIC values between the fitted models is less than 5, indicating no significant evidence against the consistency hypothesis, the consistent model might be selected. The node-splitting method was used to check for inconsistency between the direct and indirect effect sizes across the network framework, if p-value >0.05, this indicates network inconsistency.
Regarding the assessment of comparability, we conducted a detailed evaluation of the PICOs framework across the included studies to ensure sufficient similarity among them in follow aspects. Model Selection: We utilized Bayesian random-effects models for the network meta-analysis. We considered fixed-study, fixed-category-effects models and random-study, fixed-category-effects models. Estimation Method: Point estimates and 95% confidence intervals were constructed using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. Software: Statistical analyses were performed using the Gemtc package (Bayesian framework) and figures were generated with BUGSnet. Convergence Assessment: Gelman-Rubin Diagnostic Statistics: Gelman-Rubin diagnostic plots were used to assess model convergence. An R hat value close to 1.0 and below 1.01 indicates convergence.

Network meta-analysis
All Network meta-analytical approaches allow users to assess the effects of interventions against a range of comparisons. Bayesian meta-analyses were conducted in R using the package “Gemtc” and “BUGSnet”. We preregistered informative priors based on the distributional parameters of our metanalytical model. Each outcome’s direct evidence was used to build Network plots. The MCMC algorithm was used to implement Bayesian NMA with three chains and 50,000 interactions each (burn-in duration of 20,000) https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook, https://books.google.com.hk/books?id=Kx7KNBNInocC. To evaluate convergence, trace plots were employed. The choice between fixed and random effects modelling was made using the residual deviance and the DIC. We compared DICs and leverage plots between consistency models and those constructed assuming inconsistency to evaluate inconsistency using the inconsistency model technique. Additionally, we contrasted the posterior mean deviation contributions of each data point in the consistency and inconsistency models. A graphical representation of the network of studies’ descriptions was provided for the results. To display ranking probabilities and relative effects, league tables, forest plots, and surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) plots were created, higher SUCRA values indicate a more effective treatment.

Of all the studies we included, the RCT trials were 15, with a total sample size of 1,176 participants, the non-RCT trials were five, with a total sample size of 180 participants. Ranking probability
Network meta-analysis supported ranking the various interventions. From the results of the Network meta-analysis, a ranking of the effects of each intervention could be calculated. However, we recommend using cumulative probability to rank interventions rather than ranking them according to the best ranking probability, because ranking the best probability did not take into account the uncertainty of relative effect estimation and relative ranking.
SUCRA values is a ranking metric used in network meta-analysis to quantify the relative performance of different treatments. The SUCRA value for a treatment represents the proportion of competing treatments that it outperforms. Specifically, SUCRA values range from 0 to 1, where a value of 1 indicates that the treatment is always the best, and a value of 0 indicates that the treatment is always the worst. The mean SUCRA value is 0.5.
In our study, we calculated SUCRA values to rank the treatments based on their relative performance. A higher SUCRA value indicated a better ranking, meaning that the treatment was likely to be more effective or have a more favourable outcome compared to other treatments in the network. For example, if a treatment had a SUCRA value of 0.8, it suggested that it outperforms 80% of the other treatments in the network.
While SUCRA values provided a useful metric for ranking treatments, they should be interpreted with caution. One important consideration was the certainty of the evidence. SUCRA values could be derived from studies with varying levels of evidence certainty, and chance might explain differences between treatments.

Risk of bias within studies
Overall, two studies (10%) were rated as low risk, eleven (55%) had certain concerns, and seven (35%) were high risk. The most two common reasons for downgrading were “blinding participant” (n = 15) and “blinding outcome assessment” (n = 11). (In the Supplementary File 3). Also, we provided risk of bias ratings for all included studies.
All studies were rated using the Cochrane Handbook https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook. Considering that few studies explicitly blinded participants and staff, the overall risk of bias was unclear or high for most studies, and effect sizes might include expected effects as well as other biases. Considering that most of the interventions were physical, most of the blinding was difficult to implement, as could be seen by the fact that almost all of the studies were blinded to unclear or high certainty ratings were downgraded for all intervention groups because of the high risk of bias in the comparative studies.

Certainty of evidence
Owing to the small total number of participants (< 1 500), high or unclear risk of bias in the majority of trials, and moderated statistical heterogeneity (I² >42%), we downgraded the overall certainty of evidence to “low” or “very low” in CINeMA (Supplementary File 4).
Fewer than 10 trials contributed to any single direct comparison, but credible intervals remained sufficiently narrow to exclude clinically important harm (upper limit < 0.1). Node-splitting analyses revealed no important incoherence (P > 0.10) (Supplementary File 7), supporting consistency between direct and indirect evidence. Consequently, while the magnitude of benefit might be over or underestimated, the presence of a beneficial effect was unlikely to be an artefact of bias or imprecision.

Discussion

Principal findings
The primary purpose of this paper was to compare which interventions were most effective in preventing ACL injury, based on standard outcomes. In this retrospective meta-analysis and systematic review, after integrating the data, the evidence suggested that both Neuromuscular Training and Dynamic Taping interventions might have better outcomes of reducing the risk factors of ACL injuries in athletes. This is likely to be instructive for future coaches in selecting training methods as well as incorporating combinations of the above training methods into their routines. In this study, evidence was observed that Dynamic Taping could be the effective treatment, having a certain significance for the prevention of ACL injury, one of the explanation would be that, considering also that most of our included subjects were younger than 18 years of age, in these groups, it may be easy to develop faulty movement patterns, the fact faulty biomechanical characteristics of the lower extremity are often considered to be the root cause of ACL injuries. Previous studies had shown that ACL damage occurs approximately 40 milliseconds after initial ground contact, Dynamic Taping could therefore be potentially protective by reducing knee valgus on initial contact. Dynamic Taping could enhance proprioception through tactile stimulation, as some studies had shown, skin stimulation was effective in improving positional sense and locomotion. The use of an elastic bandage or tape could stimulate skin receptors and improve proprioception, thus providing additional proprioceptive and kinematic information. This might also be an explanation for why Dynamic Taping could reduce the risk factors of ACL injury. Previous studies had found that inadequate trunk neuromuscular control was associated with an increased risk of knee injury, and that Neuromuscular Training could reduce the risk of ACL injury by improving trunk stability. The results of Neuromuscular Training on landing biomechanics in athletes suggested that training improved knee angle and force control during landing, improved the biomechanical characteristics of lower extremity errors, and reduced the risk of ACL injury. Other studies had explored the relationship between neurocognitive function and non-contact ACL injury, emphasizing the role of Neuromuscular Training in improving neurocognitive function and preventing ACL injury. There is a strong link between cognitive ability, ACL injury risk and Neuromuscular Training, cognitive ability not only influences athletes’ decision-making and reactions during exercise, but also has a significant impact on neuromuscular control and coordination, all of which are strongly associated with ACL injury risk. However Neuromuscular Training was likely to improve movement control in the presence of cognitive decline. Previous meta-analyses and systematic reviews had shown that Neuromuscular Training significantly reduced the incidence of ACL injuries, and this study was consistent with the findings of previous meta-analyses. Meanwhile in this Network meta-analysis, the role of external feedback intervention from the coaches, showed a moderate intervention effect, because the benefits of coach feedback originate from the improved motor system’s ability to self-organize movement, which led to a better movement pattern (i.e., optimal kinetic solution) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7735694/. Therefore, coaches should focus on observing the wrong movement patterns of the athletes during training and correct them in time, and combine the feedback with other training to achieve better injury prevention, which is also more demanding for coaches.
Additionally, the GAA15 Training that was ranked no.1 in the SUCRA rankings, but there was not enough credible evidence to suggest that GAA15 Training could be significantly different from RE. With previous research suggesting that GAA15 Training reduced player injuries to 66%, although GAA15 was considered a Neuromuscular Training, it had effects on ACL injury prevention compared to conventional Neuromuscular Training, which may be due to the fact that most of the subjects included were youth player, and GAA15 was originally designed for adolescent athletes. Although the GAA15 Training intervention ranked first, based on the currently available, predominantly low-certainty evidence, the fact that we included only one literature in this arm and a small sample size might have contributed to the lack of a statistically significant difference in this intervention, more studies need to be included in the future, as well as in future applications, which should be carefully considered.
Furthermore, FIFA11 + has been recognized as an effective means of preventing ACL injury in previous studies, A previous Network meta-analysis showed that the FIFA11 + intervention in soccer significantly reduced the incidence of ACL, this may be due to the fact that the FIFA11 + Training improved biomechanics and stability with exercises that enhance core stability, balance and proper movement patterns. Better biomechanics during movements such as landing and cutting can reduce the likelihood of ACL injuries, however, there is still no statistically significant difference in this study, it is likely that this is due to the fact that there was not a large enough sample size to cause our Network meta-analysis to not produce a statistically significant enough difference in effect sizes with regards to FIFA11+, and also given that FIFA11 + is applied to soccer players, it should be used with caution in the future.

Mechanistic interpretation
The following explains the potential mechanisms underlying three statistically significant interventions that might prove effective.
Neuromuscular Training reduces anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury risk by enhancing lower limb neuromuscular control, optimising jump landing patterns, and strengthening core stability. Its core mechanisms include: 1 Increasing quadriceps-hamstring synergistic contraction to reduce anterior tibial translation shear forces; 2 Improving three-dimensional dynamic alignment of the hip-knee-ankle complex to decrease peak varus/valgus moments at the knee; 3 Reshaping motor cortex-spinal cord excitability through repetitive high-velocity, multi-planar movements to establish ‘safe’ automated movement patterns. Systematic reviews indicate that 12- to 24-week programmes incorporating jump-balance-anti-roll training can reduce ACL injury rates by 40–70% in female athletes. This is consistent with our findings.
Dynamic Taping immediately enhances knee joint proprioceptive input through sustained cutaneous-mechanoreceptor stimulation, thereby downregulating gamma motor neuron excitability, elevating the reflex muscle activation threshold, and reducing abnormal displacement during sudden valgus/rotational forces. Randomised controlled trials revealed a 6–10° reduction in peak knee valgus angle during single-leg stance tests post-taping, alongside a 15–20 ms advance in synchronous activation of the vastus lateralis and biceps femoris muscles. This suggests enhanced central pre-activation strategies, leading to decreased ACL loading. This may offer a plausible explanation for the potential efficacy of this intervention.
Feedback Training (visual-auditory real-time feedback) draws upon motor learning theory to enhance intrinsic error monitoring through exogenous information, thereby prompting athletes to proactively correct high-risk movements. Functional MRI studies reveal that following a six-week jump-landing feedback intervention, activation within the anterior cingulate-basal ganglia circuit diminishes while cerebellum-primary motor cortex coupling intensifies, indicating heightened movement automation; Concurrently, initial contact knee flexion angle increases by 8–12°, vertical ground reaction force decreases by 15%, and tibial anterior shear force reduces by approximately 20%, thereby lowering the probability of ACL overload.

Comparisons with previous studies
ACL injury prevention training has been the subject of numerous studies, but no analysis has yet been conducted to determine which training methods were most successful.
Previous traditional meta-analyses had focused more on whether the incidence of ACL injuries had decreased, or focus on the rehabilitation phase for patients with ACLR injuries. The Network meta-analysis method in this study creatively compared the effects of various interventions while incorporating both direct and indirect evidence. This made it easier to choose intervention strategies that work intuitively. Additionally, no single parameter, like LESS, has been employed in any of the analyses, making it challenging to compare the training effects. Moreover, this study represented the first instance of employing a network meta-analysis to examine the potential risks associated with ACL injuries, with the objective of reducing injury risk rather than lowering injury rates.

Strengths and limitations
The key strength of this study based on our knowledge, it is been the first article that focused on LESS assessment tool for same measuring outcomes, because a harmonized measurement tool would allow for a better evaluation of the usefulness of interventions for athletes at potential risk of injury, Although LESS test is not representative of all assessment tools for reviewing the risk of ACL injuries, LESS has been recognized in recent years as a very important tool for assessing potential ACL injuries. Meanwhile, LESS is a proven approach that makes field assessment easier and allows for direct comparison between studies, LESS is used to identify movement inadequacies and bad landing technique. Using LESS score enables extraction of individual biomechanical risk factors from the score (e.g., knee valgus) and comparison of these partial results between studies. We also incorporated one of advanced methodological research, the Network meta-analysis, to process the data, we used Bayesian model fitting that helps with small sample sizes, which helped us to compare the best interventions for preventing ACL injury. Moreover, we used the latest quality assessment CINeMA, which further helped us to accurately assess the credibility and quality of the results, the application of CINeMA led to a significant number of comparisons being rated as low or very low quality, which in turn limited the generalizability of the results.
Even though the majority of these interventions have never been compared side by side in trials, there were certain limitations that should be noted despite the advantages of the innovative methodology employed in this study, which permits the simultaneous comparison of multiple programs and components within a single analysis, that means, this review was subject to the constraints of the included studies, just like any other research. First, most importantly is that the inclusion of only 20 studies is the biggest limitation. Meanwhile, we lacked direct comparisons between the various interventions, we only had indirect comparisons after comparing them to conventional training, and although we used the most recent methodology of Network and took into account the differences between the consistency and inconsistency models, given our insufficient sample sizes we still believed that this might have resulted in a degree of under-evidence. Furthermore, of the 20 studies we included, we combined RCT studies and non-RCT studies. That might be another limitation that led to low confidence in the results. Although stratified analysing by heterogeneity, we did not conduct a formal sensitivity analysis that excluded high-risk studies. Consequently, if performance or detection bias was systematic, the pooled effect might be overestimated. This limitation, together with the fact that > 60% of the studies were rated as high or unclear risk of bias, downgrading the overall certainty of evidence to “low” in CINeMA; the true effect could therefore be smaller or even null. Because the number of studies was insufficient for meta-regression, we cannot statistically rule out that box height, practice trials, or rater blinding modify the pooled effect. However, the observed consistency in the direction of effect across studies, despite these protocol variations, supports the robustness of our findings, albeit with residual uncertainty. The best intervention in the SCURA rankings, GAA15, had only one study, therefore, it should be used with care.

Clinical and research implications
The interventions included in our review were those that were more common in training practice, as well as simpler and more practical for coaches to train. In order to facilitate the implementation of the training program, the evaluated interventions primarily used materials that were frequently included in the training equipment that is already in place, such as hurdles and boxes, or no additional materials. The expense of the monitors or cameras that were used in certain studies to offer feedback regarding jump landing performance may be a barrier to implementing the intervention in amateur sports. The current analysis showed that, despite the fact that proper coach education lowers the risk of injuries, focused, low-tech training interventions can dramatically lower biomechanical risk factors, avoiding the time and expense barrier associated with ongoing education in amateur sports. However, program adherence is necessary for a training intervention to be successful. When participants demonstrated high adherence, Steffen et al. found that FIFA11 + had a more favourable impact than when players demonstrated low adherence.
Emerging evidence advocates a paradigm shift from isolated injury prevention toward a continuum of prehabilitation–rehabilitation–return-to-sport for ACL injury. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Pamboris et al. 2024 provides critical clinical insight into this continuum. Pooling nine randomised controlled trials (433 participants), the authors demonstrate that open-kinetic-chain (OKC) quadriceps exercises initiated within six weeks post-ACL reconstruction (ACLR) produce superior isokinetic knee-extensor strength at 3–4 months (mean difference = 28.1 Nm; p = 0.03) and clinically relevant reductions in pain (Numeric Pain Rating Scale effect = − 1.5 to − 3.1 points; p < 0.01) without increasing anterior tibial translation when compared with delayed OKC protocols. These data challenge the traditional postponement of OKC training and support its early, load-controlled introduction as a safe and effective component of post-operative care.
To maximise functional translation and mitigate re-injury risk, OKC and closed-kinetic-chain (CKC) protocols should be embedded within a multimodal Neuromuscular framework. Neuromuscular Training—incorporating progressive plyometrics, perturbation and deceleration drills—enhances cortico-spinal excitability and inter-muscular coordination, thereby reducing peak knee-abduction moment and anterior tibial shear during athletic tasks. Dynamic elastomeric taping applied to the anterolateral knee complex augments cutaneous mechanoreceptor feedback, improves joint-position sense error by 1.2–1.8° and immediately decreases frontal-plane excursion during single-leg landing. When real-time visual or auditory feedback is superimposed on OKC/CKC tasks, athletes achieve a 8–12° increase in initial knee flexion and a 15–20% reduction in vertical ground-reaction force, facilitating the re-learning of low-risk movement patterns.
Collectively, the evidence suggests that early OKC training provides a critical window for isolated quadriceps re-conditioning, while CKC, neuromuscular, taping and feedback interventions synergistically restore dynamic joint stability and movement quality. Clinicians are encouraged to sequence OKC within the first post-operative month (90–45° range, low load, high repetition) and progressively integrate CKC and neuromuscular drills once graft healing permits. Future randomised trials should determine whether this combined rehabilitation model accelerates return-to-sport clearance and lowers secondary ACL injury rates across sex, graft type and competitive level.

Future research implications
In view of the aforementioned limitations, we propose the following research agenda to advance the field of ACL injury prevention and rehabilitation. Enhancement of methodological rigour: Well-designed RCT with adequate sample size, concealed allocation and blinded outcome assessors are urgently required to raise the certainty of evidence. Standardisation of assessment tools LESS has shown promise for identifying high-risk movement patterns; however, its predictive validity across sex, age and graft-type subgroups remains inconsistent. Future studies should therefore: integrate LESS with three-dimensional motion analysis to harmonise knee-valgus-angle metrics and facilitate cross-study comparisons. Evaluation of established prevention programmes Despite widespread adoption, the GAA15, FIFA 11 + and similar off-the-shelf protocols have been directly compared in only a handful of investigations. Head-to-head trials—especially those nested within real-world team environments—are needed to clarify: dose–response relationships (frequency, duration, intensity); the additive effect of augmented feedback (visual, auditory or haptic) on movement quality; the minimum effective dose required to maintain biomechanical gains throughout a competitive season.
Comparative effectiveness research: Network meta-analyses should rank the relative efficacy of isolated versus combined interventions (e.g., Neuromuscular Training + external feedback vs. balance + plyometric training). Caution is warranted, however, as the current evidence base contains only a limited number of trials for several promising exercise modalities. Harmonisation of biomechanical outcomes: Heterogeneous instrumentation, marker sets and data-processing algorithms hinder meta-analytic synthesis. A consensus initiative—similar to the Vicon-Plug-in-Gait or ISB recommendations—is required to standardise knee-valgus-angle definitions, LESS scoring criteria and force-platform metrics. Equivalence testing across laboratories would accelerate data pooling and enable individual-participant-data (IPD) meta-analyses with robust bias-adjusted models. By addressing these priorities, future work can move beyond proof-of-concept studies and deliver high-certainty, personalised and context-specific guidelines for ACL injury prevention.

Conclusions
Across 20 controlled trials, Dynamic Taping and Neuromuscular Training emerged as the most consistently effective strategies for improving LESS-defined landing biomechanics and, consequently, for lowering non-contact ACL injury risk. However, the overall certainty of evidence is very low, and the available comparisons were largely indirect. These preliminary findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and used only to generate hypotheses for future, adequately powered and directly comparative randomised trials.
