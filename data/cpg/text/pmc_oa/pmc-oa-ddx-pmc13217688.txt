Sustainability in university restaurants: a scoping review of evaluation focus and strategies
Introduction
Sustainability in food systems has been widely discussed due to the environmental, social and economic impacts of collective meal production. University restaurants play a strategic role due to the large volume of meals served daily, ensuring students’ access to food security and serving as ideal environments for implementing sustainable practices. However, there are still gaps in the understanding of the main strategies adopted and the dimensions of sustainability most frequently assessed in these spaces.
Methods
This study carried out a scoping review, following the guidelines of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and writing according to PRISMA-SCR, with the aim of identifying the main sustainability strategies applied in university restaurants and mapping the stages of the production process most investigated in the scientific literature. The search was conducted in databases such as PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Virtual Health Library, and it included quantitative studies that evaluated aspects of sustainability in these restaurants. The extracted data was analyzed and categorized according to the principles of the 5Rs (Rethink, Reduce, Reuse, Recycle and Refuse).
Results
58 studies were included, 52.54% from South America, with Brazil predominating. Most of the research focused on the post-distribution phase (70.69%), with a focus on waste management and food waste. The sustainability principle most frequently addressed was “Reduce” (91.4%), followed by “Rethink” (50%), while “Reuse” and “Refuse” were little explored. Strategies aimed at changing menus and educational campaigns targeting consumers were the predominant interventions.
Conclusion
Sustainability in university restaurants has been mostly addressed through waste reduction, with limited focus on structural changes and on the broader 5Rs framework. Future studies should integrate environmental strategies with nutritional adequacy, dietary quality, and student health to maximize the impact of sustainable initiatives in these settings.

Introduction
Sustainability in food systems has been a topic of growing global interest, especially in light of the challenges posed by climate change and the increasing demand for food. In this context, the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of the 2030 Agenda provide a crucial guiding framework for assessing and directing sustainability practices within food systems, especially in the context of collective meals. Goals such as zero hunger and food security (SDG 2), responsible consumption and production (SDG 12), climate action (SDG 13), and the protection of water and land resources (SDGs 6 and 15) are particularly relevant, as they highlight the need to integrate environmental, social, and economic concerns throughout the stages from cultivation to meal distribution. Thus, aligning meal production with these goals involves not only reducing environmental impacts—such as emissions, pesticide use, and waste—but also promoting equity in access, nutritional health, and transparent governance in collective food service units.
Over the years, these systems have undergone numerous modifications, from harvesting to meal distribution, reflecting the transition from traditional agricultural systems to more specialized and industrialized models characterized by intensive monoculture and the use of chemical agents. In this context, it is essential to consider the environmental, social, and economic impacts of meal production for collectives, given its significant role within the food system.
The production of meals at institutional food services involves different stages of the production process that can generate significant environmental impacts. From ingredient selection to waste disposal, each phase can influence the sustainability of the food system, whether through natural resource consumption, waste generation, or greenhouse gas emissions. Therefore, rethinking strategies to minimize these impacts within food services Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) is essential to promoting food sustainability.
Among institutional food services, university restaurants stand out as a relevant setting, as they provide balanced meals to a large number of students, staff, and faculty. In addition to meeting the nutritional needs of the academic community, university restaurants have significant potential to contribute to sustainable food systems. Through practices such as planning more sustainable menus, optimizing the use of inputs, reducing food waste, and properly managing waste disposal, these restaurants can serve as reference models in applying sustainability principles in food services.
When addressing sustainability in university restaurants, it is essential to examine how the different stages of the production process influence this context, given the large volume of meals served daily, their role in ensuring students’ food security, and their potential to positively shape young adults’ eating habits. Meal production in these settings involves essential steps, including raw material reception and storage, pre-preparation, preparation, cooking, distribution, and disposal. Efficient planning of these stages can reduce environmental impacts, optimize resources, and minimize waste, making the system more sustainable.
Given this potential, various studies have sought to assess sustainability in university restaurants, addressing criteria such as energy and water consumption, waste management, carbon footprint, and practices that encourage more sustainable food choices. However, there is still no consensus regarding which sustainability dimensions should be prioritized in university restaurants, nor a standardized framework to evaluate them. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to compare findings across studies, identify critical gaps, and guide evidence-based interventions to improve sustainable practices in these settings.
This scoping review aims to identify the main sustainability strategies applied in university restaurants and analyze in which stages of the production process scientific knowledge is most concentrated. The study seeks to understand how these strategies impact waste reduction, resource optimization, and the adoption of sustainable practices in academic food services.

Methods
We conducted a scoping review following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology. We reported it according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-SCR) extension. This study was registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) [https://osf.io/gp8y3/].

Data sources and search strategy
Searches were conducted in ten electronic databases, including MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Scopus, and the Virtual Health Library (BVS). Through BVS, we aggregated local databases by retrieving records from the following sources: (“LILACS” OR “VETINDEX” OR “BDENF” OR “IBECS” OR “WPRIM” OR “INDEXPSI” OR “BBO”). Additionally, local studies were identified through manual screening of reference lists from selected studies and searches in Google Scholar. The last search was conducted on November 24, 2024.
The search strategies were developed by a specialist and refined through discussions with the entire research team. The descriptors were identified in Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), Embase Subject Headings (Emtree), and Health Sciences Descriptors (DeCS) and were adapted for each investigated database. These terms were combined using the boolean operator AND, while synonyms were combined using the boolean operator OR. The full search strategies are provided in Appendix 1.

Research question
The “PCC” acronym (Population, Context, and Concept) was used to formulate the central research question: P (Population): University restaurants; C (Context): Meal production stages (where knowledge is concentrated); C (Concept): Sustainability. Thus, the guiding research question for this scoping review was: “How has sustainability been defined and addressed in research on university restaurants, and what are the main areas of knowledge concentration (defined based on the 5Rs) within this topic?”

Eligibility criteria
We included studies that covered observational and/or interventional quantitative research, including correlational, ecological, cohort, experimental, and cross-sectional studies. The focus was on studies that evaluated sustainability parameters in university restaurants. The exclusion criteria were studies addressing theoretical frameworks, literature reviews (including systematic and scoping reviews), letters, editorials, and qualitative research such as interviews and case studies, as well as mathematical modelling studies and studies that did not focus exclusively on university restaurants.

Study selection and data extraction
The studies were exported in ‘ris’ format into the Rayyan Qatar Computing Research Institute (Rayyan®) application for systematic reviews. To ensure consistency in study evaluation, two reviewers participated in the first screening stage (titles and abstracts) and the second stage (full-text reading). Two independent reviewers analyzed any discrepancies.
A data extraction table was developed and tested in an Excel® 2013 spreadsheet, containing the following variables: reference, study location, meal production stage, sustainability assessment instrument, presence of intervention and type of sustainability intervention, frequency of the word “sustainability” and its synonyms (Food waste; Food loss; Sustainability; Sustainable; Waste; Reduction; Reuse; Recycling) in the text, and sustainability dimensions assessed (rethink, reduce, reuse, recycle, refuse) (Appendix 2).

Results synthesis
A narrative synthesis of the data was conducted, considering the characteristics of the studies. The results of the included studies were summarized according to the meal production stage.

Outcome variable
The definition of sustainability in university restaurants was described based on the conceptualization provided by the authors and applied in the analyzed studies. We examined the frequency and context of sustainability-related terms in the evaluated articles.
For English texts, we searched for the keywords food waste, food loss, sustainable, waste, reduction, reuse, and recycling; in Portuguese, sustentabilidade, sustentável, desperdício, perda, redução, reutilização, and reciclagem; and in Spanish, sostenibilidad, sostenible, desperdicio, pérdida, reducción, reutilización, and reciclaje.
Additionally, we assessed the instruments used to measure sustainability and the interventions implemented to promote sustainable practices in university restaurants, categorizing them according to the 5R principles (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Refuse, Rethink).

Categorization of articles according to the 5Rs
To classify the included studies according to the 5Rs of sustainability (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Refuse, and Rethink), the interventions and practices reported in each article were analyzed. For each category, the number of studies addressing that principle was counted, and the results were expressed as a percentage of the total included publications. This analytical approach also allowed for the identification of how the 5R principles align with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), which promotes the efficient use of resources and waste reduction, and SDG 13 (Climate Action), which emphasizes strategies aimed at mitigating environmental impacts. By relating the 5Rs framework to the SDGs, this classification provides a broader understanding of how the sustainability practices presented in the analyzed studies contribute to global sustainability agendas.

Categorization of articles according to categories overlap
For greater methodological transparency, we explain here the denominators and counting rules used in the different analyses. The reference set used in all analyses corresponds to the total number of studies included in the review (n = 58).
Classification by assessed audience (perspective): Studies were stratified into three mutually exclusive categories: “manager-only perspective” (n = 28), “customer-only perspective” (n = 4), and “both perspectives” (n = 26). The percentages presented for these three categories were calculated based on the total denominator (n = 58). For example, 28/58 = 48.3% refers to studies with an exclusively managerial perspective. This operation produces a complete partition of the study universe (28 + 4 + 26 = 58).
Analysis by production process stage: For the frequencies by stage (e.g., menu planning, purchasing, distribution, post-distribution, etc.), the numerator for each category corresponds to the number of studies that addressed that specific stage (for example, post-distribution: n = 41). Since a single study often evaluated more than one stage of the production process, these categories are not mutually exclusive; meaning the same article could be counted in multiple stages. Therefore, the percentages by stage were also calculated in relation to the total denominator (n = 58) and represent the proportion of studies that addressed each stage (for example, 41/58 = 70.7% of the studies evaluated post-distribution). In none of these calculations is the denominator the “manager-only” subset (n = 28); when we report “post-distribution (n = 41; 70.7%)”, we mean 41 studies out of the total 58, and not 70.7% of the subgroup of studies with only a managerial perspective.
Analysis of interventions: When percentages are reported relative only to the studies that implemented interventions, the denominator is the number of interventional studies (n = 32; 55% of the total). Thus, for example, “consumer-targeted interventions (n = 20; 62.5%)” indicates 20/32 = 62.5% of the interventional studies.
Classification according to the 5Rs (Reduce, Rethink, Reuse, Recycle, Refuse): Both presentation formats were maintained: (a) the description of the combinations of principles (e.g., “rethinking + reducing: n = 23; 39.7%”) expresses the proportion in relation to the total number of studies (n = 58); and (b) the aggregated frequency of mentions of each “R” regardless of combinations (e.g., “Reduce: n = 53; 91.4%”) also uses the total denominator (n = 58). It is also important to clarify here that a single study could be classified under more than one “R” (overlapping categories), which is why the sum of the frequencies per R exceeds 100%.

Analysis of sustainability-related keywords
To determine the distribution of citations using sustainability-related keywords, we first calculated the total number of occurrences of relevant terms across all articles (total N). Using this total, we applied a proportional allocation (“rule of three”) to determine the percentage of keywords appearing in different sections of the articles (e.g., abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion). This method allowed us to visualize where authors most frequently emphasize sustainability concepts within their manuscripts.

Our comprehensive search of the databases resulted in 888 studies. After removing duplicates (N = 276), 612 titles and abstracts were evaluated. The initial textual analysis covered 60 studies, of which 24 were excluded according to the eligibility criteria previously described (Appendix 2). All studies were published between 2008 and 2024 (Table 1). Of the total studies evaluated, 55.2% (n = 32) were published in the last five years, ranging from 2020 to 2024, and were written in English (n = 33), Portuguese (n = 24), or Spanish (n = 1). Among the included studies, 52.5% (n = 31) were published in South America, 23.7% (n = 14) in North America, 1.7% (n = 1) in Central America, 16.9% (n = 10) in Europe, and 3.4% (n = 2) in Asia. Regarding the evaluated countries, 50.8% (n = 30) of the studies were conducted in Brazil, 23.7% (n = 14) in the United States, 5.1% (n = 3) in the United Kingdom, and 3.4% (n = 2) in Sweden. Additionally, 15.2% of the articles came from other countries, such as the Netherlands, Greece, the Philippines, Turkey, Honduras, Uruguay, France, China, and Germany, each representing 1.7% (n = 1).
Regarding the assessed audience, 28 (48.3%) studies evaluated concepts from the manager’s perspective, 4 (6,9%) studies from the client’s perspective, and 26 (44.8%) included both perspectives. Among the stages of the meal production process, the most frequently analyzed stages from the manager’s perspective were menu planning (n = 18; 31.0%), purchasing process [e.g., family farming] (n = 7; 12.1%), transportation (n = 0; 0%), receiving (n = 1; 1.7%), storage (n = 1; 1.7%), pre-preparation (n = 3; 5.2%), preparation (n = 1; 1.7%), distribution [meal in trays, portioning] (n = 10; 17.2%), and post-distribution [waste management, plate waste, leftovers] (n = 41; 70.7%). Furthermore, the 30 (51.7%) studies focused on the customer analyzed menu/meal perception (acceptability) and/or awareness campaigns regarding sustainability in some aspects (sometimes indirectly), such as food waste.
Of the 32 studies (55% of the total) that used interventions to assess sustainability-related aspects, most focused on consumers (n = 20; 62.5%), including students and general customers, exploring aspects from menu reconfiguration and the application of nudges to the introduction of environmental labels to encourage more sustainable food choices. Additionally, some interventions targeted the environment (n = 6; 18.7%), concentrating on food waste management through the implementation of systems such as biodigesters and composting. Moreover, two studies addressed the service sector (n = 2; 6.2%), focusing on changes in the availability and organization of meals for consumers. The first intervention was the implementation of a trayless dining system, altering consumption dynamics by removing trays used by customers to carry food. Another study analyzed tray removal in a university restaurant as a strategy to reduce food waste. Other studies addressed actions related to employees (n = 2; 6.25%), focusing on training for sustainable practices. Finally, one study (n = 1; 3.13%) addressed both service and consumers, while another study (n = 1; 3.13%) integrated environment and consumers.
Furthermore, when evaluating the instruments used to measure sustainability and the types of interventions adopted to promote sustainable practices in university restaurants, we conducted a categorization according to the Rs of sustainability. This categorization also enabled us to relate the identified practices to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), which encourages the efficient use of resources and waste reduction, reinforcing the alignment of sustainable actions in university restaurants with the global sustainability agenda. We observed that most studies (39.7%; n = 23) addressed rethinking and reducing together, followed by those that exclusively addressed reducing (32.8%; n = 19). Rethinking was explored in isolation in 5.2% (n = 3) of the articles, while 8.6% (n = 5) analyzed the relationship between recycling and reducing. Furthermore, 3.4% (n = 2) discussed reducing and reusing, and the same proportion (3.4%; n = 2) focused exclusively on recycling. Additionally, 1.7% (n = 1) of studies analyzed the combination of reducing, reusing, and recycling, 1.7% (n = 1) addressed rethinking, reducing, and recycling, 1.7% (n = 1) explored rethinking, reducing, reusing, and recycling, and finally, 1.7% (n = 1) studied the relationship between rethinking, reducing, and reusing.
Overall, the analysis of the frequency with which each of the 5Rs was addressed, independent of combinations with other principles, is summarized in Fig. 2. It reveals that reducing was the most prominent principle, featured in 91.4% (n = 53) of the articles, followed by rethinking, present in 50% (n = 29) of the studies. Recycling was mentioned in 17.2% (n = 10) of the publications, while reusing was addressed in 8.6% (n = 5). Notably, the principle of refusing was not identified in any of the analyzed studies. These findings underscore that impact reduction has been the primary focus of sustainability initiatives within university restaurants, whereas strategies centered on reusing and, especially, refusing materials remain significantly underexplored in the current literature.
To understand the conceptualization and application of sustainability in studies on university restaurants, we analyzed the frequency and context in which sustainability-related terms were mentioned in the selected articles (Table 2). The distribution of sustainability-related terms revealed specific patterns across different sections of scientific texts. It was observed that 22% (n = 1031) of occurrences were concentrated in the introduction, suggesting an initial emphasis on contextualizing the topic. In the objectives, the terms appeared in 1.1% (n = 53), while in the methods, they accounted for 8.3% (n = 393), reflecting their relevance in the methodological approach of the studies. In the results section, they represented 6.3% (n = 301) of mentions, and in the discussion section alone, 11% (n = 519). When results and discussion were combined, the frequency was 9.8% (n = 447), while the discussion associated with the conclusion accounted for only 0.2% (n = 9). In the conclusion, the terms were found in 4.9% (n = 231) of cases, while in the abstract, the presence was 6.2% (n = 289). Keywords, which help in indexing studies, concentrated 1.3% (n = 62) of occurrences, while titles presented an incidence of 0.8% (n = 38). Additionally, 20.2% (n = 944) of mentions occurred in references, indicating strong theoretical support, and 7.7% (n = 377) were associated with notes and figures, highlighting the visual and explanatory support used to represent sustainability concepts in the analyzed studies.

Discussion

Growing scientific interest
The results of this review demonstrate that sustainability in university restaurants has been increasingly addressed in the scientific literature, as 55.2% (n = 32) of the publications were conducted in the last five years. This trend aligns with the growing global concern regarding sustainability and food systems.

Geographical distribution
Another important point is the concentration of research in South American countries (52.5%; n = 31), highlighting a regional concern with implementing sustainable practices in university settings. This is particularly relevant given the social assistance context and public policies predominant in Brazil.

Focus on meal production stages
The analysis of meal production process stages in university restaurants indicates a particular focus on the post-distribution phase, where 70.7% of studies concentrated on waste, leftover, and food residue management. This emphasis underscores the need to reduce food waste, a critical issue in terms of sustainability and operational efficiency. Menu planning also stood out, with 31% of studies evaluating this stage, highlighting the importance of considering social, economic, environmental, and nutritional aspects when designing menus.

Consumer and staff interventions
Consumer-targeted interventions were predominant, with 62.5% (n = 20) of studies focusing on strategies such as menu reconfiguration, the application of “nudges,” and the inclusion of environmental labels to encourage more sustainable food choices. Additionally, interventions targeting the environment (18.7%; n = 6) and staff (6.2%; n = 2) were identified, highlighting efforts to improve waste management and train teams in sustainable practices.

Focus on the 5Rs framework
When evaluating the instruments and interventions adopted, the studies were categorized according to the 5Rs of sustainability. The results show that most publications focus on reducing environmental impact, emphasizing the principle of ‘Reduce.’ However, practices such as ‘Reuse’ and ‘Refuse’ received little attention in the literature. The low frequency of studies investigating material reuse suggests that this strategy is still underexplored in university restaurants, despite its potential to minimize waste and promote a more sustainable cycle.
The predominance of “Reduce” suggests that sustainability actions in university dining services are still focused on operational efficiency, especially in reducing waste and the rational use of resources.
However, this focus may indicate a narrow interpretation of sustainability, limited to mitigating environmental impacts, without addressing the broader transformation of the food system. Recent studies show that in university settings, the predominance of reduction strategies without integration into institutional policies or educational practices limits the potential for structural and behavioral change.
When “Reduce” dominates and practices such as “Reuse” and “Refuse” remain underdeveloped, there is a risk of neglecting more upstream solutions such as redesigning menus, procurement policies, and educational strategies that could prevent waste generation at its source. This limitation reflects a pattern also observed in other food service settings, where the lack of integration between sustainability, circular economy, and nutritional health undermines broader outcomes.

Unexplored practices
Moreover, the absence of publications addressing ‘Refuse’ indicates that practices aimed at eliminating the use of non-sustainable materials, such as disposable plastics and unnecessary packaging, have not yet been widely discussed in the scientific literature. This gap points to the need for studies that investigate broader transformation strategies in university restaurant production processes. Future research could explore integrated approaches that consider all the Rs of sustainability, offering a more comprehensive perspective on sustainable practices in university restaurants [78; 79].

Textual analysis
The textual analysis of the studies allowed for the identification of the distribution of sustainability-related terms across different sections of the articles. The results reveal that 22% of occurrences are concentrated in the introduction, 1.1% in the objectives, 8.3% in the methods, 6.3% in the results, 11% in the discussion, 9.8% when results and discussion are combined, 0.2% in the discussion associated with the conclusion, 4.9% in the conclusion, 6.2% in the abstract, 1.3% in the keywords, 0.8% in the title, 20.2% in the references, and 7.7% in notes and figures. These findings suggest that authors frequently contextualize the topic by grounding it in previous literature, reinforcing the theoretical importance and documentary basis for sustainable approaches [27,,29, 30, ].

Implications for practice
Sustainability in university restaurants should not be limited to the environmental dimension. The way food is selected, prepared, and served has direct implications for dietary patterns and cultural habits, and also represents an opportunity to cultivate sustainable values among students. Integrating environmental management with nutrition and education can strengthen both health promotion and sustainable development.The incorporation of educational components such as awareness campaigns, participatory menus, or transparent communication about the sustainable origin of food, can transform the university restaurants into a space for learning and engagement, aligned with universities as agents of social change and innovation. The existing gap regarding the definition of sustainability and its application in university restaurants highlights the importance of studies with multifaceted interventions. These interventions should encompass aspects ranging from menu planning to waste management and consumer education, integrating the entire meal production chain in this setting.

Limitations of this review
This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, some uniterms were unindexed in descriptor databases (MeSH, DeCS, and Emtree), which may have limited the comprehensiveness of the literature search. Additionally, the inclusion of studies from the gray literature, while providing valuable insights, introduces variability in methodological rigor.
Language restrictions also represent a potential source of bias, as studies published in languages other than English, Portuguese, or Spanish may have been excluded, potentially omitting relevant evidence from other regions. Furthermore, the geographical concentration of included studies in South America (particularly Brazil) limits the generalizability of the findings to university restaurants in other contexts, where policy frameworks, cultural practices, and resource availability may differ. The predominance of quantitative studies, especially those focusing on food waste and post-distribution practices, may have led to an underrepresentation of qualitative perspectives, such as stakeholders’ perceptions and institutional decision-making processes. This imbalance may limit understanding of the broader socio-cultural and organizational factors that influence sustainability practices. Despite these limitations, the diversity of sources analyzed provided a broad and contextualized perspective on sustainability in university restaurants. The review highlights underexplored areas and offers a foundation for future research, particularly in regions and aspects of sustainability that remain poorly investigated.

Reccomendations for future research
Future research should consider integrated approaches that encompass all 5Rs of sustainability, as well as institutional policies that promote the implementation of these practices in university restaurants. Importantly, these strategies should also be examined in terms of their intersections with nutritional adequacy, dietary quality, and student health, ensuring that sustainability initiatives not only reduce environmental impacts but also strengthen the nutritional role of university restaurants in supporting healthier eating habits and overall well-being.

Conclusion
The findings of this scoping review demonstrate that sustainability in university restaurants has been explored in the scientific literature, with a significant concentration of studies focusing on the post-distribution phase, particularly on waste management and food waste reduction. Most of the analyzed interventions emphasize strategies to minimize environmental impact, primarily aligning with the principle of “Reduce,” while approaches related to “Reuse” and “Refuse” remain underexplored.
Additionally, the majority of research focuses on consumer-targeted actions, such as menu modifications and awareness campaigns, whereas structural and institutional interventions in food services are less frequent. This scenario highlights the need for studies that investigate sustainability more comprehensively, incorporating changes in production processes, resource management, and the adoption of practices that extend beyond waste reduction.
