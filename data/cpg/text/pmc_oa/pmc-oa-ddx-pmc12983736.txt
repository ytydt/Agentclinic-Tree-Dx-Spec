2016 sJIA-MAS criteria and a step-up therapeutic approach in KD-MAS: insights from a combined cohort and literature review
Abstract
Objectives
To describe diagnostic framework classification, treatment patterns and outcomes in Kawasaki disease-associated macrophage activation syndrome (KD-MAS) using a single-centre cohort and a structured descriptive synthesis of published cases to inform hypothesis generation and future refinement of diagnostic and management strategies for KD-MAS.
Methods
We performed a retrospective single-centre cohort study. MAS was classified by haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)-2004, HLH-2009 or 2016 systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA)-MAS criteria. Data were abstracted around a prespecified MAS window; severity was indexed by haemophagocytic syndrome diagnostic score (HScore) and the association between HScore and treatment escalation was assessed using Firth’s logistic regression. In parallel, we conducted a literature review.
Results
In our centre, incidence was 0.6% (22/3786); mean age was 3.72 years; coronary involvement was 77.2%. The proportions of clinician-diagnosed KD-MAS cases fulfilling each framework were HLH-2004 14/22, HLH-2009 18/22 and 2016 sJIA-MAS 20/22; 11 met all three. Cytopenias, liver dysfunction and coagulopathy were frequent; management followed stepwise escalation from intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) or corticosteroid monotherapy to IVIG plus corticosteroids and, when required, to adjunct immunosuppressive/biologic therapy; similar patterns appeared in the literature. HScore was higher with intensified therapy than with standard therapy (median 274 vs 199; p=0.020). Each 10-point HScore increase was associated with higher odds of escalation (OR 1.44 univariable; 1.37 adjusted).
Conclusion
The 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria demonstrate the highest proportion fulfilling criteria in our cohort for KD-MAS. Treatment in our cohort and in published cases generally reflected a stepwise, typically progressing from IVIG±glucocorticoids to adjunct immunosuppressants/biologics. Higher HScores co-occurred with escalation, suggesting they capture baseline severity; prospective multicentre studies are needed to test incremental value and risk-stratification thresholds.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
Kawasaki disease-associated macrophage activation syndrome (KD-MAS) lacks dedicated diagnostic and treatment guidelines, which contributes to diagnostic uncertainty and may increase the risk of misclassification and potential overtreatment.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
In a 2015–2025 single-centre cohort (22/3786 KD admissions), the 2016 systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA)-MAS criteria captured more clinician-diagnosed KD-MAS cases than haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-2004/2009 and relied on routine tests.
Across our cohort and published cases, treatment commonly followed a step-up pattern from intravenous immunoglobulin±glucocorticoids to adjunct immunosuppressive/biologic therapy, and higher baseline HScore was associated with escalation.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
The study supports using the 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria as a pragmatic early screening/triage framework for suspected KD-MAS and describes a real-world stepwise diagnostic and treatment approach for KD-MAS.
It also motivates prospective multicentre studies to validate haemophagocytic syndrome diagnostic score (HScore)-based risk stratification and to standardise escalation pathways.

Introduction
Kawasaki disease (KD) is an acute systemic vasculitis of unknown aetiology that predominantly affects paediatric populations. The condition is driven by cytokine-mediated inflammation, primarily targeting medium-sized and small-sized arteries. This dysregulated immune response induces both vascular damage and predisposes patients to macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome that is generally regarded as a rheumatic disease-associated form of secondary haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (sHLH) that typically develops in the context of rheumatic or autoimmune diseases. This syndrome results from uncontrolled proliferation and activation of macrophages and cytotoxic T cells, culminating in a cytokine storm that manifests clinically with persistent fever, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, coagulopathy and markedly elevated ferritin levels. While MAS occurs most frequently in systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA), affecting approximately 10% of patients, its association with Kawasaki disease (KD-MAS) is considerably rarer.
Current clinical management of KD-MAS relies heavily on diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines developed for sJIA-MAS, despite significant pathophysiological differences between these conditions. This management paradigm, combined with the challenges of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)-resistant KD, creates substantial management difficulties. Furthermore, KD-MAS presents unique clinical challenges, including frequent underdiagnosis and problematic overtreatment. To address these issues, we conducted a retrospective analysis of single-centre KD-MAS cases and complemented it with a structured, descriptive synthesis of published cases. This combined approach leverages granular patient-level data while providing broader contextualisation from the literature. We aimed to: (1) compare the overlap and classification coverage of commonly used diagnostic frameworks (HLH-2004, HLH-2009, and the 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria), (2) describe and summarise real-world treatment strategies for KD-MAS and (3) explore whether haemophagocytic syndrome diagnostic score (HScore) is associated with treatment escalation intensity in an exploratory, hypothesis-generating manner. Our findings are intended to be descriptive and hypothesis-generating, helping to contextualise diagnostic classification and real-world treatment escalation in KD-MAS and to inform the design of future prospective, multicentre studies.

Methods

Patients
This retrospective cohort included patients with KD treated at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University from January 2015 to March 2025. We then identified KD-MAS cases by querying International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision diagnosis codes in the electronic medical record. KD-MAS was diagnosed by the treating clinicians, typically in the context of clinical worsening and hyperinflammatory features.
All enrolled patients met the 2017 American Heart Association (AHA) diagnostic criteria, including classic, incomplete and atypical KD as defined by the AHA and MAS diagnosis required meeting at least one of the following criteria: HLH-2004, HLH-2009 or the 2016 sJIA-MAS classification criteria. We excluded: (i) multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C), defined by established criteria plus evidence of current or recent SARS-CoV-2 infection (positive RT-PCR/antigen during admission or within the preceding 2–8 weeks). During 2020–2023, febrile admissions underwent routine pre-admission RT-PCR testing and repeat in-hospital testing; any SARS-CoV-2-positive case was classified as MIS-C and excluded. Additional exclusions included: (ii) primary HLH, malignancy-associated HLH and other non-KD hyperinflammatory syndromes and (iii) insufficient data to ascertain MAS frameworks, defined as absence of ferritin and fewer than two additional core MAS variables within the prespecified MAS window. Demographic, laboratory, treatment and outcome data were abstracted from the electronic medical record. For this study, raw clinical and laboratory values surrounding the MAS window were extracted, with the index timepoint defined as the earlier of (i) the first documented hyperferritinaemia or (ii) initiation of treatment escalation. Disease severity was quantified using the HScore. If a required HScore component was not available within the prespecified window, the HScore was not computed for that patient. For each HScore component, we extracted laboratory values within a prespecified MAS assessment window centred on the index timepoint (±72 hours). Where feasible, values obtained prior to the first dose of escalation therapy were preferentially used; otherwise, the closest value within the window was used. Methylprednisolone was the acute-phase glucocorticoid, whereas dexamethasone was used only within HLH-directed regimens and was analysed separately. Escalation was defined as adding ≥1 immunosuppressive/biologic agent beyond IVIG plus corticosteroids.

Literature review
We conducted a review across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library (January 2000–19 June 2025) using combinations of terms for Kawasaki disease and macrophage activation/haemophagocytic syndromes (online supplemental table 1). Although the search and selection process followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) principles, this literature component was intended as a narrative review rather than a full systematic review with pooled estimates.
Inclusion criteria: (i) patients aged <18 years with AHA-defined KD; (ii) concomitant MAS diagnosed by HLH-2004, HLH-2009, 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria or explicit clinical diagnosis; (iii) reporting ≥1 key domain (diagnostic standard, major clinical/laboratory features, treatment/outcome).
Exclusion criteria: studies published before 2000 were excluded; reviews, editorials, correspondence, abstracts without primary data or studies with indeterminate diagnoses. Disagreements at either stage were resolved by discussion.
Records retrieved from all sources were first de-duplicated in EndNote. Two reviewers then independently screened titles/abstracts against the prespecified eligibility criteria, followed by independent full-text assessment of potentially eligible reports. No machine-learning prioritisation was used, and automation was limited to duplicate detection and on-platform filters/tags. Reasons for full-text exclusion were recorded a priori and are reported in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram. When multiple reports referred to the same study, they were collated and treated as a single study.
Reporting bias assessment: case reports/series were appraised using the Institute of Health Economics (IHE) 8-item tool. Observational studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort and case-control designs and summarised descriptively.
We present literature-derived counts and proportions descriptively based on study-reported data. All denominators reflect cases with available data for each variable and therefore vary across outcomes and parameters. Given substantial heterogeneity and reporting/publication bias, these summaries should not be interpreted as pooled estimates and no meta-analytic pooling was performed. Where multiple publications appeared to refer to overlapping patients, we collated them to avoid double counting of narratives. Findings should be interpreted with caution owing to heterogeneity, incomplete laboratory windows and publication/reporting biases.

Statistical analysis
In our cohort, interframework concordance was visualised using Venn diagrams. HScores were compared across escalating treatment intensities with the Wilcoxon test and outcomes were tabulated by therapeutic tier. To explore the association between HScore and treatment selection based on our centre’s cases, we employed Firth’s penalised likelihood logistic regression to mitigate bias arising from small sample size and potential complete or quasi-complete separation. HScore was modelled as a continuous variable and scaled per 10-point increment, so that reported regression coefficients correspond to the OR for each 10-point increase in HScore.
Descriptive statistics were calculated using Microsoft Excel (mean±SD or median (IQR) for continuous data; n (%) for categorical data). All analyses were conducted in R software, V.4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 95% CIs for proportions were calculated using the Wilson score method. Between-group differences were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous data, as appropriate; p<0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Literature review
The study selection process is summarised in online supplemental figure 1. A total of 1729 records were identified from databases. After removing 1056 duplicates, 673 records underwent title/abstract screening, of which 573 were excluded. We sought to retrieve 100 reports for full-text assessment; two reports could not be retrieved. Consequently, 98 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility, and exclusions were due to ineligible publication type (n=29), non-relevant study population (n=11), insufficient data (n=7) or publication outside the prespecified eligibility period (n=3). Ultimately, 48 studies were included in the review.
The quality of the evidence base was heterogeneous and largely limited by reporting and design constraints. For the included case reports/case series, exposure/outcome ascertainment and general reporting were usually adequate, supporting basic clinical description; however, case definition/selection was frequently unclear, alternative diagnoses were often only partially excluded and dose-response elements were rarely assessable, with follow-up commonly insufficiently documented (online supplemental table 3). The observational studies were of moderate methodological quality by NOS, but their interpretability was primarily constrained by limited control for confounding and uncertain follow-up adequacy (online supplemental tables 4 and 5). Accordingly, literature-derived findings should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and they are subject to substantial selection and reporting bias.

Immune function markers were available in smaller subsets: decreased NK-cell activity was noted in 3/5 (60.0%) and elevated sCD25 in 4/5 (80.0%). Treatment and outcomes
In our centre’s 22-patient KD-MAS cohort (table 4), we observed a stepwise therapeutic escalation: from IVIG or corticosteroid monotherapy to IVIG plus corticosteroids, and further to regimens incorporating immunosuppressive or biologic agents. In our cohort, IVIG alone was used in one patient with a favourable outcome (1/1) and no deaths (HScore 178; n=1). Among patients receiving IVIG plus glucocorticoids, outcomes differed by dose: high dose (>10 mg/kg/day) was given to nine patients with 7/9 (77.8%) favourable outcomes, one death and one treatment abandonment; lower dose (≤10 mg/kg/day) was given to six patients with 6/6 (100%) favourable outcomes and no deaths or abandonment. The mean HScore in the IVIG+glucocorticoid category was 199 (n=14; HScore calculation excluded the abandonment case). Escalation to IVIG+glucocorticoids with additional immunosuppressive/biologic therapy yielded favourable outcomes in all treated patients: one agent in 4/4 (100%) and ≥2 agents in 1/1 (100%); the mean HScore in this intensified group was 274 (n=6), consistent with higher inflammatory burden at baseline. One patient treated with HLH-based elements did not achieve a favourable outcome (0/1) and died.

In our centre’s cohort, two transferred patients initially received pulse corticosteroids at outside hospitals with transient improvement but later deteriorated and died during rapid clinical worsening, and escalation beyond corticosteroids management was not achieved prior to death. Collectively, the observed stepwise treatment pattern and the adverse outcomes associated with delayed escalation prompted us to investigate whether an objective severity index might relate to treatment intensity.
These observations motivated an exploratory analysis to determine whether HScore is associated with treatment intensity. So we explored HScore in a subset of 19 patients for whom it was calculated, comparing those who underwent intensified therapy (addition of immunosuppressive or biologic agents) with those who received standard therapy (IVIG with corticosteroids). HScore was higher in the intensified group than in the standard-therapy group. Compared with the steroid regimen, patients receiving intensified therapy had higher HScores (median 274 (IQR 233.5–300.5) vs 199 (162–223)); Wilcoxon rank-sum (W=12, p=0.020). Patients receiving intensified therapy had higher HScores than those on standard regimens (figure 2).

Statistical analysis using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test demonstrated a significant difference between the two groups (p=0.020), suggesting a potential association between higher disease severity and the need for escalated immunomodulatory treatment.
To further examine this relationship, after excluding the single IVIG-monotherapy remission case, we used Firth’s penalised logistic regression to examine factors associated with treatment escalation (addition of ≥1 immunosuppressive/biologic agent±plasma exchange). In the univariable model with HScore as a continuous predictor, each 10-point increase in HScore was associated with higher odds of escalation (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.45; p=0.003). In the multivariable model entering HScore and time-to-treatment simultaneously, the association for HScore persisted (adjusted OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.17; p=0.002), whereas time-to-treatment showed a directionally positive but imprecise and non-significant association (table 6). These exploratory findings suggest that higher HScores, consistent with greater inflammatory severity, were associated with clinician-driven therapy intensification, although estimates are limited by small sample size, few events and potential confounding by indication.

Discussion
KD-MAS is a rare but potentially life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome emerging during acute KD, yet it can be challenging to distinguish from IVIG-refractory KD. The reported frequency of KD-MAS (including in our cohort) is markedly lower than the incidence of MAS reported in other paediatric rheumatic diseases, suggesting that under-recognition and diagnostic misclassification may contribute to an underestimation of the true burden of KD-MAS. Notably, some patients with a relatively mild KD-MAS phenotype may achieve clinical remission with standard IVIG therapy alone, as described in earlier reports. In addition, corticosteroids administered for presumed IVIG-refractory KD may inadvertently suppress an unrecognised coexisting KD-MAS, further obscuring its true incidence. Other KD-mimicking hyperinflammatory states further add to this diagnostic complexity. For example, MIS-C may present with KD-like features such as persistent fever, rash, hepatic dysfunction and coagulopathy and is more frequently accompanied by severe organ involvement, creating substantial overlap with MAS and severe KD in both clinical phenotype and illness severity. MIS-C frequently necessitates escalation beyond IVIG, including systemic glucocorticoids and/or biologic agents. Because similar escalation decisions are also common in severe KD-MAS, therapeutic convergence in critically ill children may contribute to diagnostic uncertainty and misclassification unless aetiological and syndrome-specific features are systematically assessed. Finally, limited clinical vigilance and the absence of a diagnostic gold standard increase the risk of delayed recognition or misdiagnosis, which is clinically consequential because earlier diagnosis and prompt immunomodulatory treatment may improve outcomes, whereas delayed recognition may permit rapid progression to organ dysfunction.
Given the potential for misdiagnosis and underestimation of KD-MAS incidence, we next described framework-specific classification coverage. It is important to note that clinician diagnosis is not fully independent of the evaluated frameworks, because clinical judgement may be informed by these criteria in routine practice. Moreover, differences in fulfilment proportions are influenced by test availability and timing, rather than necessarily reflecting biological sensitivity. Classification coverage differed substantially among the evaluated criteria. Although all three frameworks (HLH-2004, HLH-2009 and 2016 sJIA-MAS) share core features such as fever and hyperferritinaemia, they differ markedly in structural components and data requirements. The routine laboratory thresholds in the 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria were developed and validated in paediatric populations; therefore, from the perspective of baseline patient characteristics, they may be more suitable than HLH-based frameworks for identifying KD-MAS. Consistent with this, the 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria showed the highest fulfilment proportion and more consistent assessability, likely due to its reliance on routinely available laboratory parameters rather than specialised immunological tests. In contrast, the HLH-based criteria depend heavily on advanced immunological tests—such as natural killer (NK) cell activity and soluble CD25 (sCD25)—which are technically demanding, costly and not routinely accessible in many clinical settings. Among patients with KD-MAS, these parameters demonstrated only moderate positivity rates, with decreased NK-cell activity present and elevated sCD25 of tested cases, further limiting their diagnostic utility. Given both the high resource burden and suboptimal detection rates (online supplemental table 8), such immunological markers are ill-suited for routine screening in KD-MAS. These findings suggest that the 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria may be more feasible to operationalise in real-world settings because they rely on routinely available laboratory measures. Accordingly, the higher fulfilment of the 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria should be interpreted as reflecting greater real-world feasibility and assessability, rather than inherently better case identification. Because MAS can progress rapidly, in which delayed recognition may lead to rapid clinical deterioration, a readily applicable framework using routine tests may support earlier clinical suspicion and timely treatment escalation, potentially mitigating disease progression. In this context, the 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria may serve as a pragmatic early screening/triage tool for suspected KD-MAS in routine practice, while definitive diagnosis should remain clinician-adjudicated and, where feasible, complemented by further evaluation.
Our cohort’s coronary artery involvement was markedly higher than that reported in other studies (online supplemental table 9). However, direct cross-study comparisons are inherently challenging, because the KD-MAS literature is largely composed of small case reports/series and small single-centre cohorts, and coronary outcomes have been reported using non-uniform definitions, which limits comparability. Moreover, our observed prevalence may in part reflect referral and severity bias: as a tertiary referral centre, our KD-MAS case mix is enriched for clinically complex, deteriorating or treatment-refractory patients transferred from outside hospitals, which may over-represent severe phenotypes at higher coronary risk.
The treatment of KD-MAS remains particularly challenging due to its rarity and the lack of disease-specific management guidelines, leading to frequent extrapolation from treatment strategies established in other forms of MAS. While high-dose glucocorticoids have traditionally been emphasised as first-line therapy in rheumatic MAS, our analysis of KD-MAS cases (tables3  4) revealed a stepwise therapeutic pattern. Specifically, patients received treatments ranging from IVIG or glucocorticoids alone to a combination of IVIG and glucocorticoids, and further to regimens incorporating additional immunosuppressive or biologic agents. Favourable outcomes were observed across each tier of this treatment spectrum, including in cases managed with IVIG alone or IVIG plus corticosteroids. Patients receiving IVIG and corticosteroids also demonstrated high response rates, while those requiring immunosuppressive or biologic agents achieved comparable or improved outcomes, particularly when multiple immunosuppressive or biologic agents were combined. In contrast, HLH-1994/2004-directed protocols were associated with the lowest response rate and accounted for the majority of deaths, suggesting that aggressive cytotoxic regimens may not confer additional benefit in the KD-MAS population and may carry greater treatment-related risk. Because treatment intensity was chosen based on clinical judgement and overall perceived severity, these findings describe a clinician-driven escalation pattern in KD-MAS, in which treatment intensity appeared to track overall disease severity rather than a prespecified protocol, suggesting a severity-driven stepwise management approach in practice. It is noteworthy that, unlike severe autoinflammatory diseases such as sJIA or systemic lupus erythematosus, KD is a self-limiting systemic inflammatory condition routinely managed early with IVIG, mitigating inflammation promptly. According to the MAS threshold pathogenic model, KD’s inherently mild inflammatory milieu, combined with early treatment intervention, likely explains the less aggressive therapeutic strategies required for KD-MAS compared with other MAS subtypes.
In the exploratory analysis, we assessed whether HScore was associated with clinician-driven treatment intensification. Using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, we found that patients who received intensified therapy had significantly higher HScores than those treated with standard regimens. Consistently, a Firth’s bias-reduced logistic model indicated that each 10-point increase in HScore was associated with greater odds of escalation. It is worth emphasising that treatment escalation is a clinician-driven decision rather than an objective outcome and may be influenced by confounding by indication and centre-specific practice patterns; therefore, this association should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and consistent with HScore capturing baseline severity rather than implying causality. Collectively, these findings suggest that HScore may be a useful standardised severity marker to support clinician-driven escalation decisions, but require validation in prospective, adequately powered multicentre studies with prespecified escalation criteria.

Limitations
This study has several limitations relevant to interpretation. First, the KD-MAS sample was small (n=22), which limits statistical power and precision, constrains multivariable adjustment and increases the risk of imprecise estimates. Second, this was a single-centre, retrospective cohort from a tertiary referral paediatric hospital, which may introduce selection and misclassification biases. Accordingly, the case mix may over-represent clinically complex or treatment-refractory patients, and the findings may not fully generalise to general paediatric settings. Third, data completeness and selection/reporting biases are inherent to retrospectively assembled cohorts and literature-derived cases; a proportion of published cases were available only at the aggregate level, limiting patient-level analyses and uniform application of diagnostic criteria or outcome definitions. Fourth, missingness may have biased the observed fulfilment proportions across frameworks, potentially favouring criteria that rely on routinely available tests; moreover, the lack of an independent reference standard for KD-MAS precludes formal assessment of diagnostic accuracy, so these proportions should be interpreted as descriptive classification coverage; genetic testing was not performed, so we cannot exclude an underlying genetic predisposition/primary HLH in a minority of cases. Fifth, the retrospective design introduces confounding by indication: treatment decisions were clinician-driven based on the totality of clinical information rather than HScore alone, so observed associations should not be interpreted as causal. Sixth, the small sample size, despite use of Firth’s bias reduced logistic regression, constrains multivariable adjustment and yields imprecise estimates. In addition, limited sample size and incomplete availability of specialised tests precluded robust quantitative head-to-head comparisons across diagnostic frameworks. Finally, this single-centre experience may limit generalisability. Prospective, adequately powered multicentre studies with standardised data collection, prespecified diagnostic criteria, appropriate handling of missing data and external validation of HScore-based stratification are needed.

Conclusion
The 2016 sJIA-MAS criteria demonstrate the highest proportion fulfilling criteria in our cohort for KD-MAS. Treatment in our cohort and in published cases generally followed a stepwise approach, typically progressing from IVIG±glucocorticoids to adjunct immunosuppressants/biologics. Higher HScores co-occurred with escalation, suggesting they capture baseline severity; prospective multicentre studies are needed to test incremental value and risk-stratification thresholds.
