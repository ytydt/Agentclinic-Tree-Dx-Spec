Management of Drug-induced Dyspepsia in Children: Current Evidence and Clinical Approach
Abstract
Drug-induced dyspepsia (DID) represents an often overlooked cause of upper gastrointestinal (GI) discomfort in children, frequently mimicking functional dyspepsia or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). This review synthesizes current evidence on the diagnosis and management of pediatric DID, integrating expert consensus and available clinical data. Accurate diagnosis of pediatric DID depends on a thorough medication history, covering prescription, over-the-counter (OTC), and supplement use, alongside symptom correlation with drug exposure. Standardized tools such as the EMPACIP Likert dyspepsia severity scale may improve diagnostic precision and enable symptom monitoring. A stepwise management approach involves identifying and discontinuing or substituting the offending agent, implementing dietary and lifestyle modifications, and initiating pharmacologic therapy when needed. H2-receptor antagonists (H2RAs) are recommended as the preferred therapy due to their rapid onset, safety profile, and suitability for on-demand use, while proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are suggested to be reserved for refractory or severe cases with mucosal injury. Prokinetic agents may have a role in select cases with motility-related symptoms, though evidence remains limited. Long-term or prophylactic use of acid-suppressive medications should be approached cautiously, with regular reassessment and tapering to prevent unnecessary exposure. Significant knowledge gaps persist regarding pediatric-specific diagnostic tools, epidemiology, and long-term outcomes. Future research should focus on validating symptom scales, clarifying drug-specific risk profiles, and developing integrated care models. An algorithmic, evidence-informed approach can help standardize care and improve outcomes for children affected by DID.

Introduction
Dyspepsia refers to a collection of upper gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, including epigastric pain or burning, nausea, early satiety, postprandial fullness, and sometimes vomiting or bloating. In children, clinical manifestations vary with age, ranging from feed-associated irritability in infants and peri-umbilical pain in younger children, to heartburn and indigestion in older children. According to the Rome IV criteria, dyspepsia is defined as chronic or recurrent epigastric pain or discomfort persisting for at least several weeks. It may arise from organic, functional, or drug-induced causes.
Drug-induced dyspepsia (DID) refers to dyspeptic symptoms that develop as an adverse reaction to medication exposure. Symptoms such as epigastric pain, postprandial fullness, early satiety, and nausea typically appear or worsen soon after the initiation of a new drug. Drug-induced dyspepsia may involve subjective complaints (e.g., discomfort or burning) and, when investigated, objective findings such as gastritis or mucosal erosions. Pathophysiologic mechanisms include direct mucosal irritation, altered gastric motility, and disruption of acid secretion. Commonly implicated medications in children include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), aspirin (via inhibition of protective prostaglandins), iron and potassium supplements (chemical irritation), tetracyclines, corticosteroids, and even micronutrients such as zinc. Notably, withdrawal from acid-suppressive therapy can paradoxically cause rebound dyspeptic symptoms through transient hypergastrinemia.
Distinguishing DID from functional or organic dyspepsia can be challenging due to overlapping symptom profiles and the high baseline prevalence of dyspepsia in children. No pediatric-specific diagnostic criteria currently exist for DID. Establishing causality, therefore, relies on a detailed medication history, a close assessment of symptom onset relative to drug exposure, and exclusion of alternative causes. Structured symptom assessment using Likert-type scales has been proposed to quantify severity and guide clinical evaluation. However, symptom scores alone cannot reliably differentiate functional from organic etiologies.
Drug-induced dyspepsia is of clinical relevance in pediatrics for several reasons. It may reduce adherence to essential therapies such as iron or antibiotic regimens; mimic more serious GI disease, leading to unnecessary investigations; and, in cases of mucosal injury, result in erosions, ulcerations, or bleeding. Furthermore, recurrent symptoms may impair quality of life, cause feeding difficulties, and negatively impact nutritional status in younger children. Although dyspepsia accounts for a large proportion of pediatric cases, medications remain an important and often under-recognized cause of dyspeptic symptoms. Given the absence of pediatric-specific clinical trials, management of DID in children is largely extrapolated from adult evidence. Current practice varies considerably due to this evidence gap. This review aims to summarize the etiology, pathophysiology, diagnostic approach, and management strategies for DID in pediatric populations. We will describe mechanisms by which medications provoke dyspeptic symptoms, review available evidence for both non-pharmacologic and pharmacologic treatments, including the safety, efficacy, and comparative advantages of H2-receptor antagonists (H2RAs) vs proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and other acid-modifying agents, and present an integrated algorithmic approach to pediatric DID.

Etiology and Pathophysiology
Drug-induced dyspepsia develops through several interacting mechanisms that disrupt normal GI structure and function. In children, these mechanisms often overlap and include direct mucosal injury, altered gastric acid secretion, impaired motility, and systemic or neural effects. The specific presentation and severity depend on the drug involved, the dose, the formulation, the duration of exposure, and host factors.

Direct Mucosal Injury
Direct mucosal damage is the principal mechanism in many cases of DID. Several medications act as topical irritants, causing injury to the epithelial surface and predisposing to inflammation or ulceration. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and aspirin are the most extensively studied examples. They cause both topical injury and systemic inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX-1/COX-2), resulting in reduced prostaglandin synthesis. This decreases mucus and bicarbonate secretion, impairs mucosal blood flow, and weakens epithelial repair, leading to gastritis and peptic ulceration. Iron and potassium supplements may adhere to the gastric mucosa, causing focal erosions and ulcerations, especially with slow-release preparations. Tetracyclines and bisphosphonates can cause pill esophagitis or gastritis if taken without adequate water or when the patient is recumbent. Corticosteroids may impair mucosal defense mechanisms by inhibiting prostaglandin-mediated repair responses and reducing the normal alkaline tide following acid exposure. Microscopically, these processes are characterized by oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, and increased epithelial permeability, which together compromise mucosal integrity.

Altered Gastric Acid Secretion
Disturbances in acid secretion are another major contributor to DID. Drugs that reduce prostaglandin synthesis (e.g., NSAIDs, corticosteroids) or otherwise interfere with mucosal buffering can cause excessive acid exposure or reduce protective capacity. Conversely, abrupt withdrawal of PPIs may trigger rebound acid hypersecretion due to transient hypergastrinemia, stimulating parietal cells and leading to dyspeptic pain. Although H2RAs and PPIs rarely provoke dyspepsia directly, sudden discontinuation can cause transient rebound symptoms.

Altered GI Motility
Changes in gastric and duodenal motility can precipitate or exacerbate dyspeptic symptoms by prolonging contact of irritant substances with the mucosa and increasing gastric distension. Opioid analgesics and anticholinergic agents delay gastric emptying, resulting in bloating and early satiety. Calcium-channel blockers and nitrates relax the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), worsening reflux and heartburn. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and certain chemotherapeutic or immunosuppressive drugs can cause nausea through effects on the central or enteric neural pathways. Antibiotics, particularly macrolides, may induce gastric dysrhythmias or alter motility indirectly through microbiota disruption. Pediatric studies demonstrate that impaired gastric emptying and antral dysmotility correlate with symptom severity in functional dyspepsia, suggesting a similar contribution to DID.

Other Contributing Mechanisms
Additional factors that may contribute to the pathogenesis of DID include increased mucosal permeability, allowing back-diffusion of acid and pepsin into deeper tissue layers, oxidative stress from drug metabolism or metal ion oxidation, and impaired mucosal repair resulting from reduced blood flow and epithelial turnover. Neurogenic mechanisms and psychophysiologic factors, including visceral hypersensitivity, may also influence symptom perception in susceptible pediatric patients.

Common Causative Drug Classes in Children
Several classes of drugs are frequently implicated in pediatric DID (Table 1). Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are the most common offenders and are associated with both dyspeptic symptoms and mucosal injury. Even short courses of ibuprofen or ketorolac can induce epigastric pain or bleeding, particularly in toddlers. In children with chronic inflammatory conditions receiving long-term NSAIDs, endoscopic abnormalities have been documented in up to one-third of patients. Corticosteroids such as prednisolone and deflazacort can exacerbate upper GI symptoms and increase mucosal vulnerability, especially when used concurrently with NSAIDs. Antibiotics, including erythromycin, clindamycin, azithromycin, and amoxicillin/clavulanate, are recognized causes of nausea and dyspeptic discomfort, largely through effects on motility and intestinal microbiota. Iron and zinc supplements, particularly in tablet form or at high doses, are well-documented irritants that may cause nausea, burning, and reactive gastritis. Other implicated agents include calcium-channel blockers, bisphosphonates, and certain psychotropic medications.

Host and Pediatric-specific Factors
The likelihood and severity of DID are influenced by several host-related factors. Younger children have smaller gastric volumes and immature mucosal defenses, increasing vulnerability to high local drug concentrations. Children with chronic cardiac or rheumatic diseases often require long-term NSAIDs or steroids, further elevating risk. Coexisting Helicobacter pylori infection, although less common in children, and a prior history of ulcer disease also increase susceptibility. Nutritional deficiencies can also impair mucosal healing, and polypharmacy may produce additive injury through overlapping mechanisms.

Interaction of Mechanisms
In most pediatric cases, multiple mechanisms act concurrently. An NSAID may suppress prostaglandin-mediated protection, slow gastric emptying, and increase epithelial permeability, thereby amplifying exposure to acid and luminal irritants. These interacting effects are further influenced by developmental physiology, frequent medication use, and dosing variations relative to body weight in children. Short-term exposure may cause transient dyspeptic symptoms without visible mucosal damage, whereas prolonged or high-dose use increases the risk of erosions, ulceration, or bleeding.

Clinical Diagnosis and Assessment of DID
Accurate diagnosis of DID in children requires a systematic and methodical clinical approach. Because the presenting symptoms are often non-specific and overlap with conditions such as functional dyspepsia, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), infections, or other GI disorders, detailed clinical information and structured assessment are essential. Diagnosis is primarily clinical and relies on identifying a temporal relationship between the introduction of a medication and the onset of dyspeptic symptoms, followed by improvement after discontinuation or a dose reduction. The use of validated symptom scales, such as the EMPACIP Likert dyspepsia severity scale, may enhance diagnostic precision and allow for standardized symptom monitoring. A methodical, history-driven approach remains the cornerstone of diagnosing DID in pediatric patients.

Detailed Medication History
A thorough and structured medication history forms the foundation for diagnosing DID. This includes documenting all prescribed drugs, especially those commonly associated with dyspepsia, such as nonsteroidal NSAIDs, corticosteroids, iron, and zinc supplements. Details of each medication, such as name, dose, frequency, route of administration, and formulation (tablet, liquid, immediate- or extended-release), should be recorded. Over-the-counter (OTC) medications, including non-prescription NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac), antipyretic combinations, and herbal remedies, are a frequent but often overlooked source of dyspeptic symptoms. Nutritional supplements, particularly iron or zinc preparations in high doses or solid formulations, can directly irritate the gastric mucosa.
Equally important is documenting the duration of medication use, timing of symptom onset, and any recent changes in dose or formulation. A clear temporal relationship, symptom development soon after drug initiation, and improvement upon withdrawal or dose adjustment strongly support the diagnosis. The clinician should also assess for concurrent medications that increase GI risk (e.g., combinations of NSAIDs and steroids or use of antibiotics that disrupt mucosal defense) and patient-specific risk factors such as age, nutritional status, underlying GI disease, and Helicobacter pylori infection.

Symptom Evaluation and Severity Assessment
Because dyspeptic symptoms in children are subjective and varied, structured evaluation using validated symptom scales improves diagnostic accuracy and facilitates treatment monitoring. The Rome IV criteria for functional dyspepsia in children and adolescents provide a standardized framework to identify dyspeptic symptoms, ensuring they are bothersome, meet defined frequency and duration thresholds, and are not explained by other conditions. Several validated instruments are available for pediatric use. The children dyspepsia symptoms questionnaire (CDSQ), applicable to children aged 5–17 years, distinguishes dyspeptic cases from non-dyspeptic cases and differentiates functional dyspepsia from organic dyspepsia. It evaluates key symptoms, epigastric pain, burning, belching, early satiety, nausea, and postprandial fullness, using Likert-type scales. The Likert dyspepsia severity scale presented in the EMPACIP is a valuable tool for standardizing the assessment of symptom intensity in DID (Fig. 1). It categorizes dyspeptic severity associated with commonly used pediatric drugs and drug combinations based on clinician perception. This five-point scale (1 = no complaints; 2 = Mild; 3 = moderate; 4 = significant; 5 = severe) quantifies symptom burden and enables longitudinal tracking of symptom changes in relation to medication exposure or withdrawal.
EMPACIP's Likert dyspepsia severity scale
This five-point Likert format (1 = no complaints to 5 = severe complaints) can also be used by patients or caregivers for self-assessment. This tool enables symptom monitoring before, during, and after therapeutic adjustments, such as discontinuing the offending medication, switching to an alternative agent, or initiating supportive treatment with acid-suppressive therapy (e.g., H2RAs). Other Likert or visual analog scales (VAS) may also be used for assessing individual symptoms such as pain, nausea, or fullness.

Assessment for Red Flags
Although DID is often benign and self-limited, clinicians must remain vigilant for alarm features that may indicate organic pathology. Red flags include unintended weight loss, persistent vomiting or hematemesis, GI bleeding (melena), dysphagia or odynophagia, severe or refractory abdominal pain, family history of GI malignancy, and clinical signs of anemia or malnutrition. The presence of any of these features warrants prompt further evaluation, including endoscopy or imaging studies, to rule out ulceration, erosions, or other structural lesions.

Physical Examination and Selective Investigations
Physical examination findings in DID are often non-specific but may reveal epigastric tenderness, pallor suggestive of anemia, or signs of nutritional compromise. Laboratory testing should be guided by clinical context. A complete blood count and iron studies may help identify iron-deficiency anemia or iron overload related to supplementation. Testing for H. pylori infection is appropriate in regions with moderate to high prevalence or when dyspeptic symptoms persist despite appropriate therapy. Endoscopy or abdominal ultrasonography should be reserved for cases presenting with alarm features or when symptoms fail to resolve after withdrawal of the suspected medication. Routine imaging is not indicated for uncomplicated dyspepsia.

Differential Diagnosis
The differential diagnosis of pediatric dyspepsia includes functional dyspepsia, GERD, gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and dietary intolerances. Drug-induced dyspepsia should be suspected when symptoms develop shortly after initiating a new medication or altering its dose, particularly in the absence of other causes. In contrast to classical GERD, dyspeptic pain in DID is not typically related to posture or nocturnal symptoms and often occurs with nausea or postprandial fullness. Establishing temporal correlation with drug exposure remains the most reliable diagnostic clue.

Management of DID in Children
Management of DID in children requires a structured, stepwise approach that integrates identification and removal of the causative agent, implementation of lifestyle and dietary measures, and judicious use of pharmacologic therapy. The clinical strategy aims to alleviate symptoms, prevent recurrence, and minimize unnecessary pharmacologic exposure while safeguarding essential treatments. Incorporation of validated symptom assessment tools, such as the EMPACIP Likert dyspepsia severity scale, facilitates individualized care and monitoring of therapeutic response.

Step 1: Identification and Withdrawal or Substitution of Offending Medication
Discontinuing or modifying the causative drug remains the cornerstone of DID management. Whenever possible, the offending agent should be stopped, or its dose reduced, to facilitate mucosal healing. If the medication is essential, substitution with a less irritating alternative is recommended, such as replacing NSAIDs with acetaminophen for pain control or using inhaled rather than systemic corticosteroids in asthma exacerbations. When NSAIDs are indispensable, switching to a selective COX-2 inhibitor (if pediatric use is approved) may reduce GI toxicity.
In cases where substitution is not feasible (e.g., life-saving antibiotics, chemotherapy), concurrent gastric protection using an H2RA or PPI can be considered. However, empirical prophylaxis with PPIs is generally discouraged in children except for high-risk scenarios such as critical illness, prolonged mechanical ventilation, or previous ulcer disease. Formulation changes, such as using coated, buffered, or liquid preparations of iron or zinc supplements, or administering them with food, may also reduce mucosal irritation.
Risk stratification tools such as the EMPACIP Likert severity scale may help identify drugs or combinations that pose a high risk of dyspepsia (e.g., NSAIDs with corticosteroids, high-dose iron or zinc), guiding the need for substitution or prophylactic acid suppression. Step 2: Non-pharmacologic and Lifestyle Interventions
Non-pharmacologic strategies are important adjuncts and may suffice in mild cases. Key measures include dietary and behavioral modifications, avoidance of cofactors, and supportive care.

Dietary and Lifestyle Modifications
Children should avoid spicy, fatty, or acidic foods and carbonated beverages. Smaller, more frequent meals are preferable to large meals, and eating close to bedtime should be avoided. Posture-related measures, such as not lying down immediately after eating and elevating the head of the bed, can help minimize reflux and dyspeptic symptoms.

Supportive Care and Education
Hydration, nutritional adequacy, and parental education are essential. Families should be informed that symptoms typically resolve once the offending medication is discontinued or modified. Food diaries may assist in identifying exacerbating dietary factors.

Psychosocial Support
Stress and anxiety can amplify dyspeptic sensations, particularly in adolescents. Addressing psychosocial contributors through reassurance, relaxation strategies, or referral for cognitive-behavioral therapy can enhance symptom control.

Pharmacologic Therapies
When drug withdrawal and lifestyle modifications are insufficient, pharmacologic therapy may be introduced based on symptom severity and patient age. The main classes used include antacids, H2RAs, PPIs, and prokinetic agents.

Antacids and Surface Agents
Antacids (e.g., calcium carbonate, magnesium hydroxide) neutralize gastric acid and provide rapid but short-lived relief within 15–30 minutes, lasting 30–60 minutes. They are suitable for acute, on-demand symptom control but are not recommended for chronic use due to potential side effects such as diarrhea (magnesium salts) or aluminum toxicity in infants. Calcium carbonate is commonly used (5–15 mg/kg/dose as elemental calcium), while sucralfate, a mucosal protective agent, may be tried (10–40 mg/kg/day in divided doses), although evidence in pediatric DID is limited.

H2-receptor Antagonists
H2-receptor antagonists (cimetidine, ranitidine, famotidine, and nizatidine) suppress acid secretion by blocking histamine H2 receptors on gastric parietal cells. They have a rapid onset (~30 min) and duration of 4–6 hours, making them useful for intermittent or on-demand use. Pediatric studies demonstrate that H2RAs effectively reduce dyspeptic symptoms, with favorable safety profiles. EMPACIP consensus guidelines favor H2RAs as first-line acid suppression for moderate DID, citing their faster onset and fewer long-term safety concerns compared with PPIs.

Proton Pump Inhibitors
Proton pump inhibitors irreversibly inhibit the H+/K+ ATPase enzyme, producing potent, sustained acid suppression lasting up to 24 hours. They are indicated in severe or refractory DID, particularly when endoscopy confirms erosive gastritis or ulceration. Despite their efficacy, safety concerns limit PPI use in children. Reported risks include gut microbiota alteration, increased susceptibility to enteric and respiratory infections (including Clostridioides difficile), nutrient malabsorption (calcium, magnesium, and vitamin B12), and bone fragility. For short-term therapy, omeprazole 0.7–1 mg/kg/day (max 20 mg) or lansoprazole 1–3 mg/kg/day may be used, with tapering to prevent rebound acid hypersecretion.

Prokinetic Agents
Prokinetics are reserved for cases where dyspepsia is dominated by early satiety or postprandial fullness due to delayed gastric emptying. Metoclopramide and domperidone can enhance gastric motility and increase LES tone. However, both carry significant safety concerns: Metoclopramide may cause extrapyramidal side effects in up to 9% of pediatric users, while domperidone has been linked to QT prolongation, warranting ECG monitoring. These drugs should be prescribed only for short durations, under specialist supervision, and when other measures fail.

Monitoring and Safety Considerations

Efficacy Monitoring
Clinical response should be evaluated after 1–2 weeks of therapy using validated tools such as the EMPACIP dyspepsia severity scale. Symptom diaries can document frequency and severity changes over time. Endoscopic reassessment may be warranted for persistent or worsening symptoms.

Safety Monitoring
Regular follow-up is essential to detect adverse effects or drug interactions, particularly when using H2RAs or long-term PPIs. Monitoring should include growth parameters, nutritional status, and laboratory screening for anemia or micronutrient deficiencies when prolonged acid suppression is used.

Duration and Reassessment
Short-term therapy is preferred for both H2RAs and PPIs. Continuous H2RA use beyond 6–8 weeks is rarely required in DID due to tachyphylaxis, while PPIs should be limited to the minimum effective dose and duration (usually ≤2 weeks). When discontinuing PPIs, step-down or tapering strategies can help prevent rebound hyperacidity.

Integrated Algorithmic Approach to Pediatric DID
An algorithmic, evidence-informed approach provides a structured and rational framework for managing DID in children. The first and most crucial step is to identify and remove the offending agent by reviewing all medications and supplements, discontinuing or substituting those known to cause dyspepsia. Lifestyle and dietary modifications should then be implemented, including encouraging smaller, more frequent meals, avoiding irritant foods such as spicy or fatty dishes, and maintaining upright posture after eating. If symptoms persist despite these measures, pharmacologic therapy should be initiated, beginning with H2RAs for on-demand or continuous use, and escalating to PPIs if there is inadequate response or evidence of mucosal injury. Symptom severity scales, such as the five-point EMPACIP Likert dyspepsia severity scale, should be used at baseline and follow-up to guide escalation or de-escalation of therapy. In cases where motility-related symptoms such as postprandial fullness or early satiety predominate, prokinetic therapy may be considered, but only when other measures fail and with close safety monitoring. Regular reassessment after 1–2 weeks is essential to evaluate clinical response, taper or discontinue therapy once stable, and refer persistent or refractory cases to pediatric gastroenterology for further evaluation.

Knowledge Gaps and Future Directions
Pediatric DID remains poorly characterized, with most knowledge extrapolated from adult studies and expert consensus. There is no standardized definition or epidemiologic data describing the true prevalence or natural history of DID in children, and the proportion of dyspeptic symptoms directly attributable to medications is unclear. Diagnostic tools such as the EMPACIP Likert dyspepsia severity scale require validation in pediatric populations, and age-specific questionnaires or biomarkers are needed to distinguish DID from functional dyspepsia. Management strategies are also based largely on adult evidence, with few controlled pediatric trials comparing interventions such as H2RAs vs PPIs or evaluating the role of non-pharmacologic approaches. The long-term safety of acid-reducing medications in children remains uncertain, particularly regarding potential effects on the microbiome, nutrient absorption, and bone health. Developing validated, integrated clinical pathways, potentially embedded within electronic health systems, could improve diagnostic consistency and management. Addressing these gaps through well-designed pediatric studies will be critical to establish evidence-based strategies for diagnosing and treating DID in children.

Conclusion
Drug-induced dyspepsia is an under-recognized but clinically important cause of upper GI symptoms in children. Careful evaluation of medication history, symptom pattern, and temporal relationships is essential to distinguish DID from functional or organic dyspepsia. Management should follow a structured, stepwise approach, beginning with identification and discontinuation or substitution of the offending drug, implementation of dietary and lifestyle modifications, and initiation of pharmacologic therapy only when necessary. Among available treatments, H2RAs offer a practical first-line option for most pediatric cases due to their rapid onset, favorable safety profile, and suitability for on-demand use, while PPIs should be reserved for refractory or severe cases with documented mucosal injury. Continuous monitoring, appropriate tapering, and avoidance of unnecessary prolonged therapy are vital to minimize adverse effects. Given current evidence gaps, further pediatric-specific research is needed to refine diagnostic criteria, validate assessment tools, and establish optimal management pathways for this population.

Clinical Significance
Recognizing DID is essential to prevent misdiagnosis, unnecessary investigations, and prolonged acid-suppressive therapy in children. This review provides clinicians with a clear, evidence-informed approach to identifying medication-related symptoms, using structured assessment tools, and implementing stepwise, rational management. Applying these strategies can improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce inappropriate treatment, and enhance care for children with dyspeptic symptoms.
