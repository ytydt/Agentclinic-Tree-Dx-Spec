Developing a Comprehensive Framework for Cost-Effectiveness Evaluation in Metastatic Castration-Sensitive Prostate Cancer: Insights from a Systematic Review
Background and Objectives
Metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC) imposes a significant economic burden and necessitates more cost-effective treatment strategies. The variability among the components of published economic evaluation models leads to methodological inconsistencies, underscoring the need for an optimal framework to minimise unwarranted structural variation. This paper reviews existing economic evaluations, establishes a comprehensive framework and aims to support future economic evaluations and decision-making in mCSPC.
Methods
A systematic literature review (SLR) was conducted to identify relevant economic evaluations in mCSPC. Health technology assessments (HTAs) by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, and Canada’s Drug Agency were reviewed to gather insights on critiques and limitations. On the basis of these findings, a comprehensive cost-effectiveness modelling framework was established. Furthermore, two additional SLRs were conducted to identify cost and resource utilisation inputs, as well as health state utility scores derived from published studies and HTA assessments.
Results
Markov models and partitioned survival models (PSMs) were commonly reported in literature and published HTA evaluations. Despite the strong precedence of PSMs, we propose an optimal framework for mCSPC utilising a semi-Markov structure. This approach offers increased flexibility, allowing transition rates from progressed states to depend on time since progression occurred. We also present key sources of cost and utility data identified in the SLR.
Discussion
This work aligns with methodologies recommended by the Innovative Medicine Initiative (IMI) PIONEER external group and published studies. The optimal framework, including healthcare resource utilisation and utility data, consolidates existing modelling precedents in mCSPC and will assist the cost-effectiveness assessment of treatments for this condition.

Introduction
Prostate cancer is among the most commonly diagnosed cancers in men. Despite advancements in early detection and treatment, a considerable proportion of cases progress to advanced stages, such as metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC). mCSPC is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates and has significant economic implications for healthcare systems. This growing financial burden underscores the importance of cost-effective treatment approaches.
Multiple treatment options exist for managing mCSPC, including androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), novel hormone agents, such as abiraterone acetate, apalutamide, enzalutamide, and darolutamide, and combination therapies with chemotherapy. Numerous economic evaluations have explored the cost-effectiveness of these treatments; however, the applicability of these evaluations is often limited by differences in model structures, data sources, and underlying assumptions.
Cost-effectiveness models for mCSPC commonly utilise one of several established frameworks, each with unique strengths and limitations. Markov models and partitioned survival models (PSMs) are frequently employed to evaluate treatment pathways and patient health states over time. Both approaches make use of health states; one distinguishing feature between the two approaches is that in a Markov model, transitions between states are estimated (referred to as a state-transition approach), whereas in a PSM, health state occupancy is estimated directly using extrapolations of overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) curves. In addition, more complex patient-level approaches, such as discrete event simulation (DES), have also been used in this area.
Existing literature discusses the appropriateness of various model structures for oncology indications, concluding that the differentiating factors between PSMs and state-transition models (STMs) mostly concern the way that time-to-event data are used. While PSMs can be developed without access to individual patient data (IPD), the resulting extrapolations have limited scope for sensitivity analyses, opening the risk that uncertainties could be poorly represented to decision-makers. Although both approaches should produce very similar estimates of outcomes and results for the clinical trial timeframe, Woods et al. highlight recent work demonstrating substantively different survival extrapolations beyond the trial period, between PSMs and STMs, emphasising the need for proper consideration of each approach during model conceptualisation.
In addition to the overarching differences in modelling approach, more nuanced structural considerations can significantly impact a model’s validity and its applicability to real-world outcomes. These considerations include the choice between time-homogeneous (i.e. models in which each cycle represents the same duration of time) and time-inhomogeneous (i.e. models which allow for varying cycle lengths) Markov models, as well as the use of semi-Markov models that allow transition rates to depend on time in the current state. Multi-state models (MSMs) are a lesser-known type of state-transition model that are suitable for modelling a series of competing or sequential events, such as progression and survival in the case of oncology modelling. A 2017 study comparing an MSM approach with standard partitioned survival and Markov modelling found that multi-state models provide a more comprehensive and detailed representation of patient pathways when compared with PSMs, and can capture transitions between states more effectively, leading to improved estimates of survival probabilities.
Each economic evaluation conducted within mCSPC presents a unique decision problem, defined by factors such as the intervention and comparators to be assessed, the specific disease subgroup, the perspective taken and the outcome of interest. This complexity necessitates some variation in model components. However, not all variations between model frameworks are beneficial; often, they arise from inconsistent methodological decisions made by modellers.
While methods have been proposed to assess and account for structural uncertainty in economic modelling, there are opportunities to reduce unwarranted structural variation by establishing ‘optimal’ model frameworks in commonly studied disease areas. Published examples of such frameworks already exist. For example, in obesity, a framework has been developed and its predictive validity tested using cross comparison with real-world evidence. This structure has been used in the appraisals of two of the three obesity medications subsequently assessed by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). Similarly, a model referred to as the ‘York model’ has been viewed as an optimal framework for the evaluation of treatments for psoriatic arthritis. NICE routinely develop economic models as part of their clinical guidelines, and these frameworks often set the precedent for subsequent health technology assessments (HTAs). Other examples of widely adopted frameworks include the HERCULES model for evaluations in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, a relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis framework that was developed by Sheffield Centre for Health Research for NICE and has since been used as an optimal framework, and the UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) model for type 2 diabetes mellitus. These successful examples from other disease areas inspire the development of an optimal model framework for mCSPC. Establishing such a framework would not only improve the quality of economic evaluations but also facilitate more informed decision-making in clinical practice.
Quality and relevance of data inputs is another contributing factor to the accuracy of an economic evaluation. High-quality, consistent data on treatment costs, healthcare resource use (HCRU) and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) are essential for strengthening model validity. In contrast, variability or gaps in these data can introduce uncertainty and lead to conclusions that may not be applicable to real-world settings. The reliability of model outputs is directly tied to the appropriateness of data inputs; if models are populated with low-quality or poorly suited data, the resulting estimates will reflect these limitations, diminishing the model’s relevance and accuracy in real-world decision-making.
Addressing all these challenges requires careful selection of model structures, as well as a commitment to using high-quality, relevant data that accurately represent patient experiences and HCRU.

Methods
The methodology comprised a multi-step approach to develop a plausible economic model for mCSPC. First, a systematic literature review (SLR) of previously published economic evaluations was conducted to identify relevant modelling approaches and methodologies. The search was conducted in November 2021, December 2023, and November 2024 across several databases, including Embase®, MEDLINE®, EconLit, and Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews (EBMR) using keywords including ‘prostate cancer’, ‘mHSPC’, ‘castration-sensitive’, ‘hormone naïve’ (Supplementary Information). The quality of the included studies was assessed using Drummond’s checklist (Supplementary Information).
In addition, an in-depth review of past HTA submissions in mCSPC by NICE and Canada’s Drug Agency (CDA) was conducted, focusing on critiques and limitations related to model structure, assumptions and data robustness. Both agencies publish detailed assessments of their evaluation of submitted models. The comprehensive template for the optimal model is provided in the Supplementary Information.
Our approach was further informed by a peer-reviewed reference model for health economic evaluations for advanced prostate cancer by IMI PIONEER group and the guidelines from the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) and the Society for Medical Decision Making (SMDM) taskforce; in particular, the report on good research practices in conceptualising a model. In line with the ISPOR-SMDM guidelines, we first conceptualised the problem (i.e. need for reliable economic model that reflected the treatment pathway for mCSPC to guide decision-making on the costs and consequences of competing treatments), and then we conceptualised the model itself, guided by subject experts, published literature and subsequent taskforce guidelines on appropriate modelling methods. Combined, these insights informed the design of an ‘optimal’ model framework that addressed identified gaps and adhered to HTA methodological standards. The developed model was then subject to internal validation, whereby the CDA validation tool was employed to ensure it was robust and valid for decision-making at the point of submission (Supplementary Information).
Two additional SLRs were conducted to gather relevant HCRU and health state utility value (HSUV) inputs using Embase®, MEDLINE®, EconLit and Evidence-Based Medicine Reviews (EBMR) databases. HSUV studies were quality checked using the Papaioannou checklist. HCRU studies were not quality checked owing to the heterogeneous nature of the study designs included in the SLR. HCRU and HSUV inputs were incorporated to ensure the framework would most accurately reflect real-world clinical practice and economic considerations, thereby creating a comprehensive and reliable basis for decision-making in mCSPC.
All SLRs were conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The complete eligibility criteria can be found in the Supplementary Information.

Results

Literature Review of Published Economic Evaluations
Database searches were conducted in November 2021 and updated in December 2023 and November 2024, identifying 1467 titles after duplicate removal (November 2021: n = 799; December 2023: n = 436; November 2024: n = 232). Of these, 131 full texts were reviewed (November 2021: n = 79; December 2023: n = 25; November 2024: n = 27). An additional HTA search identified 16 appraisals (November 2021: n = 3; December 2023: n = 11; November 2024: n = 2) and two conference abstracts (November 2021: n = 1; December 2024: n = 1), resulting in 53 included studies (November 2021: n = 23; December 2023: n = 20; November 2024: n = 10). Data extraction prioritised studies from Europe, Canada and the USA, yielding 36 studies, while 17 studies from other regions were excluded. The most common model type in the literature was the Markov model (n = 14), followed by the PSM (n = 7). Other model types used included a combination of decision tree and Markov model (n = 1) and (non-Markov) state transition model (n = 1). In contrast, of the 12 HTA submissions, 8 used a PSM, with all but 1 of the NICE submissions opting for this approach (Table 1). This may be owing to the ability to validate OS extrapolations from a PSM against external data, or the perception that there are insufficient data to accurately determine transition probabilities in Markov models.
The time horizon across studies ranged from 2 years to a lifetime, with lifetime being the most commonly adopted (n = 10). Of these, the definition of lifetime varied between 20 years (n = 3) and 30 years (n = 2), or was not specified (n = 7). The model inputs are summarised in Table 1.

Framework for Cost-Effectiveness Analysis
Although PSMs have more commonly been applied in submissions to HTA bodies, these types of models are often selected to disentangle disease progression from death, where models have relied solely on OS and PFS inputs. These are also selected owing to a lack of more complete survival data, which can distinguish pre-progression survival (pre-PS) and post-PS from OS.
A more methodologically robust choice of model structure is that of a Markov multistate model, which simulates time to progression (TTP), time to death pre-progression (i.e. pre-PS), and time to death post-progression (i.e. post-PS). Increased flexibility is afforded to our modelling approach by adopting a semi-Markov structure. This allows the transition rates from the progressed state to depend on time since progression; in a Markov model the rates can depend only on time since the start of modelling. Discrete-time semi-Markov models, using cycles, can be implemented in Microsoft Excel® or R statistical programming language using tunnel states for each cycle; continuous-time semi-Markov models can also be implemented in R using the hesim package, for example.
An innovative method for approximating transition rates was developed by Pahuta et al., which can separately estimate disease progression and death before progression when reconstructing OS and PFS data using the Guyot method. This approach was recently applied in the IMI PIONEER (semi-Markov) model to simulate patient outcomes in prostate cancer. The method ties in OS and PFS event and/or censoring times to reconstruct the event times for transitions needed of a Markov model. The authors provided methods for estimating transition rates, but these relied on assumptions that rates were time-homogeneous, which is a limitation for oncology models. Parametric models can instead be estimated directly using the calculated event and censoring times for each transition rate and an assumption that, during trial follow-up, transition rates are time-homogeneous (i.e. exponential PFS), to estimate the transition rate from progression-free to post-progression. If available, we also recommend using alternative evidence, such as IPD from clinical trials or registry data, on post-PS rates.
However, the Pahuta et al. algorithm has not been extensively validated. If anomalies are identified when reconstructing the transition data, alternatives should be considered. One option is multistate network meta-analysis (NMA), which uses OS and PFS survival curves to estimate transition rates of an underlying semi-Markov model. A potential challenge with multistate NMA is that it may need fractional polynomial survival models for transition rates, which have been found to give implausible extrapolations. Another is to make a common assumption that PFS treatment effects are a reasonable proxy for TTP treatment effects, and that OS treatment effects can be a proxy for pre-PS and possibly post-PS treatment effects. This assumption has been made in previously published Markov models in mCSPC.

Aligned with previous models, the ‘optimal’ framework includes three progressed disease (PD) health states, reflecting up to three lines of subsequent therapy as per local treatment patterns. The semi-Markov model, therefore, comprises five health states:

All patients enter the model in the mCSPC health state (Fig. 1). For each cycle, the proportion of the cohort who experience progression on treatment then transition to the mCRPC (PD1) health state, where they can transition through two further PD health states (PD2 and PD3), reflecting three different lines of subsequent therapy. These should be informed by current treatment options in the setting of interest. Patients can experience adverse events (AEs) at the start of therapy or die at any stage during the time horizon.
A 28-day cycle length is applied to align with the typical dosing regimen for treatments of mCSPC. A half-cycle correction is applied to reflect the fact that events and transitions can occur at any point during the cycle.
As the cohort progresses through the states of the model, the associated HCRU costs are applied accordingly, as are HSUVs.

Appropriate Model Inputs for Costs and Utility Analysis
A total of 62 studies were identified through the HCRU SLR (Supplementary Information), encompassing a wide range of methodologies and data sources. Although the majority of these studies were economic evaluations, heterogeneity was observed in terms of geographic settings, patient populations, cost years, and treatment regimens assessed. This variability highlights the challenges of harmonising data across diverse healthcare systems and economic environments.
Most studies reported direct medical costs, including drug acquisition, general practitioner (GP) visits, and other healthcare services. Drug administration costs were reported in eight studies, while costs related to adverse events were captured in seven. Notably, only one study by Stoffel addressed out-of-pocket (OOP) expenses, and none incorporated productivity losses, which may constitute a significant economic burden in real-world contexts. Drug acquisition costs were primarily derived from country-specific databases, underscoring the importance of regional cost inputs to accurately reflect HCRU.
A detailed list of studies reporting various cost and resource use parameters is provided in the Supplementary Information. However, the selection of cost and resource inputs for economic models should be tailored to the specific country context, comparators and perspective of the evaluation to ensure relevance and accuracy.
A total of nine studies were identified by the HSUV SLR (see PRISMA diagrams in Supplementary Information), but similarly to the HCRU data, these studies exhibited considerable heterogeneity in terms of geographical representation, patient demographics and the methodologies employed to derive utility values. This variability suggests that care must be taken when generalising utility values across different populations and healthcare settings.

Utility values were consistently highlighted as pivotal inputs in published economic evaluations, directly influencing the outcomes of cost-effectiveness analyses. Many of the economic evaluations, including those used in HTAs, relied on HSUVs reported in landmark clinical trials, such as LATITUDE and ARCHES (Table 2), which provided robust, trial-based measures of HRQoL. The LATITUDE trial reported mean baseline EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) utility index of 0.8 for patients with mCSPC in both the abiraterone acetate plus ADT arm (n = 597) and the ADT plus placebo arm (n = 602). In contrast, the HSUVs measured in the ARCHES trial were not reported in the primary publication (and were redacted in the HTA submission to NICE).

Discussion
In this study, we aimed to develop an ‘optimal’ framework specifically for economic models assessing treatments in mCSPC. Drawing from an extensive review of existing models, our framework aims to address the strengths, weaknesses, and intricacies observed in literature, providing a decisive recommendation on the most suitable model structure for future mCSPC model applications.
To establish the foundation of our framework, a series of SLRs were conducted to identify and synthesise existing model precedents in mCSPC. These SLRs not only ensured a robust starting point for our framework development but also allowed for previous research findings to be integrated and advanced.
Our recommendations form a framework that can be used to assess the cost-effectiveness of various treatment options for mCSPC. HSUVs and HCRU inputs, all being intervention agnostic, were identified within this study and presented for consideration alongside the ‘optimal’ model structure. Other model inputs, primarily those relating to efficacy and safety, are essential for the parameterisation of a model for mCSPC, but these were not included within the scope of this study as these are inherently treatment-specific.
Our semi-Markov approach requires separation of disease progression from death using OS and PFS data. Reconstructing TTP and pre-PS can be achieved for non-trial comparators using an innovative method developed by Pahuta et al.. The method uses joint OS and PFS event times and assumes that during trial follow-up, transition rates are time-homogeneous to estimate the transition rate from progression-free to post-progression survival. A limitation with this approach is that the estimation of transition rates requires the model to be time-homogenous; as such, the method can only be solved precisely for the exponential case.
While the IMI PIONEER model utilises the Pahuta et al. method relying on time-homogeneous transition rates and focuses exclusively on OS and PFS to model transitions, our proposed framework offers greater flexibility and precision. Unlike the IMI PIONEER model, which is patient-level and limited to a narrower set of parametric models (exponential, Weibull, lognormal, and log-logistic), our cohort-based semi-Markov model accommodates a comprehensive range of standard parametric and spline models, allowing for time-inhomogeneous transition rates. Furthermore, our model distinguishes between multiple transition states –TTP, pre-PS, and post-PS—enabling a more granular and accurate representation of patient outcomes in both mCSPC and mCRPC, which is important in the context of treatment sequencing decision-making. This structure also permits the use of reconstructed IPD from published sources, enhancing applicability when original IPD is unavailable. This could include real-world evidence on post-PS associated with mCRPC. Collectively, these features make our framework more adaptable for economic evaluations in mCSPC, supporting diverse data inputs and providing a robust alternative to the IMI PIONEER model’s constraints.
In instances where the Pahuta et al. method is not suitable, an option is that OS and radiographic PFS could be used for non-trial comparators as proxies for pre-PS and TTP, respectively. However, employing these proxies may introduce certain biases. For example, while radiographic PFS provides valuable information regarding disease progression, it may not fully account for the nuances of TTP, particularly in cases where rapid progression occurs or when there are differing definitions of progression across studies.
To overcome these limitations, a further possibility is to utilise multistate NMA, as discussed in the recent literature by Jansen et al.. This approach allows for the simultaneous consideration of multiple treatment options and their associated health states, providing a more complete understanding of the relationships between efficacy outcomes. This method also mitigates some of the biases associated with using single proxies by allowing for the incorporation of a broader range of data and treatment pathways, ultimately leading to a more accurate representation of the disease progression landscape in mCSPC.
Despite these advancements, the ‘optimal’ framework and the associated HSUV and HCRU inputs we present within that framework are not free of limitations. The framework is a simplistic representation of a heterogeneous condition, as is typical of economic models. For instance, it does not consider the efficacy of subsequent treatments and therefore cannot identify optimal sequences. For a decision problem requiring comparison of determined sequences, adaptations to the optimal framework would be required. The usefulness of this additional complexity for decision-making would be determined by the extent of available efficacy data for each treatment according to its place in the sequence, which is often scarce.
In addition, the framework assesses treatments at a cohort level rather than at a patient level, meaning that variability in patient outcomes is not accounted for. This contrasts with the patient-level IMI PIONEER model. While a microsimulation or a DES is likely to offer more flexibility than cohort models, a DES previously submitted to NICE for mCRPC was deemed to be unnecessarily complex and lacking in transparency, while cohort-level models have previously been considered suitable for decision-making in this disease.
Most of the identified economic evaluations highlighted direct medical costs significantly influencing healthcare budgets and economic outcomes, with drug acquisition emerging as the predominant cost driver. Country-specific databases were the primary sources of HCRU data, while HSUVs were primarily obtained from key trials such as LATITUDE and TITAN. Given the identified data gaps, particularly regarding OOP expenses and productivity losses, future research should aim to gather more comprehensive data in these areas to enhance the robustness of economic evaluations.
Individual susceptibility traits, such as genetic testing, were considered for inclusion in the optimal model. However, their incorporation was excluded owing to increased healthcare resource utilisation and costs associated with variant-driven personalised therapies, which may limit cost-effectiveness. Furthermore, limited global access to genetic testing restricts the model’s applicability, as inclusion would confine use to settings where such testing is available.
A few more limitations inherent to this work should be highlighted. First, while the proposed optimal structure is suitable for mCSPC treatments where the timing of progression to mCRPC is critical, its generalisability to mCRPC populations previously treated with androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPis) may be limited owing to uncertainty in the effectiveness of subsequent treatment (e.g. after two lines of ARPi). However, it may still be applicable in cases where ARPis have not been previously used. Ongoing research is exploring the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of treatment sequencing in mCRPC using an adapted version of this framework. Second, the use of discrete cycle approximations in semi-Markov models with tunnel states may not fully capture continuous patient transitions. Although continuous-time models could address this, the short cycle length employed (28 days with half-cycle correction) likely minimises any impact on results. Third, the separation of disease progression from death using published Kaplan–Meier data on OS and PFS, while conceptually sound, faces practical challenges. The IMI PIONEER model’s use of Pahuta’s method was limited by its time-homogeneity assumption, leading to the use of OS and rPFS as proxies for pre-PS and TTP in non-trial comparators, though the accuracy of this approach remains uncertain. Lastly, users of the framework must populate time-to-event outcomes using IPD or reconstructed IPD from Kaplan–Meier curves with the appropriate survival parameters required to extrapolate outcomes (e.g. shape and scale parameters for a Weibull distribution). Alternatively, HRs (on pre-PS and TTP), obtained from published sources, could be used to parameterise the model provided that there is a comparator included in the model with survival already extrapolated (whether using IPD or reconstructed IPD using KM curves) against which the HR could be applied. When only OS and PFS data are available, these may serve as proxies for TTP and pre-PS, with the option to incorporate real-world or trial-based survival data for post-progression survival outcomes.
Resource constraints or time pressures, particularly in the preparation of evidence for an HTA submission, often restrict the exploration of structural uncertainty in economic modelling. Comparisons between fully executed models (as opposed to conceptual frameworks) in published case studies, such as that by Cranmer et al. (2020), have demonstrated substantial differences in cost-effectiveness results between MSM and PSM approaches in oncology. Further refinement of the optimal framework could be achieved with each use, and case studies in mCSPC specifically are encouraged. For instance, the impact of reducing the number of health states (either fewer post-progression states or removal of the pre-PS state) could be assessed in further research via the parameter constraints approach described by Thom et al..

Conclusions
Our work closely aligns with the methodologies recommended by the IMI PIONEER group. We provide practical and specific recommendations for parameterising a semi-Markov structure using multi-state modelling, which is still relatively novel and underutilised compared with traditional approaches. The resulting optimal framework consolidates existing modelling precedents in mCSPC and serves as a vital resource for researchers, agencies and organisations developing future models in this area.
