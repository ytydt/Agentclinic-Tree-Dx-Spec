Lateral lumbar interbody fusion versus single position prone transpsoas approach: A comprehensive bibliometric analysis
Background
Lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) and single position prone lateral (PTP) approaches represent significant advances in minimally invasive spine surgery, yet comprehensive comparative analysis of their research trajectories remains limited.
Purpose
This bibliometric analysis aims to comprehensively compare the scientific landscapes surrounding LLIF and PTP approaches by examining publication trends, citation patterns, authorship networks, and thematic evolution.
Methods
We systematically searched Scopus, PubMed and Google Scholar from inception through September 2025 for articles addressing LLIF and single position prone lateral approaches. Bibliometrix package in R was used to complete the bibliometric analysis. We included the following parameters: publication output, citation metrics, journal distribution, author productivity, international collaboration networks, keyword co-occurrence, and thematic mapping in the analysis.
Results
We identified 823 LLIF publications and 107 single position prone lateral publications. LLIF accounted for 20,707 total citations with an h-index of 70 and a mean of 31.93 citations per document, whereas PTP accumulated 915 citations with an h-index of 17 and a mean of 8.44 citations per document. Although LLIF has a larger volume of publications, PTP has progressed more rapidly, showing annual growth rates of 44% compared with 30% for LLIF during comparable developmental periods. The United States contributed 45.6% of LLIF and 71.8% of PTP publications, with LLIF research spanning 42 countries and PTP 16 countries. Core research themes for both techniques evolved from technical descriptions to comparative effectiveness studies, and the substantial overlap in authors and institutions indicates complementary rather than competitive research trajectories.
Conclusions
LLIF represents a mature surgical approach with a strong evidence base while PTP demonstrates accelerating research interest, with growth trajectories exceeding those of LLIF during comparable developmental stages. Based on current publication trends and thematic evolution, future research on PTP should prioritize rigorous comparative studies, long-term outcome evaluations, cost-effectiveness analyses, and patient-centered outcome research to strengthen the evidence base and guide clinical adoption.

Background
The evolution of lumbar interbody fusion has been driven by the imperative to reduce surgical morbidity while ensuring reliable fusion outcomes. The traditional anterior and posterior approaches, while effective, carry considerable risks and often require prolonged recovery. In 2002, Wolfla and colleagues described a retroperitoneal lumbar interbody fusion using a lateral approach for managing failed multilevel arthrodesis, thereby establishing the foundation for lateral access to the lumbar spine. Building upon this pioneering work, a paradigm shift occurred in 2006 when Ozgur et al. refined the technique into a minimally invasive transpsoas approach, popularized as extreme lateral interbody fusion (XLIF) and broadly termed lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF).
The conceptual elegance of this technique lies in accessing the disc space through a retroperitoneal lateral corridor in the lateral decubitus position, avoiding anterior great vessels and posterior neural structures. As a result, strong evidence has accumulated supporting its efficacy in treating degenerative disc disease, adult spinal deformity, and adjacent segment disease. These studies consistently demonstrate restoration of disc height, sagittal alignment correction, and indirect neural decompression.
Despite these advantages, the lateral decubitus position introduces workflow complexities that prolong operative time, including repositioning and additional anesthesia transitions. These limitations prompted the development of the single-position prone transpsoas (PTP) approach, which maintains the benefits of the lateral access while eliminating positioning constraints. In the prone position, surgeons can directly access the posterior spine for decompression or hardware revision when necessary, which offers greater procedural versatility. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that PTP may enhance sagittal alignment through improved lordosis correction compared with traditional lateral techniques.
By performing the entire procedure in a single position, surgeons can seamlessly integrate lateral interbody fusion with posterior instrumentation without repositioning, thereby streamlining surgical workflow and reducing overall procedural time. However, the approach also presents unique challenges, including altered anatomical landmarks and modified neuromonitoring considerations.
Despite the growing increase in publications and clinical adoption of PTP, the literature remains fragmented and heavily skewed toward early descriptive studies. The relative novelty of the PTP approach compared to the well-established LLIF procedure creates an asymmetry in the available evidence. As a result, it remains unclear whether PTP represents a true evolution and potential replacement of LLIF, a complementary alternative suited to specific indications, or simply a temporary trend reflecting the broader push toward single-position surgery.
The present study addresses this knowledge gap through a comprehensive bibliometric analysis comparing the scientific landscapes of LLIF and PTP approaches. Our primary objective is to analyze and compare publication trends, citation patterns, authorship networks, and thematic evolution for both techniques. Secondary objectives include identifying influential works, analyzing international collaboration patterns, mapping conceptual structures through keyword analysis, and projecting future research trajectories. By providing this comprehensive bibliometric analysis, we aim to provide an evidence-based understanding of the current state of knowledge surrounding these techniques, and thus clarify existing knowledge gaps, facilitate informed clinical decision-making and identify priority areas for future research.

Methods

Search strategy and data collection
A comprehensive systematic search was conducted to identify all relevant publications addressing LLIF and PTP approaches. The search encompassed 3 major bibliographic databases: Scopus, PubMed/MEDLINE, and Google Scholar selected for their comprehensive coverage of medical literature. The search period spanned from database inception through September 2025.
For the LLIF, we employed a Boolean search strategy incorporating multiple terminological variations to maximize retrieval sensitivity. The search string included terms such as ``LLIF,'' ``XLIF,'' ``DLIF,'' ``transpsoas approach,'' and ``lateral access'' combined with ``lumbar fusion'' or ``interbody fusion.'' Medical subject headings (MeSH) terms were utilized when available.
For the PTP approach search, we utilized terms including ``prone transpsoas,'' ``prone lateral,'' ``single position,'' ``prone LLIF,'' ``prone position lumbar fusion'' combined with ``transpsoas'' and related anatomical descriptors. Given the relative novelty of this technique and potential variability in nomenclature, we manually reviewed results to exclude irrelevant studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Inclusion criteria were established a priori to ensure appropriate study selection. Publications were included if they met the following criteria: original research articles or review articles describing or analyzing LLIF or PTP approaches. Additionally, only articles published in peer-reviewed journals in the English language were included in the analysis. We excluded publications that would not meaningfully contribute to the bibliometric analysis, including nonpeer reviewed articles, conference abstracts, technical notes, errata, letters to editors without original data, and articles where LLIF or PTP were mentioned only tangentially without substantive discussion.

Analytical framework
All bibliometric analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0) with the bibliometrix package, facilitated through the Biblioshiny web-based interface for interactive visualization. Publish or Perish software supported citation analysis and h-index calculations. This analytical framework has been extensively validated in medical bibliometric studies and enables reproducible analyses across multiple dimensions.
The analytical framework included descriptive analysis of publication output, with temporal distribution and annual growth rates calculated using compound annual growth rate formulas. Citation analysis assessed total citations, average citations per document, h-index, and identification of highly cited publications to evaluate research impact. The h-index, defined as the number of papers with at least h citations, provides a balanced measure of productivity and influence. Authorship and institutional analyses examined individual contributions, collaboration patterns, and institutional productivity. Journal analysis identified preferred publication venues and applied Bradford’s Law of Scattering to determine core journals. Keyword analysis incorporated both author-provided and index terms to explore research themes and conceptual evolution. Descriptive statistics, including means, medians, and ranges, were calculated for all bibliometric indicators, and comparative analysis between LLIF and PTP techniques was performed using standardized metrics.

Results

Publication landscape and temporal evolution
The comprehensive literature search identified a total of 823 publications addressing LLIF and 107 publications focused on the PTP approach. This substantial disparity in publication volume reflects both the longer history of the LLIF technique, which was introduced in the early 2000s, and its broader adoption across spine surgery practices globally. PTP, introduced approximately 5 years ago, expectedly demonstrates a smaller absolute publication count but reveals dynamic growth patterns.

LLIF publications demonstrate a characteristic maturation pattern with an initial lag phase from 2002 to 2009, representing early adoption. This was followed by rapid exponential growth from 2009 to 2022, with annual publication counts increasing from 3 in 2009 to a peak of 94 papers per year in 2022, corresponding to a compound annual growth rate of 30%. This phase coincides with widespread technique adoption, proliferation of training programs, and multicenter studies. Subsequently, publication output has declined from 2022 to 2025, decreasing from 94 to around 41 publications per year.
In contrast, PTP publications show an exponential growth characteristic of emerging techniques. Publications started in 2020 with 4 papers, increasing steadily to 25 papers in September 2025, yielding a compound annual growth rate of 44%.

Citation analysis and research impact
Citation metrics provide a quantitative assessment of research impact and influence within the academic community. The LLIF literature accumulated 20,707 total citations since inception, with a mean citation rate of 31.93 citations per document. The h-index for the LLIF corpus is 70, indicating that 70 papers have received at least 70 citations each. The g-index, which weights highly cited papers more heavily, is 113 for LLIF, further confirming the presence of influential works.
On the other hand, PTP literature has accumulated 915 total citations with a mean of 8.55 citations per document. The h-index for PTP is 17 and the g-index is 26. When accounting for the different publication histories through citations per year since publication, PTP demonstrates an average citation rate of approximately 3.6 citations per year compared to 4.2 citations per year for LLIF papers (excluding the 2006 seminal paper with an exceptionally high citation count).
Analysis of the most highly cited papers reveals the intellectual foundations of each approach. The ten most-cited LLIF publications received between 263 and 1,382 citations. These works define the technique, establish safety, and demonstrate clinical efficacy. The most cited LLIF study, published by Ozgur et al. in 2006 (1,382 citations), introduced the extreme lateral interbody fusion (XLIF) technique as a novel, minimally invasive retroperitoneal transpsoas approach and provided early evidence of feasibility without requiring an access surgeon.
Subsequent highly cited contributions include large prospective multicenter series delineating complication profiles in 600 patients; investigations into cage subsidence and implant sizing; comprehensive systematic reviews comparing LLIF with other interbody fusion techniques; and studies evaluating perioperative outcomes in adult degenerative scoliosis. Early safety analyses documenting the minimally invasive advantages of the lateral approach further established LLIF as a safe, effective alternative to traditional anterior and posterior fusion, characterized by reduced blood loss, shorter hospitalization, and comparable fusion rates.
In contrast, highly cited PTP papers reflect an emerging but rapidly maturing body of literature. The most cited paper, published by Lamartina and Berjano in 2020 (75 citations), introduced the prone single-position XLIF (Pro-XLIF) and demonstrated its feasibility and safety in a prospective pilot study, reporting outcomes equivalent to the lateral decubitus technique with shorter operative times. Other influential publications include cadaveric feasibility analyses and early clinical series validating anatomical reproducibility (, 56 citations); technical descriptions enabling simultaneous anterior and posterior column access (, 51 citations); and multicenter radiographic studies demonstrating improved segmental lordosis compared to lateral positioning (, 45 citations, 38 citations).
Further contributions include systematic reviews comparing single-position and dual-position techniques, showing reductions in operative time and hospitalization (, 37 citations, 26 citations), as well as radiographic analyses confirming enhanced L4-L5 access and improved positional lordosis in prone position (, 28 citations).

Source analysis and journal distribution
Analysis of journal dissemination patterns reveals the disciplinary focus, specialization, and research visibility. LLIF research has appeared in 118 distinct journals, indicating broad disciplinary appeal across neurosurgery, orthopedic surgery, spine surgery, and related specialties. The journal distribution follows Bradford's Law of Scattering, with a core group of highly productive journals, a larger set of moderately productive journals, and a long tail of journals publishing occasional LLIF papers. The core zone (Zone 1), defined as journals publishing at least 38 LLIF papers, includes 5 journals: World Neurosurgery, European Spine Journal, Spine, Journal of Neurosurgery: Spine, and Global Spine Journal. These journals collectively account for 37.1% of all LLIF publications (305 of 823 articles). The most productive journal for LLIF research is World Neurosurgery, which published 84 papers, representing 10.2% of the total corpus. The second through fifth most productive journals are European Spine Journal (n=67 publications), Spine (n=59), Journal of Neurosurgery: Spine (n=57), and Global Spine Journal (n=38).
PTP research has appeared in 36 distinct journals, fewer than LLIF but still reflecting substantial publication breadth. Publications are more concentrated in core journals, with 4 journals comprising the core zone (Zone 1) and accounting for 37.4% of all PTP articles (40 of 107). The top journal is World Neurosurgery, with 15 publications (14.0% of the corpus), followed by European Spine Journal (n=10), Operative Neurosurgery (n=8), International Journal of Spine Surgery (n=7), and Journal of Neurosurgery: Spine (n=7).

Author analysis and collaborative networks
Author productivity analysis revealed that research output in both LLIF and PTP approaches is driven by a limited number of highly active contributors. For LLIF, the most prolific author produced 43 publications, with the top ten authors accounted for 39% of publications, indicating a moderate concentration of output within a defined group of experts. On the other hand, the most productive author in PTP contributed 14 publications, and the top ten authors represented 82% of all papers in this domain. Interestingly, overlap between leading LLIF and PTP authors was minimal, with only one individual appearing in both top-ten lists.

Geographic distribution and international collaboration
Country-level analysis reveals national patterns of research productivity and scientific leadership. For LLIF, the United States dominates with publications representing 45.6% of the global corpus, followed by Japan with 23.9%, China with 13.3%, Italy with 4.8%, and South Korea with 4.7%.
PTP research demonstrates marked geographic concentration, with the United States producing 244 publications (71.8% of total output), followed by Brazil (9.7%), Australia (6.5%), Singapore (2.1%), and Japan (1.8%).

Temporal analysis of geographic distribution reveals evolving patterns of international participation. Early LLIF research was predominantly concentrated in the United States between 2002 and 2010, with international contributions first emerging from China in 2010 (8 publications) and Italy in 2011 (2 publications). Japan entered the field in 2016 with 29 publications and rapidly expanded its output to 138 publications by 2019, to become the second leading contributor. This progression suggests an 8 to 10 years lag between the initial introduction of this technique and the significant international research activity.
For PTP, geographic distribution analysis demonstrates accelerated international expansion since its introduction in 2020. The initial publications originated exclusively from the United States (5 publications in 2020), followed by international participation beginning in 2021 with contributions from Brazil (11 publications) and expansion to Australia in 2022 (4 publications). By 2023, Japan produced its first PTP publications (6 publications), and by 2024 to 2025, Singapore, Egypt, Italy, and Thailand had published their first articles.
When analyzing international collaboration, clear patterns of knowledge exchange appear across countries. For LLIF, the United States maintains extensive partnerships with numerous countries, with the strongest connections to Japan, Australia, Korea, and China. This reflects established research collaborations and surgeon training pipelines. In addition, regional clusters appear in Europe, Asia, and South America, where geographic proximity and shared language facilitate cooperation. Similarly, PTP research shows the United States as the central hub for international collaboration. Meanwhile, Brazil functions as a secondary hub for South American research, maintaining substantial links with both the United States and regional partners, including Argentina and Chile. Finally, the emergence of Australia as a major collaboration partner suggests that Australian centers act as bridges between American innovation hubs and the broader Asia-Pacific region.

Keyword analysis and conceptual structure
Keyword co-occurrence analysis demonstrated distinct thematic patterns between the 2 techniques. In LLIF literature, the most frequent keywords included spinal fusion, lumbar vertebrae, and demographic descriptors such as female, male, aged, and middle-aged, along with terms related to retrospective studies and treatment outcomes.
In contrast, PTP literature used more technique-specific terms, like lateral lumbar interbody fusion, transpsoas, lordosis, complications, and single-position surgery. Temporal keyword mapping showed different patterns of thematic evolution between the 2 approaches. Early LLIF publications focused on general clinical and anatomical terms, while recent studies increasingly incorporated outcome-related keywords. Early PTP publications emphasized procedural terminology, and later research increased focus on outcomes and complications.

Discussion
This comprehensive bibliometric analysis highlights the evolving research landscapes of LLIF and PTP approaches. The large difference in publication volume, with LLIF producing about 7.7 times more papers, reflects its earlier introduction rather than differences in clinical importance or research quality. When considered at comparable stages of development, PTP research has grown much faster, with a 44% annual rate compared with 30% for LLIF. This rapid expansion shows strong research engagement and growing recognition of the technique’s potential to overcome limitations of the lateral decubitus workflow. Despite its recent introduction, PTP studies have accumulated citations rapidly, underscoring strong academic and clinical interest in single-position surgery.
These trends reflect a typical trajectory for new surgical techniques as they progress from early experimentation to broader adoption. Specifically, LLIF's publication trajectory shows a classic maturation pattern with an initial lag phase during technique development, followed by rapid growth as the technique gained widespread adoption, and finally stabilization as evidence became more comprehensive. The current plateau does not indicate declining interest but rather reflects literature maturity, where refinement and focused indications become priorities. The possibility that some surgeons are shifting toward PTP may contribute to this stabilization, though confirmation of this hypothesis would require analysis of surgical volume data beyond the scope of this study.
In contrast, PTP research shows faster progression through similar developmental phases. In comparison, PTP reached in 3 to 5 years what took LLIF 7 to 10 years. This suggests rapid adoption and intense research interest in establishing its safety profile, technical nuances, and comparative effectiveness.
Accordingly, the h-indices of 70 for LLIF and 17 for PTP confirm meaningful academic impact of both techniques, while the relatively close citation rates per publication indicate strong interest in PTP despite its young history.
Journal distribution analysis offers a clear view of the specialty specific adoption patterns and interdisciplinary dynamics of these 2 approaches. Our data identify World Neurosurgery as the leading publishing venue for both LLIF and PTP literature. This pattern suggests that the early development and dissemination of lateral access techniques has been strongly influenced by neurosurgery.
LLIF, with its 2 decade history, has achieved broad representation across multidisciplinary spine journals with substantial orthopedic representation, including Spine and European Spine Journal. In contrast, PTP research remains concentrated in neurosurgery specific and operative technique journals. This distribution indicates that early adoption of PTP is largely driven by neurosurgeons with expertise in single position approaches, prone access, and intraoperative neuromonitoring. As PTP literature matures and technique standardization progresses, we anticipate a widening of publication into orthopedic leaning and multidisciplinary spine journals, reflecting the trajectory observed for LLIF.
Interestingly, although overlap among leading authors is limited, the presence of some contributors across both techniques suggests continued refinement by experienced lateral access surgeons rather than a shift to entirely new research groups. This pattern reflects ongoing evolution within an established surgical approach.
Geographically, the United States remains the primary driver of research and innovation, supported by a strong academic infrastructure and historical development of lateral approaches. Growing contributions from Japan, South Korea, and China reflect increasing international expertise and adoption of minimally invasive spine surgery. LLIF research spans 42 countries, illustrating global adoption, whereas PTP contributions from 16 countries indicate limited but growing international uptake. The shorter delay between U.S. introduction and worldwide adoption of PTP (compared to the 8-to-10-year delay for LLIF) highlights the advantage of building upon an established access strategy rather than introducing a completely new concept.
Taken together, these results provide a comprehensive overview of how lateral access surgery continues to mature across both techniques. However, the interpretation of bibliometric patterns must consider inherent limitations.
First, although the search strategy and database selection were comprehensive, some relevant publications may have been missed. The inclusion of only English-language articles may have excluded valuable studies from non-English-speaking countries with strong spine surgery research programs. Even so, the predominance of English in medical publishing and the use of major international databases likely reduced the impact of this limitation.
Second, citation metrics, while informative, do not fully capture research quality or clinical significance. Highly cited papers may reflect controversy, early publication timing, or author prominence rather than superior scientific merit. Furthermore, industry relationships and financial conflicts of interest may influence both publication patterns and citation behavior. While systematic analysis of financial disclosures across 930 publications spanning 2 decades proved infeasible given inconsistent historical disclosure practices, we acknowledge this as an important consideration when interpreting citation patterns and research trajectories.
Third, keyword analysis depends on author-selected terminology, which may prioritize discoverability over precise thematic representation. Finally, the data cutoff may not include the most recent publications undergoing peer review.
Despite these considerations, the findings provide useful insight into technique adoption and development, which in turn informs the clinical implications for surgeons, training programs, and healthcare systems. For practicing spine surgeons, understanding the evolution of the evidence base for each technique supports informed approach selection. LLIF benefits from extensive long-term data and large cohort studies, which provide strong confidence in established indications.
In contrast, PTP literature remains less mature regarding long-term outcomes and rare complications. This warrants a cautious adoption guided by careful patient selection and continuous outcome monitoring. Additionally, as many surgeons are skilled in both positioning strategies, choice can be personalized to anatomical constraints, surgical goals, and efficiency considerations associated with single positioning.
For training programs, comprehensive lateral access education that includes both approaches ensures mastery of transpsoas anatomy, neuromonitoring, and interbody fusion principles, enabling future spine surgeons to adapt technique choice to individual scenarios.
This study also identifies critical knowledge gaps and priorities for future research. Currently, high-quality studies that provide a head-to-head comparison of both techniques remain sparse. In fact, most available comparisons rely on retrospective designs that are prone to selection bias. Therefore, future work should include prospective randomized trials or registry studies with standardized outcome measures. In addition, long-term PTP outcomes beyond 2 years, including fusion, revision, and adjacent segment degeneration rates, require further evaluation.
Moreover, economic analyses incorporating operative costs, anesthesia, complications and length of stay are needed to validate workflow efficiency claims.
Similarly, patient-centered outcomes, rare complications, learning curve analyses, and registry-based studies could inform training standards. Finally, further research on technological tools such as navigation, robotics, and advanced imaging could clarify their role in improving safety, accuracy, and efficiency across both positioning approaches.

Conclusion
This bibliometric analysis shows that LLIF is a mature technique supported by strong long term evidence, whereas the PTP approach is a rapidly developing method that builds upon established lateral access principles to address positioning limitations. PTP’s accelerated growth reflects modern surgical adoption patterns, in which refinements of known techniques spread faster than entirely novel procedures. Moreover, the strong overlap in contributing authors suggests that these approaches are complementary evolutions rather than competing alternatives. However, important knowledge gaps remain, including the need for high-quality comparative studies, long-term outcomes, economic evaluation, and patient-centered research. Addressing these priorities will support evidence-based, personalized use of lateral access strategies and help optimize outcomes in minimally invasive spine surgery.
