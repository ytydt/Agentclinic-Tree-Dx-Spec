A community-centered approach to psychedelics

Summary
The renewed interest in psychedelics for mental health treatment presents both promise and complexity. Substances like psilocybin and MDMA have shown potential in addressing conditions such as PTSD and depression, particularly when administered in supportive contexts. Yet as these therapies move toward mainstream adoption, concerns arise about rapid medicalization, corporate monopolization, and the possibility of replicating past public health failures. Drawing on structural lessons from the opioid and ketamine markets, this health policy review examines how regulatory frameworks, commercial incentives, and cultural narratives could shape the risks and benefits of psychedelic incorporation into therapeutic and community settings. Potential environmental harms and the marginalization of Indigenous knowledge systems further complicate the landscape. In response, this paper outlines a Community-Centered Approach to Psychedelics (C-CAP), a flexible policy framework that emphasizes pluralism, participatory governance, and culturally grounded harm reduction. By prioritizing equity, sustainability, and shared decision-making, C-CAP offers an alternative to both prohibition and corporate dominance, aiming to support safe and inclusive psychedelic practices within and beyond clinical settings.
Dubbed the “psychedelic renaissance,” the renewed interest in psychedelics represents a significant shift in how mental health treatment is being imagined—offering possible alternatives to conventional therapies. Classic psychedelics like psilocybin, DMT, and LSD, along with non-classic substances like MDMA, have demonstrated potential in addressing conditions such as PTSD and depression, particularly when administered in supportive settings (Radakovic et al.). For many individuals with treatment-resistant symptoms, preliminary findings suggest these substances may open the door to meaningful short-term relief—typically lasting from several weeks to a few months—before tapering in effect, with results that remain mixed over time (Reiff et al.; Goodwin et al.). For instance, Bogenschutz et al. found that while psilocybin reduced heavy drinking in alcohol use disorder, effects varied and questions remain about long-term efficacy across populations.
While early results are promising, they must be interpreted with caution. Psychedelics are not miracle cures; they present real risks, especially when implemented at scale or in unregulated environments. Recent regulatory decisions and scientific developments reflect this tension. The FDA’s rejection of MDMA-assisted therapy by Lykos Therapeutics—while seen by some as a setback—was viewed by others as a necessary check on premature enthusiasm (Stone). Simultaneously, the retraction of three influential studies in Psychopharmacology (Mahase) has contributed to a more tempered outlook. Rather than derailing the field, these events serve as reminders of the importance of rigor, transparency, and long-term evaluation.
In parallel, concerns about adverse effects are resurfacing, particularly as public interest outpaces regulation. Conditions like Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD) may affect up to 4% of users, and lingering symptoms—including paranoia, physical discomfort, and cognitive disruption—are reported by a substantial subset of participants (Evans et al.). These risks are often overshadowed in popular media narratives and public discourse, where the therapeutic potential of psychedelics is amplified—a dynamic sometimes referred to as the “Pollan Effect” (Noorani; Aday et al.). While compelling, such narratives risk overlooking unresolved safety questions, differential effects across populations, and long-term outcomes that remain underexplored.
As interest in psychedelics intensifies, the U.S. finds itself at a critical juncture. Central to this moment are two long-standing dynamics: American individualism, which tends to prioritize personal solutions over systemic reform, and overmedicalization, which reframes structural problems as private dysfunctions in need of pharmaceutical intervention (Phelan, Link, and Tehranifar; Spencer, Garnett, and Miniño). Whether psychedelics deepen this pattern or disrupt it will depend not just on clinical evidence, but on the broader cultural, corporate, and policy frameworks through which they are introduced. Although psychedelics differ pharmacologically and historically from substances like opioids and ketamine, these prior cases offer structural lessons—not predictions—about how cultural logics and market incentives can shape treatment landscapes. The goal is not to equate these substances, but to illustrate how systemic forces can influence access, oversight, and the emergence of unintended consequences. These dynamics underscore the need for governance models that resist both commercial exploitation and medical overreach. While important frameworks like the Nagoya Protocol focus on bioprospecting protections, and critiques like Psychedelic Capitalism warn of corporate enclosure (Secretariat of the Convention on Biological Diversity; Brownlee and Walby), both stop short of addressing how psychedelic access should be structured at the community level, or localized groups with shared cultural, geographic, or experiential ties. This paper builds on their insights to propose a more grounded, systems-oriented approach.

The profit motive and the risks of synthetics and extracts
To fully assess the risks of psychedelics, we must look beyond clinical promise and examine the legal and commercial landscapes in which these substances are being introduced. One central concern is the role of corporate actors—particularly pharmaceutical companies—in shaping access, marketing, and use. This worry is not speculative; historical analogies offer sobering context. The opioid epidemic—driven by a multifaceted set of structural, cultural, and clinical factors—nonetheless remains a cautionary tale. While opioids were initially approved for severe pain management, aggressive pharmaceutical marketing in the U.S. not only increased prescribing but also reshaped cultural understandings of pain, normalizing long-term opioid use across diverse clinical contexts. These campaigns worked both directly—by targeting physicians—and indirectly, by influencing patient expectations, public health discourse, and institutional norms (Hadland et al.; Van Zee). Though not the sole cause of the crisis, this marketing significantly contributed to its escalation (Spencer, Garnett, and Miniño). While psychedelics differ in both pharmacology and addiction risk, this example illustrates how commercial incentives—if left unchecked—can redirect therapeutic practices in harmful ways. Brownlee and Walby argues in Psychedelic Capitalism, argue that these patterns reflect a deeper tendency to privatize healing—transforming communal or Indigenous knowledge and practices into exclusive, profit-driven commodities. They show how corporate control over psychedelic access mirrors broader extractive systems aimed at ownership and marketization of traditionally public or sacred resources.
A more contemporary and closely aligned example may be racemic ketamine. Originally approved as an anesthetic, it is now widely used off-label for mental health treatment, particularly for depression, PTSD, and suicidal ideation (Grunebaum et al.; Stuart et al.). A 2023 inventory found that most outpatient ketamine clinics offer intravenous, intramuscular, or lozenge forms of ketamine to treat mood and anxiety disorders, often without long-term monitoring or psychiatric supervision (Pacilio et al.; Stuart et al.). While ketamine’s dissociative effects may partially underlie its antidepressant and anti-suicidal properties (Shinohara et al.), these same effects—particularly when delivered in high doses or poorly monitored settings—have been associated with adverse outcomes including dissociation, psychosis, and neurotoxicity (Fan et al.; Strous et al.; Short et al.). The expansion of clinics operating outside insurance networks, often under cash-pay models, raises ethical and clinical concerns about commercialization and the mismatch between financial incentives and patient safeguards (Schatzberg; Harding). This case suggests how financial structures and limited oversight may shape risk landscapes.
MDMA may be poised for a similar trajectory. Though not yet approved for therapeutic use, MDMA is frequently discussed as a future “blockbuster drug.” As a synthetic compound, MDMA differs from classic plant-based psychedelics like psilocybin or ayahuasca in both production and profile. However, the synthetic vs. natural distinction is not a simple safety binary: LSD, for example, is synthetic and generally considered to have a favorable safety profile. Conversely, MDMA has been associated with serious adverse events in both clinical and recreational contexts. Between 2005 and 2011, emergency department visits related to MDMA increased by approximately 100% in the U.S., with symptoms such as hyperthermia, dehydration, and serotonin syndrome frequently observed (SAMHSA). These risks are often exacerbated in unsupervised, high-stimulation environments like clubs and festivals, where MDMA use is frequently mixed with alcohol or other substances. Here again, the analogy is not about inevitability, but about how setting, regulation, and commercialization can interact with pharmacological risks.
As psychedelics gain cultural legitimacy and legal traction, the stakes increase. For example, synthetic THC vaping products proliferated following marijuana decriminalization, leading to over 2,800 lung injury cases by early 2020—an outcome tied not to cannabis itself, but to unregulated, profit-driven innovation in product delivery (Navon, Ghinnai, and Layden). Similarly, synthetic psilocybin edibles (e.g., gummies) have been linked to a tripling in poison control calls among adolescents between 2018 and 2022 (Stone; Farah et al.). These episodes reflect how gaps in oversight—not the underlying compound alone—may contribute to public health harm. They underscore what could happen when speed and scale override community input, long-term safety, and public health alignment. If psychedelics follow a similar path—not by virtue of the molecules themselves, but through the structural conditions of commercialization—then even substances with low addiction potential may still contribute to unintended harms.

Overmedicalization and American cultural logics
At the heart of psychedelic overmedicalization is a convergence of cultural values and structural forces—most notably, American individualism, the corporatization of healthcare, and the systemic neglect of public infrastructure. U.S. culture often elevates personal responsibility over collective solutions, encouraging medicalized fixes for problems rooted in inequality, trauma, and chronic stress (Phelan, Link, and Tehranifar). But this isn’t just cultural—it’s institutional. Market-driven healthcare systems, fragmented mental health services, and for-profit treatment models all push interventions toward quick fixes that can be sold, prescribed, and scaled.
Psychedelics now face the possibility of being pulled into this same logic. Though their mechanisms and histories differ from past treatments, they confront a familiar institutional terrain. Rather than addressing upstream causes like poverty or racialized trauma, American health systems often reach for individual-level solutions. Consider the explosion in weight-loss medications and adolescent bariatric surgeries—over 1.2 million children were prescribed weight-related drugs in 2020, and surgery has been approved for teens as young as 13 (Lee et al.; Messiah et al.). In mental health, more than six million children received antidepressants in 2021, despite ongoing concerns about increased suicide risk among adolescents (Zablotsky and Ng; Boaden et al.). Yet these highly individualized interventions remain inaccessible to many low-income and minoritized communities—not only due to cost and geographic disparities, but also because U.S. health systems prioritize downstream treatment over upstream investment in equitable care infrastructure(Gondi and Chokshi; Himmelstein et al.). These patterns do not imply that psychedelics will follow the same path, but they offer cautionary analogies about how U.S. health systems could absorb new treatments into existing inequities. The diagnosis of dysfunction has expanded, but the social roots of distress remain largely untouched.
Even therapy—often framed as the alternative to overmedicalization—can mirror these same tendencies. McKay and Jensen-Doss show that certain therapeutic models may unintentionally reinforce feelings of fragility, especially when focused narrowly on trauma. Shrier, in Bad Therapy, controversially argues that therapy culture sometimes cultivates dependence rather than resilience. Whether or not one agrees, the critique reveals broader concerns: that healing is increasingly being routed through highly individualized, pathologizing lenses.
This landscape is further shaped by social media. On platforms like Instagram and TikTok, mental health content circulates alongside ads and influencer endorsements, often blurring the line between education and marketing. The U.S. Surgeon General has warned that teens who spend more than three hours per day on social media are twice as likely to report depression or anxiety, with girls especially vulnerable to body image distress and disrupted sleep. Meanwhile, influencer-promoted psychedelics are beginning to gain traction, backed in some cases by pharmaceutical companies (Willis and Delbaere 2022; Oversight Board (Meta)). A peer-reviewed study of WEGO Health’s campaigns found that 51% of patients report trusting healthcare “patient influencers”—substantially more than lifestyle influencers, at just 14%—highlighting how influencer marketing is increasingly leveraged to shape public trust in pharmaceuticals (Willis and Delbaere). Other research highlights that health influencers often act as intermediaries in complex online health ecosystems, amplifying content with commercial or aesthetic appeal rather than grounded in clinical evidence (Zou, Zhang, and Tang; O’Brien, Ganjigunta, and Dhillon).These dynamics underscore the need for deeper scrutiny of how trust, aesthetics, and algorithmic authority could shape emerging health behaviors in the psychedelic space.
In short, psychedelics are entering a mental health ecosystem already shaped by overdiagnosis, commercialization, and algorithmic influence. Yet a parallel current exists. As Foti shows in her ethnography of community science, patients and providers are beginning to imagine new models of healing—rooted not in institutional medicine, but in collective care, spiritual practice, and grassroots ethics. Absent, too, is any integration of benefit-sharing models like those outlined in the Nagoya Protocol, which emphasize reciprocity, environmental sustainability, and Indigenous governance. Whether psychedelics reinforce dominant medical systems or help build something different will likely depend on how they are regulated, marketed, and made accessible—and who gets to shape that process.

The case for Community-Centered approach to psychedelics
As psychedelics move from underground use to public policy, the central question becomes: How should they be regulated—and by whom? Rather than defaulting to medical monopolies or purely market-driven systems, this paper proposes a Community-Centered Approach to Psychedelics (C-CAP)—a model grounded in equity, cultural responsiveness, environmental ethics, and democratic participation. While legal frameworks like the Nagoya Protocol offer critical upstream protections—such as ensuring prior informed consent (Secretariat of the Convention on Biological Diversity), access and benefit-sharing (ABS), and preservation of traditional knowledge—they stop short of addressing how psychedelics are delivered, integrated into care, or governed on the ground. Similarly, critiques of psychedelic capitalism (Brownlee and Walby) have illuminated the potential dangers of pharmaceutical monopolization, extraction of Indigenous knowledge, and IP-driven commercialization. But these critiques are largely diagnostic, while the C-CAP does not assume psychedelics will follow identical trajectories. Instead, it asks what governance models are necessary to avoid the possibility of repeating such patterns.
C-CAP builds on both of these contributions while moving beyond their limits. It is not a legal framework, nor is it simply a cultural critique. Instead, it is a prescriptive, bottom-up model designed to empower communities through localized governance, decentralized care structures, and harm-reduction-oriented delivery systems. It explicitly asks: What policies strengthen community infrastructure and cultural sovereignty—and what policies reproduce medical monopolies, exclusion, or ecological harm? In doing so, it shifts the conversation from ethics and ownership to implementation and equity. By centering not just what psychedelics are used but how they are accessed, integrated, and regulated, C-CAP offers a grounded, pragmatic alternative for reshaping psychedelic policy.
Scientific contributions like the REBUS hypothesis (Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics) and the Hopkins-Oxford Psychedelics (HOPE) Ethics Consensus (Villiger and Trachsel; Jacobs et al.) offer essential insights into how psychedelics work and how they should be ethically administered. REBUS helps explain how psychedelics may relax entrenched cognitive priors, while HOPE outlines principles of informed consent, trauma-informed care, and cultural sensitivity. While often discussed in therapeutic contexts, recent work by Nardou et al. suggests that psychedelics might reopen critical periods for social reward learning—even outside formal clinical settings—raising the possibility that REBUS may also help explain mechanisms relevant to relational, communal, and informal uses. These insights, while preliminary outside clinical trials, offer valuable hypotheses for understanding non-medical outcomes. However, these frameworks are largely confined to clinical settings. They are not policy blueprints. C-CAP takes these ethical commitments further by applying them at the level of service delivery—embedding informed consent, cultural responsiveness, and integration into public and community health systems.
For centuries, Indigenous communities have used psychedelics in ceremonial, relational, and collective settings—often with minimal documented harm and a strong emphasis on integration, social cohesion, and ecological respect (Fotiou). While caution is needed to avoid romanticizing or generalizing these traditions, their longevity and embeddedness offer important lessons. Many users outside of clinical settings also report building safe and effective knowledge through informal networks, peer guidance, and embodied experience (Kruger et al.). Studies even raise the possibility that some psychedelics—like psilocybin—may offer therapeutic benefits without requiring professional psychotherapy (Goodwin et al.). These realities demonstrate that effective psychedelic use is not confined to licensed medical spaces. C-CAP recognizes and legitimizes these alternative forms of expertise, integrating them into a pluralistic model of care. Rather than theorizing ideal conditions, C-CAP builds on existing grassroots practices and hybrid systems already emerging in state-level reform and underground care networks.
Still, the C-CAP model resists false dichotomies between “natural” and “synthetic.” Lab-produced psychedelics like synthetic psilocybin offer significant advantages: precision in dosage, scalability, and reduced ecological harm, especially for species like peyote or the Incilius alvarius toad, which are increasingly threatened by overharvesting (Muneta; Meyer; Romero). Indigenous perspectives on synthetics are not uniform—some communities reject them entirely, while others adapt and reinterpret them within evolving traditions (Celidwen et al.). A community-centered approach therefore calls not for a singular path, but for pluralism: systems that allow for both natural and synthetic forms, regulated equitably and shaped by diverse values. Unlike top-down pharmaceutical regulation, which often imposes standardized delivery across populations, C-CAP embraces culturally flexible and community-specified approaches—recognizing that safety and healing are contextually defined. By drawing on—but not reducing itself to—precedent cases and community traditions, C-CAP offers a model that is informed by history, attuned to context, and open to iterative refinement.
Environmental ethics must also be central. Illegal harvesting on Indigenous lands, especially of slow-growing plants like peyote, threatens both ecosystems and sacred practices. The Western demand for ayahuasca has contributed to Amazonian deforestation, and the use of Incilius alvarius in psychedelic ceremonies has endangered frog population’s. While international agreements like the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and the Nagoya Protocol have brought global attention to issues of access and benefit-sharing, they are limited in scope. Their enforcement mechanisms remain weak, and they focus heavily on bioprospecting, leaving downstream issues—such as distribution, cultural sovereignty, and ethical commercialization—largely unaddressed. Partnering with Indigenous and local communities to promote sustainable cultivation, protect land rights, and educate consumers is not just ethical—it is essential. At the same time, it is important to avoid idealizing Indigenous communities as uniformly conservationist. Like all societies, Indigenous groups are internally diverse, shaped by a range of economic, political, and cultural realities. Ethical engagement should be guided by international frameworks such as the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and the Nagoya Protocol, which emphasize reciprocity, benefit-sharing, and co-governance (Secretariat of the Convention on Biological Diversity). C-CAP therefore aligns with the spirit of Nagoya but extends its application: rather than narrowly regulating genetic resources, it creates space for community-driven governance and participatory implementation, grounded in reciprocity but focused on policy infrastructure.

From principle to practice: operationalizing C-CAP
How, then, can the Community-Centered Approach to Psychedelics (C-CAP) be operationalized? As a normative framework still in development, C-CAP resists rigid blueprints or universal protocols. Its strength lies in adaptability: rather than prescribing what communities must do, it begins with the question of what they need. In some contexts, psychedelics may offer meaningful tools for healing; in others, they may be unnecessary, unsafe, or culturally incompatible. The process starts not with the molecule, but with the community—its legal landscape, historical context, health infrastructure, and collective priorities. C-CAP invites practitioners, policymakers, and local actors to co-create systems that assess whether and how psychedelics can be integrated ethically. Where appropriate, it encourages potential policy tools that reflect this pluralism: advisory councils composed of community stakeholders, legal carve-outs for traditional practices, cooperative licensing and funding models, and participatory evaluation structures that elevate lived experience. These mechanisms are not universal requirements, but illustrative pathways—provisional tools that must be adapted to context, and in some cases, may not be necessary at all. The goal is not to impose structure, but to invite iteration, accountability, and self-determination as core design principles.
To translate these values into practice, C-CAP proposes a flexible, community-led assessment pathway. Rather than assuming psychedelics are beneficial, this participatory decision-making process begins by asking whether they are needed at all—and proceeds step-by-step toward ethical integration only where appropriate. Critically, the above pathway is not a rigid checklist, and is not intended to be a specific guide, rather C-CAP can be viewed as iterative tool. Communities may adapt or expand its steps to reflect their specific needs, values, and historical experiences. In this way, C-CAP moves beyond access to psychedelics as a goal, and instead foregrounds intentionality: when, how, and whether these substances should play a role in community health and healing.
One example that loosely reflects C-CAP’s values is Switzerland’s heroin-assisted treatment (HAT) model, which emerged through close collaboration between civil society organizations (CSOs) and public health authorities (Liebrenz et al.). These programs offered regulated access to heroin for individuals with severe opioid dependence—grounded in harm reduction, social integration, and community trust. While the substances and social context differ, the design logic—community engagement, adaptive oversight, and non-punitive access—offers instructive parallels. In C-CAP terms, this reflects what ethical access can look like when shaped through local need and stakeholder participation rather than imposed from above. The lesson is not to replicate HAT, but to recognize how community-governed delivery systems can expand care options without defaulting to commercial or institutional control.
Another model that echoes the participatory spirit of C-CAP is the Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement (PPIE) framework, exemplified by the UK’s TLC (Therapies for Long COVID) project (Aiyegbusi et al.). In this initiative, patients were not just study subjects but co-developers—shaping research design, interpretation, and dissemination. While Long COVID and psychedelic policy differ in scope, the procedural ethic is similar: knowledge and implementation are strongest when co-produced with those most affected. Applied to C-CAP, this reinforces the stepwise commitment to inclusive engagement and lived experience as foundational inputs—not afterthoughts—in guiding decisions about whether and how psychedelics should be introduced into community health strategies.
Emerging models in the U.S. already reflect many of the principles embedded in the C-CAP decision-making framework. For example, Colorado and Washington have adopted decriminalization and home cultivation policies that allow individuals to grow psilocybin mushrooms—a policy that inherently shifts control to local actors and reduces dependence on commercial supply chains (Fotiou). Similarly, Cannabis Social Clubs (CSCs) in Europe provide insight into how closed, community-based networks can regulate substance use while minimizing commercialization and youth exposure activity (Decorte et al.). Informal analogues are visible in U.S. contexts as well: Jewish communities in New York have integrated psilocybin into ritual to address intergenerational trauma, while “psychedelic mommies” in Colorado share cultivation and harm-reduction knowledge within trusted peer circles (Wilensky; Sherry). These examples are not endorsements or templates, but case studies that illustrate how community-defined needs, values, and safety structures can shape psychedelic practice—mirroring the iterative, contextual logic outlined in C-CAP’s operational framework.
Ultimately, C-CAP envisions a policy future grounded not in universality, but in contextual integrity. Just as its framework begins by asking who the community is and whether psychedelics are even appropriate, its long-term vision resists governance by lobbyists, venture capital, or elite institutions alone. Instead, it centers governance by those most affected—patients, practitioners, Indigenous leaders, civil society, and everyday users. This model does not prescribe fixed outcomes; it invites communities to define safety, shape access, and determine relevance. If psychedelic capitalism asks who profits, and the Nagoya Protocol asks who owns the source, then C-CAP adds a critical layer: who governs care, who decides if psychedelics are needed, and how can collective agency be built into every step of the process?

Conclusion: where do we go from here?
The psychedelic renaissance marks a moment of extraordinary potential—and profound uncertainty. Psychedelics may offer genuine relief for conditions like PTSD and depression, particularly among those underserved by traditional treatments. Yet therapeutic promise alone is not enough. What unfolds from this moment will depend not only on the molecules themselves, but on the systems that deliver them: the cultural norms, economic structures, and policy choices that shape who has access, under what conditions, and to what end. Corporate infrastructure can bring important benefits—standardized dosing, rigorous trials, and broader distribution. But as the histories of opioids and ketamine clinics reveal, profit-driven models often reproduce harm, especially when oversight is weak and equity is secondary. Psychedelics are not exempt. Without clear guardrails, they risk deepening disparities, reinforcing exclusion, and prioritizing market expansion over community well-being.
Critics of decentralized, community-led approaches raise legitimate concerns—and they are not without merit. Community settings can carry real risks: trauma survivors may be retraumatized by untrained guides; peer-led integration circles may lack safeguards for coercion or exploitation; and informal networks often struggle to provide follow-up care or accountability mechanisms. Empirical studies on underground and nonclinical use show that while many participants report benefits, others experience harm—especially in the absence of trusted relationships or accessible support systems (He et al.; Noorani). These are not reasons to abandon community-led approaches, but reminders that ethical intention must be matched with thoughtful design. A truly community-centered model does not assume inherent safety—it builds it through cultural humility, structured decision processes, and iterative safeguards. C-CAP, in this sense, is not anti-institutional; it is context-responsive and ethically grounded. It seeks to rebalance power in how psychedelic care is imagined and implemented, without assuming that decentralization alone guarantees justice or well-being.
This paper contributes in two key ways. Theoretically, it foregrounds how structural forces—corporate interests, regulatory frameworks, cultural logics—shape not just access to psychedelics but the meaning of their use. Practically, it proposes a flexible decision-making pathway and highlights real-world models aligned with C-CAP: from home cultivation in Colorado to Cannabis Social Clubs and underground integration circles. These alternatives illustrate that psychedelics can be used ethically, safely, and collectively—when their adoption is grounded in local priorities, equitable design, and community-led assessment. Whether psychedelics become tools of healing or instruments of inequality will not be decided in laboratories alone. That outcome will be shaped by public imagination, political will, and the frameworks we choose to build. A community-centered approach asks not just what psychedelics can do, but who they serve, who decides, and what kind of future they make possible. The psychedelic renaissance is not a revolution in waiting—it is a test of our priorities. The path forward is not guaranteed, but it remains ours to choose.
