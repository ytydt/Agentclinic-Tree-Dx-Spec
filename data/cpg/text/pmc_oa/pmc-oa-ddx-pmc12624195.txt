Morpho‐Histo‐Cytological Evaluation of Oral Tissues in Burning Mouth Syndrome: A Systematic Review
ABSTRACT
Background
Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS) is an idiopathic condition characterised by chronic oral burning pain without clinically evident lesions. Despite its prevalence and impact on quality of life, the pathophysiology of BMS remains poorly understood, limiting diagnostic and therapeutic options.
Objective
To systematically review histological, morphological and cytological changes in oral tissues of BMS patients, with a focus on epithelial cells and nerve fibres, to identify potential biomarkers and inform future research directions.
Methods
A systematic search of electronic databases and grey literature was conducted through October 2024, identifying case–control studies assessing histological or cytological features of oral tissues in adults diagnosed with BMS according to validated criteria. Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute tool, and evidence quality was evaluated with GRADE.
Results
Fourteen studies involving 214 BMS patients and 130 controls were included. Findings consistently reported reduced epithelial nerve fibre density and increased expression of nociceptive markers such as TRPV1, NGF and P2X3 in BMS tissues. Subepithelial alterations, including increased keratinisation and altered expression of cannabinoid receptors (CB1/CB2), were also observed. Methodological heterogeneity and risk of bias were notable, precluding meta‐analysis. The overall quality of evidence was very low.
Conclusion
Histological evidence supports neuropathic changes in BMS, involving both neuronal and non‐neuronal cells. While these biomarkers hold promise for understanding BMS pathogenesis, standardisation of techniques and further research, including development of animal models, is needed to establish diagnostic and therapeutic utility.
This systematic review on Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS) included 14 studies that examined histological changes. 12 studies used incisional biopsies and 2 used exfoliative cytology. Histology and molecular biology techniques were used to assess morphological, phenotypical and genomic changes in oral tissues, primarily from the tongue. The main outcomes were the intraepithelial nerve fiber density, the expression of biomarkers such as receptors (TRPV1, P2X, NGF, CB1, CB2…), ion channels (NaV1.7, NaV 1.8…), enzymes (pepsin, tryptase, caspase, PGP9.5…), Interleukins (IL‐17), neuropeptides (SubstanceP) and constituants such as keratins (K10, K16), Desmogleins (Dsg1‐3), Laminin, b‐Tubulin …), S100 protein etc. present on neuronal and non neuronal cells. These analyses reflect trophic and functional changes related to the pathophysiology of BMS.

Introduction
Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS), also referred to as stomatodynia or glossodynia when limited to the tongue, is an idiopathic disorder characterised by unexplained oral burning pain. According to the 3rd edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD3), BMS is defined as ‘an intraoral burning or dysaesthetic sensation, recurring daily for more than 2 hours/day over more than 3 months, without clinically evident causative lesions’. The International Classification of Orofacial Pain (ICOP) further classifies BMS as an idiopathic pain adding ‘with or without somatosensory changes’ to the definition . Differential diagnosis includes secondary BMS for which a cause is found. BMS affects approximately 1% of the general population worldwide, with prevalence increasing with age, particularly among post‐menopausal women. The condition is largely associated with comorbidities, among them anxio‐depressive conditions are the most common. The condition significantly impairs patient's quality of life and current treatments, either topical or systemic remain largely unsatisfactory. The development of new therapeutic strategies is hindered by the poorly understood etiopathogenesis.
Diagnosis of BMS is primarily clinical, based on patient history and physical examination, often supported by specific questionnaires. Ongoing debates persist regarding the establishment of internationally validated diagnostic criteria, with some authors even proposing a nomenclature change to ‘Burning Mouth Disorder’ to better reflect the idiopathic and heterogeneous nature of its presentation. Differential diagnoses must rule out secondary forms of BMS caused by nutritional deficiencies, hormonal imbalances or systemic diseases.
In this context, identifying reliable biomarkers for BMS diagnosis and management would be an important step in understanding the disease. Several approaches have been explored, including brain imaging, quantitative sensory testing (QST) and liquid biopsies that is, blood or salivary tests. However, each of these methods has limitations. Advanced imaging techniques, such as functional MRI, have provided valuable insights into central nervous system alterations such as higher fractional anisotropy and intracellular volume fraction in BMS but remain impractical for routine clinical use. QST results are controversial, as BMS patients may present with or without somatosensory changes, complicating interpretation. Liquid biopsy research has investigated various molecular biomarkers, such as neurotrophic factors, cytokines, peptides and protein hormones, in saliva or blood, but results remain inconclusive. Recent metabolomic data on saliva point towards alterations in tyrosine pathways, echoing the hypothesis of neuronal dopamine function involvement. Other hypotheses such as impaired neuronal function reflecting in levels of alpha‐enolase, IL18 and KLK13 need confirmation.
Histological analysis of oral tissue biopsies enables the direct observation of potential structural and cellular alterations. Such approaches have been used to investigate changes in salivary glands or epithelium morphology and cytology. Lauritano et al. 1998 followed by Lauria et al. 2005 made significant progress in the field by applying immunohistochemistry to assess neural alterations, reporting a reduction in the density of epithelial and subepithelial nerve fibres in the tongue. Based on these findings, they proposed that BMS may represent a subgroup of small‐fibre neuropathies. Subsequent studies confirmed these neural alterations and identified additional biomarkers such as TRPV1, P2X3, NGF and Artemin. Morphological changes—especially those affecting peripheral nerves and epithelial tissues—have consistently been observed in BMS patients, supporting the hypothesis of underlying neuropathic and trophic abnormalities. Surgical biopsies are the most commonly used techniques for assessing biological changes in the oral tissues. However, alternative methods, such as exfoliative cytology, where cells are sampled by scraping the oral mucosa, can also provide key information, especially when combined with morphological or genetic (qPCR) analysis. These observations are important since they are the keystones for clinical identification and for developing an animal model of BMS that accurately reproduces human clinical evidence. Today there is no satisfying animal model of BMS available.
The aim of this study was therefore to systematically review the histological evidence in oral tissues of BMS patients, identify potential disease biomarkers, and provide insights for future research. Both general architecture, morphology and cytological aspects were analysed, with a special focus on epithelial cells and nerve fibres.

Methods

Protocol and Registration
This systematic review conformed to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses PRISMA Checklist. The protocol was registered in the international Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under number CRD42025638793.

Eligibility Criteria
We included case–control studies that evaluated the morphological and/or histological characteristics of adults with BMS. We included studies that diagnosed BMS with validated criteria, such as clinical examinations or with international criteria, such as ICHD‐3, ICOP or IASP which state that BMS is an intraoral burning or dysaesthetic sensation, recurring daily for more than 2 h/day over more than 3 months, without clinically evident causative lesions. Morphological and histological characteristics should be assessed through tongue or oral biopsy or exfoliation. Overall, the inclusion criteria were based on the PECOS methodology: Population (P): Adults; Exposure (E): BMS; Comparison (C): Healthy Subjects or other comparison group without BMS; Outcome (O): Morphological and histological characteristics; Studies (S): Case–Control. No data, sex or language restrictions were applied to the search strategy. In this review, studies could assess morphological and histological characteristics of oral tissues using surgical biopsies or exfoliative cytology. Reported histological methods could include haematoxylin–eosin staining, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence targeting neuronal markers (e.g., PGP9.5, β‐tubulin, S100), epithelial proteins (e.g., keratins 10, 14, 16; desmogleins) and receptors/ion channels (e.g., TRPV1, CB1, CB2, NGF, P2X3, Nav1.7–1.9). Cytological approaches could comprise cytomorphometric and cytomorphological analyses, as well as molecular assays such as qPCR for specific gene expression (e.g., Nav channels, Artn).
The exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) Studies in animals; (2) Studies with burning mouth as a symptom of other diseases or secondary BMS; (3) Studies with BMS and comorbidities associated or no control group; (4) Studies where BMS was not evaluated thought a valid diagnostic criteria; (5) Not retrieved; (6) Studies where the morphological and histological were not evaluated through biopsies; (7) Literature reviews, intervention studies, letters, case reports (< 10 cases) and personal opinions.
Patients with comorbidities or secondary BMS were excluded when attributable to another diagnosed condition. Secondary conditions included nutritional deficiencies, hormonal disorders, systemic diseases, local oral pathologies or other medical conditions known to cause oral burning (e.g., oral candidiasis, lichen planus, Sjögren's syndrome, diabetes mellitus). Studies including BMS with associated comorbidities were also excluded.

Information Sources and Search
Detailed individual search strategies were developed, in English, for each bibliographic electronic database: EMBASE, PubMed (Including Medline), Scopus and Web of Science. A grey literature search was performed on Google Scholar and Open Grey. All database searches were conducted from the starting coverage date through October 21st, 2024. More information on the search strategies was provided in Table S1 (which can be found online). Furthermore, the authors hand‐searched the reference lists of the selected articles for any additional references that might have been missed in the database searches. All references were managed, and the duplicated hits were removed by reference management software (EndNote X7 Basic‐Thomson Reuters, New York, USA).

Study Selection and Data Collection Process
Study selection followed a two‐phase process. In phase one, two authors (A.L.P. and A.G.D.S) independently evaluated the titles and abstracts of all identified electronic database citations. In phase two, the same authors evaluated full‐text data. They independently screened papers in phases one and two, applied the eligibility criteria, collected key information from the selected studies and cross‐checked the information. The selection from phase two was based solely on the full‐text assessment of the studies. When disagreement appeared, a third author (Y.B.) was involved to make a final decision about the inclusion or exclusion of studies.
For each of the included studies, the following items were recorded: author(s), year of publication, country, sample size (n), population information, mean age and standard deviation or range, percentage of females; characteristics of BMS group; characteristics of control group, type of biopsy and method; criteria for evaluating and diagnosing BMS; main results on BMS and biopsies; and conclusions of the work. When the required data were not complete, the reviewers (A.L.P. and A.G.D.S) attempted to contact the study authors to retrieve any unpublished information. Three attempts were made in a 30‐day period, by email for the first, second and last author.

Risk of Bias in Individual Studies
The methodological quality of the included observational studies were evaluated through Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools to assessed risk of bias in case–control studies. The answers could be ‘yes’ ‘unclear’ ‘no’ or ‘not applicable’ Decisions about scoring were agreed upon by all reviewers before beginning critical appraisal. The same two reviewers (A.L.P. and A.G.D.S) worked out any differences regarding data analysis. A third author (Y.B.) was involved to steer decisions in case of uncertainty. Following these answers, the risk of bias was categorised as follows: (1) Low risk of bias, if all criteria were met, (2) Unclear risk of bias, if one or more criteria were not exactly described and (3) High risk of bias, if one or more criteria were not met.

Study Outcomes and Summary Measures
We considered the results from morphological and histological evidence in oral tissues of BMS.

Synthesis of Results
Statistical pooling of data using meta‐analysis was planned whenever trials were considered combinable and relatively homogeneous in relation to design, interventions and outcomes. Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated either by considering clinical (differences about populations and results), methodological (design, and risk of bias) and statistical characteristics (effect of studies) or by using inconsistency indexes (I 2).

Confidence in Cumulative Evidence
A summary of the overall certainty of evidence available was presented using ‘Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation’ (GRADE) Summary of Findings (SoF) tables, using GRADEpro software.

Results

Study Selection
The initial database search identified 2011 studies. After removing duplicates, 1486 studies remained; 1446 of them were excluded after title and abstract revision, resulting in a final number of 46 articles. Furthermore, 200 studies were found with Google Scholar, and 58 with OpenGrey. Ten of them were selected for full‐text review. No additional study was selected from a search of the reference lists of the selected studies, and no studies were considered based on experts' suggestions. Consequently, 56 studies became part of phase 2. During this phase, 38 studies were excluded (reasons for exclusion may be found in Table S2). Fourteen studies were included for qualitative synthesis. A flowchart of the process of identification, inclusion and exclusion of studies is shown in Figure 1.

Study Characteristics and Results of Individual Studies
Out of the 14 studies, sample size ranged from 7 to to 56 BMS patients, and from 1 to to 20 controls; with a total of 214 BMS and 130 controls, mostly women, ranging from 50% to 100% of the sample. Mean age in the BMS group ranged between 57.1 to 67.5 years old; and from 40 to to 67.4 years old in the control group. Studies were conducted in Brazil, Denmark, Finland, Italy, Japan, Spain and UK. All studies were published in English.

Risk of Bias in Individual Studies
Risk of bias was heterogeneous among studies. Using JBI Critical Appraisal Tools, 6 studies were classified as unclear risk of bias, and 08 as high risk of bias. No study was classified as low risk of bias. The unclear risk of bias pertained to strategies to deal with confounding factors, as means of multi‐ or univariate regressions. Structural Epithelium Changes
Tissue architecture status has been evaluated in some studies with contrasting results. Histological alterations: Integrity of tongue mucosa remained unchanged in BMS as well as the oral mucosa.

Intraepidermal Nerve Fibre (IENF) Density
IENF was significantly reduced in BMS patients compared to controls in four studies; but remained unchanged in one. Density of innervation depended on the severity/location of symptoms in that is, not altered in ‘tip only’ BMS patients but decreased in those with extended symptomatology.

Specific Immunoreactivity
TRPV1, NGF and P2X3‐positive fibres were significantly elevated in BMS patients, correlating with pain levels. However, no significant changes were found in markers for other nerve fibres (e.g., Nav1.7, Nav1.9).

Molecular and Cellular Changes
Increased expression of TRPV1 and CB2, along with decreased CB1 expression, was reported in the tongue epithelial cells of BMS patients. Furthermore, enhanced mRNA expression for Artn was reported.
Cellular hypertrophy, increased keratinization and altered proteoglycan/collagen balance were observed in BMS cases . Keratinocyte cytoplasm was homogeneously labelled for keratin 16 in BMS patients, with a more intense staining than controls, without differences for Desmoglein 1 and 3, CK10, CK14.

Cytological Findings
An increased nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic area ratio was observed in BMS epithelial cells. BMS patients exhibited a predominance of intermediate epithelial cells compared to the more superficial cells in controls. Treldal et al. evidenced subgroups of BMS patients with signs of epithelial atrophy.
Increased expression of oestrogen receptors (Erα) was found in the oral mucosa in subgroups of BMS patients.

Gene Expression
No statistically significant difference in sodium channel gene expression (mRNA for Nav1.7, Nav1.8, Nav1.9) was found between BMS and controls. However, Artn mRNs was significantly increased in BMS compared to CTRL.

Inflammatory Changes
Mast cells Tryptase: Arantes et al. found no changes in mast cell density. Borsani et al. found a decreased CB1 and increased CB2 expression in tongue epithelial cells.

Synthesis of Results
The data from the included papers were very highly? heterogeneous, making a meta‐analysis infeasible. So, a qualitative description was performed. The main methodological issue pertained to strategies to deal with confounding factors such as age, sex and psychosocial status that were not always collected.

Confidence in Cumulative Evidence
The overall quality of evidence identified using GRADE's SoF tables was rated as very low (Table S4), because 8 of the 14 studies presented a high risk of bias, which downgraded one point; and the study design was labelled as observational studies (case–control), which downgraded two points.

Discussion
This systematic review included fourteen studies investigating morphological, histological and cytological alterations in oral tissues of patients with Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS). Twelve studies used surgical biopsies, while two employed exfoliative cytology. The findings provide evidence of cellular and molecular changes in epithelial cells and nerve fibres, distinguishing BMS patients from healthy controls.
These results align with those from recent reviews although previous analyses often focused on other diagnostic modalities such as quantitative sensory testing (QST) or neuroimaging and did not comprehensively address histological biomarkers. Furthermore, this review also provides technical insights into methodological variability, which may influence future research and clinical standardisation.

Interpretation of the Findings

Morphological and Cytologic Characteristics of the Oral Epithelium
Several studies sought to identify histological or cytological markers specific to BMS, based on the hypothesis that the oral mucosa may exhibit disease‐related alterations. For example, Wandeur et al., using exfoliative cytology, reported normal overall epithelial cell morphology but observed significant morphometric changes in BMS patients, including nuclear and cytoplasmic alterations suggestive of epithelial atrophy and disrupted cellular maturation. However, they did not explore how these changes might relate to pain perception. Jorgensen et al. (2010), in a preliminary study published as an abstract, found no signs of inflammation but observed reduced epithelial thickness and flattened epithelial ridges in the tongues of BMS patients, alongside diminished oestrogen receptor expression. A subsequent study by the same group examining the buccal mucosa reported similar findings: absence of inflammation in most cases, but epithelial atrophy in approximately 50% of patients, possibly associated with upregulation of oestrogen receptors.
Sardella et al. provided evidence of altered expression of keratin 16, a possible marker of epithelial cell injury or dysfunction, which was homogenous in the epithelial cells of BMS patients and not in CTRL subjects. Other epithelial markers (Desmogleins, cytokeratin K10 etc.) were not altered. However, the semi‐quantitative methods used in the study were not well described, limiting the reliability/reproducibility of these findings. Borsani et al. used immunohistochemistry to evaluate epithelial expression of specific biomarkers involved in pain and inflammation (receptors TRPV1, CB1 and CB2). They found increased expression of TRPV1 and CB2 and a decrease in CB1 receptors in BMS patients compared to CTRL subjects, suggesting that these receptors are involved in the physiopathology of BMS.
Overall, studies reported few gross architectural changes in oral tissues, suggesting that BMS is not associated with overt epithelial pathology. Nevertheless, immunohistochemical techniques have revealed subtle but consistent cellular alterations, particularly in pain‐ and inflammation‐related pathways.

Innervation

Total Innervation
Studies on BMS have focused so far on the density of nerve fibres in the epithelium and sub‐epithelium, using different neuronal markers. The most used markers were PGP9.5 (UCH‐L1) a homeostasis neuronal marker, beta‐tubulin 3, Neurofilament and peripherin, which label constituents of the cytoskeleton (respectively microtubules and neurofilaments). The Schwann cell marker S100 has also been used as a surrogate marker of innervation targeting Schwann cells surrounding neurons. Several studies have reported an overall decrease in Intraepidermal nerve fibres density (IENFD) in the tongue and oral tissues of BMS patients, in particular within epithelial plexuses, while the subepithelial plexus is not affected. This supports the hypothesis of a general or peripheral fibre neuropathy underlying BMS, as suggested by Lauria et al. and further supported by QST and electrophysiological findings. These authors found in a sample of 11 BMS patients (including 8 patients with symptoms localised at the tip of the tongue and 5 with extended symptoms) an overall decrease in IENFD, which has been confirmed in further studies and especially in a well‐controlled study with matched age sex subject and following guidelines and criteria for inclusion. However, a more complex and somehow contradictory picture seems to emerge from the work of Penza et al. who identified an overall difference in IENFD when examining their whole sample of 51 BMS patients compared to 9 control subjects from a previous study, but found differences according to the location of the pain: patients with ‘Tip only’ BMS had normal IENFD, whereas patients with ‘extended’ symptoms showed a decrease in IENFD. Despite methodological issues in the definition and comparison of the subgroups, this difference might reflect a clinical feature and raises the question of whether the location of the symptoms is related to a step of the disease or a different physio‐pathological process. In addition, one study by Arantes et al. found no change in nerve density in BMS patients compared with CTRL. Another finding of this review is the heterogeneity in the methods between the different studies to calculate IENFD, regarding the tissues examined. Except for Treldal et al. who examined another site of the oral mucosa, all the biopsies examined the anterior tongue surface, including epithelium and sub‐epithelium. However, the surface of the anterior tongue is covered with filiform papillae (FilP) and fungiform papillae (FunP) which have a different pattern and density of innervation and can both participate in painful sensations. Lauria (2005) and Penza (2010), for example, examined only the innervation of fungiform papillae, while others do not specify the presence of fungiform papillae refer to papillae only in the results section, without precising FunP or FilP. This can make a difference since the FunP display the highest density of nerve fibres in the tongue epithelium. In addition, for calculating IENFD, several authors refer to the method of Lauria et al. (European guidelines) for skin biopsy in order to normalise the procedure of counting and preparation, but it should be noticed that the skin does not contain papillae, either FunP nor FiP, making the method difficult to implement for the tongue surface.

Specific Nerve Labelling
While the total amount of nerve fibres measured as IENFD is an important measure, it does not indicate which type of nerve fibres are affected in BMS. Some studies looked at this qualitative aspect of innervation through the presence of neurotrophic factors conditioning nerve growth and homeostasis such as NGF and Artemin (Artn) and their receptors, or through ion channels (Nav1.7, Nav1.8, Nav1.9) known to be expressed in primary afferent nociceptors. Both NGF, the ligand of the NGF receptor TrkA and Artn, a ligand of the GDNF receptor were increased. NGF and GDNF control different neuronal subpopulations. Only Nav1.8 expression was significantly increased at the protein level but not at the gene level, when Nav1.7 and 1.9 were unchanged. Some receptors involved in pain and thermo‐sensation were searched: TRPV1, a receptor activated by heat, acidity and inflammatory mediators such as oxidised lipids and external ligands such as capsaicin, was found to be increased in nerves of BMS patients, as was the pronociceptive neurotrophin NGF, compatible with the burning sensation associated to TRPV1 activation. This is especially interesting since a correlation has been found with the expression of TRPV1 and NGF and the intensity of pain in BMS. It should, however, be noticed that, even if some studies using capsaicin, an agonist of the TRPV1 receptors, suggest that TRPV1 is responsible for the burning sensation, no study yet has clearly proven this hypothesis. Other pain‐related receptors such as TRPA1 (implicated in the production of inflammatory and neuropathic pain) or ASIC (acid sensor ionic channel) could also be candidates, especially since BMS patients avoid acids and spicy food. The lack of clinically available antagonists for humans explains this lack of knowledge. TRPV1 immunoreactivity was also increased in epithelial cells, suggesting that not only nerve fibres contribute to the etiopathogenesis of BMS. P2X3 immunoreactivity was also increased in epithelial nerve fibres. The P2X purinergic receptor is activated by extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which is released during tissue injury or inflammation and chronic pain including OFP; in addition, P2X3 expression, as that of TRPV1, is regulated by oestrogens possibly involved in BMS.

Implications and Integrative Interpretation
First, BMS is associated with neuropathic changes, which help to decipher the etiopathogenesis; considering QST, imagery and saliva studies together with these neuropathic changes result in a complex picture of generalised changes involving nervous, glial and epithelial cells. Second, these changes are not ‘all or none’. There is yet no specific marker for BMS that would be found only in CTRL subjects, pointing out that clinical diagnosis is still the standard of diagnosis. Only quantitative changes for innervation or other biomarkers have been evidenced along with discrete qualitative cytologic changes. This raises the question of whether there is a continuum of changes or a discontinuous evolution as evoked in the first part of the discussion. Tip only might relate to stress‐induced BMS when extended BMS might relate to advanced neuropathy. In the first case, BMS pain would be caused by a central sensitisation caused by stress as described in the concept of stress‐induced sensitization and in the second case would be the result of the histopathological changes observed in the tongue, reflecting general changes (see further discussion). This raises the question of the mechanism of pain in BMS. Since no correlation was evidenced in the reviewed studies between the severity of the symptoms and the magnitude of the histological changes, the pain cannot be solely assigned to the decrease of epithelial nerve fibres. Third, our knowledge is still very limited, and the available studies looked at small parts of the whole picture. A better understanding of the innervation of the human tongue is necessary and the following points need to be discussed:

Origin of the Labelled Nerves
Trigeminal versus Chorda tympani (CT): The anterior part of the tongue, covered with FilP and FunP, is innervated, for the sensory nerves, by the trigeminal (V) and CT‐VII nerves. The V conveys tactile and interoceptive (temperature, pain, pruriception, chemoception etc.). The VII relays taste information. Filiform papillae (FilP) are innervated by the V and fungiform papillae (FunP) by the V and VII; in addition, as shown in animal studies, in the FunP, nerve fibres of the VII are distributed inside the taste bud and V afferents outside the TB. FilP and FunP convey tactile innervation when taste is only processed in FunP. In rodents, as well as in humans, both FilP and FunP are innervated by interoceptive nerve fibres. In this regard, the FunP is a special sensory organ, crucial for oral perceptions integrating at the first step taste, tactile and interoceptive information. In the articles reviewed in this study, the data are difficult to interpret, especially for FunP, since the innervation and the bulk of innervation arising from the subepithelial plexus can belong either to CT or V. Therefore, the meaning of the P2X3 increase is difficult to embrace since purinergic (P2X3) labelling is, in animals, mainly found within taste buds (intragemmal) originating from CT fibres when perigemmal innervation is mainly composed of peptidergic nerve fibres of trigeminal origin, suggesting a role in taste rather than in pain. Many studies report an association between dysgeusia and BMS although a recent well‐controlled study challenged this view. Autonomic innervation: In addition to the sensory innervation, animal studies evidenced an autonomic innervation in the tongue which can be labelled with nerve biomarkers such as the enzyme Tyrosine Hydroxylase (TH). The presence of autonomic nerve fibres has not been searched in the human tongue with BMS, but autonomic symptoms such as xerostomia are frequently reported. In addition, autonomic alterations have been evidenced in BMS patients. Therefore, it cannot be excluded that the decrease in IENFD might involve autonomic nerve fibres.

Subtypes of Nerves
If pain in BMS is supposed to come from trigeminal nerve dysfunction, deciphering the origin of the signal is more complex and requires identifying the subtypes of nerves involved. The view of interoceptive versus exteroceptive sensory pathways conveying respectively by small neurons with unmyelinated nerve fibres and large neurons with myelinated fibres has challenged our understanding of nociception and pain as well as that of touch and homeostasis function. Nociception arises from two categories of neurons: small unmyelinated neurons connected to amyelinic fibres (C‐Neurons), and small to medium‐sized neurons connected to thinly myelinated nerve fibres (Aδ Neurons); the C‐fibre neurons are broadly divided into peptidergic neurons containing neuropeptides such as substance P and calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP) and non‐peptidergic neurons expressing IB4 (isolectin B4)‐binding protein. NGF controls the survival, differentiation and target innervation of both peptidergic and non‐peptidergic sensory neurons in dorsal root/trigeminal ganglion neurons. Non‐peptidergic primary afferents are firstly dependent on NGF and later on GDNF (glial cell‐derived neurotrophic factor). Based on scRNA‐seq (single‐cell RNA sequencing) obtained in rodent studies, this knowledge has been updated recently as follows: type C including C‐Cold (TRPM8high, expressing TRPM8), C‐Nociceptors MH (=PEP1, including 4 subtypes, expressing among other TRPV1, TRPA1, TRPM3 and Piezzo2), type Aδ H nociceptors (PEP2, expressing TRPV1, ASIC1), for the main types. Recent studies on tongue innervation in mice fit with this classification and can be useful for BMS studies.
Although each sensory neuron subtype expresses a repertoire of receptors, there is an overlap between the neurons; for example, in rodents, TRPV1 is found in both C‐fibres and Aδ fibres and in C‐fibres some receptors such as Piezzo 2 are expressed in different subtypes. Temperature‐sensitive afferents, which express TRPV1, not only respond to noxious heat and capsaicin but are also modulated by the acidity of the chemical milieu of inflammation, a frequent clinical feature of BMS patients avoiding spicy and acidic foods. TRPA1, thought to be restricted to a subset of TRPV1 afferents of the peptidergic subclass, has a wider distribution than does TRPV1, is expressed by all neuronal sizes in the sensory ganglion of mice and is found in the human tongue. Therefore, the use of TRPV1 labelling is interesting for understanding the mechanism of the pain but is not sufficient to determine the precise neuronal subtype involved. Shinoda et al. found an upregulation of artemin, a ligand of the GDNF family receptor α3 (GFRα3) in BMS patients and a mirror increased artemin protein expression in the keratinized epithelium of the tongue, in a mouse model of BMS, pointing to a role of non‐peptidergic sensory neurons in BMS. However, this mouse model of BMS is closer to a chemical neuropathy than to a true clinical representation of BMS.

Non‐Nociceptive Neurons
Recent animal studies also evidenced mechanical innervation of fungiform and filiform papillae indicating that not all the nerve fibres are dedicated to pain or interoceptive information, raising the question of the origin of the decreased IENFD in BMS. Fungiform papillae are convergently innervated by multiple Piezo2+ trigeminal afferents, whereas single trigeminal afferents branched into multiple adjacent filiform papillae. Although the neuronal mechanical sensor Piezo2 is expressed in non‐nociceptive neurons, it also plays a role in pain. For example, capsaicin inhibits Piezo2 function and Piezo2 upregulation in pain‐afferent neurons following trigeminal nerve injury contributes to neuropathic pain development. Moreover, its upregulationmay be modulated by proinflammatory cytokines such as IL‐6, suggesting a link between mechanical transduction, inflammation and changes in sensory function in BMS patients.

Non‐Neuronal Cells
In addition, Schwann cells, labelled in some BMS studies with S100 marker, can participate in nociception, as evidenced in animal studies, raising the question of which type is important for pain in BMS. Other cell types such as epithelial cells may modulate neuronal sensitivity and play a role in the etiopathogenesis of BMS: for example, Borsani et al. found an increased expression of TRPV1 in epithelial cells in BMS versus CTRL, along with its translocation to the plasma membrane. TRPV1 is not limited to pain but contributes in humans to several other cellular functions such as cell proliferation, barrier function and inflammation, and interestingly in concert with TRPA1. The same group also evidenced a decrease in CB1 and an increase in CB2 receptors in epithelial cells, which are known modulators of nerve function. Taken together, this suggests that not only neurons, but also non‐neuronal cells may participate in the burning sensation experienced by BMS patients.

The Pain Is Not Always the Result of Increased Nociception
Pain sensation is mainly a cerebral construct resulting from the integration of neuronal activities. Both the network configuration, as well as the context of the perception, is important as illustrated respectively by the gate control model and the thermal grid paradigm. According to the gate control theory, nociceptive inputs from small fibres are inhibited at the spinal cord level by non‐nociceptive tactile inputs. These are supposed to be Aδ fibres but recent evidence in animals indicates that C‐LTMR expressing TAFA 4 peptide can do the same. Then, a decrease in nerve fibre density might translate to a pain sensation. It has been suggested that a relative reduction in the activity of Aδ fibres mediating cooling that in normal conditions inhibits the polymodal C‐fibre nociceptor system results in unmasking or disinhibition of the system and consequent burning pain. Jaaskelainen 2012 suggested that according to some thermal QST results, hypofunction within the Aδ fibre class mediating innocuous cooling may be relatively more frequent than C‐fibre hypofunction in primary BMS. This disinhibition phenomenon occurs irrespective of the level of the lesion within the cool fibre mediated system; damage either within the peripheral or central nervous system will lead to clinically similar representation In addition, although a recent study using Conditioned Pain Modulation (CPM) evidenced ascending dysfunction with QST, another study showed alteration of descending controls although, suggesting that BMS pain might be due to altered central controls.

Symptoms
Studies found no significant correlation neither between IENFD and intensity of the pain (Lauria et al.; 2007, Penza et al.2010), nor between IENFD and duration of the symptoms, though Lauria et al. observed a trend towards significance. There is only one study linking pain symptoms and histological findings. Yilmaz et al. (2007) found a positive correlation between the baseline pain score and TRPV1 (p = 0.0143) and NGF fibres (p = 0.0252) but no correlation with global IENFD. Additionally, they found a significant correlation between baseline and post‐capsaicin pain (p = 0.0006). The study was performed in a small sample of patients (N = 10 in each group). Beneng et al. 2010 observed a trend but an insignificant correlation between pain symptoms and P2X labelling also in a small sample lacking statistical power. Treldal et al. evidenced a link between pain symptoms and the presence of biomarkers: immunoreactivity to the oestrogen receptor was most intense in the group of patients relieved by local anaesthetic application, whereas the patients who were not tended to have higher plasma levels of proinflammatory cytokines. They did not, however, assess IENFD. This approach echoes the idea of using local anaesthesia to sort BMS patients according to distinct pathogenic mechanisms: peripheral neuropathy versus systemic inflammation. It also echoes attempts to phenotype BMS patients with QST, and may help to find subgroups of patients and dissect out different types of BMS. The value of QST has been already discussed elsewhere and will not be here, but overall results indicate that thermal sensitivity seems to be significantly altered in patients with BMS compared to controls, suggesting a small‐fibre neuropathy. In addition, the variability in QST results suggests that small fibre neuropathy may not be present in all BMS patients and study protocols were highly variable and heterogeneous. Combining biopsy with response to treatment biomarkers and QST is interesting although difficult to implement in clinics.

Tissues Biopsies vs. Other Procedures
Biopsies need to prove their innocuity. The procedure is painful and, for example, Puhakka et al. used biopsies from cadavers in order to limit the number of CTRLs subjects. It can also be questioned, especially in patients with a neuropathic condition such as BMS, if biopsies can be harmful, leading to additional persistent pain. We did not find an answer in the BMS literature and looked at other diseases using biopsies procedures. Salivary accessory glands biopsy used for Sjögren disease diagnosis seems innocuous but delayed effects can be misevaluated. It would be interesting to recall the patients from BMS studies for a long‐term follow‐up to evaluate pain over time, since the baseline data are already available. In this regard, exfoliative cytology, by avoiding surgical lesions, emerges as a promising non‐invasive alternative to surgical biopsy. This method, easy to perform, allows for providing different information at the cellular and molecular levels, offering a useful tool to evaluate neuroinflammatory changes and identify neuropathic biomarkers.

Prospective

Call for an Animal Model
This review summarises the knowledge relative to tissue changes focusing particularly on the innervation. However, the information is scarce and focused on a few biomarkers when many other candidates could also be informative. This emphasises the need for an animal model of BMS. As pointed out in a recent review, there have been few attempts to develop an animal model of BMS which consists mainly of dry tongue models, overexpression of artemin, and CT transection. Dry‐tongue models based on hyposalivation cannot be taken as models since BMS patients report generally normal values; genetically engineered overexpression of artemin, as a putative agent of sensitisation after Shinoda's observations is rather a model of chemical neuropathy than BMS. Boucher et al. tested the hypothesis of CT transection disinhibiting trigeminal pathways after Bartoshuk's hypothesis , but not all BMS patients display taste alterations. Interestingly, in their study, the ovariectomised group displayed increased oral sensitivity to capsaicin, in line with previous observations and reflecting human conditions, that is, increased aversion to spicy food and capsaicin. In line with these observations, Kobayashi et al. reported recently an increased sensitivity to capsaicin in ovariectomised rats associated with a decrease of IENFD in the tongue, mimicking some of the phenotypic characteristics of BMS; however, with a short‐term observation period (14 days). Further studies are needed to propose a valid animal model of BMS integrating the key features of BMS, that is, hormonal deprivation and anxio‐depressive conditions possibly related to stress. Such a model should reflect yet known biomarkers of BMS, as pointed out in this review, and allow the exploration of others such as TRPA1 and ASICs receptors, but many others such as specific neuron enolase, AchR, and other TRP receptors, etc. expressed in sensory neurons innervating the tongue and epithelial cells.

Standardisation of Procedures
Lauria et al. proposed for skin biopsies a standardisation of the procedures, however, this was not followed by further authors, and many elements differ from study to study: (1) the surgical piece: the punch biopsy [3 mm–6 mm] diameter was the most used technique for the tongue. But other formats have been used. (2) Thickness of sections: the guidelines for skin biopsies are 50 μm but many authors use different thickness, range [7–50 μm]. (3) the histological technique and biomarkers: some use immunohistochemistry, others immunofluorescence. Some authors used, for example, the neuronal markers PGP9.5 or B tubulin or others, single or mixed (4) the area of observation in the tongue, especially the inclusion of fungiform papillae in the biopsy. This review suggests differentiating counting in FunP and FilP areas since they are both important and may reveal differences. (5) Other areas than the tongue might also be important. For example, the retroincisal palate is also frequently reported but contains no FunP nor FilP. (6) Types of nerve fibres altered in BMS, using specific biomarkers and new classification, as pointed out in the discussion with specific biomarkers.

Improving Evidence From Studies
The high variability across studies included in this review limited the feasibility of statistical pooling, and therefore the I 2 statistic was not calculated. Several clinical and methodological factors likely contributed to inter‐study discrepancies. Clinically, heterogeneity arose from differences in diagnostic criteria for BMS (e.g., ICHD‐3, ICOP, IASP or author‐defined), wide variation in symptom duration (ranging from 6 months to over 20 years), demographic differences such as age and sex distribution, and subgroup classifications (e.g., ‘tip only’ vs. ‘extended’ BMS). Methodological variability included differences in tissue sampling (punch vs. incisional biopsies, buccal vs. lingual mucosa, inclusion or exclusion of fungiform papillae), histological techniques employed (haematoxylin–eosin, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, qPCR), and the range of biomarkers assessed (e.g., PGP9.5, β‐tubulin, TRPV1, NGF, CB1/CB2, Nav1.7–1.9). Additional variation in section thickness, staining protocols and quantification methods (per mm of epithelial length, per papilla or per epithelial area) further complicates direct comparison. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings, as they may partly explain the conflicting results observed between studies.
Most of the studies included in this review were biopsy‐based case–control studies with small sample sizes—typically 9 to 12 participants per group—except for Treldal (N = 21) and Penza et al., who included 56 patients in the BMS group. However, their control group was derived from a previous study with a small sample size (N = 9). Given the variability in results highlighted in this review, larger‐scale studies are clearly warranted. Such studies would not only bolster evidence‐based knowledge of the disease but also facilitate better control over symptom homogeneity. Indeed, many studies indicate that BMS exhibits clinical heterogeneity and may encompass distinct conditions. Although progress has been made through the use of more stringent inclusion criteria access to well‐characterised cohorts—including quantitative sensory testing (QST), biopsies and comprehensive clinical data—would improve control over key parameters such as age, symptom location and duration. For example, the duration of symptoms at the time of biopsy was not always documented, and ranged widely—from 6 months to 27 years. Since many neurological changes associated with BMS, such as decreased nerve fibre density and phenotypic alterations in nociceptors, evolve slowly, this variability could introduce significant bias. A recent study, for instance, suggests that acute stress can trigger BMS within a timeframe where more profound neurological changes may not have had sufficient time to develop. Therefore, performing biopsies on patients who exhibit BMS clinical features but before the 3‐month period specified in the diagnostic criteria could be insightful. Notably, none of the patients included in this review had symptoms lasting less than 6 months. Additionally, conducting biopsies at different time points could reveal histological changes and their relationship to pain symptoms, particularly in patients who experience remission.
Improved matching of controls to BMS patients would also enhance our understanding of disease pathogenesis. Although most studies match participants by sex, this is often not done for age. In the reviewed biopsy studies, BMS patients were on average more than 12 years older than controls, with only two studies matching for age. This age difference is especially relevant around menopause, as gonadal hormone levels are known to influence peripheral nerve density.
Promising role for exfoliative surgery? Exfoliative studies provide no information related to nerve changes unless they translate as neuroinflammatory changes in tissues that can be evidenced. An interesting biomarker could be NGF and its receptors TrkA and P75NTR, which are expressed both in neuronal cells and epithelial cells and are implicated both in cellular trophism and neuronal function.

Conclusion
This reviewed aimed at helping to synthetize the evidence regarding histological changes in peripheral tissues in BMS, in order to have a better understanding of the disease and propose future directions for research. Although the exact pathophysiology of primary BMS is still under investigation, there is numerous clinical, histological, neurophysiological, neurochemical and connectomic evidence suggesting neuropathic and possibly nociplastic mechanisms at play. The classical clinical presentation of BMS (burning, subjective dryness sensation and dysgueusia) is coherent with a neuropathic condition, notably a small fibre neuropathy, further supported by positive scores in several neuropathic pain detection questionnaires. Histological alterations, as reviewed in the present paper, show—among other things—changes in nerve fibre density and receptor expression in BMS patients, in association with neurophysiological changes evidenced using QST or the blink reflex. The arguments towards a neuropathic component in BMS have been reviewed. Recently, some authors have raised the question of the possible nociplastic nature of burning mouth syndrome after the recent conceptualization and definition of nociplastic pain, that can be briefly summarised as ‘pain that arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of nociceptive or neuropathic pain mechanisms’. Although the question is quite complex, especially considering the still recent definition of nociplastic pain, there are arguments suggesting dysfunctional nociception that does not imply nociceptor activation or somatosensory nervous system lesion or disease. For example, dysfunctional central processing of pain in BMS patients has been evidenced using TEP scan imaging of nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathways, further supported by genetic studies of D2 dopamine receptor polymorphisms that predispose to developing burning mouth syndrome. Functional MRI studies have also suggested altered cortical activation patterns during pain processing in BMS patients (as compared to healthy controls). The connectomic data related to BMS has recently been reviewed. Nevertheless, from a clinical standpoint, it seems that other nociplastic pain conditions such as fibromyalgia, surprisingly, do not predispose to developing burning mouth syndrome. Considering the diversity in the clinical presentations of BMS it is most probable that neuropathic and/or nociplastic pain mechanisms are at play, the nature and extent of which may vary from case to case, as with other nociplastic conditions.

Bullet Points
Biopsies provided interesting information related to the etiopathogenesis of BMS and raise numerous questions.
They evidenced neuronal alterations in terms of density and expression of biomarkers.
The main findings indicate a decrease of nerve fibre density in tongue epithelium and quantitative alteration of several biomarkers such as TRPV1 in nerve and epithelial cells.
The observed changes have yet no diagnostic value since no cut‐off in the observation can be inferred from these analyses.
The idea of observing changes in subgroups does not seem well suited for diagnosis but remains interesting for tailoring treatments.
