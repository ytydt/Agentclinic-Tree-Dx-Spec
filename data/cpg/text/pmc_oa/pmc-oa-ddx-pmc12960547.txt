Comparative Evaluation of the Retention Rates of Composite Resin Pit and Fissure Sealants Placed on Permanent Molars Treated with Air Abrasion and Acid Etching: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Abstract
Aim and objectives
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the retention rates of composite resin pit and fissure sealants placed on permanent molars prepared using air abrasion versus traditional acid etching methods.
Methods
A comprehensive search was conducted across multiple databases, including MEDLINE (via PubMed), Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science since inception to date, but only in English. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with at least a 1-year follow-up, involving permanent molars treated with either air abrasion or acid etching prior to sealant placement, were included. Data extraction and risk of bias assessments were performed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. Meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3 with log odds ratios (ORs) as effect size metrics.
Results
Four RCTs were included in the review. Meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in sealant retention between air abrasion and acid etching. The combined log OR was −0.727 [95% confidence interval (CI): −2.055 to 0.602], indicating a slight, nonsignificant trend favoring acid etching. Heterogeneity was moderate across studies. The overall quality of evidence was rated as moderate using the grading of recommendations, assessment, development, and evaluations (GRADE) framework.
Conclusion
There is no clear advantage of air abrasion over acid etching regarding the retention of fissure sealants on permanent molars. While air abrasion presents a minimally invasive alternative, acid etching remains the more established and effective technique. Further high-quality RCTs with larger sample sizes and standardized methodologies are needed to confirm these findings and explore other clinically relevant outcomes.

Introduction
Dental caries remains one of the most widespread chronic diseases globally, affecting both primary and permanent teeth. It is a multifactorial disease that begins with the demineralization of tooth enamel, eventually progressing to the formation of cavitated lesions if untreated.
According to studies, up to 90% of caries in permanent posterior teeth are found in pits and fissures, which are deeply grooved portions of the teeth's occlusal surface.
The accumulation of plaque bacteria in deep fissures accelerates the progression of carious lesions, which, if left untreated, can lead to irreversible damage to the enamel and dentin. In response to this, various preventive strategies have been introduced to arrest or slow the progression of caries. One of the most effective methods is the application of dental sealants, which create a physical barrier over the occlusal surface of the tooth, effectively preventing plaque accumulation and subsequent caries development.
Sealants work by filling the pits and fissures, creating a bond between the tooth and the resin that prevents the invasion of cariogenic bacteria. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the retention of sealants decreases over time, with some reports indicating up to 60% failure of sealants within the first few years after application. Studies also indicate that sealants can fail at a rate of 5−10% annually, despite their correct application. The effectiveness of the sealant, therefore, is directly linked to how well it bonds to the enamel surface and the ability of the clinician to prepare the tooth adequately before placement.
The standard technique for preparing the tooth surface for sealant placement requires acid etching. This enhances the bonding strength and retention of the sealant. However, while acid etching increases the surface area and enhances bonding, it may not be entirely effective in removing the debris, biofilm, and pellicle that can be present in the deep pits and fissures. Residual debris may hinder the formation of a strong bond between the enamel and the sealant, leading to reduced retention.
One promising alternative to acid etching is air abrasion, a minimally invasive technique that has gained popularity in recent years. This technique has the advantage of not only increasing the surface area of the enamel, similar to acid etching, but also effectively removing biofilm and debris from the deep fissures. Furthermore, air abrasion can subside the requirement of acid etching, streamlining the sealant placement process and potentially improving retention by ensuring a cleaner, debris-free surface.
It has been demonstrated that air abrasion enhances sealant retention by efficiently prepping the enamel and cleaning fissures that may be difficult to access with traditional etching techniques. Some studies have reported positive outcomes with air abrasion, while others have shown no significant improvement in retention compared to traditional acid etching methods.
Several studies have examined the role of air abrasion in enhancing sealant retention, but the findings have been mixed, with some studies showing comparable results to acid etching, while others indicate no significant difference.
The purpose of this review is to evaluate whether air abrasion offers superior retention rates compared to acid etching, and to identify the most effective enamel preparation method to enhance sealant longevity and prevent caries in permanent molars. This review will synthesize existing clinical studies, focusing on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with follow-up periods of at least 1 year, in order to provide reliable evidence on the optimal surface preparation technique for pit and fissure sealant placement. By addressing this gap in the literature, the review aims to guide clinicians in selecting the most effective technique to ensure the long-term success of dental sealants and, ultimately, improve preventive care in dentistry. Methods

PROSPERO Registration
This systematic review and meta-analysis were registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration ID: CRD42024509328). The review protocol adheres to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) 2020 guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and rigor.

Search Strategy
Search was conducted using the following databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science using a combination of MeSH terms and free-text keywords. The MeSH terms used for this systematic review include “Dental Caries” to describe the condition of interest, particularly focusing on “Permanent Molars” for the targeted population. To address the intervention and exposure, terms such as “Pit and Fissure Sealants,” “Resin Sealants,” and “Composite Resins” are relevant. For the enamel preparation techniques, terms such as “Air Abrasion,” “Enamel Etching,” and “Acid Etching” were used, with specific reference to “Phosphoric Acid” for acid etching. Additionally, terms such as “Retention Rate,” “Sealant Failure,” and “Dental Restoration Failure” are important for evaluating the outcomes of sealant retention. To ensure comprehensive coverage of the topic, other useful MeSH terms may include “Tooth, Permanent,” “Tooth Surface,” “Dental Adhesives,” and “Treatment Outcome.” The search strategy also includes terms such as “Randomized Controlled Trials,” “Follow-Up Studies,” and “Prospective Studies” to filter relevant study designs. Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion Criteria
Studies of pit and fissure sealants in permanent molars.
Studies evaluating the impact of acid etching and air abrasion on the rate at which pit and fissure sealants are retained in permanent molars.
Studies published in the English language only.
RCTs with follow-up of at least 1 year.
All studies published since inception to date.

Exclusion Criteria
Case reports, animal studies.
Review articles.
In vitro studies.

Study Selection and Data Extraction
The study selection was performed by two reviewers VR and DS, who independently screened titles and abstracts. Subsequently, the full text of all qualified articles was extracted and checked by the same two reviewers.
Cases of disagreement between the reviewers were resolved by the senior reviewer AJ. Studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, and the reasons for the same were recorded. Relevant data from all the included studies were plotted into a specific Excel (Microsoft) spreadsheet.
The following characteristics of each study were extracted: author and publication year, tooth, study design, age, sample size, intervention and control group, outcome, and follow-up.

Main Outcome(s)
The primary outcome was the retention rate of pit and fissure sealants applied to permanent molars. Retention is defined as the presence of the sealant over time, assessed at follow-up intervals of at least 1 year.

RevMan software (version 5.3) was used to perform statistical analysis and data synthesis. Q and I2 statistics were used to evaluate the heterogeneity of the studies. Data were pooled using a random-effects model to compute combined summary effects, which were graphically displayed in forest plots with 95% confidence intervals.

Quality of Evidence Assessment
The grading of recommendations, assessment, development, and evaluations (GRADE) approach is a method for assessing the quality of evidence in systematic reviews. The quality of evidence represents the extent to which authors are sure that an estimate of the effect is accurate. The GRADE approach results in an assessment of the quality of a body of evidence in one of four grades:
High: Authors are very confident that the true effect lies close to that of the estimate of the effect.
Moderate: Authors are moderately confident in the effect estimate. The true effect is likely to be close to the estimate of the effect, but there is a possibility that it is substantially different.
Low: Author's confidence in the effect estimate is limited: The true effect may be substantially different from the estimate of the effect.
Very low: Authors have very little confidence in the effect estimate. The true effect is likely to be substantially different from the estimate of effect.

Results
The systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) aimed to compare the effectiveness of air abrasion and acid etching in sealing permanent molars using two distinct effect size metrics: log odds ratio (OR) and risk difference (RD). These analyses combined the results from three studies: Deepika et al., Bendinskaite et al., and Kanellis et al.

Study Selection
The electronic search of the four databases yielded 1,136 records. No unpublished or ongoing trials were found. After exclusion of duplicates, a total of 500 articles were included in title and abstract screening. Four hundred eighty articles that addressed studies on primary teeth, studies with different outcomes and interventions, studies with follow-up of less than 1 year, in vitro studies, and studies with unclear methodology were excluded.
Twenty full-text articles remained to be examined for eligibility. Eight articles were excluded because of different outcome (three studies evaluated surface roughness of pit and fissure sealants, two studies evaluated clinical efficacy of pit and fissure sealant in prevention of secondary caries, three studies evaluated color stability and marginal adaptation of pit and fissure sealant), four articles excluded because of follow up less than 1 year and four articles were excluded because of different intervention (studies with laser as intervention). Finally, a total of four articles were included for review. The study selection process has been shown in the form of a flow diagram in Figure 3.

Statistical Analysis

Effect-size: Log Odds Ratio (OR)
The meta-analysis used the random-effects model to combine the studies, with a τ2 of 0.8103, indicating substantial variability across the studies. The I² value of 62.54% suggests high heterogeneity, meaning there are some differences between the studies shown in Figure 4. The H² value of 2.67 indicates that the variation in results is larger than expected by sampling error alone.
Deepika et al.: The log OR for this study was 0.129 (95% CI: −0.575, 0.832), meaning there was a small positive effect of air abrasion on retention, though the result was not statistically significant.
Bendinskaite et al.: This study found a log OR of −1.533 (95% CI: −4.506, 1.440), indicating a potential negative effect or a very weak association between air abrasion and retention. However, the wide confidence interval suggests uncertainty in the estimate.
Kanellis et al.: This study showed a Log OR of −1.551 (95% CI: −2.872, −0.230), which also suggests a negative effect of air abrasion on retention compared to acid etching, but the result is statistically significant at the lower end of the confidence interval.
Meta-analysis forest plot of comparison between acid etching and air abrasion for retention of pit and fissure sealants
The combined estimate for the Log OR (theta) was −0.727 (95% CI: −2.055, 0.602), which suggests a potential trend toward a negative effect for air abrasion, but the result was not statistically significant (z = −1.07, p = 0.2836). The test for homogeneity (Q = 5.53, p = 0.0629) suggests that there is no significant difference in results between the studies.

Heterogeneity and Study Variability
There is moderate heterogeneity in the results, as indicated by the I² values of 62.54% (log OR). This suggests that while there is some variation between the studies, the overall evidence does not support a significant difference between the two techniques. The differences in study designs, sample sizes, and follow-up durations might contribute to the observed variability.

Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of air abrasion compared to acid etching for the retention of fissure sealants on permanent molars.
The results of the meta-analysis, based on Log OR, indicate that there is no statistically significant difference between air abrasion and acid etching in terms of sealant retention. Although some studies, such as Deepika et al., observed a small positive effect of air abrasion, these results were not statistically significant. Similarly, Kanellis et al. found a negative effect of air abrasion on sealant retention, but this finding was not consistently supported across all studies. The combined estimates from the meta-analysis further reinforce these observations, showing a slight trend toward lower retention rates for air abrasion, though not reaching statistical significance.
The Bendinskaite et al. found no significant difference between the two techniques in terms of sealant retention. Therefore, it can be inferred that both methods are relatively comparable in maintaining the longevity of fissure sealants, with no compelling evidence favoring one technique over the other.

Potential Reasons for Lack of Significant Difference
There are several factors that might explain the lack of statistically significant differences between air abrasion and acid etching. First, the variability across studies could be attributed to differences in study design, sample size, and duration of follow-up. For instance, studies such as Deepika et al. and Kanellis et al. had smaller sample sizes compared to Bendinskaite et al., which could contribute to variations in the observed effects. The results of our systematic review and meta-analysis align with some of the findings from the study comparing acid etching (AE) and air abrasion (KCP-1000) by Kanellis et al., for placing sealants in an elementary school setting. In our meta-analysis, we found no significant difference in sealant retention between air abrasion and acid etching, which corresponds to the findings for occlusal surfaces in the school-based study, where air abrasion showed a similar retention rate to acid etching (96% vs 97%, respectively). In both studies, air abrasion demonstrated similar or slightly inferior retention compared to acid etching, but the study in the school setting raised the possibility of lower retention on certain tooth surfaces, a factor that could not be thoroughly explored in our analysis due to the limited studies available.
Second, while air abrasion is often praised for being minimally invasive and avoiding the use of acids, its effectiveness in removing surface debris and pellicle from deep pits and fissures may still be limited compared to the traditional acid etching technique. In comparison to our study, the results from the trial conducted by Hamilton et al. investigating the use of air abrasion for treating questionable incipient pit-and-fissure carious lesions also highlight the complexities of air abrasion.
When comparing the statistical results of our study with the trial conducted by Hamilton et al., several similarities and differences emerge. In our systematic review and meta-analysis, using a random-effects model, we analyzed the log OR for retention rates of air abrasion versus acid etching across three studies. The summary theta for log OR was −0.727, with a 95% confidence interval ranging from −2.055 to 0.602, indicating no significant difference in sealant retention between the two methods (p = 0.2836). On the other hand, the study on incipient lesions reports no statistically significant difference in caries prevention or air abrasion's efficacy in comparison to natural progression (p > 0.05). This highlights that while air abrasion might be beneficial in some situations, its effect on caries prevention is not statistically significant when compared to traditional methods, as shown by the lack of difference in caries development between the treatment and control groups.
Another possibility is that the air abrasion system, while effective in creating surface roughness, may not fully remove the soft, organic debris that could impair bonding. Furthermore, air abrasion alone might not provide the same level of enamel surface conditioning as acid etching, which increases the surface area and enhances the formation of resin tags that contribute to the retention of the sealant.

Clinical Implications
From a clinical standpoint, the results of this meta-analysis suggest that clinicians may continue to rely on acid etching as the primary method for preparing the enamel surface before the application of fissure sealants.
However, air abrasion still holds potential as a useful adjunct to traditional sealant placement procedures. Its potential advantages, such as being less invasive and avoiding the use of acids, may appeal to patients and practitioners looking for less discomfort and shorter procedural times. Additionally, air abrasion may be particularly useful in situations where fissures are deep or difficult to clean or to remove biofilm with traditional acid etching methods.

Limitations and Future Directions
Future research must address a number of limitations, even if the results of this systematic analysis offer valuable insights into the relative efficacy of acid etching and air abrasion. First, the studies included in this meta-analysis varied in terms of methodology, with differences in follow-up durations, sample sizes, and inclusion criteria. To provide more reliable conclusions, future research should aim for larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and more consistent study designs to better understand the long-term effectiveness of both techniques.
Second, most studies included in this review did not fully address the potential confounders that could affect sealant retention, such as the operator's technique, patient-related factors (e.g., age, oral hygiene, caries risk), and the type of resin sealant used. These variables should be carefully controlled in future studies to ensure that the results are more generalizable and reflective of real-world clinical outcomes.
Additionally, there is a requirement for more high-quality RCTs that evaluate not only sealant retention but also other clinically relevant outcomes, such as the incidence of secondary caries, occlusal wear, and patient satisfaction.

Conclusion
In conclusion, the results of this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that there is no clear advantage of air abrasion over acid etching for sealant retention on permanent molars. Both techniques appear to be equally effective in maintaining the longevity of dental fissure sealants. While air abrasion offers a minimally invasive approach that may be appealing in certain clinical situations, acid etching remains the more established and widely used technique. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are needed to definitively determine the effectiveness of air abrasion in comparison to acid etching for fissure sealant retention.
