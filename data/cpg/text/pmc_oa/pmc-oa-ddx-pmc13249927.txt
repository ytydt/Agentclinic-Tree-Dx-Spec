Fibularis brevis tendon variants: a classification-based surgical and radiological approach
Purpose
Fibularis brevis tendon (FBT) variants are under-recognised and may alter both imaging interpretation and operative strategy. The purpose of this review was to synthesise the available anatomical, radiological, and surgical literature on distal FBT variants and to frame this evidence through a classification-based approach.
Methods
This study was designed as a narrative review. Anatomical/cadaveric, imaging, and surgical publications relevant to distal FBT morphology and clinical interpretation were synthesised using the Olewnik classification as the organising framework. No formal systematic-review protocol or meta-analysis was undertaken.
Results
The available literature describes two main distal attachment types of the FBT: Type I and Type II, the latter further subdivided into subtypes IIa–IIc. The most consistent findings concern recognition of accessory slips and fused components, differentiation of variants from longitudinal split tears or pseudomass-like findings, and appreciation of how atypical insertions may influence distal operative exposure. The strongest evidence remains anatomical and imaging-based, whereas direct validation of classification-guided treatment is still limited.
Conclusion
The Olewnik classification provides a useful anatomical framework for radiological reporting and preoperative planning, but it should presently be regarded as clinically promising rather than fully validated. A structured, variant-aware interpretation can improve communication and operative preparation in patients with lateral ankle pain, tendon pathology, or instability.

Introduction
The fibularis brevis tendon (FBT) is an important dynamic stabiliser of the lateral ankle and foot, contributing to eversion, lateral column support, and resistance to inversion stress. Although its standard insertion into the tuberosity of the fifth metatarsal is well recognised, variation in distal attachment is not uncommon and may influence both imaging interpretation and operative exposure.
In clinical practice, accessory slips, bifid insertions, or fusion with the fibularis tertius tendon (FTT) may be mistaken for longitudinal split tears, incomplete ruptures, or mass-like lesions, particularly in patients with chronic lateral ankle instability or persistent lateral foot pain. The objective of this review is to synthesise anatomical, radiological, and surgical evidence on FBT variants using the classification proposed by Olewnik et al. as the central framework for clinical interpretation.

Methods
This manuscript was prepared as a narrative review of the literature addressing distal FBT morphology and its radiological and surgical relevance. The material considered in the synthesis included anatomical and cadaveric studies, MRI and ultrasonography papers, and surgical publications discussing fibularis tendon pathology, chronic lateral ankle instability, and variant-related operative challenges.
The review was structured around the distal attachment classification proposed by Olewnik et al.. Standard anatomy, variant morphology, imaging features, and practical surgical implications were synthesised for each type. Because the available evidence is heterogeneous and predominantly descriptive, the present work was intended as an interpretative synthesis rather than a systematic review, formal evidence synthesis, or meta-analysis.

Results

Standard anatomy and rationale for classification
In the classical pattern, the fibularis brevis muscle arises from the distal lateral fibula, courses together with the fibularis longus beneath the fibular retinacula, and inserts into the tuberosity at the base of the fifth metatarsal. This arrangement supports eversion and dynamic lateral stabilisation, while its close relationship to the fibular groove, retinacula, and fifth metatarsal makes accurate anatomic recognition important for both imaging and surgery.
Against this baseline, classification becomes clinically relevant because distal FBT variation may change the apparent tendon contour on MRI or US and may alter the extent of surgical exposure required distally. The classification proposed by Olewnik et al., summarised in Table 1, is therefore useful not merely as an anatomical description but as a practical framework for communicating variant-specific diagnostic and operative issues.

Distal attachment patterns and integrated clinical relevance
Based on 102 lower limbs, Olewnik et al. distinguished two main distal attachment types: Type I, present in 70.6% of specimens, and Type II, present in 29.4%, with Type II further subdivided into subtypes IIa, IIb, and IIc. Type I corresponds to the standard single-band insertion into the lateral tuberosity of the fifth metatarsal and serves as the reference configuration for both radiological and operative assessment. (Fig. 1).
Schematic representation of Type I distal attachment of the fibularis brevis tendon (FBT)
Type IIa includes an accessory dorsal slip with partial fusion to the FTT and insertion at the dorsal base of the fifth metatarsal. This configuration is clinically important because the accessory band may simulate a longitudinal split tear on MRI or dynamic US if the insertion level and continuity of the tendon are not carefully assessed. (Fig. 2A).
Type IIb is characterised by an accessory band that splits into lateral and medial components. The lateral band inserts into the dorsal surface of the base of the fifth metatarsal, whereas the stronger medial band inserts into the middle part of the fifth metatarsal shaft. Compared with Type I, this variant creates a more complex distal footprint and may require wider operative exposure when both slips must be identified or repaired. It is also the variant in which abnormal traction or irritation along the fifth metatarsal is most plausibly relevant, especially when symptoms are localised distally. (Fig. 2B).
Type IIc is characterised by an accessory band that splits into lateral and medial components. The lateral band inserts into the dorsal surface of the base of the fifth metatarsal, whereas the medial band fuses with the FTT; in the anatomical study, this fusion gave rise to the fourth dorsal interosseous muscle. On imaging or at surgery, this configuration may mimic a pseudomass or unusual soft-tissue structure and carries the greatest risk of intraoperative misidentification. At the same time, it should be stated clearly that the current strength of the classification lies primarily in anatomical description; external clinical validation, interobserver reliability, and outcome-based confirmation remain limited. Imaging assessment and diagnostic pitfalls
MRI is widely used for morphological assessment of the FBT because it enables multiplanar evaluation of tendon continuity, insertion level, and the relationship of the tendon to the FTT and surrounding structures. In practical terms, axial and oblique axial proton-density fat-suppressed sequences are often helpful when a split tear or accessory slip is suspected, whereas higher-resolution or isotropic acquisitions may further improve visualisation of complex distal branching patterns.
Dynamic US provides complementary real-time assessment of tendon continuity, excursion, and the behaviour of accessory slips during ankle and foot movement. This is particularly helpful in equivocal cases, because a mobile accessory slip may be followed dynamically to its insertion, whereas a true tear more often presents as discontinuity, irregularity, or abnormal sheath-related findings.
From a reporting perspective, imaging should not be limited to the statement that the tendon is “split” or “abnormal”. Whenever variant anatomy is suspected, the report should describe the number of distal components, the level of insertion, any fusion with the FTT, and whether tendon continuity is preserved. Surgical implications and classification-guided strategy
Variant recognition has practical operative consequences, although the published evidence mainly supports anatomy-informed planning rather than a validated type-specific treatment protocol. Type I usually allows standard lateral exposure and straightforward repair or tenodesis when indicated. In contrast, Type IIa may require more dorsal tracing of the accessory slip, Type IIb may require broader distal exposure to define both insertions, and Type IIc may demand more careful dissection because the fused or bulky component can obscure expected landmarks.
The principal structures at risk remain the sural nerve, the superior and inferior fibular retinacula, and the tissue planes around the retromalleolar region and distal fifth metatarsal. For this reason, the practical value of preoperative MRI and US lies not only in diagnosis but also in anticipating where dissection may need to be extended and where atypical anatomy may be encountered intraoperatively. Discussion
The literature consistently indicates that distal variation of the FBT is not rare, yet its interpretation remains fragmented across anatomical, radiological, and surgical publications. The main advantage of a classification-based reading is that it translates descriptive anatomy into a framework that can be used during routine reporting and operative planning. In practical terms, such a framework may help standardise how distal insertion patterns are described across anatomy reports, imaging reports, and surgical records, thereby reducing ambiguity when variant morphology is encountered. This is particularly relevant in the lateral ankle, where an accessory slip can be overcalled as a tear and where an unexpected distal attachment may complicate limited surgical exposure. This broader concept of distal variability is also illustrated by the case report of Ruzik et al., which described an unusual fibularis brevis insertion with multiple terminal bands and communication with the fibularis tertius tendon, reinforcing that uncommon distal configurations may be encountered in both anatomical and clinical settings.
A second important point is that the present evidence is uneven. The classification of Olewnik et al. is anatomically clear and clinically plausible, but it should not be presented as already fully validated for routine decision-making. Most available data are descriptive, and direct studies evaluating reproducibility, interobserver agreement, or procedure-specific outcomes according to FBT type remain scarce. Accordingly, the greatest present value of the classification is diagnostic and preoperative rather than prescriptive: it helps clinicians recognise what they are seeing and anticipate technical implications, but it does not yet dictate one preferred treatment pathway for each variant. This limitation should be made explicit to avoid overstating the current level of evidence.
The distal insertion pattern may also be relevant in traction-related pathology around the fifth metatarsal. In particular, variants extending dorsally or toward the shaft may modify the distribution of force at the tuberosity or more distally along the metatarsal, which may be relevant in athletes and in patients with recurrent lateral foot pain or injury at the base of the fifth metatarsal. At present, however, this relationship should be considered clinically plausible rather than definitively proven, and it warrants dedicated outcome-oriented study.
This review has its own limitations. It is a narrative synthesis, not a systematic review, and the available literature is heterogeneous with respect to study design, terminology, and clinical endpoints. In addition, many practical surgical recommendations are necessarily derived from anatomical and imaging reasoning rather than from comparative outcome trials. Future work should therefore focus on external validation of the classification, interobserver agreement in imaging interpretation, prospective correlation between FBT type, treatment strategy, and postoperative outcome, and the development of more standardised reporting language for distal FBT morphology.

Conclusion
The Olewnik classification offers a practical anatomical framework for understanding distal FBT variability and for improving communication between anatomists, radiologists, and surgeons. Its greatest present value lies in variant-aware interpretation of MRI and US and in preoperative anticipation of atypical distal insertions. At the current stage of evidence, the classification should be used as a clinically useful guide rather than as a fully validated treatment algorithm.
