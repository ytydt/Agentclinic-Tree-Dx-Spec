Reinitiating lung development: a novel approach in the management of bronchopulmonary dysplasia
Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is the predominant chronic lung disease in preterm infants, linked with various adverse long-term outcomes. Multiple prenatal and postnatal risk factors can impede lung development, leading to BPD. Current management of BPD relies heavily on pharmacotherapies and alterations in ventilatory strategies. However, these interventions only mitigate BPD symptoms without addressing underlying alveolar, vascular, structural, and functional deficiencies. Given the retarded lung development in infants with BPD and the limitations of existing modalities, new therapeutic approaches are imperative. The induced differentiation of stem/progenitor cells and the spatiotemporal expression patterns of growth factors associated with lung developmental processes are critical for lung development reactivation in BPD, which focuses on stimulating pulmonary vasculogenesis and alveolarization. This review summarizes the process of lung development and offers a comprehensive overview of advancements in therapies designed to reinitiate lung development in BPD. Furthermore, we assessed the potential of these therapies for maintaining lung homeostasis and effectively restoring pulmonary structure and function through stem/progenitor cells and growth factors, which have been widely researched.

Introduction
In 2019, approximately 10.9% of live births were preterm worldwide, amounting to a total of 15.2 million newborns. Advances in maternal–fetal medicine and neonatology over the past decade have notably improved neonatal survival rates, particularly among preterm infants. However, complications stemming from preterm birth, notably bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), which are linked to adverse long-term outcomes, remain a leading cause of mortality among children aged ≤ 5 years. BPD, affecting approximately 45% of neonates with a gestational age (GA) < 29 weeks, persists despite improvements in survival facilitated by medical advances. The diagnostic criteria established by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) in 2001 and 2018 are current widely used. The decline in “old” BPD due to pulmonary surfactants and advanced mechanical ventilation contrasts with the emergence of “new” BPD, marked by alveolar hypoplasia and pulmonary microvascular dysplasia. Various prenatal and postnatal risk factors disrupt or delay lung development, leading to BPD and long-term complications such as hyperresponsive airway disease, oxygen dependence, and restricted exercise capacity. With BPD constituting a continuum of pulmonary disorders from fetal stages to adulthood, managing these conditions in infants remains clinically complex.
The management of BPD, categorized into early, evolving, and established phases, has witnessed significant advancements. These include alterations in ventilatory strategies and the use of pharmacotherapies, such as the intratracheal administration of budesonide and a surfactant, caffeine, low-dose hydrocortisone or dexamethasone, and vitamin A. However, these interventions only mitigate BPD symptoms and do not address underlying alveolar and vascular structural deficiencies. Novel therapies are urgently required to tackle delayed lung development in preterm infants with BPD, the increasing number of complications, and the limitations of current treatments.
Reinitiating lung development aims to enhance pulmonary vascularization, development, and regeneration while supporting the alveolarization process in BPD. Combined with conventional therapies, they may enhance long-term respiratory outcomes. Stem/progenitor cells are pivotal in preserving lung structure and facilitating tissue repair in lung disorders, while growth factors are cytokines that regulate and stimulate cell proliferation. These therapies seek to enhance the multipotency of various stem/progenitor cells and activate the repair and regenerative capabilities of existing cells. Although replacement approaches involving stem/progenitor cells and growth factors are established in managing adult and pediatric diseases, their application in neonatal diseases, especially in preterm infants, remains experimental. Therefore, this review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the advancements in reinitiating lung development for the treatment of BPD.

Basic biological processes of lung development
Lung development progresses through embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, and alveolar phases, with the alveolar stage extending into adolescence (Fig. 1). During embryonic development, the lung bud appears in the foregut endoderm. It undergoes repetitive branching, leading to the generation of a bronchial tree and pulmonary vascular structures. Branching morphogenesis results in the formation of airways and peripheral acinar buds during the pseudoglandular phase, which later develop into alveoli. In the canalicular phase, respiration becomes feasible as the acinus forms, the blood–gas barrier develops, airway epithelial cells differentiate, and primitive gas-exchange units are produced. Primitive alveoli emerge during the saccular phase, while terminal saccules form and respiratory bronchioles develop simultaneously as distal bronchioles stretch, branch, and widen. During the saccular–alveolar transition phase, peripheral saccules undergo further expansion, augmenting the gas exchange area, with alveolar type 1 (AT1) cells gradually covering the surface. Meanwhile, AT2 cells undergo differentiation and increase secretion of surfactant lipids and proteins, which are then deposited in lamellar bodies. Postnatal alveolar development and differentiation is crucial for preterm newborns, occurring primarily in the first two years after birth.

The development of the lungs continues into adolescence. The first four phases of human lung development are usually completed in utero, with the alveolar phase occurring primarily after birth
Susceptibility to BPD is closely associated with variations in lung alveolar development, particularly in extremely preterm infants subjected to additional oxygen or mechanical ventilation during the canalicular or saccular phases. Prematurity itself significantly impairs alveolarization and proper lung growth, leading to long-term complications, including persistent structural changes, respiratory tract infections and asthma-like symptoms, and an increased prevalence of BPD-related pulmonary hypertension. Current pharmacotherapeutic approaches only alleviate observable BPD symptoms, failing to address aspects such as compromised alveolarization and inflammation, necessitating the investigation of alternative treatment methods. Consequently, identifying novel and viable therapeutic approaches is crucial to enhance the quality of life of preterm birth. Recent focus has turned to reinitiating lung development, aiming to restore stem/progenitor cell multipotency and stimulate cell proliferation and the repair capacity of already differentiated cells. In the following sections, we summarize recent advancements in this field.

Multipotency of stem/progenitor cells in BPD
Stem/progenitor cells play a pivotal role in the maintenance and repair of lung structure and function. Changes in quantity and efficacy of lung stem/progenitor cells occur in newborns with BPD, emphasizing the potential of external stem/progenitor cells to protect or restore injured lung tissues, given their ability for self-renewal and differentiation into numerous cell types. The essence of stem cell differentiation is cell fate determination and transformation. During early embryonic development, primordial cells undergo a process of division, differentiation, and tissue formation, resulting in a lineage of cells with increasing degrees of differentiation. Induced differentiation of stem cells is required for stem cells to express specific genes and thus differentiate into specific cell types. Endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and epithelial progenitor cells are pluripotent stem cells capable of differentiation. These cells could serve as reactivating lung development processes for BPD (Table 1). Stem cell therapy refers to the transplantation of healthy stem cells into the patient’s body and the induction of stem cell differentiation to achieve tissue regeneration and repair. The efficacy of these therapies is attributed to stem cell proliferation, differentiation in vivo, and intercellular communication, fostering lung healing. Clinical trials and preclinical studies on cell therapy demonstrate favorable outcomes in reducing BPD incidence and promoting lung repair and development.

Stem/progenitor cell therapies for BPD in clinical trials
For the time being, stem cell therapy has not entered the stage of widespread clinical application due to neonatal intricacies, particularly in preterm infants. Although few in number, clinical trials exploring MSCs, mononuclear cells (MNCs), and human amniotic epithelial cells (hAECs) for BPD treatment, primarily derived from autologous umbilical cord blood (UCB), umbilical cord tissue (UCT), the placenta, amniotic fluid, or bone marrow, have demonstrated great potential. We will present them individually in the sections that follow.

MSCs
MSCs are considered optimal repair cells owing to their genetic stability, pleiotropic properties, and simple isolation and proliferation. Originating from the splanchnic mesoderm during the embryonic stage, MSCs differentiate into specialized mesenchymal lineages via paracrine signals and mitochondrial regulation. These lineages play a crucial role in lung morphogenesis processes such as airway branching from embryonic to alveolar phases, alveolarization in the alveolar phases, and vascular development in the pseudoglandular phase. Peribronchial mesenchyme MSCs facilitate smooth muscle and endothelial cell differentiation, with fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10) stimulating conductive airway growth and Wnt and Sonic hedgehog (SHH) pathways regulating epithelial branching. The crosstalk between mitochondrial networks and other cell signaling pathways may influence MSC development, migration, and paracrine signaling during airway branching. MSCs also differentiate into alveolar epithelial cells via paracrine signals, such as SHH and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Mesenchymal cells release VEGF, FGFs, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) to influence alveoli formation, growth, and differentiation. Mesenchymal mitochondrial activity and distribution, controlled by the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway, play crucial roles in alveolarization. Depleting β-catenin in MSCs disrupts vascular patterns. MSCs interact with damaged lung tissue through paracrine signaling of anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic bioactive substances in the microenvironment via extracellular vesicles (EVs). Therefore, exogenous MSCs and MSC-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) therapies have been utilized to inhibit numerous pathogenic pathways in BPD and restart lung development. A prospective longitudinal study of 200 preterm infants demonstrated that UCB and UCT collections generate significantly elevated levels of hematopoietic stem cells (HPSC) and MSCs in neonates with BPD. A systematic analysis encompassing 53 studies and evaluating 15 distinct cell-based treatments for preclinical BPD identified MSCs as the most effective treatment in rodent hyperoxic models. Pilot clinical trials assessing allogeneic MSCs derived from UCB for BPD treatment in preterm infants with a GA of 23–28 weeks, as well as three phase I and one phase II dose-escalation study, demonstrated potential efficacy and short-term safety through intratracheal or intravenous administration. Ongoing clinical trials are continued to evaluate the benefits of MSCs in the treatment for BPD, with a two-year follow-up trial confirming no long-term adverse effects in young children.
Given the limitations of MSC therapy, such as higher risk of immunogenicity and challenges in production, sterilization, and storage, cell-free interventions focusing on the MSC secretome provide an attractive new approach for BPD treatment or prevention. EVs, which act as the secretome of MSCs, imitate MSC characteristics and play a significant role in their paracrine actions. Intratracheal administration of human, clinical-grade MSC-EVs demonstrated better results than MSC in alveolarization and pulmonary vascularization parameters in a BPD rat model. A phase I/II clinical trial has commenced to assess the safety and efficacy of endotracheal administration of umbilical cord MSC-EVs to preterm newborns with a GA of 23–28 weeks (NCT06279741, Registration Date: 2023-12-28). Hence, MSC-EVs present great potential as an innovative therapeutic approach for BPD, offering a cell-free technique that circumvents the issues associated with viable MSC treatment.

hAECs
hAECs, sourced from the amniotic membranes of full-term placentas, exhibit characteristics of both embryonic and pluripotent stem cells. They can differentiate into the three primary germ layers and exhibit properties such as non-rejection, non-matchmaking, limited proliferation, and non-tumorigenicity, ensuring their therapeutic safety. Beyond their anti-inflammatory effects, hAECs may differentiate into lung epithelial cells, demonstrating anti-fibrotic abilities and mitigating peripheral pulmonary artery remodeling to improve lung function. Moreover, hAEC treatment restores alveolar structure and increases pulmonary capillary bed density, contributing to pulmonary angiogenesis in the pseudoglandular phase. Like MSCs, hAECs influence T cell regulation and immune responses by encouraging macrophage polarization toward a pro-repair M2 phenotype through paracrine pathways.
In preclinical animal models of BPD, hAECs exhibited anti-inflammatory effects by reducing inflammatory cell presence and the levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, TGF-β, interferon-γ, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-10, and IL-6. Early treatment with hAECs (12 h after hyperoxia initiation) proved more effective than later treatment (4 days after hyperoxia initiation) in a BPD preclinical model. Additionally, term hAEC-EVs displayed therapeutic efficacy in a mouse model of BPD-like pulmonary injury, unlike preterm hAEC-EVs. In a phase I pilot trial involving six EPIs with established BPD, a single intravenous infusion of 1 × 106 hAECs/kg was safe and feasible (ACTRN12614000174684). Encouraging results from the 2-year follow-up of this study suggest that low-dose allogeneic hAECs can be safely administered to preterm infants. The safety of earlier and higher doses of hAECs for the prophylactic and reparative BPD treatment is being assessed in a phase I dose-escalation trial (ACTRN12618000920291).
Unlike MSCs, hAECs can be readily and abundantly isolated without the need for cell expansion or manipulation. Moreover, allogeneic hAECs are safe and well-tolerated by preterm infants. However, randomized placebo-controlled dose-escalation trials are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of hAECs in preventing BPD in high-risk infants.

MNCs
MNCs, primarily composed of HPSCs and MSCs, were initially viewed as supplements for human stem cell transplantation, serving as an additional reservoir of stem cells with a significant quantity of primordial multipotent stem cells, progenitor cells, and regulatory T cells. MNCs include a variety of unique pluripotent progenitor cell populations capable of self-renewal, differentiation, and tissue regeneration, restoring function post-injury. Furthermore, MNCs can be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells, increasing their phenotypic conversion potential. Infusion of cord blood-derived MNCs (CB-MNCs) exerted considerable anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory effects, induced alveolarization in the alveolar phase, and facilitated tissue healing. CB-MNC transplantation notably improved overall lung motion while preserving pulmonary vascular flow homeostasis.
In a preclinical investigation using a typical mouse model of BPD, both CB-MNCs and umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) were effective in treating BPD. However, CB-MNC transplantation was superior to UC-MSCs administration in restoring lung morphogenesis and balancing inflammatory responses. In a clinical study (NCT03053076) involving 31 preterm infants with a GA < 35 weeks, the infusion of autologous cord blood MNCs (ACBMNCs) markedly reduced the duration of mechanical ventilation and the need for supplemental oxygen. Similarly, in a single-center, prospective, non-randomized placebo-controlled trial (NCT02999373), compared with 33 controls, 29 very preterm infants (VPI) with GA < 32 weeks who received an intravenous infusion of fresh ACBMNCs within 24 h of enrollment showed a significantly lower incidence of moderate or severe BPD. Furthermore, a 2-year follow-up revealed better long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes in the intervention group than the control group. Additionally, infusing CB-MNCs shortly after delivery decreased proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and C-reactive protein, while increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10. An ongoing multicenter randomized placebo-controlled trial (NCT04440670) is investigating the efficacy of ACBMN infusion in preventing BPD in EPI with GA < 28 weeks.

Stem/progenitor cell therapies for BPD in preclinical studies
In addition to clinical trials and advantageous applications of lung development reactivation therapies facilitated by stem/progenitor cells in BPD, several preclinical findings from animal studies on EPCs and epithelial cells have revealed the protective effect of these cells on lung structure. Lung-resident stem/progenitor cells belong to epithelial cells deriving from the endoderm and endothelial and mesenchymal cell lines originating from the mesoderm (Fig. 3). Importantly, malfunction or depletion of these cells contribute to BPD pathogenesis.

EPCs
Pulmonary vasculature plays a critical role in both normal and impaired lung development. Several paracrine and juxtacrine interactions among epithelial, mesenchymal, and vascular progenitor cells govern the formation of the pulmonary vasculature. EPCs contribute significantly to lung injury recovery by participating in microvasculature development and repair in the alveolar stage. EPCs can develop into hematopoietic and lymphatic cells, characterized by surface markers such as CD34, CD133, CD31, VEGF receptor (VEGFR)-2, stem cell antigen-1, VE-cadherin, von Willebrand factor, and FLT-1 (VEGFR-1). Essential signaling pathways in EPCs, including VEGF-A, SHH, Forkhead Box-F1, Angiopoietin 1, and Ephrin, critically regulate pulmonary microvascular development. Enhanced EPC mobilization and angiogenesis are correlated with endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), underscoring the importance of NO in EPC-induced angiogenesis. Consequently, EPCs play a pivotal role in modulating lung vascular development and homeostasis in newborns with BPD. Currently, significant progress has been made in clarifying the association between critical angiogenic growth factors, lung-resident EPCs, and BPD.
Clinical investigations provide additional evidence of EPC impairments in neonatal pulmonary injury. EPCs are categorized into two distinct cell types: endothelial colony-forming cells (ECFCs) and myeloid angiogenic cells. A study including 62 preterm newborns revealed a notable reduction in ECFCs in the UCB of those who developed moderate or severe BPD, whereas elevated levels of ECFCs were associated with protection against BPD development. ECFCs from preterm infants are particularly vulnerable to hyperoxia, resulting in a decline in their overall functional ability. These findings support further investigation into the therapeutic capabilities of EPCs in animal models. Intravenous administration of human UCB-derived ECFCs to newborn mouse models exposed to hyperoxia improved lung function, vascular growth in the alveoli and lungs, colony and capillary network formation ability, and lowered PH. Using ECFCs from human UCB is feasible, effective, and safe. In a hyperoxia-induced BPD mouse model, embryonic stem cell-derived EPCs successfully integrated into the lung microvasculature, facilitated vascular remodeling in the alveoli, increased the number of local and circulating endothelial cells, and promoted alveolarization. An in vitro study revealed that exosomes derived from EPCs contribute to paracrine secretion, promoting angiogenesis by enhancing endothelial cell functions such as proliferation and migration in response to oxidative stress. Well-designed preclinical investigations are essential for establishing a firm foundation for developing safe pro-angiogenic cell products for clinical use.
However, the immunogenicity of EPCs limits their potential as an allogeneic source of cell-based therapy. Additionally, the risk of tumor formation poses another drawback to using EPCs as a therapeutic agent. Nevertheless, several possible remedies, including EPC paracrine actions, ex vivo genetic modifications, and alternate cell sources, are currently under investigation.

Basal cells
In the airways, basal cells (BCs) represent the earliest and most basic form of epithelial progenitor cells and exhibit self-renewing ability. This process occurs both under normal conditions and in response to acute lung injury to restore the balance and stability of the lungs. Tumor protein 63, cytokeratin (KRT) 5, and podoplanin are widely recognized markers for identifying BCs. Under normal circumstances, BCs exhibit increased KRT8 expression prior to their differentiation into luminal cells, forming an intermediate KRT8 + basal luminal precursor cell. Luminal precursor cells in the intermediate stage can further differentiate into secretory cells (SCs), ciliated cells, and pulmonary neuroendocrine cells (PNECs). Studies on mice have shown that BCs are generated during prenatal growth and contribute to the population of luminal cells.
Furthermore, BCs significantly influence the regeneration and repair processes and may also be associated with the development of chronic lung disease. Nonetheless, our understanding of BCs in preterm infants remains limited, owing to constraints in isolating these cells. Shui et al. developed a minimally invasive method for isolating BCs and tested their behavior and differentiation programs in nasopharyngeal aspirates. They demonstrated that BCs unique to preterm infants, compared to term infants, had reduced ciliary function as they differentiated and exhibited a gene expression program promoting cell proliferation while inhibiting functions such as autophagic cell survival and control of mitochondrial quality. This finding holds promise for the developing therapeutic interventions targeting neonatal lung diseases such as BPD.

SCs
The main SCs associated with BPD pathogenesis include club cells (CCs), variant club cells (VCCs), and goblet cells (GCs). CCs are recognized as secondary stem cells in the airways as they are precursors of ciliated and mucus-secreting cells. They produce Clara cell secretory protein (e.g., CC10 and CC16), bronchiolar surfactants, and proteases. Decreased CC16 levels indicate the initiation of pulmonary injury, a significant contributor to BPD development. Moreover, premature newborns’ ciliated cells exhibit reduced rhythmic movement of their cilia, rendering the epithelium more susceptible to external stimuli and damage. BCs replenish CCs, and their differentiation is maintained via Notch signaling. The basal-like cells that appear after injury are the offspring of clara cell secreted protein+ (CCSP+) cells and may differentiate into pseudostratified airway epithelial cells. VCCs, a subset of CCs that lack cytochrome P450 2F2 (CYP2F2) expression, exhibit damage resistance and may be regarded as progenitors of epithelial tissue in the small airways after injury. They can regenerate damaged epithelium through self-renewal and differentiation into ciliated cells and GCs in the pseudoglandular stage, which can self-renew and transform into mixed ciliated epithelial cells. GCs exhibit many vesicle-bound mucin granules that help them form mucus, yielding a mucous gel layer contributing to the mucociliary cleaning process and captures pathogens. GCs hyperplasia and metaplasia can occur in patients with long-term pulmonary disorders, suggesting that stem cell pool deficiency may impair host defense and disrupt the regular turnover and renewal of epithelial cells.
Newborns with BPD exhibit fewer CCs, and their bronchiolar epithelium expresses less CC-derived protein secretoglobin (SCGB) 1A1, possibly due to the additional oxygen supplements administered to the infants, which may rapidly reduce CCs. Preterm newborns with low CC16 levels in the UCB are more likely to develop BPD. CC16 not only promotes epithelial proliferation and preventing oxidative stress but also suppresses pulmonary inflammation and enhances airway regeneration, as observed in a mouse model.

PNECs
PNECs, a relatively rare (< 1%) subset of airway multifunctional epithelial cells, have recently gained attention as intrapulmonary sensors linked to many pulmonary diseases. PNECs, generated from the endoderm together with BCs, are the first cells to originate from airway progenitors. They are distributed throughout the bronchial tree, with their quantity increasing from the bronchi to the terminal bronchioles. The Notch and gastrin-releasing peptide receptor (GRPR) signaling pathways actively contribute to differentiation between PNECs and non-PNECs. The notch signaling pathway also regulates PNEC transformation into club and ciliated cells throughout the regeneration process. Further, PNECs function as chemosensory cells that are increasingly recognized as the primary epithelial cells responsible for perceiving oxygen levels and chemical or physical stimuli in the airways. Optimal clustering of PNECs is crucial for limiting the infiltration of the immune system into the lungs of neonates. As occasional hypoxic events are associated with BPD, hypoxia is most likely to trigger PNEC overactivation and hyperplasia. Increased bombesin-like peptide (BLP) and gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP) levels in PNECs have been suggested as underlying causes and possible treatment targets for BPD. Moreover, in BPD, BLP governs the alveolarization in the alveolar stage and angiogenesis processes in the pseudoglandular stage. GRP, similar to other BLPs, influences the contractile function of smooth muscle cells and effectively triggers bronchoconstriction through the GRPR.
The number and peptide composition of PNECs are altered in infants with BPD. Cullen et al. discovered that newborns with higher amounts of BLP in their urine between 1 and 4 days after birth had a 10 times higher risk of BPD. Urinary levels of GRP during the first week after birth are positively associated with BPD diagnosis. Administering BLPs directly into the trachea of mice increased the number of alveolar myofibroblasts and the thickness of the alveolar walls. Deletion of the mouse Grpr gene may alleviate these BPD-like symptoms, indicating that excessive BLP synthesis in PNECs may contribute to BPD development by activating GRPR signaling.

Bronchioloalveolar stem cells
Bronchioloalveolar stem cells (BASCs), a population of lung-resident stem cells at the bronchioalveolar duct junctions (BADJs), play a role in the regeneration of both the distal airways and alveoli. They influence the survival, proliferation, and migration of alveolar epithelial cells and bronchiolar CCs via the PI3K/AKT pathway. BASCs expressing cellular markers of AT2 cells (surfactant protein C and secretoglobin 1A1) can undergo self-renewal and develop into AT1 cells, AT2 cells, CCs, and ciliated cells. BASCs have the potential for in vivo lung repair and regeneration. In a hyperoxia-induced BPD mouse model, compared with the controls, treatment with bone marrow-derived MSCs or MSC-conditioned medium improved lung parenchymal and vascular damage and increased BASC levels. MSCs and MSC-derived substances (such as VEGF and basic FGF) may activate BASCs and assist in repairing alveolar cells after pulmonary injury in patients with BPD. However, BASCs account for < 1% of the overall pulmonary cell population and are confined to the BADJs, limiting their use in BPD treatment. Further research is also needed on the complex interactions between signals that control homeostasis, regeneration, and tumorigenesis before using BASCs in clinical practice. Moreover, additional research is necessary to evaluate the control of BASC growth, viability, specialization, and development.

Alveolar progenitor cells
The distal alveolar zone is covered by squamous type 1 and cuboidal type 2 alveolar epithelial cells, commonly known as AT1 and AT2, respectively. AT1 cells are mostly responsible for gas exchange, whereas AT2 cells maintain lung integrity. AT2 cells are alveolar progenitors with self-renewal abilities. Following pulmonary injury, AT2 cells undergo rapid proliferation and differentiation into AT1 cells during the saccular–alveolar transition phase, which play a crucial role in maintaining balance in the alveolar environment. The AT2 cell regeneration capacity renders them attractive for future therapeutic intervention. Impairment of AT2 cells is well recognized as the pathological foundation for BPD progression. During BPD, the alveolar epithelium endures injury, and the process of transforming AT2 cells into AT1 cells is retarded, simultaneously increasing the transdifferentiation of AT2 cells into mesenchymal cells and enhancing epithelial–mesenchymal transition. Notably, the proliferation of AT2 cells was increased in preterm baboons receiving oxygen therapy and mechanical ventilation, as well as in humans with severe BPD. Isolated AT2 cells were injected to prevent structural and functional lung damage in a well-established oxygen-induced BPD mouse model. The results verified the procedure’s effectiveness and safety of administration. Nonetheless, further studies are required to address the challenge of effectively isolating an adequate number of AT2 cells. In addition, researchers have recently discovered several subpopulations of AT2 cells based on their level of activation, adding complexity to the potential treatment strategies using these cells.

Pluripotency of growth factors in BPD
Growth factors are a group of cytokines that regulate and promote cell growth. Deficiencies in key growth factors during the fetal period increase the risk of preterm birth, leading to organ dysfunction and poor long-term outcomes, particularly in BPD. A key feature of BPD is persistent inflammation, which leads to impaired alveolarization and aberrant response to proliferation and differentiation signals while also disrupting cell–cell communication. The transformation of endodermal cells into lung epithelial cells requires the expression and involvement of numerous cytokines, which are essential for early lung morphogenesis and growth, as well as for the development of the pulmonary vasculature. Dysregulation of several growth factors, such as VEGF, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), FGF10, and TGF-β, plays an important role in the pathogenesis of BPD. The next generation of replacement therapy using growth factors might represent a new mode of treatment for BPD by regulating pulmonary morphogenesis, angiogenesis, and alveolar regeneration. VEGF
From the embryonic stage, VEGF, whose expression levels are highest in the lung, plays a key role in fetal development and blood vessel formation. Large pulmonary capillary beds are essential for efficient gas exchange between alveoli and capillaries. Lung capillary maturation is a key process modulated by VEGF and regulates angiogenesis under physiological and pathological conditions. VEGF is secreted by MSCs and influences EPC differentiation. MSCs can differentiate into endothelial cells in vitro under VEGF treatment. VEGF acts on cells through two different tyrosine kinase receptors: VEGFR-1 and VEGFR-2. VEGF expression is affected by hyperoxia and is regulated by IGF-1 through the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and AKT signaling pathways, thereby affecting endothelial repair, regeneration, and angiogenesis.
A retrospective cohort study reported that lower plasma VEGF levels during the first week of life were associated with respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) in preterm newborns. Further, a pilot study involving 43 premature infants born at a GA < 34 weeks revealed that BPD may be linked to reduced tracheal and gastric VEGF levels in the first week of life. Moreover, a mouse model with reduced VEGF expression exhibited increased apoptosis of alveolar and bronchiolar cells, expanded airspaces, and persistent effects for a minimum of 8 weeks. This underlines the crucial regulatory functions of epithelial-expressed VEGF in pulmonary development and function. MSC-EVs effectively mitigated hyperoxic injuries in newborn rats, both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, VEGF administration via EVs appeared to play a crucial role as a paracrine agent in protecting against lung injury caused by hyperoxia.

IGF-1
IGF-1 promotes mitosis, stimulates cell proliferation and DNA synthesis, and is required for lung development. In addition, IGF-1 promotes the transformation of AT2 to AT1 and contributes to lung tissue development by regulating the alveolar epithelium and airway BCs in a paracrine or autocrine manner. IGF-1, as a downstream factor of the VEGF signaling pathway could play a protective role in angiogenesis and promote endothelial differentiation and regeneration. In addition, IGF-1 may attenuate postnatal injury-induced pulmonary fibrosis by reducing TGF-β activity. The regulation of biological processes associated with BPD via IGF-1 is intricately linked to the initiation and progression of BPD in premature infants. Hyperoxia disrupts the interaction between IGF-1 and its receptor, IGF-1R, thereby impeding the growth of pulmonary tissue and obstructing the development of normal alveoli and microvasculature, ensuing pathological alterations in BPD.
An observational study showed a significant association between BPD severity and decreased serum IGF-1 levels in 90 preterm newborns with a GA of < 32 weeks. Two weeks of daily intraperitoneal administration of recombinant human IGF-1/binding peptide 3 (rhIGF-1/rhIGF-BP3) stimulated pulmonary angiogenesis, protected the development and structure of the alveoli, enhanced pulmonary function, and triggered AT2 and pulmonary endothelial cell proliferation in a preclinical study. A phase II clinical study investigating the efficacy and safety of rhIGF-1/rhIGF-BP3 in preventing retinopathy of prematurity revealed that the risk of BPD decreased significantly in 61 treated newborns. A phase 2b multicenter randomized study is ongoing to determine whether rhIGF-1/rhIGF-BP3 administration prevents severe BPD in EPI with GA < 28 weeks (NCT03253263).

FGF10
FGF10 is a key growth factor expressed by the lung mesenchyme that promotes organ morphogenesis. From the embryonic phase, FGF-10 regulates branching morphogenesis, promotes the extension and branching of developing airways, stimulates epithelial cell proliferation, and facilitates alveolar regeneration. Its expression is restricted to mesenchymal niches located in the vicinity of AT2 cells in the alveoli and lipofibroblasts (LIFs), a subpopulation of fibroblasts. As a potential new source of FGF10, LIFs contain lipid droplets, the latter being the main source of lipids for the production of surfactant lipoproteins in AT2 cells. The mesenchymal release of FGF10 is associated with increased VEGF expression in the epithelium. Alveolar LIFs-derived FGF10 stimulates the local proliferation of AT2 cells by FGF10-FGF receptor (FGFR)2 activation, which leads to the differentiation into AT1. Adventitial fibroblast-derived FGF10 promotes FGFR2 and FGFR1 signaling in the vascular smooth muscle and endothelial compartments, stimulating pulmonary vasculature remodeling. FGF10 signaling is dysregulated in several pulmonary diseases, including BPD. FGF10 may be a target of the inflammatory process in BPD. Interactions between NF-κB, Sp1, and Sp3 lead to the inhibition of Fgf10 expression.
Mohamed et al. discovered that low concentrations of FGF-10 in the UCB could contribute to both the development and severity of BPD. Reduced levels of FGF10 in a mouse model of BPD caused their early death due to severe damage to the alveoli, delayed development of AT2 cells, and reduced production of surfactants. Furthermore, an in vivo study demonstrated that the administration of recombinant FGF10 induced de novo alveologenesis in a mouse model of BPD.

TGF-β
The TGF-β superfamily of growth factors performs various roles in development, homeostasis, repair, and fibrosis by activating both canonical (Smad) and noncanonical signaling pathways. These pathways play a crucial role in various phases of fetal and postnatal lung development, as well as in several key aspects of BPD pathogenesis, such as parenchymal fibrogenesis and restructuring of the pulmonary vascular system. TGF-β isoforms show distinct temporal differences in their expression patterns during lung growth: TGF-β1 and TGF-β3 are present during the initial saccular development stage, while TGF-β2 is found in mature epithelial cells. Additionally, other members of the TGF-β protein superfamily play critical roles in both normal lung development and the pathogenesis of BPD. For instance, bone morphogenetic protein signaling contributes to normal branching morphogenesis in the developing lung, occurring during both the saccular and alveolar stages of development. Growth differentiation factor 15 possesses anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and anti-apoptotic properties. Its deficiency accelerates neonatal hyperoxic lung damage and developmental alveolar arrest. Medications such as azithromycin, caffeine, postnatal steroids, and retinoic acid, used to prevent and treat BPD, may interfere with TGF-b signaling.
In a cross-sectional study of 121 premature births who later developed BPD, TGF-β1 expression in tracheal aspirates within the first week of life showed an increase. Thrombospondin (TSP-1) is a TGF-β1-activating anti-angiogenic protein. By inhibiting the up-regulation of TGF-β1 activity mediated by TSP-1 in a neonatal BPD-like rat model, vascular hypoplasia and chronic pulmonary hypertension were prevented, and alveolar hypoplasia was partially ameliorated. Further exploration of more targeted therapeutic strategies for TGF-β and its pathways in the management of BPD in large clinical and preclinical studies is still needed in the future. Direct targeting of TGF-β to prevent BPD has long been regarded as a promising strategy; nevertheless, it is not possible to implement a comprehensive inhibition of TGF-β function.

Conclusion
Cellular analysis techniques have significantly enhanced our understanding of stem/progenitor cells, cytokines, and the genetic basis of lung development in preterm infants. Recognizing the limitations of traditional BPD therapeutic protocols, lung development restoration therapies appear very promising for maintaining lung homeostasis and effective regeneration and repair of lung structure and function. The findings of the latest or ongoing clinical trials and preclinical studies on lung development reinitiation demonstrate favorable outcomes in reducing BPD incidence and promoting lung repair and development. The induced differentiation of stem/progenitor cells and the spatiotemporal expression patterns of growth factors associated with lung developmental processes are critical for cellular therapeutic strategies for BPD. This review represents a comprehensive summary of the advancements in lung development-reinitiating therapies for BPD. However, several interesting questions regarding lung development restoration therapies should be addressed in future studies. Further clinical trials are needed to establish conclusive and convincing associations. This review will aid our understanding of lung development- reinitiating therapies for BPD, providing possibilities for the optimization of future clinical trials and contributing to the advancement of cell-based precision medicine.
