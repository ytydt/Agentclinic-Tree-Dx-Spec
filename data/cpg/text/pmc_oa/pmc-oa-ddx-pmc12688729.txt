Travoprost intraocular implants: pioneering a new approach in ocular hypertension management
The increasing incidence of glaucoma and other ophthalmic diseases associated with elevated intraocular pressure has spurred the development of improved treatment strategies and therapies, aiming for better patient adherence and reduced treatment burden. While travoprost, a highly effective topical prostaglandin FP analogue, is frequently prescribed for patients with increased intraocular pressure, it presents challenges such as complex dosing regimens, poor adherence, and an increased risk of side effects stemming from chronic topical use. This comprehensive narrative review systematically investigates the contemporary status of travoprost intraocular implant, a device specifically engineered for sustained drug delivery to overcome the limitations of conventional topical travoprost formulations.The review synthesizes findings from studies discussing these implants and critically analyses the safety, efficacy profiles and patient tolerability detailed, including the two recent Phase 3 trials that led to its FDA approval, contextualizing them against other treatment modalities and therapies.While the advent of the iDose TR device marks a significant mark in the treatment and management of glaucoma and OHT, this review briefly explores other avenues for building on this technology and its potential impact on the future of glaucoma care.

Intraocular pressure (IOP), also known as ocular hypertension (OHT), occurs when the pressure inside the eye rises above normal levels. The mean normal IOP is approximately 15 mmHg, while patients with glaucoma typically have a mean IOP of 18 mmHg, with even small increases in pressure contributing to optic nerve damage. This pressure is maintained by a delicate balance of fluid production and drainage within the eye. When this balance is disrupted, typically due to impaired drainage in the trabecular meshwork, pressure builds and can lead to damage to the optic nerve. The optic nerve is responsible for transmitting visual information to the brain, and sustained high IOP is a major risk factor for glaucoma, affecting 76 million individuals aged 40–80, ranking as the second leading cause of blindness. While not everyone with high IOP develops glaucoma, early detection and management of increased pressure are crucial for maintaining eye health and preventing vision loss. Numerous studies demonstrate that reducing IOP can mitigate the risk of developing glaucoma in individuals with OHT and decelerate the progression of optic nerve damage and vision loss in those already affected by the condition. Hence, all approved treatments for glaucoma, whether pharmacological or surgical, prioritize reducing IOP to safeguard vision and effectively manage the condition. Clinical assessments for OHT include detailed ophthalmological examinations, such as gonioscopy, optic nerve evaluation, visual field tests, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) for retinal measurements. Additionally, assessing ocular blood flow (OBF) and choroidal thickness (CT) through Color Doppler imaging (CDI) and OCT can provide valuable insights into the risk factors and progression of OHT to glaucoma, aiding in the prevention of optic nerve damage. One such factor is lens thickness, where an increased lens thickness (>4.1 mm) has been associated with narrowing of Schlemm’s canal, increased pigment deposition in the trabecular meshwork, and reduced aqueous drainage, further contributing to elevated IOP and glaucoma risk.
This study provides a comprehensive overview of the pathophysiology and current treatment options for ocular hypertension (OHT). By evaluating the effectiveness of travoprost and comparing it with other treatment modalities, we highlight the superior benefits of prostaglandin-based treatments, particularly intraocular implants, over traditional methods such as eye drops or laser therapy. Our findings underscore the critical role of travoprost intraocular implants in managing elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) and preventing the progression to glaucoma. The integration of these innovative implants into future treatment strategies offers significant promise for enhancing patient care outcomes and maintaining a high quality of life.

Learning points
This review aims to provide a concise overview of the recently approved Travoprost Intraocular implant. Based on multiple successful trials, it elaborates on the long-term efficacy and safety of Travoprost Intraocular implants in comparison with other alternative treatment pathways including various prostaglandin analogues and other implants designed for glaucoma medication delivery, particularly those utilizing novel drug delivery systems. The review also highlights how these implants show immense potential in overcoming challenges related to patient adherence and medication persistence, which are critical factors in managing chronic conditions like glaucoma. Furthermore, it seeks to equip clinicians with appropriate knowledge on incorporating such implants into existing treatment regimens to improve patient adherence and potentially offer a more cost-effective long-term option while critically assessing the clinical implications and economic benefits of Travoprost Intraocular implants, offering a comprehensive perspective on their role in optimizing glaucoma management strategies.

Materials and methods

Literature search and data sources
A comprehensive literature search was performed utilizing multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The objective was to identify pertinent studies related to increased IOP. The search encompassed studies published up to the most recent available date in the respective databases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The selection criteria were carefully defined to ensure the inclusion of relevant and high-quality studies. Studies were considered if they focused on the pathophysiology, treatment, or management of elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) or ocular hypertension (OHT), specifically in relation to travoprost intraocular implants. Eligible studies included clinical trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, and other peer-reviewed literature available in English. Additionally, only those studies were included that presented measurable clinical outcomes such as reduction in IOP, safety profile, or long-term efficacy. Studies were excluded if they did not specifically address elevated IOP or OHT, were published in languages other than English, or contained duplicate data or redundant findings. Furthermore, case reports, commentaries, letters to the editor, and non-peer-reviewed articles were excluded, along with studies focusing on glaucoma management without specific reference to travoprost intraocular implants. These criteria ensured a focused and methodologically sound review of the available literature.

Search terms
The search terms were meticulously selected and refined, incorporating a blend of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and pertinent keywords. A range of search terms was employed, including “ocular hypertension,” “intraocular pressure,” “pathophysiology,” “prostaglandin-based treatments,” “treatment modalities,” “travoprost intraocular implants,” and “clinical trials.” These search terms were strategically combined and adapted for each specific database to ensure an optimal and inclusive retrieval of relevant studies.

Results and discussion

Pathophysiology of OHT
The pathophysiology of OHT involves elevated IOP without the presence of detectable glaucomatous damage to the optic nerve or visual field defects. The sympathetic nervous system regulates the secretion of aqueous humour, with beta-2 receptors causing increased production. Elevated IOP results from either increased production of aqueous humor by the ciliary body or impaired drainage through the trabecular meshwork or the uveoscleral outflow pathway.
Homeostatic regulation of IOP, however, relies primarily on regulating aqueous outflow through the trabecular meshwork. This regulation occurs through modulation of the resistance of the trabecular meshwork outflow tract in the juxtacanalicular region (region bordering Schlemm canal), likely at the level of the inner wall basement membrane. IOP forces produce mechanical stress on the cells of this layer, which initiates a signal cascade leading to increased activity of matrix metalloproteinases (specifically MMP14 and MMP2) with a resultant increase in cell turnover at the level of the trabecular meshwork, allowing increased aqueous humour outflow.
In OHT, the trabecular meshwork often exhibits increased resistance to aqueous outflow, a characteristic also seen in various forms of glaucoma, including open-angle glaucoma (OAG) and angle-closure glaucoma (ACG). Changes in the cellular composition, extracellular matrix, and cytoskeletal framework of the trabecular meshwork contribute to this resistance. Additionally, age-related lens enlargement and increased thickness (>4.1 mm) contribute to elevated IOP by mechanically narrowing Schlemm’s canal, increasing pigment deposition in the trabecular meshwork, and reducing aqueous outflow, ultimately leading to increased resistance and pressure buildup.
Elevated IOP can cause compression of the posterior segment of the eye, leading to formation and reconstruction of the lamina cribrosa, resulting in axonal damage and disruption of axonal transport mechanisms. Understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms in OHT and glaucoma is crucial for developing effective treatments.

Current treatment modalities
When considering treatment for increased IOP, evaluating IOP, ocular function, and morphology is essential, with treatment choices depending on IOP severity, patient factors, and potential side effects. Tailored pressure-lowering treatments should be regularly reassessed through follow-up visits to ensure they effectively minimize the risk of disease progression. This comprehensive approach sets the foundation for discussing treatment options and optimizing patient care.

Pharmacological treatment/eye drops
The first-line approach for managing elevated IOP, specifically in cases of OAG and OHT, is pharmacological intervention. Topical glaucoma medications are commonly prescribed to reduce elevated IOP in these individuals. Prostaglandin analogues (PGAs) such as bimatoprost, latanoprost, tafluprost, and travoprost are often the first-line therapy because they effectively lower IOP, are well-tolerated, and are considered safe for systemic use.
Eye drops are the cornerstone of glaucoma treatment, providing a convenient and effective method to lower IOP and preserve vision. These drops come in various classes, each with its own mechanism of action targeting different aspects of IOP regulation. However, it is important to recognize that some eye drops, such as β-blockers and α-2 agonists, may have potential systemic side effects, necessitating careful consideration when prescribing them. Adherence to medication can be challenging for some patients due to factors like forgetfulness, difficulty in administration, or side effects. In cases where eye drops are insufficient in controlling IOP or are not tolerated well, other treatment options may be considered.

Laser
Lasers play a crucial role in the treatment of various types of increased IOP. Argon and YAG lasers have been mainstays in ophthalmic practice for years, effectively addressing these conditions. More recently, diode lasers have emerged as valuable tools in glaucoma management, providing treatment options, particularly in cases where medication alone fails to control the disease. Selective Laser Trabeculoplasty (SLT) has become the preferred first-line treatment for OAG and OHT over medication, based on comprehensive evidence. SLT provides stable, drop-free IOP control to many patients for at least three years, reducing the need for surgery while offering lower overall costs and comparable Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) outcomes.
While SLT effectively lowers IOP in glaucoma, it may lead to complications such as elevated IOP, iritis and choroidal effusion. Vigilant monitoring and prompt intervention are crucial for successful outcomes.

Surgery
When managing increased IOP, surgery often becomes a consideration, especially when the medications or laser therapy prove insufficient. Cataract surgery alone or combined with procedures like trabeculectomy represents a significant strategy, particularly in ACG cases. However, the efficacy of cataract surgery alone might be limited in OAG scenarios regarding IOP reduction. In such instances, combining cataract surgery with other surgical approaches becomes essential to achieve a more significant decrease in IOP and effectively manage the condition. For mild to moderate glaucoma, microinvasive glaucoma surgery is often performed in conjunction with cataract surgery, while more advanced cases require traditional trabeculectomy or tube shunt procedures for effective long-term pressure control.
Moreover, various techniques of circumferential Schlemm’s canal surgery have proven effective in managing different types of adult and pediatric glaucomas. However, long-term comparative studies are needed to further evaluate the safety and efficacy of these procedures.
Current treatment methods for increased IOP, though effective, have their own limitations and potential side effects. One of the biggest challenges is patient adherence, as eye drops can be difficult to administer and cause discomfort and may be easily rinsed out, requiring frequent administration and leading to poor patient compliance. Many medications also have side effects, such as ocular irritation and systemic effects like fatigue or dizziness. Additionally, laser therapy may not be effective for all patients, with success rates varying significantly and diminishing over time. It is also associated with post-procedure complications. Surgical procedures, often essential, come with notable risks such as infection, bleeding, and adverse reactions to anesthesia. There is also the potential for vision loss or permanent damage, which can significantly affect a patient’s quality of life. Therefore, there is a need for alternative treatment methods to administer IOP-lowering medication without relying on daily eye drops or laser treatments.

Travoprost intraocular implants offer a promising solution to many issues noted with conventional methods of ocular drug therapy. While ensuring that therapeutic concentrations of medication are consistently achieved at the ocular surface, this novel delivery system eliminates the need for frequent eye drop administration. Owing to its narrow dimensions, it anchors well into the trabecular meshwork in the anterior chamber from where it elutes regular doses of travoprost, a potent prostaglandin, over an extended period of time. A survey in France shows travoprost to have a comparably longer effectiveness profile than latanoprost, hence requiring fewer patients to undergo new treatment or surgery, offsetting the higher acquisition cost. The implant currently exists in two forms: fast-acting and slow-acting sustained-release implants, both showing favorable results in their respective clinical trials as summarized in Table 1.
This modern alternative to glaucoma treatment offers several advantages over traditional methods. The localized drug delivery system reduces the need for frequent administration, improving compliance and comfort without compromising efficacy. By delivering the medication directly to the eye with an elution profile that maintains nearly constant travoprost concentrations, IOP fluctuations are minimized which would otherwise predispose patients to progressive visual field damage, an adverse effect commonly seen with patients who continue topical medication. This method is also more convenient for patients who have difficulty administering eye drops, particularly those with poor dexterity or cognitive impairments. The high level of safety can be attributed to achieving the desired efficacy with a micro-elution rate of travoprost released intracamerally, thus reducing the daily dose of travoprost by an order of magnitude (at least 10× lesser dose) compared with the topical pulsatile delivery of travoprost via eye drops.
The implant is set to function for 4–5 years, after which it is replaced with a new one, ensuring long-term management of glaucoma without the need for frequent intervention. The travoprost intraocular implant is designed to function for 4–5 years, after which it requires replacement due to the gradual depletion of the drug reservoir. Over time, the implant continuously releases travoprost at a controlled rate, but once the medication is exhausted, a new implant is necessary to maintain effective IOP control. Having established itself as a singular, lasting alternative, it is also a cost-effective approach, provided that the commercial price of the implant is accounted for, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Its biodegradable nature also vouches for it as an ecologically safe choice, with minimal environmental impact compared to single-use eye drop bottles. Integrating travoprost intraocular implants in future treatment plans has evident potential to improve patient care without compromising the patients’ quality of life.

Mechanism of action of travoprost intraocular implants
Activation of FP receptors: Travoprost mimics prostaglandins due to its structural similarity to prostaglandin F2α (PGF2α), an endogenous lipid compound. Chemically, it resembles PGF2α and can, therefore, effectively bind to and activate the prostaglandin F2α receptor, also known as FP receptor, which is located in the ciliary muscle of the eye. These receptors are coupled to G proteins that generate inositol trisphosphate and diacylglycerol. These intracellular messengers act as triggers for protein kinase C, causing the ciliary muscle to relax and the uveoscleral outflow pathway to expand. This process ultimately allows more aqueous humor to flow through the eye, contributing to a decrease in intraocular pressure in conditions such as glaucoma.
Modulation of MMPs: Additionally, travoprost’s mechanism of action involves the regulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), an enzyme family crucial for the remodeling of tissues’ extracellular matrix. Travoprost is known to increase the expression of MMPs in the trabecular meshwork, such as MMP-1 and MMP-3, where the aqueous humor drains. This leads to the remodeling and restructuring of the trabecular meshwork, improving the efflux of aqueous humor and resulting in a reduction in IOP.
Potential Outflow Pathways:
The mechanism of action of the Travoprost intraocular implant centers around its sustained-release drug delivery system, designed to provide long-term IOP control with minimal patient intervention. Understanding how this implant works requires insight into both the pharmacology of travoprost and the innovative delivery mechanism of the implant itself. Berdahl et al state that the travoprost intraocular implant reduces IOP in patients with OAG or OHT by continuously releasing travoprost into the eye, which increases the outflow of aqueous humor through the uveoscleral pathway. Clinical trials and FDA approval for travoprost intraocular implants
Presently, three clinical trials have successfully evaluated the safety and efficacy of travoprost implants compared to regular eye drop solutions.
The initial study commenced as a phase II, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, introductory drug trial where 154 subjects were randomly assigned to receive either a fast-release implant, slow-release implant, or a sham procedure followed by topical timolol 0.5% for a 36-month evaluation period. The study ensured all participants had an initial baseline IOP of 25 mmHg before the implants were inserted. At 36 months, significant clinical and statistical IOP-lowering effects were noted after a single administration of the travoprost implant compared to BID timolol. Mean IOP reductions ranged from 7.6 to 8.8 mmHg for the FE implant, 7.3 to 8.0 mmHg for the SE implant, and 7.3 to 7.9 mmHg for timolol (P < 0.0001 for all groups at all visits) . The study documented no notable corneal endothelial cell loss or conjunctival hyperemia in either of the iDose groups. The trial also documents a 63% and 69% decrease in usage of glaucoma medication post-operatively in the fast and slow-eluting groups compared to a 45% decrease in the timolol arm of the study.
Likewise, two randomized, double-masked, prospective phase III clinical trials have also been conducted to analyze the efficacy of travoprost. Both these phase III trials have a nearly identical protocol, design, size, predefined endpoints, and randomization and involve a total of 1,150 participants across 89 clinical sites in the USA. Both implant arms (fast- and slow-release) in the phase III iDose TR pivotal trials displayed non-inferior results in comparison to the active comparator arm (topical timolol) through 3 months. As for NCT03519386, in 3-month period, the mean IOP changes from baseline ranged from −6.6 to −8.5 mmHg in the SE-implant group, from −6.6 to −8.4 mmHg in the FE implant group, and from −6.5 to −7.7 mmHg in the timolol group across the six timepoints. While for NCT03868124, the mean IOP reduction was −6.21 (0.27) mmHg for the SE-implant group, − 6.79 (0.27) mmHg for the FE-implant group, and −6.76 (0.26) mmHg for the timolol group. The implants exhibited strong safety profiles, with no corneal surface complications or endothelial cell loss. Additionally, no cases of periorbital fat atrophy were reported. Conjunctival hyperemia occurred in 3% of participants receiving the slow-release iDose TR, and the most common adverse event was mild, transient iritis, observed in 6% of participants in both phase III trials. Mode of administration
The administration of the travoprost implant is a precise surgical procedure that involves securely embedding the implant anchor within the sclera at the anterior chamber angle of the eye. This intraocular implant, made from microscopic titanium, facilitates the gradual release of travoprost. This procedure must be conducted under aseptic conditions, involving the utilization of sterile gloves and sterile drapes. On the day of surgery, an antibiotic drop is administered 30 minutes before the procedure begins. Prior to the surgery, the eye should be anaesthetized using general, retrobulbar, peribulbar, or topical anesthesia as per standard operating room procedures. An intracameral miotic can also be injected to deepen the angle prior to insertion of the iDose TR.
The iDose TR implantation procedure should be conducted under magnification to ensure a precise view of the anterior chamber angle. It is recommended to perform the implant surgery from the temporal side, using a clear corneal incision in that region. The implant will then be inserted through the angle and into the sclera on the nasal side.
The surgical procedure begins with the gonioprism being gently placed on the cornea using light-touch gonioscopy. The microscope is adjusted to focus on the trabecular meshwork, ensuring a clear view of all angle structures at the nasal implant site. The surgeon then makes a precise incision, around 2.4 mm in magnitude, in the transparent cornea at the temporal limbus location. Subsequently, a cohesive viscoelastic substance is introduced into the anterior chamber of the eye to optimize visualization of the angle.
The insertion of the implant is a meticulous process executed with precision. Vigilance is exercised to avoid inadvertent contact with the lens or cornea. Advancing towards the anterior chamber angle, the implant is directed towards the trabecular meshwork, aiming for secure anchoring through tissue compression. Following the firm embedding of the implant anchor in the sclera, a pause ensues to allow tissue relaxation. Subsequently, the implant is released from the inserter, with thorough observation to prevent displacement. Slight pressure is applied to confirm full anchorage into the scleral tissue. Finally, the withdrawal of the inserter concludes this intricate procedure.
Subsequent steps involve irrigating and aspirating the anterior chamber with balanced salt solution to eliminate residual viscoelastic, with pressure applied to the posterior edge of the incision if necessary for thorough removal. With the completion of these steps, the surgical procedure concludes, ensuring precise placement and functionality of the iDose TR implant in the trabecular meshwork.

Dosage
An intracameral implant, comprising 75 micrograms of travoprost, pre-filled within a single-use, disposable inserter, is designed for single-dose administration.

Side effects
The travoprost implant is effective in reducing IOP in patients with glaucoma or OHT but comes with a range of potential ocular side effects. Clinical trials have documented several side effects, including blepharitis (5.8%), darkening of the iris and periocular skin, cystoid macular edema, endophthalmitis, dry eyes (5.9%), eye pain (7.8%), foreign body sensation in the eye (7.6%), cataract formation (13.4%), decreased visual acuity, and intraocular infections.
In a study conducted on pediatric patients, the most frequent adverse effects related to treatment in the travoprost cohort were ocular hyperemia (17%) and eyelash growth (7%). Iris pigmentation changes are common with PGs, with over 90% of susceptible patients experiencing darkening within 2 years of therapy, especially with prolonged treatment.
The travoprost intraocular implant is also associated with various ocular infections such as conjunctivitis, iritis, eye pruritus, and blepharitis. To alleviate the risk of these ocular infections, fluoroquinolone antibiotics are administered prophylactically starting 1 day before surgery and continued for 1 week following the implantation of the travoprost intraocular implant or sham surgery.

Future prospects
The travoprost intraocular transplant has demonstrated potent efficacy in reducing IOP for up to 36 months following a single administration, significantly decreasing the need for topical IOP-lowering medications. Recent advancements have identified travoprost nanoemulsion as a promising novel drug delivery system, prepared using low-energy techniques. Studies show that travoprost nanoemulsions exhibit suitable nanodroplet size, zero potential, pH, refractive index, controlled release, and sufficient stability under accelerated conditions. Moreover, the nanoemulsion formulation is safe and non-irritant to ocular surfaces, making it a promising ocular delivery system capable of reducing IOP by 31% (peak; 32%–29%) and 29% (trough; 32%–25%).
Advancements in the drug delivery system or formulations could further enhance the efficacy of travoprost, potentially improving its market position. Additionally, more clinical trials should be conducted to achieve better clinical outcomes, to assess the safety and effectiveness of the drug, and explore novel formulations or delivery systems to improve its efficacy, convenience, and patient adherence. The affordability of travoprost is also a crucial factor, as high cost can impact patients’ adherence to prescribed doses. To address this, travoprost should be made available at an affordable price or pharmaceutical companies should offer a patient assistance program or prescription saving cards to help eligible individuals afford their medications.
Researchers might explore combining travoprost with other glaucoma drugs or treatments to achieve synergistic effects, leading to better patient outcomes. Since travoprost intraocular implant is a minimally invasive delivery technique, ongoing research may lead to the development of even less invasive techniques, reducing surgical risks and improving patient comfort. Future research should also aim to enhance its bioavailability and sustain its release due to its water-insoluble and oily nature.
Overall, the future prospects of travoprost intraocular implants appear promising, with ongoing research and technological advancements poised to improve treatment outcomes and quality of life for patients with OHT.

Limitations
Like any other drug, travoprost has its limitations. It is not recommended for children younger than 16 years due to its adverse side effects. As this implant involves a surgical procedure, many patients may not be suitable for the surgery or may prefer non-invasive treatment options. Surgical procedures carry risks, including inflammation, infection, and damage to intraocular structures. Patients allergic to travoprost or any ingredient in this medicine cannot undergo the travoprost intraocular implant. The duration of effectiveness of the implant is another concern, as it may be limited and require replacement, minor surgery or additional interventions over time. As the travoprost intraocular implant is relatively new, there is limited clinical experience and data available regarding its long-term safety and effectiveness. More clinical trials are needed to ensure the long-term safety and effectiveness of the travoprost intraocular implant.

Conclusion
In conclusion, the research underscores the significant potential of travoprost intraocular implants as a superior alternative for managing elevated IOP. The pathophysiology of OHT and its progression to glaucoma necessitate innovative treatment approaches. Travoprost implants offer a sustained, consistent drug delivery system that addresses the limitations of conventional methods such as eye drops and laser therapy. Clinical trials have demonstrated the implants’ efficacy in reducing IOP with minimal side effects, ensuring better patient compliance and comfort. The promising results highlight the implant’s capacity to maintain stable IOP levels, thereby minimizing the risk of optic nerve damage and preserving vision. Future integration of travoprost intraocular implants into treatment regimens could revolutionize glaucoma management, enhancing patient outcomes and quality of life.
