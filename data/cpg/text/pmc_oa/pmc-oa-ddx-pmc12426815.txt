Evaluation of Newborn Screening for Diseases Using C5‐OH as a Marker: Systematic Review of the Literature and Evaluation of 17 Years of C5‐OH Screening in the Netherlands
ABSTRACT
In 2007, the Dutch newborn screening (NBS) program was expanded to include C5‐OH as a marker to screen for three inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs): 3‐methylcrotonyl‐CoA carboxylase deficiency (3‐MCCD), 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA lyase deficiency (HMGCLD) and holocarboxylase synthetase deficiency (HLCSD). This study evaluates the effectiveness of C5‐OH as an NBS marker by analyzing data from neonates screened in the Dutch NBS program from 2007 to 2023 and by reviewing the literature on various IEMs detected by an elevated NBS C5‐OH concentration worldwide. Of the 126 neonates referred on the basis of elevated C5‐OH concentrations in the Netherlands, 46 were true positive cases. No missed cases in the Netherlands have been reported so far, resulting in a positive predictive value of 38.3% and a negative predictive value of 100%. Strikingly, there was notable overlap between C5‐OH concentrations of true and false positive cases. The systematic review included 58 articles and showed that C5‐OH concentrations of patients with different IEMs reported in the literature were insufficiently distinctive to differentiate between these diseases. While C5‐OH can be used to detect patients with 3‐MCCD, HCLSD, and HMGCLD, its value is limited by the overlap of C5‐OH concentrations between affected and unaffected neonates and among patients with different diseases. This emphasizes the need for improvement of the screening strategy and potentially the use of additional markers to increase its specificity.

Introduction
In 2007, the newborn screening (NBS) program in the Netherlands was extended with 13 inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs). The marker C5‐OH, measured and quantified using tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), was chosen as a primary screening marker, aiming to detect three different IEMs: 3‐methylcrotonyl‐CoA carboxylase deficiency (3‐MCCD, MIM #210200 and #210210), 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA lyase deficiency (HMGCLD, MIM #246450) and holocarboxylase synthetase deficiency (HLCSD, MIM #253270). The aim of this implementation was to allow early detection and start of treatment for these diseases to prevent disease complications and improve long‐term outcomes.
In all three diseases, the elevation of C5‐OH results from impaired catabolism of the amino acid leucine. Patients with 3‐MCCD present with a wide range of clinical manifestations. While the majority of patients remain asymptomatic, others may suffer from metabolic decompensations with hypoglycemia, hyperammonemia, ketonemia, and severe metabolic acidosis after triggering events such as an infection or fasting. Of the patients with HMGCLD, 50% present in the neonatal period and nearly all within the first year of life with acute, severe metabolic decompensations consisting of hypoglycemia, hyperammonemia, and metabolic acidosis during catabolism due to fasting, an infection, or increased protein intake. Patients with classical HLCSD present with metabolic acidosis and hyperammonemia, usually in the first days to weeks of life, although patients presenting later within the first 2 years of life have been reported as well.
In addition to the three aforementioned target diseases, it is known that there are several other conditions that might cause an elevated C5‐OH concentration, including a nutritional biotin deficiency and other IEMs such as beta‐ketothiolase deficiency (BKT; MIM #203750) and 3‐methylglutaconyl‐CoA hydratase deficiency (MGA, MIM #250950), complicating screening for the target diseases using C5‐OH. Furthermore, maternal 3‐MCCD can cause an elevated C5‐OH concentration in the neonate due to placental transmission of the marker.
Seventeen years after its inclusion in the Dutch NBS program, we evaluated the effectiveness of C5‐OH as an NBS marker for detecting 3‐MCCD, HMGCLD, and HLCSD to provide a comprehensive assessment of the current screening strategy and to evaluate the effectiveness of NBS for these rare IEMs. We analyzed the results of 17 years of NBS using C5‐OH as a marker in the Netherlands and performed a systematic review of the literature on the use of C5‐OH as a marker in NBS programs worldwide.

Methods

Evaluation of C5‐OH as a Marker in the Dutch NBS Program

Study Population and Data Sources
Data of all neonates screened for C5‐OH concentrations between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2013, and between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2021, were included in this study. Analysis of all neonates screened since 2007 was technically not feasible due to the large amount of data that would need to be processed. Therefore, two time spans were chosen that are representative of the periods in which two different tandem mass spectrometry kits were used to perform the analyses, as explained in the next section. Data were used anonymously and included: age at heel prick sampling, sex, gestational age, birth weight, and dried blood spot (DBS) C5‐OH concentration (μmol/L). Data were extracted from the registration system Praeventis of the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM, Bilthoven, the Netherlands).
Before 2023, heel prick data of all children could be used for scientific research, unless parents objected. Since January 1, 2023, explicit parental consent is required. Data of neonates whose parents objected to (2007–2023) or have not given consent (from 2023) for the use of their data for research purposes were excluded.
In addition, NBS C5‐OH concentrations and anonymized outcomes (true positive or false positive) of all neonates referred to a metabolic department of a Dutch University Medical Center (UMC) because of an elevated NBS C5‐OH concentration (≥ 1.0 μmol/L) between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2023, were analyzed. C5‐OH concentrations and the corresponding outcomes were retrieved from the registration system NEORAH of the RIVM. The total number of patients diagnosed with each of the three C5‐OH associated diagnoses was retrieved from the Dutch Diagnosis Registration Metabolic Diseases (DDRMD). Figure 1 provides an overview of the specific data obtained from each database.
Flow diagram illustrating what data is retrieved from the Praeventis, NEORAH, and DDRMD database and by who the data is collected. Since the patient data was received anonymously, data across these databases could not be linked, preventing the ability to associate records from the same individuals.
After referral to a metabolic department of a Dutch UMC, neonates with an elevated C5‐OH concentration at NBS underwent analysis of the carnitine profile in plasma and organic acids in the urine. Depending on the suspected target disease, enzyme activity was measured or genetic testing was performed according to the Dutch care pathways to confirm the suspected diagnosis. The treating physician recorded the final diagnoses in the DDRMD. The outcome in the DDRMD, rated as true positive or false positive, is subsequently recorded in the NEORAH database of the RIVM. A true positive case was defined as a neonate with an elevated NBS C5‐OH concentration, who, according to the information in the DDRMD, was subsequently diagnosed with one of the three target diseases (3‐MCCD, HLCSD or HMGCLD). A false positive result was defined as a neonate with an elevated NBS C5‐OH concentration who did not have any of the target diseases.

Sample Collection and Analysis
As part of the Dutch NBS program, DBS on filter paper are collected by heel prick. According to the protocols, the samples should be collected between 72 and 168 h after birth. For this study, all samples collected between 3 and 14 days after birth were included. The collected samples were analyzed in one of the five regional screening laboratories in the Netherlands. Until 2018, the NeoBase Non‐derivatized tandem MS Kit (Revvity, Turku, Finland) and a Waters Micro tandem MS instrument (Waters, Milford, MA, U.S.A.) were used. As of 2018, the NeoBase 2 Non‐derivatized tandem MS Kit (Revvity, Turku, Finland) and a Xevo TQD MS (Waters, Milford, MA, U.S.A.) were used. C5‐OH concentrations greater than 10 μmol/L and smaller than 0.1 μmol/L were reported as > 10 μmol/L and < 0.1 μmol/L, respectively. To allow analysis of these data, values of respectively 10 μmol/L and 0.05 μmol/L were used. Since the implementation of C5‐OH as a marker in the Dutch NBS program, the cut‐off value (COV) for C5‐OH measured in DBS has been 1.0 μmol/L. This COV has been established based on the results from the analysis of approximately 10 000 NBS samples, which were used to determine the reference value for C5‐OH in the screening population combined with the scientific literature available at the time. In all five screening laboratories in the Netherlands, the same MS/MS equipment, MS kit, and COV have been used.
Prior to the transition to the new MS/MS platform and kit in 2018, all screening laboratories conducted a verification of this platform and kit. This demonstrated that the new kit and instrument were comparable to the old one and that the results from the different laboratories were comparable; consequently, it was decided to not adjust the cut‐off value for C5‐OH. Neonates with an elevated NBS C5‐OH concentration are referred to the metabolic department of a UMC in the Netherlands for clinical and biochemical evaluation.

Study Outcomes
DBS C5‐OH concentrations of all neonates screened in the Dutch NBS laboratories were compared between the selected periods. C5‐OH concentrations of neonates with a negative, true positive, and false positive screening result were compared. To evaluate the effectiveness of C5‐OH as a marker in the Dutch NBS program, the positive and negative predictive value (PPV and NPV, respectively) was calculated. As a secondary outcome measure, the incidence of 3‐MCCD, HMGCLD, and HLCSD was calculated. 95% confidence intervals (CI) for incidence values were calculated using the Wilson score interval. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 28 and Microsoft Excel 365 Version 2402.

Systematic Review

Research Question
The primary research question of the systematic review is which IEMs are diagnosed following an elevated C5‐OH concentration detected through NBS. Secondary research questions are what NBS C5‐OH concentrations have been reported in these diseases, whether the NBS C5‐OH concentration can be used to differentiate between these diseases, what COVs are used for C5‐OH as a NBS marker, and what associated PPV and sensitivity of C5‐OH are reported in the literature.

Search Strategy
A literature search was conducted in the OVID MEDLINE and OVID Embase databases to collect published articles on C5‐OH as an NBS marker. Initially, the search was performed in 2021, with an update to include the results until February 21, 2024. The search strategy is displayed in Tables S1 and S2. The resulting articles were imported into the web‐based application Rayyan which was used for deduplication and selection of the articles. Two authors (A. M. B. and M. A. for initial search, M. A. and R. A. for updated search) independently screened the titles and abstracts for eligibility. Of the selected articles, the full texts were independently assessed for eligibility by M. A. and R. A. Discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. If no consensus could be reached, a third author (A. M. B.) made the final decision on study inclusion. Web of Science and Scopus were used for cross‐checking the references of included articles for potentially missed relevant publications, which were assessed by M. A. and R. A.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
We aimed to include all articles reporting on the use of DBS C5‐OH as a marker in NBS and reporting absolute NBS C5‐OH concentrations of neonates with a confirmed diagnosis. Articles written in languages other than English or Dutch were excluded.

Data Extraction and Analysis
Data extraction was performed separately by two authors (M.A. and R.A.) using a predefined template. Because of the heterogeneity of NBS programs in the different countries, the results were presented in a descriptive manner instead of a meta‐analysis. Mean and median C5‐OH concentrations as well as (interquartile) ranges were used to describe the data. If C5‐OH concentrations were reported for individual patients, mean and median C5‐OH concentrations were calculated. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 28 and Microsoft Excel 365 Version 2402.

Evaluation of C5‐OH as a Marker in the Dutch NBS Program

NBS Characteristics
Data of a total of 1 326 400 NBS samples collected during the two selected time periods (2010–2013 and 2018–2021) were available, of which 1 315 861 (99.2%) first heel prick samples were included for analysis of the reported DBS C5‐OH concentrations. Between 2010 and 2013, 35 neonates were referred because of a positive screening result for C5‐OH. Between 2018 and 2021, 42 neonates had a positive screening result for C5‐OH. This corresponds to 1:19413 (0.005%) and 1:15152 (0.007%) screened neonates, respectively (Table S3).

C5‐OH Concentrations and Diagnosis of Referred Neonates
In the complete screening period between 2007 and 2023, 126 neonates had a positive screening result for C5‐OH and were referred for further diagnostic evaluation. Data on the outcome were available for 120 neonates. Of these 120 neonates, 46 were confirmed to have a true positive screening result (44 diagnosed with 3‐MCCD, 2 with HMGCLD and 0 with HLCSD), resulting in a PPV of 38.3%. The remaining 74 neonates had a false positive screening result, which included diagnoses of maternal 3‐MCCD and two cases of BKT, neither of which is a target disease in the Dutch NBS program. No false negative cases of any of the target diseases have been reported so far, resulting in an NPV of 100%.
Based on 2 974 660 children screened in the Netherlands between 2007 and 2023, the incidence of 3‐MCCD and HMGCLD amounts to 1 in 67 606 (95% CI: 50 364–90 750) and 1 in 1 487 330 (95% CI: 407 872–5 423 652) screened children, respectively. Since no cases of HLCSD were found, the incidence could not be calculated.
C5‐OH concentrations were higher in DBS of neonates with a true positive screening result compared to neonates with a false positive screening result 3.9 (1.4–6.6) and 1.2 (1.0–2.2) μmol/L, respectively (median (IQR); p < 0.001), although concentrations showed notable overlap (Figure 3 and Figure S2).

Systematic Review

Study Eligibility
The search strategy yielded 2644 unique articles published until February 21, 2024. After title and abstract screening as well as full text assessment, 55 articles were included. Cross checking of the references yielded three additional relevant articles, resulting in a total of 58 articles. Figure 4 shows the selection process of the systematic review.
Flowchart showing the selection process of the systematic review. Modified from Page et al. (2021).

Cut‐Off Values and Effectiveness of Screening
The NBS COV used for C5‐OH was reported in 49 articles and ranged from 0.19 to 1.5 μmol/L. Figure 5 shows the COVs for C5‐OH as reported by the included articles, categorized by country. As a result of differences between regions within countries and adjustments made over time, for some countries, multiple COVs are shown.
Used COVs for NBS C5‐OH as reported by the included articles, categorized by country. The COV used in the Netherlands is added as reference.
Different tandem MS kits were used to quantify C5‐OH, and samples were taken at different ages after birth. Table S4 provides an overview of the different MS/MS kits used as reported in the articles. No relation was found between the kit used or the age at the moment of sampling and the COV for C5‐OH (data not shown).
Only three articles reported a PPV for C5‐OH for detecting 3‐MCCD. Koeberl et al. reported a PPV of 1.5% when using a COV of 0.86 μmol/L, which rose to 30.8% after adjusting the COV to 1.36 μmol/L. Zytkovicz et al. reported a PPV of 4% when using a COV of 0.80 μmol/L. Cheng et al. reported the lowest PPV of 0.60%, using a COV of 0.48 μmol/L.
Khan et al. reported a sensitivity of 50.00% (95% CI 32.68%–67.32%) for C5‐OH to detect 3‐MCCD and HMGCLD, using a COV of 0.5 μmol/L. Zhang et al. reported a sensitivity of 100% for C5‐OH to screen for 3‐MCCD and HLCSD with a COV of 0.38 μmol/L.

Discussion
This study evaluates the effectiveness of C5‐OH as an NBS marker by reviewing Dutch screening data and provides an overview of the literature on C5‐OH as an NBS marker worldwide.
As shown by the analysis of the data of the Dutch NBS program, the PPV of C5‐OH as an NBS marker for 3‐MCCD, HMGCLD, and HLCSD is quite low at 38.3%. The considerable overlap over the full range of C5‐OH concentrations makes it difficult to distinguish between true and false positive cases. Although no false negative cases have been reported in the Netherlands to date, a considerable number of 3‐MCCD patients diagnosed by NBS had a C5‐OH concentration just slightly higher than the COV. It is therefore not unlikely that there are also Dutch neonates who remained undiagnosed after a false negative NBS C5‐OH result due to a C5‐OH concentration just below the COV. This might be explained by the low clinical penetrance of 3‐MCCD. The metabolic centers and associated laboratories in the Netherlands have reported a substantial increase in the number of patients diagnosed with 3‐MCCD since the inclusion of this disease as a target disease in the Dutch NBS program compared to the number of clinically diagnosed patients in the period before NBS screening. The increased number of 3‐MCCD patients also includes asymptomatic mothers who were diagnosed with 3‐MCCD after a positive NBS screening result in their children, which further supports the low clinical penetrance of 3‐MCCD. This is in line with observations in Israel and the results of the systematic review described above. Due to the low clinical penetrance of 3‐MCCD, many, perhaps even the majority of the 3‐MCCD patients, will probably remain asymptomatic.
On the basis of the results of the Dutch NBS, the calculated incidence of 3‐MCCD in the Netherlands amounts to approximately 1 in 67 500 individuals and is comparable to the incidence reported in other countries, but much lower than the incidence of 3‐MCCD of 1 in 2615 reported in the Faroe Islands. In the latter geographically isolated population, a founder effect may explain the relatively high incidence of 3‐MCCD.
Our systematic review showed that a wide range of COVs are used for C5‐OH in the different NBS programs worldwide. This variability demonstrates that the optimal threshold for C5‐OH as a marker for each of the specific target diseases is unclear. The wide range of COVs might partly be explained by the heterogeneity between the different NBS programs, as described below in the section on the limitations of our study. Furthermore, the systematic review showed that an elevated C5‐OH concentration identified through NBS is associated with a wide range of diagnoses. Moreover, it emphasizes the considerable variability in reported C5‐OH concentrations among patients with a specific IEM and the overlap between C5‐OH concentrations in patients with different IEMs. The overlap between NBS C5‐OH concentrations of patients with different C5‐OH associated IEMs limits the ability to reliably distinguish between diseases using solely C5‐OH as an NBS marker. Neonates born to mothers with 3‐MCCD seemed to have higher C5‐OH concentrations than neonates diagnosed with any of the diseases, but due to the heterogeneity of the articles included, this could not be tested statistically.
Based on the findings in the Dutch C5‐OH NBS program as well as those described in the literature and considering the burden of false positive screening results, further optimization of NBS programs for diseases associated with elevated C5‐OH concentrations is highly warranted. In general, optimization of NBS programs is possible through several adjustments, such as adaptation of the COV, addition of a first‐tier marker, the use of discriminative marker ratios, or the addition of second‐tier testing. The results of this study imply that true and false positive NBS results cannot be reliably distinguished by adaptation of the C5‐OH COV alone, since there is notable overlap between C5‐OH concentrations in both groups (Figure 3). Inclusion of an additional marker or a marker ratio might be helpful in differentiating between patients and unaffected neonates. Several potentially useful NBS markers or marker ratios have been suggested in the articles included in this systematic review, such as free carnitine (C0) for 3‐MCCD, propionylcarnitine (C3) for MCD, C5‐OH/C0 ratio for 3‐MCCD, MCD, and HMGCLD, C5‐OH/C3 ratio for 3‐MCCD and C5‐OH/C8 ratio for 3‐MCCD, MCD, and HMGCLD. In a study performed by Rips et al., the mean C0 concentration in neonates with 3‐MCCD was significantly higher than in neonates born to mothers with 3‐MCCD. The ratios of C5‐OH/C0 and C5‐OH/C8 were significantly lower in neonates with 3‐MCCD than in maternal 3‐MCCD cases . In a study performed by Lund et al., however, two patients with HLCSD with a false negative result for C5‐OH also had a normal C5‐OH/C0 ratio. Smon et al. reported the C5‐OH concentration and C5‐OH/C8 ratio in both 3‐MCCD patients and heterozygous carriers. Among five 3‐MCCD heterozygotes with an elevated C5‐OH concentration at NBS, four had a normal C5‐OH/C8 ratio, suggesting that this ratio may help to differentiate heterozygotes from 3‐MCCD patients. However, the effectiveness of NBS using these markers has not been evaluated in these articles and is also outside the scope of our current study.
In addition to NBS based on quantification of acylcarnitines or acylcarnitine ratios, second‐tier genetic testing has proven to be useful by helping to differentiate between true 3‐MCCD patients and heterozygous neonates, who may also have an elevated NBS C5‐OH concentration, and thus reducing the number of false positive cases . Tangeraas et al. described the Norwegian expanded NBS program in which Sanger sequencing of HLCS and HMGCL genes was used as a second‐tier test after a positive NBS C5‐OH result. In this study, the combination of biochemical and second‐tier molecular methods has led to an increase in the PPV of the entire NBS program from 26% to 54%. Although 3‐MCCD was not a target disease in the Norwegian NBS program and the PPVs reported were those of the overall Norwegian NBS program, this demonstrates the potential added value of second‐tier genetic testing for C5‐OH associated diseases.
Interpretation of NBS results is further complicated by placental transmission of the C5‐OH carnitine‐ester, which may lead to a positive screening result for the neonates and subsequent detection of asymptomatic mothers with 3‐MCCD. However, C5‐OH concentrations in this group of neonates will decrease over time. A second heel prick after, for example, 7 days might prevent referral of these children. This, however, will increase the risk of delayed diagnosis and start of treatment in true positive cases, especially in those diseases known to metabolically decompensate in the neonatal phase. Furthermore, Cho et al. demonstrated that transfer of C5‐OH via breast milk might cause persistent elevations in C5‐OH concentrations in breast‐fed neonates of mothers with 3‐MCCD.
When measuring and quantifying C5‐OH using an underivatized MS/MS analysis with direct infusion, that is, without chromatographic separation, the carnitine‐ester C5‐OH cannot be distinguished from the succinyl–/methylmalonyl‐carnitine‐ester (i.e., C4‐DC) based on its mass over charge (m/z) ratio. Therefore, the level of C4‐DC contributes to the concentration of the “C5‐OH” marker measured in NBS. Both in the Netherlands and in the literature, no C4‐DC‐associated diseases have been reported in neonates who were referred based on an elevated C5‐OH concentration.
Overall, while effective adjustments to the current NBS strategy using C5‐OH are not readily available, it remains questionable whether NBS for 3‐MCCD, HMGCLD and HLCSD using C5‐OH is justifiable. The large number of false positive screening results put a burden on both the families and the healthcare system.
Whether the current screening program can be justified depends not only on the effectiveness of the screening marker, but also on additional factors such as the cost of screening, the clinical course of the associated diseases, and the burden experienced by individuals diagnosed, including those who will probably remain asymptomatic. Accordingly, a judgment cannot be made solely on the basis of this study's findings, and further research is warranted.
Globally, there is considerable variation in the use of C5‐OH as a marker for NBS. Furthermore, there are regional differences within some countries. New Zealand discontinued screening for six different carboxylase deficiencies because of the limited clinical benefit for the screened children. Similarly, Germany and Israel have stopped screening for 3‐MCCD. Denmark does use C5‐OH as a marker for NBS, but only HLCSD is a C5‐OH‐target disease in the Danish NBS program, and second‐tier genetic screening is used to detect HLCSD in cases of an elevated C5‐OH concentration.
There are several limitations to our study. In order to analyze the effectiveness of C5‐OH as an NBS marker, data from three different databases had to be combined. The patient data across these databases, however, could not be linked, preventing the ability to associate records from the same individuals.
While the Dutch NEORAH database did provide information on whether the screening result was a true or false positive result, it did not contain information about the final diagnosis or how the final diagnosis was established in the metabolic centers. Because the final diagnosis is not included in the database, it is not known which of the false positive referrals were due to (asymptomatic) maternal disease or a non‐target disease. Additionally, it was not possible to compare NBS C5‐OH concentrations of Dutch 3‐MCCD and HMGCLD patients. However, the results of the systematic review do give insight into the C5‐OH concentration in neonates with different diseases.
The different NBS programs reported in the systematic review were very heterogeneous in various aspects, including the COVs used, the time of sampling, the MS/MS kit used to quantify C5‐OH concentrations, the requirement for repeat testing after an initial positive screening result as well as the programs' target diseases or the use of other (second‐tier) markers, which were often not clearly described. Furthermore, different criteria were used to define a confirmed diagnosis. Due to this heterogeneity, it was difficult to compare the different NBS programs and not possible to perform a meta‐analysis of the extracted data.
It is also important to note that there might be some overlap between the results presented in the articles included. The studies conducted by McHugh et al. and Arnold et al. included patients who were submitted by clinicians internationally, and thus might also concern patients reported in other studies included in the review.
In conclusion, our study demonstrates that, while C5‐OH can be used in an NBS program to detect patients with 3‐MCCD, HCLSD, and HMGCLD, the value of this marker is limited by the degree of overlap of C5‐OH concentrations observed in neonates with and without these diseases as well as among patients with other, non‐target diseases. This emphasizes the need for further improvement of the algorithm used to screen for these IEMs.
