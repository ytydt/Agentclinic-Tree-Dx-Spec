Unfinished business in virtual reality: Development and preliminary evaluation of an empty chair intervention grounded in emotion-focused therapy
The immersive capabilities of virtual reality (VR) make it a promising medium for psychotherapeutic interventions. This randomized controlled trial aimed to develop and evaluate a VR-based adaptation of Emotion-Focused Therapy for Unfinished Business (EFT-UFB). Specifically, we examined its effects on unresolved emotional experiences, self-compassion, self-protection, self-criticism, and perceived stress. Participants (N = 52) were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 26) or a waitlist control group (n = 26), with assessments conducted at pre-test, post-test, and follow-up. The primary between-group analyses revealed no statistically significant differences between the intervention and control groups at post-test (unfinished business: t(50) = −2.00, p = .051, Cohen's d = −0.38, 95 % CI [−0.77, 0.01]) or at follow-up (t(50) = 0.60, p = .551, d = 0.08, 95 % CI [−0.31, 0.47]). Within the intervention group, unfinished business demonstrated a large pre–post reduction that was sustained at follow-up, whereas other effects were small or transient. Effect size estimates suggested small advantages for the intervention arm across several outcomes, including unfinished business (d = −1.01 vs. 0.44 in controls), self-criticism (Inadequate Self d = −0.47), and perceived stress (helplessness d = −0.33; self-efficacy d = 0.37). Other domains, including self-compassion and self-protection, showed negligible or inconsistent differences. While the VR-based EFT-UFB did not outperform the waitlist condition, these pilot findings provide preliminary evidence of feasibility and potential benefits for unfinished business. Importantly, the intervention was effectively delivered by a non-psychotherapist (a trained psychologist), underscoring its potential scalability within digital mental health applications.
Highlights
This RCT tested a VR-based Emotion-Focused Therapy for Unfinished Business (EFT-UFB).
Between-group differences were not statistically significant.
In the VR group, unfinished business decreased and gains held at follow-up.
Results show preliminary feasibility of EFT-UFB in VR, warranting larger trials.

Introduction
Anxiety and depressive disorders are among the most prevalent mental health conditions globally, constituting a leading cause of disability worldwide. Current estimates indicate that a substantial portion of the population will experience a mental disorder in their lifetime, underscoring the critical public health importance of these conditions.
However, beneath these broad diagnostic categories, a significant number of individuals present with persistent, unresolved interpersonal injuries. This phenomenon is conceptualized within emotion-focused therapy as unfinished business. It is characterized by lingering negative affective states stemming from past relational transgressions, betrayals, or failures in attachment. These unresolved emotional schemas often manifest internally as pervasive self-criticism, impaired capacity for self-compassion, leading to chronic physiological and psychological stress. Empirical research indicates that such maladaptive patterns are strongly associated with elevated levels of emotional distress and are significant predictors of broader impairments in overall mental health functioning.
At the same time, mental health services worldwide are struggling to meet this growing demand. Therapists face unprecedented levels of burnout and turnover, with limited capacity to provide intensive psychotherapeutic care. These challenges underscore the urgent need for scalable, accessible, and innovative interventions that can complement traditional therapy.
Virtual reality (VR) has emerged as one promising tool in this area. The strongest evidence base comes from Virtual Reality Exposure Therapy (VRET), which has consistently been shown to be as effective as in vivo exposure for anxiety disorders, phobias, and PTSD. More broadly, VR has also been applied in stress management, social skills training, and emotion regulation. Its immersive qualities, presence, embodiment, and interactivity are theorized to enhance emotional engagement and therapeutic learning.

Virtual reality
Virtual Reality (VR) is commonly considered a technology that induces virtual immersion in the digital world, using computer graphics simulation that allows users to immerse themselves in an interactive three-dimensional world, full of different sensory and emotional experiences. While using VR, individuals enter computer-generated simulations of real-life situations. Users can interact with the virtual environment, and the environment responds to the user's actions in real time. VR simulations can elicit emotional, psychological, and physical reactions such as anxiety, sweating, or joy, like real-life reactions.

Unfinished business
The concept of unfinished business refers to unresolved emotional experiences from the past that continue to exert influence in the present, often in the form of intrusive thoughts, unprocessed emotions, or maladaptive relational patterns. These experiences are typically linked to significant interpersonal encounters such as abandonment, criticism, neglect, or loss that have not been adequately processed or integrated into the individual's emotional life. A significant other can be, for example, a family member, partner, or friend, but may also encompass a broad range of individuals who have played a meaningful, both constructive and detrimental, role in an individual's life and have failed to fulfill their interpersonal needs.
One of the most effective experiential techniques for working with unfinished business is the Empty Chair, particularly as developed within Emotion-Focused Therapy (EFT) and Gestalt traditions. Chair work allows clients to externalize and engage directly with significant others, such as a critical parent, an absent partner, or a boss, thus enabling emotional resolution through structured dialogue. By creating a safe therapeutic space where these dialogues can occur, clients are allowed to express unspoken feelings, reclaim their voice, set boundaries, and process lingering negative feelings.
However, traditional chair work requires a trained therapist, specialized setting, and in-person delivery, which may limit its availability and scalability. VR adaptations of therapeutic methods can address several of these limitations. First, VR interventions can be standardized and delivered outside traditional therapy rooms, making them more accessible and scalable in digital mental health contexts. Second, VR allows the client to embody perspectives and interact with virtual representations of others, potentially amplifying the experiential depth of the technique. Finally, VR may reduce the reliance on therapist presence and enable delivery by trained non-psychotherapists, which is critical for dissemination.

Self-compassion
Self-compassion is a healthy way of relating to oneself and helps to alleviate negative emotions in times of suffering, whether the suffering is caused by failure, perceived inadequacy, or general life difficulties. described compassion as “being moved by the suffering of another and wanting to help them” (p. 289). From an evolutionary perspective, conceptualized compassion as comprising six core attributes: sensitivity, sympathy, empathy, care/motivation, distress tolerance, and non-judgment. When these attributes are directed inward, the result is self-compassion, a process that many individuals find challenging.
Self-compassion has been found to promote emotional resilience and psychological well-being. It is associated with greater happiness, optimism, and life satisfaction and negatively associated with depression, anxiety, stress, and rumination. Physiologically, individuals with higher self-compassion levels demonstrate more adaptive stress responses, supporting its role as a beneficial self-regulatory mechanism.

Self-protection
Self-protection encompasses the ability to set and maintain personal boundaries, particularly in response to mistreatment or emotional threat. A key aspect of self-protection is self-protective or assertive anger, which arises from unmet needs and serves to defend one's integrity and rights.
Self-protective or assertive anger can be a healthy/adaptive emotional response in many contexts (yes, including mistreatment of self thus unmet needs in self) but also when witnessing mistreatment of others (so perhaps unmet needs in self and/or others). This form of anger differs from reactive anger, which is characterized by irritability and hypersensitivity to external triggers.
Self-protection is closely linked to assertiveness, which entails recognising one's right to express personal thoughts, feelings, and needs openly and respectfully. Assertiveness fosters autonomy and emotional safety and is essential for maintaining healthy interpersonal dynamics.

Self-criticism
Self-criticism is defined as a pervasive pattern of negative self-evaluation that manifests in feelings of worthlessness, guilt, and failure, particularly in response to unmet personal standards. Self-critical individuals often engage in excessive self-monitoring and harsh judgment, and they tend to fear disapproval and rejection. Research has shown that self-criticism is a transdiagnostic factor linked to various psychological disorders and is associated with poorer psychotherapy outcomes. In contrast, self-compassion is predictive of emotional well-being, healthy relationships, and better therapeutic progress.
proposed that self-criticism is not a unitary phenomenon but can be differentiated into at least two distinct subtypes. The Inadequate Self reflects feelings of inferiority, weakness, or personal failure, while the Hated Self is characterized by intense self-directed hatred, disgust, or a desire to punish oneself. In contrast, they also identified the Reassured Self, which embodies warmth, acceptance, and self-soothing capabilities, a protective factor associated with lower psychopathology and greater emotional resilience.
Emotion-focused therapy (EFT) specifically targets these maladaptive internal processes by facilitating emotional transformation. One of the core goals of EFT is to transform critical inner voices through the activation of adaptive emotional responses, such as self-compassion, self-protection, and self-reassurance. The therapeutic technique of chair work is central to this process as it enables clients to engage in direct emotional dialogues between the inner critic and the vulnerable self. These experiential encounters help clients express unmet needs, access adaptive emotions, and reorganize their internal experience.

Stress
Stress is defined as a non-specific physiological and psychological response to demands or threats, initially conceptualized by. As the understanding of stress evolved, introduced the term “stressor” to distinguish between the stimulus and the organism's response. Stressors, whether internal or external, disrupt homeostasis and activate the stress response system. Central to the stress response is the autonomic nervous system (ANS), which regulates involuntary bodily functions through the sympathetic (SNS) and parasympathetic (PNS) branches. Under stress, the SNS triggers the “fight or flight” response, leading to increased heart rate, respiration, blood pressure, and energy mobilization through hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol. Conversely, the PNS facilitates recovery and restoration of homeostasis.
Stress is a multifaceted phenomenon that can trigger both physical and psychological changes, such as emotional dysregulation, irritability, and anxiety. Consequently, prolonged stress becomes a detrimental factor that impairs emotional regulation and interpersonal relationships. The emotional instability induced by stress diminishes one's capacity for effective communication, thereby heightening the probability of dissatisfaction and conflict.

Emotion-focused therapy for unfinished business intervention (EFT-UFB)
The intervention EFT-UFB is grounded in the principles of Emotion-Focused Therapy (EFT), enriched by the latest empirical findings in the fields of self-compassion, self-criticism, self-protection, and stress regulation. The content of the intervention was developed by the first author (JH) based on her prior work in creating mental health interventions and her extensive therapeutic experience. To ensure the integrity and theoretical consistency with the EFT model, the script for the intervention was reviewed and commented on by Professor Leslie Greenberg, the founder of Emotion-Focused Therapy. From a technical perspective, the virtual environment was developed in collaboration with the Faculty of Mathematics, Physics, and Informatics at Comenius University in Bratislava (FMFI UK). The third author (ZBH) led the technical development of the intervention. This interdisciplinary collaboration allowed for the creation of an immersive and emotionally resonant VR space designed to support and enhance the therapeutic process. Data collection and intervention administration were carried out by the second author (LO), a qualified psychologist with relevant counselling experience, who completed specific training focused on the steps and techniques for implementing this therapeutic intervention. The psychologist followed a structured protocol manual. A session checklist and ongoing supervision supported fidelity. Although no formal adherence ratings were conducted, all sessions were recorded to ensure consistency.
The session began with a brief orientation, informed consent, and baseline measurements. Upon entering the VR intervention, the client was fitted with motion-tracking sensors and a head-mounted display. Participants were then embodied into a virtual avatar through a VR calibration process, allowing them to experience the intervention from a first-person perspective within a controlled immersive environment.

The experience began in an acclimatization room, a neutral virtual environment where the client could interact with basic objects and observe themselves in a mirror (Fig. 1). This mirror, which is also present in the main intervention room (see Fig. 2), supports the sense of embodiment by allowing clients to perceive their avatar moving in synchrony with their own body. This phase was designed to foster a sense of presence and identification with the virtual body, enhancing the authenticity of emotional engagement. (See Fig. 3.)
Once calibration and orientation were complete, the client was transported to the intervention room, where two virtual chairs were placed opposite each other in the center of the space. These virtual chairs were spatially aligned with two physical chairs in the lab, reinforcing the participant's sense of presence and the ability to switch roles physically. Participants were invited to identify a significant figure from their personal history with whom they experienced unresolved, persistent negative feelings. As we mentioned before, the intervention room involved two chairs: one was occupied by the participant, and the other was imagined to be the seat of the chosen significant other, represented by a responsive avatar. The avatar's facial expression could be switched between four programmed emotions (neutral, angry, sad, compassionate). The therapist, monitoring the session externally, selected these expressions based on the participant's description of the unfinished business figure (e.g., “critical,” “distant,” “supportive”). The intention of the avatar's presence was to provide an anchor for projection and to enhance the realism of the dialogue as sometimes clients have difficulties imagining somebody in an empty chair. According to, clients may experience chair work as artificial or even silly, particularly when asked to imagine another person in an empty chair. A difficulty was also observed by, who noted clients' reluctance and resistance to entering into this imaginative frame.
The therapist guided the process verbally from outside the VR environment. Participants were guided to visualize a significant other (with whom they have unfinished business) in the opposite chair and to express their thoughts and emotions directly toward them. This enactment was structured to help clients access primary emotional responses and articulate them in first-person dialogue. A distinctive feature of the VR adaptation was the use of voice and movement recording. When participants enacted the unfinished business persona, their speech and body movements were captured and later replayed during role reversals. In this way, when seated in the self-chair, participants directly confronted their own words and gestures voiced by the avatar of the other. This experiential loop was intended to deepen perspective-taking and cognitive emotional integration, a therapeutic process that is technically difficult to reproduce in face-to-face settings.
Following the initial expression, participants switched roles by sitting in the other chair and adopting the perspective of the significant other. They were encouraged to embody and respond as this figure, reflecting the negative or invalidating behaviors previously described. This role reversal deepened the emotional exploration and made space for participants to confront internalized representations of the other. Throughout the session, alternating perspectives continued, with participants repeatedly shifting between their viewpoint and that of the significant other imagined. Each round progressively moved from initial emotional disclosure and confrontation toward the identification of unmet needs, boundary setting, and eventually the articulation of assertive and protective responses. The final phase involved evoking a compassionate stance from the perspective of the significant other, allowing participants to receive the kind of emotional response they had long desired but never experienced.

The present study
Despite the rapid growth of VR research, no randomized controlled trial to date has examined an empty-chair intervention delivered fully in VR. Existing applications have instead relied on related chairwork principles, such as two-chair dialogues or targeting internal critical voices, primarily in the service of reducing self-criticism and enhancing self-compassion. To our knowledge, the closest related study evaluated a VR embodiment protocol targeting emotion regulation skills, using a traditional face-to-face empty chair condition as the control group. These studies demonstrate the feasibility of translating experiential dialogue methods into immersive environments, but none have directly implemented an EFT-based unfinished business protocol in VR. Thus, it remains unknown whether chair dialogues in immersive settings can reproduce or extend the benefits of traditional face-to-face methods.
The aim of this study is to develop the Emotion-Focused Therapy for Unfinished Business (EFT-UFB) intervention for virtual reality and to test its effect on levels of unfinished business, self-compassion, self-protection, self-criticism, and levels of experienced stress. The primary outcome was a reduction in unfinished business, with secondary outcomes including self-compassion, self-protection, self-criticism, and perceived stress. By integrating EFT with immersive VR technology, this study contributes to the emerging literature on digital mental health interventions and represents the first RCT to test a VR-based adaptation of empty chair work.
This study was designed as a randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating a novel VR intervention, Emotion-Focused Therapy for Unfinished Business (EFT-UFB). Participants were recruited through online advertisements. After providing informed consent, they completed baseline assessments and were subsequently randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or a waitlist control group. The intervention group received two VR-based EFT-UFB sessions spaced one week apart, while the waitlist group completed the same assessment schedule without therapeutic contact. All participants were reassessed immediately after the intervention period and again at follow-up. Detailed information on the study procedures, randomization, intervention, and control condition is provided in the section below.

Methods

Research sample
Participants were recruited from the general population through social media platforms, social networking websites, and online forums focused on health and wellness. Participants received a book as an incentive for completing all phases of the study. All data collection procedures adhered to the ethical standards set by the institutional research committee, aligning with the principles outlined in the 1964 Helsinki Declaration. The target sample size was determined using G*Power software, applying a repeated measures ANOVA with a within-between interaction. The power analysis used the following parameters: effect size f = 0.25, α = 0.05, and power = 0.95. The analysis estimated a total sample size of 44 participants across both groups.
Inclusion criteria required participants to have no difficulty understanding the study procedures or operating VR equipment. Participants with severe psychiatric disorders, neurological conditions, or epilepsy were excluded from the study. Additional exclusion criteria included photosensitive epilepsy, difficulties with balance impaired perception of three-dimensional space, and motion sickness as these factors represent known contraindications for the use of virtual reality technologies. Participants were also excluded if they were currently engaged in another form of psychotherapy or therapeutic process to avoid interference with the study intervention and outcomes.
A total of 250 participants were initially assessed for eligibility. Out of these, 178 individuals were excluded, 60 due to not meeting the inclusion criteria, and 118 who declined participation. The remaining 71 participants were randomized into two groups. The control group initially had 37 participants, with 5 withdrawing after the pre-test phase and 1 after the first exposure, both for unspecified reasons. The experimental group consisted initially of 34 participants, of whom 3 withdrew after the pre-test phase without specified reasons, and 2 withdrew after the first exposure due to illness. During the follow-up phase, the control group had 31 participants remaining, 5 of whom did not complete follow-up measures. The experimental group had 29 participants remaining, with 3 not completing follow-up measures. Ultimately, 26 participants from each group completed all study measurements and were included in the final analysis. Details regarding participant flow through the study are presented in Fig. 1.
The primary endpoint was defined as the Group × Time interaction on unfinished business. The target sample size was determined a priori using G*Power software, specifying a repeated-measures ANOVA with a within–between interaction. Parameters included f = 0.25 (medium effect), α = 0.05, power (1–β) = 0.95, nonsphericity correction ε = 1, and correlation among repeated measures r = 0.50. This analysis indicated a required total of N = 44 participants (22 per group).
With the achieved final sample of N = 52 (26 per group), a sensitivity analysis indicated that the minimum detectable effect at 80 % power was f ≈ 0.40 (η2 ≈ 0.14, d ≈ 0.79). Thus, the study was sufficiently powered to detect large effects but may have been underpowered for smaller between-group differences. All other outcomes were treated as secondary and exploratory.
All participants were Slovak and reported no prior diagnosed mental health conditions. The experimental group consisted of 26 participants (17 women, 9 men), with a mean age of 28.58 years (SD = 9.97). In this group, 14 participants (53.85 %) reported previous experience with virtual reality (VR). Participants in this group took part in the VR exposure sessions and completed self-report questionnaires. The control group, comprising 26 participants (18 women, 8 men), had a mean age of 29.92 years (SD = 12.38). Participants were informed of their inclusion in the control condition, which involved no VR exposure (waiting list).

Procedure
The study used an experimental, randomized controlled trial (RCT) design to evaluate the effectiveness of the Empty Chair intervention implemented in virtual reality (VR). The randomized controlled trial is widely regarded as the gold standard for evaluating the efficacy of one or more interventions. The data collection process consisted of four phases.
In the first phase (pre-test), participants completed informed consent procedures and a battery of questionnaires assessing perceived unfinished business, self-compassion, self-protection, self-criticism, and stress. The instruments included the Unfinished Business Resolution Scale (UFB-RS,); the Sussex-Oxford Compassion for the Self and Others Scales (SOCS-O/SOCS-S), the Short Version of the Scale for Interpersonal Behavior (s-SIB), the Forms of Self Criticizing/Attacking and Self-Reassuring Scale (FSCRS), the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10).
Participants were then randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group using Research Randomizer with a 1:1 allocation ratio and no blocking. The random sequence was generated by an independent research assistant not involved in intervention delivery or outcome assessment. Allocation was concealed until completion of baseline assessments, at which point participants were informed of their assignment. Research assistants contacted participants to schedule sessions or follow-up assessments accordingly. The intervention therapist (LO) was blind to allocation until the participant presented for the first scheduled session.
Participants allocated to the experimental group were contacted via email or telephone, based on their preference, and scheduled for two intervention sessions at the Faculty of Social and Economic Sciences.
Participants in the waitlist control group remote completed the same assessments at identical time points but did not receive any intervention during the trial period. No therapeutic contact was provided during the waiting period, aside from the research assessments. They were informed that they would be offered the VR intervention after the final follow-up assessment. This choice of comparator reflects a pragmatic and ethically acceptable design for a first trial of a novel intervention, consistent with recommendations for early-phase digital mental health studies. The waitlist allowed us to evaluate whether the VR adaptation produced measurable benefits beyond assessment effects while ensuring that all participants could eventually access the novel treatment.
In the second phase (intervention), participants in the experimental group completed two virtual reality (VR) sessions as part of the intervention, with each session lasting 60–90 min. The sessions were spaced one week apart to allow for integration and reflection. Before and after each session, participants completed corresponding pre- and post-session questionnaires. The intervention was delivered by a licensed psychologist (LO) trained in EFT and in the VR-adapted EFT-UFB protocol. The therapist followed a structured intervention script consisting of prescribed steps. Fidelity to the protocol was supported by adherence to this script, completion of a session checklist, and ongoing supervision provided by the first author (JH). After each session, participants engaged in a short debrief with the therapist to ensure emotional stability before leaving. No adverse events were reported.
The third phase (post-test) was conducted immediately after the second VR session. Participants from both groups completed the same battery of questionnaires administered during the pre-test. In the fourth phase (follow-up), participants were re-contacted two months after the intervention and asked to complete the same set of questionnaires again to assess longer-term effects.

Measures

Tools: Virtual reality (VR)
For the virtual exposure, we chose the HTC VIVE Pro 2 (HTC). The HTC Vive Pro 2 Full Kit consists of an HMD (head mounted display); 2 separate AMOLED displays, each with a resolution of 2448 × 2448 pixels per eye; 120° field of view and standard 120 Hz refresh rate; built-in Hi-Res headset with 3D surround sound and built-in amplifier; dual camera with two microphones supporting Alert and Conversation mode; adjustable straps and infinitely adjustable eye relief (IPD system). The system also included the following sensors: HTC VIVE Tracker 3.0, G-sensor, gyroscope, proximity sensor, IPD sensor (HTC). To enable full-body motion tracking, each participant wore seven sensors placed as follows: two on the hands, two on the feet, two on the shoulders, and one on the waist. The virtual reality software was developed using the Unity application and the calibration of the sensors and the avatar creation were carried out using the Manus software.

Questionnaires

Socio-demographic data
The battery of questionnaires contained socio-demographic questions at the beginning, including items related to age, gender, marital status, highest educational attainment, number of children, occupation, mental, and physical health. To assess the subjective sense of presence in the virtual environment, participants responded to a single item adapted from the I-group Presence Questionnaire (IPQ).The item was: “In the computer-generated world, I had a sense of being there.” Participants rated their experience on a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated ‘not at all present’ and 7 indicated ‘very present’.

Unfinished business resolution scale (UFB-RS; Slovak version)
We use the Unfinished Business Resolution Scale (UFB-RS) to measure the intensity of unresolved emotional issues with a significant other. Participants indicated the extent to which various statements were true for them on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true to 5 = completely true). The original scale consists of 11 items with Test-retest reliability of the original version, which was found to be high, with a coefficient of 0.73 in a student sample and 0.81 in a clinical sample over one month. The Slovak version of the UFB-RS included 6 items and was adapted and tested on a Slovak population, demonstrating good internal consistency with a Cronbach's alpha of α = 0.80. Sample items from the Slovak version included: “I feel disturbed by my ongoing unresolved feelings (such as anger, sadness, pain, hurt, bitterness, resentment, aversion) toward this person.” The UFB-RS scale showed varied levels of internal consistency across time points in this study. At pre-test, Cronbach's alpha was α = 0.65, indicating a lower level of internal consistency. However, reliability substantially increased at post-test (α = 0.91) and remained high at follow-up (α = 0.87), suggesting strong scale consistency in the later phases of the study.

Sussex-Oxford compassion scale other-compassion/self-compassion (SOCS-O/SOCS-S; Slovak version)
The scale was created with two corresponding versions for self-compassion: The Sussex-Oxford Scale of Self-Compassion (SOCS-S) and the Sussex-Oxford Scale of Compassion for Others (SOCS-O). The Sussex-Oxford Self-Compassion Scale (SOCS-S) consists of a series of 20 statements that describe the type of relationship an individual may have with him or herself. It consists of five subscales: a) recognition of suffering, b) understanding the universality of suffering, c) empathizing with the suffering person, d) tolerating unpleasant feelings, and e) behaving or being motivated to act to alleviate suffering. It includes questions, e.g., “Am I good at recognising when I am in discomfort.” Participants rate the statements using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true, 2 = rarely true, 3 = sometimes true, 4 = often true, 5 = always true), indicating the extent to which they find the statements true. The scale was adapted and assessed on a Slovak population, where high internal consistency was found at α = 0.89.
The Sussex-Oxford Compassion for Others Scale (SOCS-O) consists of a series of 20 statements that describe the relationship with other people. It consists equally of five subscales: a) recognition of suffering, b) understanding the universality of suffering, c) empathizing with the suffering person, d) tolerating unpleasant feelings, and e) behaving or being motivated to act to alleviate suffering. It includes questions such as “Can I recognise when other people are in discomfort without having to tell me.” Participants rate the statements using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true, 2 = rarely true, 3 = sometimes true, 4 = often true, 5 = always true), indicating the extent to which they find the statements true. The scale was adapted and tested on a Slovak population, where high internal consistency was found at α = 0.93 for the Sussex-Oxford Compassion for Others Scale and α = 0.89 for Sussex-Oxford Self-Compassion Scale. The study's findings demonstrated that the multidimensional definition of compassion for others fits the scale's factor structure for the SCOS-O, but not for the SCOS-S. Over and above the five specific factors, self-compassion has two dominant components: emotional/behavioural compassion and rational compassion. Emotional/behavioural compassion has three specific components: feeling for the person suffering, tolerating uncomfortable feelings, and acting or being motivated to act to alleviate suffering. The SCOS-O/ SOCS-S scales demonstrated excellent internal consistency. The Cronbach's alpha coefficients were α = 0.877 at pre-test, α = 0.883 at post-test, and α = 0.884 at follow-up, indicating stable and high reliability throughout this study.

Short version of the scale for interpersonal behavior (s-SIB; Slovak version)
Positive assertiveness (PA/ FPA) refers to the ability to express positive feelings toward others (giving praise) and the capacity to appropriately receive and respond to praise and compliments from others.
Negative assertiveness (NA/FNA) refers to the ability to express negative feelings. Participants either stand up for their rights in public situations or tolerate the situation; either ask others to change their behavior or tolerate their irritating behavior; and either are able or unable to refuse requests.
Expression of and Dealing with Personal Limitations (PL/FPL) - refers to the ability to cope with pressure and criticism, to acknowledge one's failures, and to admit limited or no knowledge of the topic.
Initiating Assertiveness (IA/FIA) - refers to the skills needed when socializing with other people - introducing oneself to others, initiating a conversation or expressing one's own opinion.

Perceived stress scale (PSS-10; Slovak version)
We use the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) to assess the level of subjective experience of current stress. The PSS-10 is a self-report instrument consisting of ten items that measure how unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overloaded respondents perceive their lives to be. Participants rate the frequency of stress-related experiences over the past month using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 5 = Very often). The total score reflects the overall level of perceived stress. The scale includes two subscales: Perceived Helplessness (pH) and Perceived Self-Efficacy (PSE). Six negatively worded items (items 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 10), reflecting feelings of helplessness and distress (e.g., “How often in the past month have you felt nervous or stressed?”), comprise the Perceived Helplessness subscale. The remaining four items (items 4, 5, 7, and 8) assess the respondent's perceived ability to cope with stress and form the Perceived Self-Efficacy subscale (e.g., “In the past month, how often have you felt that things were going your way?”). The Slovak version of the PSS-10 was translated, adapted, and validated by. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in a Slovak population, with a Cronbach's alpha of α = 0.79. Furthermore, results of the psychometric analyses supported both convergent and discriminant validity for the Slovak version of the questionnaire. The internal consistency of the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) was high across all measurement points. Cronbach's alpha was α = 0.82 at pre-test, α = 0.87 at post-test, and α = 0.87 at follow-up, indicating stable and strong reliability throughout this study period.

Data analyses
Analyses were conducted on a per-protocol (PP) basis. Only participants who completed the allocated condition and provided post-test data were included. Participants who withdrew prior to post-test were excluded from analyses, and no imputation of missing data was performed. Consequently, analyses at follow-up were restricted to available cases.
For the data analyses, we used the IBM SPSS Statistics program, version 20 (IBM). We computed the reliability coefficients (Cronbach's alpha) for each of the scales and their dimensions. Where the data had a normal distribution, we used repeated measures ANOVA (Within-subjects effects) and paired samples t-test (Between-subjects effects), and then calculated Cohen's d as the measure of effect size. Where the condition for normal distribution was violated, we used nonparametric tests, the Friedman test, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To eliminate Type I errors, the level of statistical significance was tested post-hoc using the Bonferroni correction; the result was statistically significant at p < .017. To assess within-subject effects, we investigated changes in outcome variables at two time points: immediately after the intervention (post-test) and two months later (follow-up). This design allowed us to examine both the immediate and long-term effects of EFT-UFB on participants. In terms of between-subjects effects, we compared the experimental group with a control group to determine whether the intervention led to significantly higher or lower levels in the measured variables. By analyzing post-intervention outcomes between groups, we aimed to evaluate the relative effectiveness of the EFT-UFB intervention.

Results
The experimental group observed a decrease in IPQ scores across the three measurement points. Since the data for the IPQ in the experimental group did not follow a normal distribution, the non-parametric Friedman test was used to compare scores across the three measurement points. The results indicated that there was no statistically significant difference in the sense of presence over time, χ2(2) = 0.806, p = .668. The mean score after the first exposure was M = 5.00 (SD = 1.26), slightly decreasing to M = 4.85 (SD = 1.41) after the second exposure, and further to M = 4.69 (SD = 1.29) at post-test. While a small downward trend in the sense of presence was observed across sessions, this change was not statistically significant, suggesting that participants' subjective experience of presence in the virtual reality remained relatively stable throughout the intervention. No IPQ data were collected for the control group as they did not undergo the intervention. All statistical tests and corresponding descriptive statistics are referenced throughout the text, with data presented in Table 1, Table 7, which can be found in Appendix A.

Unfinished business
Within-subjects analyses revealed a statistically significant reduction in the level of unfinished business within the experimental group across the three measurement points. Specifically, a paired-samples t-test showed a significant decrease from pre-test (M = 19.19, SD = 4.45) to post-test (M = 15.46, SD = 5.88), t(25) = 3.114, p = .005. This reduction was maintained at the two-month follow-up (M = 15.15, SD = 5.23), with no significant difference between post-test and follow-up scores, t(25) = 0.317, p = .754. A repeated-measures ANOVA further confirmed a significant effect of time within the experimental group, F(1.815, 45.374) = 9.281, p = .001, indicating a consistent decline in unfinished business throughout the intervention period (see Table 1).
In contrast, within the control group, no statistically significant changes in unfinished business were detected across the three time points. Scores remained relatively stable from pre-test (M = 17.88, SD = 4.35) to post-test (M = 17.27, SD = 6.73), t(25) = 0.618, p = .542, and from post-test to follow-up (M = 16.12, SD = 5.61), t(25) = 1.127, p = .270. Similarly, repeated-measures ANOVA indicated no significant time effect in the control group, F(1.998, 49.947) = 1.582, p = .216.
Between-subjects comparisons did not reveal statistically significant differences in the change in unfinished business between groups. The difference in pre- to post-test change approached significance, t(50) = −2.000, p = .051, favoring a greater reduction in the experimental group; however, this effect did not reach the conventional alpha threshold. The change between post-test and follow-up remained non-significant, t(50) = 0.600, p = .551.

Self-compassion
Within-subjects analyses revealed no statistically significant changes over time in any of the self-compassion subscales within the experimental group. In the SOCS-O (Sussex-Oxford Compassion for Others) subscales, scores remained stable across all three measurement points. For instance, Recognising Suffering increased slightly from a pre-test mean of 15.46 (SD = 2.52) to 15.92 (SD = 2.74) at post-test, and remained at 15.88 (SD = 2.89) at follow-up, yet the Friedman test indicated no significant change, χ2(2) = 2.247, p = .325. Similarly, Understanding the Universality of Suffering showed no variation over time, χ2(2) = 0.560, p = .756. The subscale Feeling for the Person Suffering also remained stable, χ2(2) = 3.694, p = .158. The subscales Tolerating Uncomfortable Feelings and Acting to Alleviate Suffering yielded non-significant effects as well: F(1.379, 34.474) = 0.621, p = .486 and χ2(2) = 1.627, p = .443, respectively.
A similar trend was observed in the SOCS-S (Self-Compassion) subscales. Recognising Suffering showed no statistically significant change, χ2(2) = 1.042, p = .594. The remaining subscales also failed to demonstrate significant differences across time: Understanding Universality, χ2(2) = 0.567, p = .753; Feeling for the Person Suffering, F(1.947, 48.681) = 1.323, p = .275; Tolerating Uncomfortable Feelings, F(1.988, 49.693) = 0.055, p = .946; and Acting to Alleviate Suffering, F(1.956, 48.904) = 1.409, p = .254. Descriptive results (see Table 2, Table 3) confirm this pattern of stability.
In the control group, no statistically significant changes were observed in any of the SOCS-O subscales. For example, Understanding Universality remained stable between pre-test (M = 17.69, SD = 2.62) and follow-up (M = 17.58, SD = 2.44), with a non-significant Wilcoxon result of Z = −0.399, p = .690. In the SOCS-S subscales, one exception emerged: Feeling for the Person Suffering showed a significant within-group change across time, F(1.782, 44.538) = 4.146, p = .026. However, this effect did not survive the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold for multiple comparisons and thus was not interpreted as statistically robust.
Between-subjects comparisons revealed no significant differences between the experimental and control groups at post-test or follow-up across any self-compassion subscales. The only result approaching significance was observed in the SOCS-O subscale Acting to Alleviate Suffering at post-test, Z = −1.785, p = .074, which did not meet the conventional alpha threshold. All remaining between-group comparisons were non-significant (p > .05).

Self-protection
Within-subjects analyses indicated no statistically significant changes in self-protection scores within the experimental group across the three measurement points. Participants' levels of Negative Statements remained virtually unchanged, with a mean of 21.04 (SD = 2.90) at pre-test and 21.50 (SD = 3.15) at post-test, t(25) = −0.853, p = .402. Similarly, no significant changes were found in Expressing and Solving Personal Limitations (t(25) = 0.356, p = .725), Assertiveness Initiation (t(25) = 0.099, p = .922), or Positive Statements (t(25) = 0.372, p = .713). Scores remained consistent at follow-up; comparisons between post-test and follow-up also showed no significant differences across all subscales (all p > .6). For instance, Negative Statements were rated at 21.19 (SD = 3.58) at follow-up, with a non-significant change compared to post-test, t(25) = 0.509, p = .615 (see Table 4).
Similarly, in the control group, no statistically significant changes were detected across most subscales. Although a trend toward higher Negative Statements scores was observed from pre-test (M = 19.15, SD = 3.71) to post-test (M = 20.69, SD = 4.03), this difference did not reach significance, t (25) = −2.032, p = .053. Expressing and Solving Personal Limitations (t(25) = −0.611, p = .546), Assertiveness Initiation (t(25) = −1.007, p = .323), and Positive Statements (t(25) = 0.886, p = .384) remained unchanged between pre- and post-test. However, at follow-up, a significant decline in Assertiveness Initiation was observed, with scores decreasing significantly from post-test to follow-up, t(25) = 2.543, p = .018. All other subscales remained stable over time. For example, follow-up comparisons in Negative Statements (t(25) = 1.518, p = .141), Expressing and Solving Personal Limitations (t(25) = −0.847, p = .405), and Positive Statements (tested with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Z = −0.731, p = .465) yielded non-significant results.
Between-subjects analyses demonstrated no statistically significant differences between the experimental and control groups after the intervention. Comparisons revealed non-significant differences for Negative Statements (t(50) = −1.157, p = .253), Positive Statements (t(50) = 0.367, p = .715), Expressing and Solving Personal Limitations (Z = −0.837, p = .402), and Assertiveness Initiation (Z = −0.904, p = .366). At the two-month follow-up, this pattern persisted. Independent t-tests showed no significant group differences in Negative Statements (t(50) = 0.687, p = .495), Expressing and Solving Personal Limitations (t(50) = −0.773, p = .443), or Assertiveness Initiation (t(50) = 1.868, p = .068), though the latter comparison approached statistical significance. For Positive Statements, assessed non-parametrically, no significant group difference was detected (Z = −1.04, p = .298).

Self-criticism
Within-subjects analyses of the experimental group revealed a statistically significant reduction in the Inadequate Self subscale of the Forms of Self-Criticizing and Self-Reassuring Scale (FSCRS) following the intervention. Scores decreased from a pre-test mean of 29.27 (SD = 7.38) to 26.81 (SD = 7.53) at post-test, t(25) = 2.071, p = .049. This reduction was not maintained at the two-month follow-up, as follow-up scores (M = 27.27, SD = 7.42) were no longer significantly different from pre-test values, t(25) = 1.379, p = .180. However, between-group comparison of pre- to post-test change scores yielded a result on the verge of statistical significance, t(50) = −2.000, p = .051, suggesting a potentially meaningful effect favoring the experimental group. No significant changes over time were observed for the Reassured Self (t(25) = −0.685, p = .500; t(25) = −0.126, p = .901) or Hated Self (t(25) = 1.436, p = .163; t(25) = −0.074, p = .941) subscales.
Repeated-measures analyses of variance supported these findings. While the change in Inadequate Self approached significance over the three time points (F(1.951, 48.767) = 2.797, p = .072), no significant effects were found for Reassured Self (F(1.813, 45.325) = 0.376, p = .668) or Hated Self (F(1.962, 48.155) = 1.535, p = .226).
In the control group, no significant reductions were observed in the Inadequate Self subscale (t(25) = −0.887, p = .383) or the Hated Self subscale (Wilcoxon test: Z = −0.646, p = .518) from pre-test to post-test. However, a statistically significant increase was found in the Reassured Self subscale, with scores rising from M = 26.96 (SD = 5.94) at pre-test to M = 28.65 (SD = 6.01) at post-test, t(25) = −2.348, p = .027. This increase was not maintained at follow-up, with non-significant differences between post-test and follow-up across all subscales: Inadequate Self (t(25) = −0.227, p = .822), Reassured Self (t(25) = 1.512, p = .143), and Hated Self (Z = −0.267, p = .790).
Repeated-measures results for the control group similarly indicated no significant time effects. For Inadequate Self, the ANOVA was non-significant (F(1.837, 45.914) = 0.501, p = .593), as were results for Reassured Self (F(1.979, 49.467) = 2.656, p = .081) and Hated Self (χ2(2) = 1.605, p = .448) (see Table 5).
Between-subjects comparisons revealed no statistically significant group differences in change scores. From pre-test to post-test, group differences were non-significant for Inadequate Self (Z = −1.011, p = .312), Reassured Self (t(50) = −1.162, p = .251), and Hated Self (Z = −1.756, p = .079), although the latter approached significance. Comparisons from post-test to follow-up also revealed no significant differences: Inadequate Self (t(50) = 0.196, p = .845), Reassured Self (Z = −0.149, p = .881), and Hated Self (Z = −1.300, p = .193).

Stress
Within-subjects analyses showed no statistically significant changes in perceived stress over time within the experimental group. While the mean score for perceived helplessness (PSS-PH) decreased slightly from 19.12 (SD = 4.44) at pre-test to 18.12 (SD = 3.99) at post-test, this change did not reach statistical significance, t(25) = 1.575, p = .128. A similar pattern was observed for perceived self-efficacy (PSS-PSE), which increased from 13.00 (SD = 2.73) to 13.77 (SD = 3.15), yet the difference remained non-significant, t(25) = −1.995, p = .057. Between post-test and follow-up, no significant differences were observed either for perceived helplessness (t(25) = 0.738, p = .467) or self-efficacy (t(25) = 0.658, p = .517), with follow-up means of 17.62 (SD = 4.34) and 13.42 (SD = 2.93), respectively. These results were supported by repeated-measures ANOVAs: for perceived helplessness, F(1.965, 49.128) = 2.553, p = .089; and for perceived self-efficacy, F(1.725, 43.129) = 1.214, p = .302.
In the control group, stress levels remained similarly stable over time. For perceived helplessness, scores showed negligible variation from pre-test (M = 18.69, SD = 4.08) to post-test (M = 18.77, SD = 3.96), t(25) = −0.121, p = .905, and to follow-up (M = 18.92, SD = 4.36), t(25) = −0.307, p = .761. For perceived self-efficacy, no significant differences were found between pre-test (M = 13.42, SD = 2.19), post-test (M = 13.65, SD = 1.98), and follow-up (M = 13.23, SD = 2.85), with t(25) = −0.536, p = .597 and t(25) = 1.066, p = .297. These findings were corroborated by repeated-measures ANOVAs, which showed no significant effects for perceived helplessness, F(1.726, 43.152) = 0.070, p = .909, or perceived self-efficacy, F(1.564, 39.097) = 0.386, p = .631 (see Table 6).
Between-subjects analyses confirmed that the changes in stress levels did not significantly differ between the experimental and control groups. For perceived helplessness, the pre–post difference was not significant, t(50) = −1.200, p = .236, nor was the follow-up difference, t(50) = −0.776, p = .442. Similarly, for perceived self-efficacy, no between-group differences were found either immediately post-intervention (Z = −0.485, p = .628) or at follow-up (t(50) = 0.117, p = .908).

Effect sizes
To complement the analysis of statistical significance, Cohen's d was calculated for each construct based on the difference between pre-test and post-test scores in both the experimental and control groups. According to the interpretation guidelines effect sizes around 0.2 are considered small, around 0.5 as medium, and 0.8 or higher as large. The effect sizes were interpreted accordingly to assess the practical significance of the observed change. A complete overview of all effect sizes for both groups is presented in Table 7.
In the experimental group, the largest effect size was observed for unresolved emotional experiences (d = −1.01) indicating a large reduction in persistent negative affect. For self-compassion (SOCS-S), effect sizes across all five subscales were small. Recognising Suffering (d = 0.10), Understanding the Universality of Suffering (d = −0.04), Feeling for the Self (d = 0.11), and Tolerating Uncomfortable Feelings (d = 0.00) showed limited change, while the most improvement was found in Acting to Alleviate Suffering (d = 0.26). In other-oriented compassion (SOCS-O), small positive effects were observed in Recognising Suffering (d = 0.25), Feeling for the Person Suffering (d = 0.25), Tolerating Uncomfortable Feelings (d = 0.12), and Acting to Alleviate Suffering (d = 0.31), with a slight decrease in Understanding the Universality of Suffering (d = −0.13). Assertiveness-related outcomes (sSIB) showed small effects. Negative Assertiveness decreased (d = 0.22), and minimal changes were observed in Dealing with Personal Limitations (d = −0.06), Initiating Assertiveness (d = −0.01), and Positive Assertiveness (d = −0.07). Moderate effects were also found in dimensions of self-criticism, specifically in Inadequate Self (d = −0.47) and Hated Self (d = −0.36), pointing to meaningful reductions in negative self-relating. A small positive change was detected in Reassured Self (d = 0.11). In the domain of perceived stress, a moderate reduction was observed in Perceived Helplessness (d = −0.33), and a moderate increase in Perceived Self-Efficacy (d = 0.37), suggesting improvements in stress coping and perceived personal agency.
In contrast, the control group demonstrated only small or negligible changes. A small effect was found for unresolved emotional experiences (d = 0.44). For self-compassion (SOCS-S), all subscales produced small effects: Recognising Suffering (d = 0.05), Understanding the Universality of Suffering (d = 0.02),
Feeling for the Self (d = 0.26), Tolerating Uncomfortable Feelings (d = 0.21), and Acting or Being Motivated to Act to Alleviate Suffering (d = 0.28). For compassion toward others (SOCS-O), the following results were observed: Recognising Suffering (d = −0.02), Understanding the Universality of Suffering (d = 0.02), Feeling for the Person Suffering (d = 0.06), Tolerating Uncomfortable Feelings (d = 0.14), and Acting or Being Motivated to Act to Alleviate Suffering (d = 0.19). Assertiveness subscales varied, with the highest effect in Negative Assertiveness (d = 0.56), followed by smaller effects in Dealing with Personal Limitations (d = 0.11), Initiating Assertiveness (d = 0.18), and a slight reduction in Positive Assertiveness (d = −0.15). Among FSCRS subscales, Inadequate Self (d = −0.13) and Hated Self (d = 0.05) showed minimal change, while Reassured Self increased slightly (d = 0.40). Stress-related outcomes remained unchanged, with Perceived Helplessness (d = 0.03) and Perceived Self-Efficacy (d = 0.16) showing only minimal differences.

Discussion
This randomized controlled trial examined the efficacy of a Virtual Reality (VR) based Empty Chair intervention (EFT-UFB) in addressing unfinished business, self-compassion, self-criticism, self-protection, and perceived stress.
The central finding was that no statistically significant differences between groups were observed on the primary outcome of unfinished business at either post-test or follow-up. Although the post-test contrast approached significance (p = .051, d = −0.xx, 95 % CI […, …]), it did not meet conventional thresholds, and all other outcomes (self-compassion, self-criticism, self-protection, stress) similarly showed no significant group differences. Thus, the main hypothesis that the intervention would demonstrate superiority over the control group was not supported.
Several factors may help explain these null findings. First, the Hawthorne effect may have altered participants' behavior simply due to their awareness of being studied. Second, the mere-measurement effect may have influenced responses. Particularly in the control group, through the act of repeatedly completing self-report questionnaires, which can stimulate introspection even in the absence of active intervention. It is also possible that completing the questionnaire itself induced a mild intervention-like effect in the control group. Given the emotionally evocative nature of the items, focusing on unresolved issues with a significant other, participants may have reflected deeply on personal unfinished business during the assessment, even in the absence of any therapeutic exposure. From a methodological standpoint, this type of measurement reactivity may have prompted some degree of emotional processing or cognitive reframing, which in turn could have reduced the observable differences between groups. Such effects are consistent with findings in clinical research, which indicate that self-assessment can sometimes prompt introspection and minor therapeutic gains, especially when dealing with emotionally salient content.
Moreover, the relatively small sample size and reliance on per-protocol analyses likely limited the statistical power to detect effects of the magnitude typically observed in psychotherapy research. This lack of statistical power may have contributed to a Type II error (a false negative), obscuring a true, although small-to-moderate, intervention effect. These effects, combined with the use of a non-active control group, may have reduced the absence of significant differences between the experimental and control groups.
Although no significant between-group effects were detected, secondary analyses within the intervention arm suggested notable short-term improvements. Findings indicated a significant reduction in unfinished business among participants in the experimental group, with effects maintained at a two-month follow-up. The significant decrease in unfinished business aligns with previous research on the Empty Chair technique in Emotion-Focused Therapy (EFT), which has been shown to facilitate emotional resolution through structured dialogues with internalized representations of significant others. A secondary, yet notable, outcome was the reduction in the Inadequate Self dimension of self-criticism, suggesting a partial shift in maladaptive self-evaluative processes. Prior EFT research has emphasized that reductions in self-critical responding often emerge when clients access more adaptive emotional responses, but our data indicates that such changes may require more intensive or repeated exposure to the intervention.
To complement pairwise comparisons, repeated measures analyses were conducted to explore changes over time across all three assessment points. These results further confirmed the robustness of the intervention effect on unfinished business, as significant longitudinal improvement was found only in this domain. No significant changes over time were observed for stress, self-compassion, self-criticism, or self-protection, which is consistent with the overall pattern of small effect sizes and non-significant short-term differences. An unexpected, transient increase in the “feeling for the person suffering” subscale of self-compassion was observed in the control group; however, this finding did not withstand correction for multiple comparisons.
Contrary to expectations, the intervention did not significantly improve self-compassion, self-protection, or perceived stress. One probable explanation is the brevity of the intervention, which consisted of only two exposures (60–90-min sessions), likely insufficient to modify deeply ingrained self-relational schemas or behavioural patterns. Research suggests that cultivating self-compassion requires extended and repeated exposure to therapeutic techniques such as mindfulness, compassionate imagery, and emotional processing. Indeed, studies of compassion-focused interventions consistently demonstrate that meaningful and sustained improvements emerge only after prolonged engagement. Similarly, assertiveness (or self-protection) is not solely a cognitive or emotional process but often requires behavioural rehearsal, social feedback, and real-life application to be effectively developed. In the case of perceived stress, internal emotional work alone may be insufficient, as stress experiences are strongly shaped by external demands and environmental conditions. As such, coping skill acquisition and broader stress management strategies may be necessary adjuncts to experiential interventions.
Crucially, given the relatively small sample size, reliance solely on statistical significance tests (p-values) might obscure practically meaningful changes. In such contexts, effect sizes offer a more accurate and informative criterion for evaluating intervention outcomes. Effect sizes are not influenced by sample size in the same way p-values are, and thus they can more reliably capture clinically important effects in small-sample studies. As highlighted by, statistical significance does not always align with clinical significance.
The large effect size observed for unfinished business (d = −1.01) indicates a strong and clinically relevant impact of the intervention, despite the absence of statistically significant group-by-time interactions. Effect size analyses further support the intervention's targeted benefits. In addition to the large effect on unfinished business, a moderate effects were observed in key areas targeted by the intervention, including self-criticism, with reductions in both Inadequate Self (d = −0.47) and Hated Self (d = −0.36). Perceived stress, where Perceived Helplessness decreased (d = −0.33) and Perceived Self-Efficacy improved (d = 0.37). These changes suggest that participants not only experienced less self-critical distress but also developed greater confidence in their ability to manage stressors. Effect sizes across assertiveness domains were small, with slight improvements in Negative Assertiveness (d = 0.22), while changes in other dimensions (e.g., Initiating and Positive Assertiveness) remained minimal. This suggests that while some interpersonal shifts were evident. Importantly, changes in self-compassion and compassion for others were also observed, although effects were generally small. Within self-compassion (SOCS-S), the most notable improvements occurred in Acting or Being Motivated to Alleviate Suffering (d = 0.31) and Feeling for the Self (d = 0.25), with smaller gains in Tolerating Uncomfortable Feelings (d = 0.12), Recognising Suffering (d = 0.25), and Understanding the Universality of Suffering (d = −0.13). These outcomes suggest increased emotional engagement with personal suffering and readiness to respond, although the cognitive understanding of shared suffering may take longer to shift. Compassion directed toward others (SOCS-O) also showed selective improvements. The most pronounced was in Acting or Being Motivated to Alleviate Suffering (d = 0.26) and Feeling for the Person Suffering (d = 0.11), whereas Understanding the Universality of Suffering slightly declined (d = −0.04). These subtle effects may reflect the intervention's stronger emphasis on inward-facing (self-related) emotional processing.
In contrast, the control group showed only minor changes, with the highest effects observed in Reassured Self (d = 0.40) and Negative Assertiveness (d = 0.56), t, yet none of these reached the threshold for moderate effects However, these secondary results should be interpreted with caution. As the analyses were conducted per-protocol and the trial was not powered for multiple endpoints, within-group changes cannot be taken as definitive evidence of efficacy. Instead, they should be viewed as preliminary signals warranting replication in larger and more definitive studies.
An interesting, though statistically non-significant, observation was the gradual decline in participants' sense of presence (IPQ scores) across VR sessions. This decline may reflect habituation to the environment or a reduction in emotional engagement, consistent with prior work showing that emotional salience, rather than repeated exposure alone, sustains presence in VR. It is also important to note that, in traditional empty-chair work, clients rarely struggle to imagine the presence of the other, and the empty space itself can facilitate projection. Some studies even suggest that the absence of an external figure may evoke stronger emotional arousal than its presence. Taken together, these considerations raise the possibility that the avatar, while designed to anchor projection, did not uniformly enhance engagement and that in some contexts an empty chair might be equally or more effective. Future research should experimentally compare empty-chair and avatar-chair conditions in nonclinical populations, as examined clinical samples. Such work would help clarify whether differences in engagement are driven by the presence of an avatar versus an empty chair, and whether these effects vary across clinical and nonclinical groups.
While the successful delivery of a psychotherapeutic intervention by a non-psychotherapist professional (psychologist) demonstrates the potential for broader applicability in digital mental health and suggests possible cost reductions using a wider range of professionals. It is crucial to carefully consider the potential risks of bias. Importantly, the psychologist in this study underwent specialized training focused on the administration of the specific psychotherapeutic intervention within the context of virtual reality (VR). This training was designed to ensure an adequate level of competency, enabling effective and standardized delivery of the intervention.
Nevertheless, preserving therapeutic integrity and intervention quality is essential. A lack of specialized training may affect not only how the intervention is delivered but also the provider's ability to respond to individual needs and contextual nuances, potentially leading to suboptimal therapeutic outcomes or misinterpretation of data. Given the immersive nature of VR, assessing therapeutic effects, such as emotional engagement, presence, and real-time physiological responses (e.g., heart rate variability), could provide valuable insights into intervention efficacy. As VR and other digital tools become increasingly integral to mental health care, training programs must evolve to bridge the gap between traditional therapeutic expertise and emerging technologies.

Limitations and future research
Despite promising findings, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the sample size was relatively small (N = 52 for the final analysis), which may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle effects in secondary outcomes such as self-compassion or stress. Although the sample size was based on an a priori power analysis, future research should consider larger and more diverse populations to enhance generalizability.
Second, the intervention consisted of only two sessions, which may not have provided sufficient exposure to elicit measurable changes in more complex constructs like self-compassion and stress regulation. Prior studies suggest that emotional transformation, particularly in self-relational domains, often requires repeated and sustained therapeutic engagement.
Third, the decline in the subjective sense of presence over repeated VR exposures suggests possible habituation effects, which may have reduced the emotional impact of the intervention. Given the centrality of presence to therapeutic outcomes in VR, strategies to maintain immersion, such as dynamic feedback, increased interactivity, or tailored scenarios, should be explored in future iterations.
Fourth, although participants were screened for major psychiatric conditions, self-selection bias may have influenced results, as individuals more open to digital interventions or novel therapeutic formats may have been overrepresented. Additionally, the use of self-report measures, while standard in psychological research, introduces the potential for response bias or demand characteristics.
A further limitation is that analyses were conducted on a per-protocol rather than an intention-to-treat basis. Participants who withdrew prior to post-test were excluded, which may bias toward completers and potentially overestimate treatment effects. Future trials should implement an intention-to-treat approach with appropriate handling of missing data (e.g., mixed models, multiple imputations) to provide more robust estimates of efficacy.
Lastly, the study employed a waiting-list control group, which does not control for placebo or non-specific therapeutic effects such as attention or expectation. Future studies might benefit from the inclusion of an active control condition, such as psychoeducation or non-experiential VR exposure, to better isolate the specific effects of the intervention. More broadly, we suggest that future research should focus on directly comparing the process and outcome of the VR-based empty chair intervention with the traditional, real-world face-to-face empty chair technique. Such comparative efficacy studies are crucial for advancing the understanding of how VR interventions work in comparison with traditional therapy.

Conclusion
Despite its limitations, this study makes an important contribution to the emerging field of VR-assisted psychotherapy, particularly in demonstrating the effectiveness of delivering experiential techniques like the Empty Chair within a virtual environment. To achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms of change, future research should employ diverse methodologies, including qualitative investigations of client experience, detailed process research, and larger, well-powered outcome studies.
