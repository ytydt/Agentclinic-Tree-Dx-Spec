Continuous Pacing and Recording Technique: A Real-Time Feedback Approach for Left Bundle Branch Pacing
Left bundle branch pacing demonstrates significant clinical value in both prevention of right ventricular pacing-induced cardiomyopathy and resynchronization therapy following left bundle branch block. The current intermittent recording technique requires repeated interruptions during implantation to test parameters, increasing procedural complexity and time. In recent years, the application of stylet-driven leads and rotatable connectors combined with lumenless leads has enabled a continuous pacing and recording technique. This approach facilitates beat-by-beat monitoring of electrocardiograms and intracardiac electrograms during lead implantation, with real-time electrophysiological feedback provided to assist operators in precisely determining lead positioning and confirming left bundle branch capture. This technological innovation not only enhances procedural precision but also substantially improves operational safety. In addition, the continuous recording technique offers novel perspectives for electrophysiological research, potentially bridging cardiac pacing to advanced electrophysiological therapeutic strategies.
Left bundle branch pacing (LBBP) has emerged as a promising physiological pacing technique, offering significant clinical benefits in both prevention of right ventricular pacing-induced cardiomyopathy and resynchronization therapy for left bundle branch block. Compared to His bundle pacing, LBBP overcomes limitations such as low sensing sensitivity, high pacing thresholds, and distal conduction system disease, and it provides a simpler and more efficient implantation process. However, the commonly used intermittent recording technique necessitates repeated procedural interruptions for parameter testing, significantly increasing procedural complexity and time. Recent advancements, such as stylet-driven leads (SDLs), and a rotatable connector integrated with lumenless leads (LLLs), have facilitated the development of continuous pacing and recording technology. This technology enables real-time monitoring of electrocardiograms (ECGs) and intracardiac electrograms (EGMs) during lead implantation, offering precise electrophysiological feedback to confirm left bundle branch capture. In this review, the clinical utility of continuous pacing and recording technology utilizing LLLs is examined systematically, with emphasis on its technical advantages and future potential for advancing cardiac pacing strategies.

Implantation Steps Based on Continuous Pacing Technology with LLLs
Continuous 12-lead ECG and EGM were recorded using an electrophysiological recording system, with pacing performed via a programmer (Model 2290, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). The pacing rate was programmed to exceed the patient’s intrinsic sinus rhythm by 10-20 beats per minute to minimize interference. Continuous pacing was maintained at 2 V per 0.5 ms, with real-time monitoring of ECG and EGM dynamics to guide precise lead placement. The electrophysiological system is configured to ensure optimal visualization of intracardiac signals across devices. At our institution, the EP-Workmate electrophysiological recording system (Abbott, Plymouth, MN) was utilized, with high-pass (200 Hz) and low-pass (500 Hz) filters applied to acquire filtered unipolar ventricular electrograms for monitoring discrete electrographic transitions. Simultaneously, band-pass filters (0.5-500 Hz) were applied to monitor unfiltered ventricular current of injury (COI) signals from the electrode tip, with amplitude set to 4 mV per cm (Video 1, view video online). The connection method was functionally equivalent to that with conventional configurations (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Following puncture of the left axillary or subclavian vein, the C315 His sheath (Medtronic) was advanced into the right ventricle. Sheath angiography was performed under 30° right anterior oblique fluoroscopy to visualize the tricuspid annulus, with images saved as anatomic references for lead placement. The cathode of the LLL (Medtronic) was connected to the rotatable interface of John Jiang’s connecting cable (Xinwell Medical Technology, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China), which features a bearing-like structure to maintain uninterrupted electrical conduction during rotation, enabling real-time ECG and EGM recording. Unlike the traditional intermittent pacing method, this technology allows continuous monitoring of progressive changes in paced QRS complexes as the lead advances into the interventricular septum. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the LLL was positioned at the target implantation site, and the sheath was rotated counterclockwise to align it perpendicular to the septum. The lead body was then rotated to incrementally screw the helix into the septum. One operator monitored the lead position fluoroscopically, while another continuously assessed ECG and EGM dynamics. The core innovation of this continuous pacing and recording technology lies in the uninterrupted connection between the distal cathode and the rotatable interface. Compared to the noncontinuous pacing technique, this method enables beat-by-beat analysis of paced QRS morphology changes (eg, chest lead transition, V1 notch, S-wave presence in V5/V6, and stimulus-to-V6 R-wave peak time [Stim-V6RWPT]) and impedance variations correlated with lead depth. If paced QRS morphology transitions were deemed unsatisfactory (eg, due to endocardial entanglement, myocardial fibrosis, or scar tissue impeding lead advancement), electrode rotation was halted and the implantation site was repositioned to achieve optimal capture (Fig. 1).

The continuous changes in the pacing QRS morphology are closely related to the depth of the electrode implantation within the interventricular septum, making real-time monitoring of electrode depth a crucial aspect of the LBBP electrode implantation procedure to avoid perforation (Fig. 2). As the lead advances from the right to the left side of the septum, paced QRS morphology progressively changes. For example, the notch in the paced QRS complex in the V1 lead gradually shifts from the lowest point to the end of the QRS (forming a right bundle branch delay pattern), while Stim-V6RWPT gradually shortens. During lead advancement, impedance measurements were obtained using the programmer’s default 5 V per 0.5 ms intermittent output protocol, while adjacent QRS morphologies were analyzed during 2 V per 0.5 ms continuous pacing. If Stim-V6RWPT shortens during high-output pacing (5 V per 0.5 ms) but lengthens during low-output pacing (2 V per 0.5 ms), this indicates proximity to the left bundle branch (LBB), necessitating reduced advancement speed. If the paced QRS morphology suddenly changes (eg, an initial r-wave appears in the V1 lead and/or s wave deepening occurs in V6) and the Stim-V6RWPT of 2 adjacent QRS complexes shortens by ≥ 10 ms under constant output, electrode insertion should be stopped immediately and output reduced rapidly to assess for the presence of an isoelectric interval and discrete electrograms. If Stim-V6RWPT lengthens after output reduction, the lead should be further advanced cautiously to achieve the shortest V6RWPT and optimize LBB capture thresholds.

The number of lead rotations is not standardized, but rather needs individualized adjustment based on ECG, EGM, and electrical parameter feedback. The reason for this adjustment is that variations in septal thickness and stiffness, along with helical advancement trajectories, may be influenced by normal or pathologic anatomic differences. Additionally, the COI can be monitored in real time using this method to determine optimal lead advancement speed and procedural termination timing. Higher COI values indicate residual rotational capacity, whereas excessive rotation speed may result in torque accumulation, typically caused by friction between the lead and sheath or torsional deformation of the lead. Excessive torque release may result in sudden lead helix penetration through the endocardium, causing perforation. Therefore, when a Qr morphology (indicative of left septal pacing) is observed in the V1 lead, slow lead rotation is recommended to enhance precision in detecting subtle COI changes. The amplitude of the COI for termination is empirically set at 6-7 mV, with a value of 5 mV recommended by the European Heart Rhythm Association clinical consensus statement on conduction system pacing. Failure to monitor these parameters may increase the risk of septal perforation (Fig. 3). Residual tissue on the helix should be cleared before a reattempt. Finally, the lead depth within the septum can be evaluated through contrast injection under left anterior oblique fluoroscopy (non-mandatory).

Lead Position-Dependent LBB Capture Criteria
Continuous recording technology enables real-time observation of dynamic position/output-dependent ECG/EGM morphologic transitions during lead implantation—these observations serve as diagnostic criteria for LBB capture. LBB capture is confirmed by right bundle branch delay patterns in paced QRS morphology and the following strict criteria: (i) if paced QRS morphology changes with V1/V6 transition, and Stim-V6RWPT shortens by ≥ 10 ms at constant output (2 V per 0.5 ms) during lead advancement, nonselective LBBP is confirmed; and (ii) if paced QRS morphology changes with V1/V6 transition and isoelectric intervals and discrete components appear on electrograms with fixed Stim-V6RWPT, selective LBBP is confirmed.

Discussion

Limitations of traditional right ventricular pacing and the pursuit of physiological pacing
Although traditional right ventricular pacing has been utilized widely in clinical practice, its association with pacing-induced cardiomyopathy has raised significant concerns. To mitigate iatrogenic complications, physiological pacing techniques have remained a focus of research exploration. Although His bundle pacing is the most physiological, its limitations, including low sensing sensitivity and high thresholds, hinder widespread adoption of its use. LBBP has emerged to address these limitations; however, its Achilles' heel lies in the difficulty of precisely localizing the capturing site and determining optimal termination points during lead advancement.

Clinical challenges of intermittent recording technology
Current LBBP procedures using LLLs typically employ alligator clip connections, whose intermittent recording imposes inherent clinical limitations. Frequent reconnections during multi-step lead rotations impede efficient screw deployment. Fragmented data acquisition compromises the gold standard for LBB capture confirmation—identifying true capture through QRS morphology changes at varying voltages—potentially omitting critical electrophysiological transitions. Given that optimal LBBP outcomes require precise placement and involve subtle ECG and/or EGM changes, operators must monitor QRS and EGM characteristics continuously. Implantation depth presents further challenges—superficial placement risks ineffective capture, and excessive depth of placement increases perforation risk. These technical constraints compromise procedural safety and efficacy during electrode implantation, particularly through reduced capture confirmation precision and heightened iatrogenic injury risk, necessitating enhanced monitoring. Repeated parameter verification may mitigate risks but prolongs procedure duration. These issues intensify in septal fibrosis cases, in which tissue resistance hinders electrode insertion, and suboptimal fluoroscopic visualization increases procedural complexity and time.

Integration of SDLs with continuous recording
SDLs are being adopted increasingly in LBBP due to their superior maneuverability and stability during implantation, yielding implantation success rates comparable to those of LLLs. Despite its risks, such as helix retraction, damage, and lead fracture, shorter procedural times can be achieved with SDLs, compared to those for LLLs, even with shared implantation steps. This advantage likely stems from SDLs’ continuous recording technology, which enables real-time ECG rhythm morphology monitoring during lead advancement. SDLs’ larger diameter enhances torque transmission and forward thrust, potentially reducing implantation duration. Notably, continuous ECG and/or EGM monitoring via SDLs remains systematically unstudied—particularly regarding the clinical significance of COI monitoring in LBBP and associated electrophysiological phenomena. Outcome heterogeneity across SDL manufacturers and inconsistent success and/or complication reports persist. When applying strict dynamic capture criteria, SDLs exhibit lower LBB capture success than do LLLs. Consequently, contemporary real-world LBBP research remains focused on LLLs, and novel tools are being developed to optimize implantation workflows.

Innovations in continuous recording technology for LLLs
Continuous recording technology has been implemented successfully in LLLs via adaptor systems. Jastrzębski et al. pioneered a simplified model using tip-wound wiring, enabling uninterrupted recording during lead rotation despite potential signal instability. Subsequent development of rotatable connectors has clinically validated continuous recording protocols, with advancements currently being made in real-time monitoring devices. In LBBP procedures, dynamic ECG changes are regarded as the gold standard for confirming LBB capture, as reliance on solely V6RWPT or LBB potential detection yields insufficient precision. Real-time recording technology addresses the limitations associated with intermittent morphologic analysis of electrophysiological signals. Procedural decisions—including lead advancement for deeper fixation, termination of unstable placements, or site repositioning—are guided scientifically by real-time electrophysiological feedback. Through continuous dynamic monitoring of electrophysiological signal variations during lead implantation, precise anatomic localization within the interventricular septum is achieved. Should unsatisfactory morphologic transition be detected, implantation sites can be adjusted immediately. Concurrent monitoring of ECG and COI transitions enables safe determination of depth to prevent perforation. LBB capture is confirmed through real-time observation of lead position-dependent morphologic changes during implantation—rather than postimplantation output-dependent transitions—thereby enhancing diagnostic sensitivity. Existing testing cable has been modified with auxiliary alligator clips to permit concurrent evaluation of ring electrodes and additional leads (eg, atrial leads) without requiring supplementary cables (Supplemental Fig. S1). This technological evolution parallels transformative advances in imaging modalities (from static photography to cinematic capture) and navigation systems, transitioning from pre-planned routes to artificial intelligence-optimized real-time pathfinding. Technological advancements are anticipated to streamline and standardize procedural execution and enhance capture verification accuracy, thereby democratizing LBBP for broader clinical adoption.

Beyond optimizing lead deployment, continuous recording technology enables observation of dynamic electrophysiological phenomena during transseptal implantation. The His bundle COIs (His COIs) are recognized as diagnostic markers for His bundle capture. Real-time monitoring of His potential dynamics is enabled during lead advancement into the septum, with implantation halted immediately upon detection of abrupt morphologic shifts (Fig. 4). In a reported case, a 2:1 intra-Hisian block with a Wenckebach phenomenon was captured immediately following an iatrogenic His bundle injury. In addition, His potential propagation patterns can be analyzed systematically during lead rotation under normal conduction, right bundle branch block, and LBBB conditions. Retrograde His and LBB activation during advancement—manifesting as direct or indirect capture patterns—are observable through unfiltered EGM monitoring (Fig. 5, A and B). Variations in native ventricular potentials correlate with lead depth, reflecting anisotropic conduction properties dictated by transmural myocardial fiber orientation from the right to left septal aspects (Fig. 5C). In prior studies, use of unipolar pacing morphology in V1/V2 leads during LBBP has been compared to use of septal-origin ventricular ectopic beats to evaluate the predictive value of ECG features for arrhythmogenic substrate localization. Continuous recording technology enhances this paradigm by capturing high-resolution transseptal activation sequences, enabling deeper clinical correlations. Electrocardiographic radial depth navigation—a technique combining unipolar EGM-based depth mapping via intracardiac guidewires with continuous recording and fluoroscopic integration—holds promise for developing novel catheter-based structural therapies.

Conclusion
Real-time electrophysiological signal visualization enables continuous monitoring of electrode positioning within the interventricular septum, providing critical intraprocedural feedback during LBBP implantation. This feedback informs operators when lead repositioning or advancement cessation is required, permitting immediate intraoperative adjustments. This approach not only simplifies the procedural workflow of LBBP but also enhances procedural precision and safety, while simultaneously offering novel perspectives for electrophysiological research. By integrating lead implantation with dynamic electrophysiological observations, this technology is poised to bridge cardiac pacing with advanced electrophysiological therapies. Future innovations are expected to further optimize procedural efficiency and clinical outcomes. In this era of artificial intelligence, comprehensive implantation data recording methodologies facilitate machine-learning applications for subsequent research advancements.
