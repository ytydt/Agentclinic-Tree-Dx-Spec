Evaluation of computed tomography radiation dose metrics in Saudi Arabia
ABSTRACT
This review evaluates CT radiation dose levels in Saudi Arabia relative to national Diagnostic Reference Levels (DRLs) established by the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) and the United Kingdom (UK). A total of 31 studies published between 2015 and 2025 were reviewed, covering adult and pediatric CT protocols for head, chest, abdomen, pelvis, and multiphase scans. The review highlights variations in the volumetric computed tomographic dose index (CTDIvol), and the dose-length product (DLP) values among different institutions in KSA. For adult protocols, fifteen studies reported dose metric values below the KSA NDRLs, while 8 studies reported values below the UK NDRLs. In contrast, among pediatric studies, approximately 90% of the reported dose metrics were lower than the NDRLs in both KSA and the UK. The results emphasize the importance of implementing standardized protocols to enhance the KSA national DRLs and expand the range of examination protocols, particularly for pediatric patients.
Diagnostic imaging plays a vital role in modern medicine, facilitating precise diagnosis and effective treatment planning. However, the growing utilization of ionizing radiation in medical imaging has raised concerns about patient exposure and the long-term risks associated with cumulative radiation doses. According to the United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation (UNSCEAR) 2020/2021 Report, the estimated annual number of medical radiological examinations worldwide surged to around 4.2 billion from 2009 to 2018. This marks a substantial increase from the 1.38 billion examinations reported in 1988 and the 3.14 billion in 2008. The principles of radiation protection; justification, optimization, and dose limitation remain fundamental to ensuring safety. Although dose limits do not apply directly to patients, optimization and justification must be prioritized. Justification of diagnostic and therapeutic radiation practices necessitates that the anticipated benefits outweigh the potential risks, considering available alternative techniques devoid of medical exposure. Furthermore, the responsibility for justifying specific radiation procedures lies with appropriately qualified medical practitioners. To optimize the protection procedure, several techniques are employed, including the design of radiological equipment in compliance with the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards, calibration protocols, and quality assurance measures such as correct patient positioning and collimation. Diagnostic Reference Levels (DRLs), defined and reviewed periodically, are essential tools for optimizing patient protection in line with the ALARA principle.
The concept of DRLs has been endorsed globally by organizations such as the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP), International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and the World Health Organization (WHO). These benchmarks help identify unusually high or low patient doses in standardized imaging practices. Furthermore, countries such as the UK, France, Germany, Australia, Japan, and the US have embedded DRLs into national legislation or guidelines, with regular updates conducted via national surveys and dose audits. In Europe, the implementation of DRLs is guided by the 2013/59/Euratom directive, which mandates their incorporation into national regulatory frameworks. Additionally, the IAEA has been instrumental in promoting the adoption of DRLs in low- and middle-income countries through regional cooperation projects, training, and technical guidance. In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), growing awareness of dose optimization prompted the development of national DRLs. The Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) initiated formal guidance and national frameworks, culminating in the first SFDA DRL report in 2023, based on data from 54 hospitals. This was followed by expanded standards in 2025 to include data from a larger number of facilities, additional modalities, and introducing DRLs for specific clinical indications, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness of radiation dose optimization strategies.
Despite ongoing efforts, disparities in CT dose levels persist across institutions. The lack of a unified national database and inconsistent coverage across modalities highlights a cognitive gap in understanding how national CT dose metrics align with international benchmarks. This review evaluates CT radiation dose levels in KSA based on 31 published studies (2015–2025), covering adult and pediatric CT protocols. The study compares local data with National Diagnostic Reference Levels (NDRLs) from the SFDA and international benchmarks from the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA), focusing on CT metrics variations to identify strengths, shortcomings, and optimization opportunities. The findings aim to enhance the NDRLs framework and support standardization efforts for future improvements in radiation safety and optimization in Saudi Arabia.

Methods
The articles selected for this review were obtained from PubMed, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, EBSCO, and the Saudi Digital Library, which serve as leading sources in the fields of radiology and medical physics. The search terms for the identification of articles in Saudi Arabia were “Diagnostic Reference Levels,” “DRLs,” and “Dose Reference Levels.” It was found that 31 articles (19 articles for adult patients and 12 articles for pediatric patients) were published between 2015 and 2025, aiming to establish NDRLs across healthcare facilities in KSA for CT imaging modalities. Additionally, only publications that were written in had been in English and published in peer-reviewed journals were included in this review.
The primary units of radiation dose in CT are the volumetric computed tomographic dose index (CTDIvol), measured in mGy, and the dose-length product (DLP), measured in mGy-cm. CTDIvol indicates the average absorbed dose by a specific phantom at a given scanner output (such asa set of scanning parameters or “protocol”). DLP is account for the scan length, offering an estimate of the total absorbed dose for the same CT examination procedure and individual factors (such as weight, height, age, and sex). These metrics are utilized to report the DRL values for CT, as recommended by ICRP publication. Therefore, this review extracted those reported CT dose metrics from each study. Studies were categorized into adult and pediatric groups across key CT protocols, including Head, Chest, Abdomen, Pelvis, and multiphase scans. Results and Discussion
This review presents studies that documented radiation dose parameters of CT imaging modalities in adult and pediatric patients throughout KSA from 2015 to 2025. Variations in reported CT radiation doses were found across the studies. The SFDA’s NDRLs in KSA were set in 2023, focusing on head, AP, and chest examinations for adult diagnosis and head scans for two pediatric age groups. In contrast, the UKHSA’s NDRLs in the UK encompass broader diagnostic ranges for both adult and pediatric patients.
In adult CT examinations, a descriptive comparison of CT dose metrics is presented in Table 3 and Table 4, highlighting differences in CTDIvol and DLP parameters relative to the NDRLs in the UK and the KSA. Among the selected studies, twelve CTDIvol values were lower than the KSA NDRLs, whereas only four values were below the UK NDRLs. Regarding DLP values, fifteen studies reported values lower than the KSA NDRLs, and eight studies reported values lower than the UK NDRLs.

Significant dose variations were noted by Manssor et al, Alkhorayef et al, and Alhailiy et al.³⁴ Both Alkhorayef et al and Alhailiy et al³⁴ reported CCTA parameters. Although no current NDRLs exist for CCTA in Saudi Arabia, the reported DLP values were 384 mGy.cm and 1221 mGy.cm, respectively both exceeding the UK NDRL of 170 mGy.cm. Additionally, Manssor et al reported CT parameters for a CAP scan, with CTDIvol and DLP values reaching 23 mGy and 1494 mGy.cm, respectively, surpassing the UK NDRL of 660 mGy.cm for DLP.
These variations are likely attributable to differences in patient weight and size, as DRLs are based on standard patient and phantom models with an anteroposterior (AP) trunk thickness of 20 cm and a typical weight of approximately 70 kg. Furthermore, some of the referenced studies were published over a decade ago, during which time CT scanner technology and dose-reduction algorithms have advanced considerably. As a result, DRLs have generally decreased across most examination types as reported in ACR reports.
In pediatric CT examinations, various scan parameters have been reported for the head, chest, abdomen, and AP regions across different age groups. However, comparisons with the NDRLs of both the KSA and the UK are limited to head scans. Table 5 and Table 6 present dose parameters in relation to the NDRLs of both values. As shown in the tables, approximately 90% of the reported dose metric values were lower than the NDRLs in both KSA and the UK. These findings reflect substantial dose reductions and underscore the successful implementation of national and international standards in clinical practice.

This review illustrated a comprehensive evaluation to date of CT radiation dose parameters across Saudi Arabia, incorporating both adult and pediatric patient data. In addition, a descriptive analysis against both national and international NDRLs offers a valuable benchmark for assessing dose optimization practices throughout the Kingdom. In comparison to the KSA’s DRLs, UK’s reference levels are generally more conservative, with lower DRLs for common CT examinations. Also, certain imaging protocols for adult patients, such as CCTA and CTPA scans remain underrepresented within NDRL for KSA. Furthermore, the SFDA only reported the pediatric NDRLs for one CT scan, therefore, there is a need to cover a wide range of CT imaging protocols for pediatric patients considering patient-specific factors, such as weight and size when establishing DRLs, as younger populations are more radiosensitive. Future research is recommended to focus on expanding NDRLs to cover these less frequently assessed procedures to establishing standards across KSA institutions. These initiatives will promote harmonized practices, DRL awareness and training, standardizing imaging protocols across facilities, and enhance radiation protection strategies in line with national guidelines. Furthermore, supporting research and dose registries, and benchmarking with international DRL values are recommended as effective tools for identifying gaps and promoting best practices in radiation protection. Nonetheless, aligning practices with the recommendations of ICRP 135 and the international benchmarks, where feasible, remains an ultimate goal for ensuring quality and safety.

Study limitation
This review identified CTDIvol and DLP as the most commonly used dosimetric quantities, with average and third quartile values frequently reported. While most studies relied on average values, third quartile values are recommended for benchmarking against national and international NDRLs. Additionally, the lack of detailed statistical data, such as standard deviation, limits the ability to perform robust comparative analyses. Future studies should include more comprehensive statistical reporting to support accurate assessments and optimized DRL development.
In conclusion, this review identified and analyzed CT radiation dose parameters across Saudi Arabia. CTDIv and DLP were the most commonly used metric for dose comparison, and findings indicate that many centers in KSA adhere to both national and international benchmarks, despite existing variations. By comparing local data with national and international NDRLs, the study assessed current practices and provided insights guide future improvements.
Although KSA has made significant progress in establishing and implementing NDRLs for CT imaging, gaps in protocol standardization and limited representation of certain procedures persist. A comparison with NDRLs in the UK further emphasizes the need for harmonization and optimization of CT practices. While the SFDA’s NDRLs represent a critical advancement in radiation protection and dose standardization, full implementation and adherence across healthcare institutions, particularly those involving pediatric patients, remain a work in progress. Therefore, continued efforts in education, protocol standardization, and periodic DRL updates in accordance with ICRP Publication 135 recommendations are recommended to minimize radiogenic risks and to ensure patient safety during CT examinations.
