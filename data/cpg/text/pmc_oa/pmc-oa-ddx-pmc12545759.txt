A chromosome-level genome assembly of Fusarium foetens that causes sweet potato root rot facilitates the identification of a key Fusarium-specific pathogenicity factor
Dear Editor,
Root rot, a severe soil-borne disease, affects economically significant crops like tomato, bean, and cotton. It significantly reduces the global yield and quality of sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam.). Isolation of sweet potato root rot pathogens has been limited to a few pathogens in specific regions: Fusarium solani in China and F. oxysporum and F. solani in the United States and South Korea, which pose risks to humans and animals. Because no chemical cures exist, the presence of root rot renders fields unsuitable for cultivation. Enhancing plant resistance through genetic engineering could be an efficient solution, but there are no reported studies on pathogenic mechanisms or resistance genes in sweet potato.

Typical symptoms were observed on two main sweet potato varieties, Longshu9 and Yanshu25, in Daxing, Beijing, where root rot is most severe in China (Figure 1A; Supplemental Figure 1A–1L). Fibrous roots exhibited black spots, which led to necrosis and wilting (Supplemental Figure 1D–1I). Storage roots with a dry, cracked epidermis and black rot were toxic and showed poor germination (Figure 1A; Supplemental Figure 1J–1L). DX68 was isolated from Yanshu25, and DX75 and DX94 were isolated from Longshu9 (Figure 1B and 1C; Supplemental Figure 1M and 1N). DX94 was more pathogenic, with white, fluffy colonies and ellipsoid, 0–1 septate microconidia (Supplemental Figure 1O and 1P; Figure 1B and 1C). Phylogenetic analysis using the internal transcribed spacer region, RNA polymerase II, and translation elongation factor 1-α sequences of 66 Ascomycota fungi revealed 99.59% identity between DX94 and F. foetens NRRL 38302, a sister taxon of the F. oxysporum species complex (Supplemental Figure 2A; Supplemental Table 1).
To explore the pathogenesis of DX94, we constructed a 47.9-Mbp chromosome-level genome assembly consisting of 14 chromosomes, with 176-fold Illumina, 213-fold PacBio, and 100-fold Hi-C sequencing coverage (Figure 1D; Supplemental Table 2). The N50 of the DX94 assembly was 4.26 Mbp higher than that of the F. foetens NRRL 38302 assembly (0.28 Mbp), the only other F. foetens genome available (Supplemental Table 3). The DX94 assembly contained 97.64% of 3817 complete core genes in the sordariomycetes_odb10 dataset (Supplemental Table 2). Its consensus quality value of 52.62 and error rate of 5.48e−06 confirmed that it was a high-quality genome (Supplemental Table 2). Repetitive sequences, 14 920 putative genes, and 574 non-coding RNAs were identified in the DX94 genome and annotated using multiple databases (Supplemental Tables 2 and 4; Supplemental Figure 3A–3D).
Comparative genome analysis identified 2363 unique genes between DX94 and 13 other species from the F. oxysporum species complex (Supplemental Figure 2B–2D; Supplemental Tables 5 and 6). Over 90% of the homologs between DX94 and F. oxysporum Fo47 or F. odoratissimum exhibited non-synonymous substitution rate (Ka)/synonymous substitution rate (Ks) ratios < 1, suggesting widespread purifying selection on these genes and evolutionary conservation of their sequences (Supplemental Figure 2E). DX94 has 11 specific orthogroups that contain a total of 25 genes, including L-idonate 5-dehydrogenase (FfIdnDH) (Figure 1E; Supplemental Table 7). FfIdnDH was a unique gene in F. foetens f. sp. batatas DX94 and was amplified in 11 fungi (Supplemental Figure 3E; Figure 1F; primers are shown in Supplemental Table 8). The amplicons were exclusive and conserved in F. foetens f. sp. batatas, suggesting that FfIdnDH is a potential marker for identifying F. foetens f. sp. batatas and quantifying its biomass in disease assays of nursery soil (Figure 1F). Virulence factors, including 981 carbohydrate-active enzymes, 28 types of cell-wall-degrading enzymes, 436 cytochrome P450 enzymes, and 4456 pathogen-host interaction proteins, were also identified, as were 148 kinases and 33 phosphatases, which are critical for regulating virulence-gene expression, adapting to environmental changes, and escaping immunity (Supplemental Figure 4; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10).
Pathogens secrete effectors to manipulate host immune responses and facilitate infection. In DX94, 310 effectors were identified through filtering (Supplemental Figure 5A; Supplemental Table 11). RNA sequencing revealed that expression of the F. foetens-responsive gene FfRP752 (ID: S12752) was highly correlated with root rot susceptibility, and FfRP752 exhibited the greatest differential expression between resistant and susceptible germplasms (Supplemental Table 12). It was more strongly induced in susceptible Longshu9 than in resistant Nongda16 after infection (Supplemental Figure 5B). FfRP752 is conserved in Fusarium spp., shows minimal homology to other bacterial and fungal sequences, and has no plant homologs (Supplemental Figure 5C–5E). DeepTMHMM-1.0 predicted no transmembrane domains in FfRP752 (Supplemental Figure 5F). It localizes to the nucleus, cell membrane, and cytoplasm in both sweet potato protoplasts and Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, and to the cytoplasm in fungal hyphae (Supplemental Figure 5G). Programmed cell death assays revealed that FfRP752 reduced cell death induced by both Bcl-2-associated protein X and elicitin INF1, suggesting suppression of host immunity (Figure 1G). Invertase secretion assays showed that its signal peptide is functional in secretion (Figure 1H; Supplemental Figure 5H).
To further investigate the function of FfRP752, we generated its knockout line Δffrp752. The Δffrp752 strain exhibited notably reduced growth, and its spores were abnormally round, smaller, and less numerous than those of DX94 (Figure 1I). Δffrp752 showed lower pathogenicity than DX94, with greater accumulation of salicylic acid (SA) in the infected storage roots (Figure 1I; Supplemental Figure 5I). In addition, treatment with 16 mM SA or 4 μM carbendazim inhibited the growth of DX94 (Supplemental Figure 6).
A yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) library screen identified a host protein, the nuclear factor-YA transcription factor IbNF-YA3, as a target of FfRP752 (Supplemental Figure 7A; Supplemental Table 13). Y2H, luciferase complementation imaging, and co-immunoprecipitation assays confirmed that FfRP752 interacts with IbNF-YA3 (Figure 1J; Supplemental Figure 7B–7D). The IbNF-YA3 promoter contains several SA-responsive elements, including W-box, as-1, and TGACG motifs (Supplemental Figure 8). IbNF-YA3 expression was highest in storage roots and was significantly induced by DX94 and SA treatment (Supplemental Figures 7E–7G). Cell-free degradation assays revealed that IbNF-YA3 degradation was significantly inhibited by MG132, indicating dependence on the 26S proteasome (Figure 1K; Supplemental Figure 7H). Notably, FfRP752 effectively promoted IbNF-YA3 degradation, suggesting that it targets and facilitates the degradation of IbNF-YA3 (Figure 1K; Supplemental Figure 7H and 7I). Peptide-binding sites in FfRP752 were predicted using DMFold, and lysine substitutions were introduced at three residues (F9, A11, and Q67) within these sites (Supplemental Figure 9A). Y2H, co-immunoprecipitation, and cell-free degradation assays demonstrated that the FfRP752F9K/A11K/Q67K mutant failed to interact with IbNF-YA3 or promote its degradation (Supplemental Figure 9B; Figure 1J and 1K). The mutant strain exhibited normal growth and development but reduced pathogenicity (Figure 1I). These results indicate that the FfRP752 binding sites (F9/A11/Q67) are essential both for the interaction between FfRP752 and IbNF-YA3 and for degradation of IbNF-YA3.
We generated overexpression (OE) and RNA interference (RNAi) lines of IbNF-YA3. The fibrous roots of IbNF-YA3-OE lines showed slight necrosis and accumulated more SA than the wild type, whereas IbNF-YA3-RNAi lines were mostly necrotic (Figure 1L). The storage roots of IbNF-YA3-RNAi lines exhibited a dehiscent epidermis and black rot lesions, whereas IbNF-YA3-OE lines appeared normal (Figure 1M). These findings suggest that IbNF-YA3 enhances root rot resistance throughout the growth period of sweet potato.
To identify global targets of IbNF-YA3, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing using the IbNF-YA3-OE-3 line and identified 1994 peaks corresponding to 850 target genes (Supplemental Table 14). About 44% of the peaks were located within functional genomic regions (Figure 1N). MEME analysis revealed that the majority of DNA motifs bound by IbNF-YA3 contained specific sequences such as GCCTCGGAGGAC (Figure 1O). Notably, two of the target genes, FER (encoding receptor-like kinase FERONIA) and RALF (encoding RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR), are associated with systemic resistance and the SA-mediated signaling pathway.
In summary, we assembled a chromosome-level genome of F. foetens f. sp. batatas, developed a specific marker, FfIdnDH, for disease detection, and analyzed numerous virulence factors and effectors critical for host interaction, thus discovering the virulence effector FfRP752. This effector hijacks IbNF-YA3 to initiate pathogenesis, and OE of IbNF-YA3 enhances root rot resistance. Our findings offer valuable insights into F. foetens pathogenicity and provide a genetic basis for root rot research in plants.

Data and code availability
The raw data from F. foetens f. sp. batatas DX94 generated by Illumina, PacBio, and Hi-C sequencing, together with the DX94 genome and annotation file, have been deposited in the National Genomics Data Center database (PRJCA029467). The raw RNA-sequencing data for ND-L5, ND-M1, and ND-H8 can be obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information database under accession number PRJNA1151202, and the chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing data from IbNF-YA3 are available at the National Genomics Data Center under accession number PRJCA038943. The other datasets that support the conclusions of this article are included in its supplemental information.
