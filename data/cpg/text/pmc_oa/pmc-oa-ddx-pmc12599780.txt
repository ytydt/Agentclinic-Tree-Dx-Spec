Modern Dual Mobility Implants in Total Hip Arthroplasty: Stability, Survival, and Ion Safety Evaluation
Abstract
» Dual mobility cup (DMC) implants reduce dislocation rates in both primary and revision total hip arthroplasty (THA), particularly in high-risk patients with neuromuscular disorders, cognitive impairment, or obesity.
» The dual articulation mechanism enhances joint stability by increasing jump distance and the head-to-neck ratio, without compromising range of motion.
» Incorporation of highly cross-linked polyethylene and vitamin E–infused liners improves wear resistance, supporting broader use in younger, more active patients.
» Long-term survivorship of modern DMCs is comparable or superior to fixed-bearing implants, with a lower revision burden and durable fixation when appropriately selected and implanted.
» Systemic metal ion levels remain low in well-functioning modular DMCs, though continued monitoring is recommended in younger patients and those with reproductive potential.

Introduction
Total hip arthroplasty (THA) remains a cornerstone of modern orthopaedic surgery, offering pain relief and restored mobility in patients with end-stage hip pathology, including osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, etc.. Since its widespread adoption in the 1960s, THA has evolved into one of the most effective and cost-efficient procedures in orthopaedics, consistently demonstrating improvements in patient-reported outcomes and life quality.
Despite ongoing advancements in implant materials, design, and surgical technique, instability and dislocation persist as leading causes of early failure and revision. These complications are prevalent in high-risk patients, such as those undergoing revision THA or with neuromuscular disorders and compromised soft tissue. Instability complex causes call for implants that enhance stability while preserving durability.

The Challenge of Instability in THA
Dislocation occurs in 1% to 5% of primary cases but rises to nearly 20% in high-risk groups. Common patient-related risk factors include age, obesity, and cognitive or neuromuscular impairment, all of which affect muscular control and proprioception.
Implant-related contributors include limited jump distance, especially in small femoral heads (≤28 mm), and impingement caused by contact between the femoral neck and acetabular rim. Malpositioned acetabular components increase dislocation risk, and newer data suggest that the Lewinnek safe zones may be insufficient without considering patient-specific factors, including spinopelvic mobility. In addition, soft-tissue insufficiency, particularly in elderly or revision patients, undermines abductor function and joint capsule tension.
To address these challenges, various implant strategies have been proposed, including larger femoral heads, constrained liners, and dual mobility cups (DMCs). DMCs have gained increasing attention due to their ability to augment jump distance, minimize impingement, and provide a larger range of motion (ROM), making them a promising biomechanical solution to THA instability.

Evolution and Biomechanical Concept of Dual Mobility Implants
Developed in France by Bousquet and Rambert in 1974, DMCs were designed to minimize instability in THA. Unlike fixed-bearing designs, DMCs feature 2 articulations: an inner articulation between a small femoral head and a mobile polyethylene (PE) liner, and an outer articulation between the liner and a metallic acetabular shell.
This configuration increases the functional head size while maintaining a modular, low-profile design adaptable to various surgical contexts. By increasing the jump distance, the arc the head must travel to dislocate, mechanical disengagement is reduced, and the enhanced head-to-neck ratio reduces the impingement. It contributes to smoother joint kinematics, improved load distribution, and lower contact stresses. Collectively, these advantages provide a strong biomechanical rationale for DMCs in primary and revision THA.

Expanding Clinical Indications and Modern Applications
Initially used in elderly patients undergoing revision surgery, DMCs have evolved significantly due to advances in biomaterials, modularity, and long-term data. Their application has expanded into primary THA for high-risk patients.
DMCs are also increasingly used in complex reconstructions (post-traumatic acetabular fractures, oncologic resections) where mechanical stability is essential. The advent of highly cross-linked polyethylene (HXLPE) and antioxidant-enhanced liners has broadened their use in younger, active patients seeking both durability and stability.
Data from joint registries support this trend. According to the 2024 American Joint Replacement Registry, dual mobility (DM) use in elective primary THA stabilized at 7.5% in 2023, after peaking at 10.7% in 2020. In revision THA for instability, DMCs accounted for 34.0% of cases, nearly equal to constrained liners at 34.3%. Similarly, the Dutch Arthroplasty Registry (LROI) and Italian RIPO data show growth in DMC's utilization—from 0.4% to 7.4% over 13 years.

Biomechanical Principles and Design Advantages

Dual Articulation and the Restoration of Hip Stability
The foundation of DMC lies in its dual articulation mechanism, which redefines prosthetic hip stability. Traditional THA relies on a single articulation between the femoral head and fixed PE liner, providing adequate motion but limited resistance to dislocation, particularly in high-risk cases.
DMCs introduce 2 articulations: an inner interface between a small-diameter femoral head and a mobile PE liner, and an outer interface between the liner and the acetabular shell. This design enables self-centering behavior, as the mobile liner rotates freely during gait, enhancing stability by increasing the adequate head size and jump distance. This broader arc of movement resists subluxation and impingement across a wider range of activities.

Influence of Head-To-Neck Ratio on Impingement and ROM
A higher prosthetic head-to-neck ratio improves the ROM before impingement, enhancing construct stability. Conventional designs with small head sizes (e.g., 28 mm) offer limited impingement-free ROM, increasing the risk of edge-loading and dislocation.
DMC increases this ratio by functionally enlarging the head through its dual articulation system. This permits greater excursion before the femoral neck contacts the acetabular liner, reducing dislocation risk during everyday activities such as dressing or stair climbing. The mobile liner also accommodates off-axis loading and postural shifts, adding mechanical safety, which is beneficial in patients with altered hip biomechanics.

Adaptive Kinematics and Load Distribution
Beyond static benefits, DMCs offer dynamic responsiveness. The mobile liner pivots within the metal shell in response to gait and posture, redistributing joint forces and lowering peak contact stresses. This adaptability is particularly protective in high-risk hip positions such as deep flexion or external rotation, where conventional implants are prone to shear forces and instability.

Clinical Translation: Reduction in Dislocation Across High-Risk Cohorts
The biomechanical advantages of DMCs translate into lower dislocation rates, particularly in revision THA and high-risk patients. Registry data and meta-analyses consistently show improved outcomes compared with fixed-bearing designs.
In a multicenter registry study, Epinette et al. reported dislocation rates of 0.1% to 1.1% in over 6,000 DMC cases, with significantly improved 10-year implant survivorship. Lawrie et al. evaluated modular cobalt-chrome DMC components in patients younger than 65 years and found excellent function, low revision rates, and minimal metal ion levels at 5 years.
Long-term data confirm low wear, excellent fixation, and minimal migration, even in younger, active patients. Acker et al. demonstrated improved gait and zero dislocations in elderly patients treated with cementless DMCs. Viricel et al. reported strong outcomes in patients younger than 55 years, with a 9.8-year follow-up, reinforcing DMC's safety in high-demand populations.

Implant Survivorship

Long-Term Survivorship of Dual Mobility Constructs
DMCs have demonstrated excellent long-term outcomes in both primary and revision THA, mainly due to improvements in PE, retention rims, and surface coatings such as hydroxyapatite. As their use expands to younger, more active populations, implant longevity is essential.

Survivorship Outcomes in Contemporary Designs
Second-generation DMCs show improved outcomes over earlier models. Duhil et al. reported 10-year and 15-year acetabular survivorship of 99.2% for uncemented DMCs with plasma-sprayed titanium and hydroxyapatite coating, compared with 90.9% in first-generation implants. Similarly, Cypres et al. reported 95.9% acetabular survivorship at a mean follow-up of 11.9 years when paired with a cementless triple-tapered femoral stem.

Predictors of Long-Term Success
Multiple factors influence DMC's performance. Surface coatings enhance bone ingrowth and reduce aseptic loosening. Patient variables such as age and BMI also affect outcomes; Gallarda et al. found that higher BMI and advanced age were associated with lower modified Harris Hip Scores (mHHS), likely due to altered biomechanics and healing.
Stem-cup compatibility is another critical factor. Cypres et al. showed that matching modern DMCs with triple-taper stems improved implant stability and wear resistance.

Clinical Translation and Functional Outcomes
Numerous studies confirm the functional benefits of DMCs. Gallarda et al. and Foissey et al. reported almost no dislocations at long-term follow-up. Other studies by Vatsa et al. and Tarazi et al. showed significant mHHS improvement, correlating with patient satisfaction.
DMCs are particularly valuable in patients with neuromuscular disorders or poor soft tissue support. However, some registry data show no significant difference in overall revision rates compared with fixed-bearing THA, especially in standard-risk populations. In hip fracture patients, DM designs reduce dislocation-related revisions but may increase infection-related revisions.
These findings emphasize the need for individualized implant selection and further randomized trials to better define the risk-benefit profile of DMC.

Future Perspectives and Ongoing Concerns
While crosslinked PE has reduced wear, its long-term performance in high-demand patients remains under study. Boyer et al. noted that wear rates in DMCs are comparable with standard bearings, but long-term data on modular constructs are still emerging. Future research should refine risk stratification and assess the safety of modular cobalt (Co)-chrome (Cr) designs in active patients.

IPD: an Implant-specific Complication
While DMCs reduce dislocation risk, they introduce the unique complication of intraprosthetic dislocation (IPD), causing metal-on-metal (MoM) joint friction and accelerated wear. Prevention depends on optimizing implant selection, component orientation, and soft tissue balance.
Modern DMCs designs have improved retention features and liner materials to mitigate this risk. Identifying high-risk patients through preoperative planning enables better implant choice and positioning, thereby reducing complications.

Mechanistic Insight
IPD typically follows a 2-stage failure: mechanical wear of the PE retention rim, followed by complete liner disengagement and MoM contact.

Risk Factors and Prevalence
IPD incidence is low (0.5%-5%) but more common in early DMCs with thinner liners and less robust locking mechanisms. Poor orientation, high cup inclination, or elevated patient activity increases the risk.

Management Strategies
Treatment depends on the extent. Isolated liner exchange may be appropriate if fixation is intact, though 18% fail due to recurrence. In cases of metallosis or liner fracture, complete acetabular revision and synovectomy are advised. Nonoperative approaches may be considered for frail patients.

Future Directions
New DMC's designs include reinforced retention mechanisms and improved PE. Early detection strategies (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging, computed tomography) are essential for preventing catastrophic failure. Continued innovation and registry tracking will help reduce IPD incidence.

Metal ion release can cause local tissue necrosis, pseudotumors, and ALVAL. Systemic effects include cardiomyopathy, neurological dysfunction, and thyroid abnormalities. Systemic and Reproductive Toxicity of Metal Ions
As DMC implants see wider use in younger, more active patients, concerns about systemic metal ion exposure are growing. While local effects such as osteolysis and ALTRs are well documented, systemic Co and Cr ion dissemination may lead to multiorgan toxicity, including reproductive complications.

Clinical Data and Translational Relevance
Though human data are limited, MoM implant studies offer insights. In 1 cohort of 150 women with MoM hips, 21% reported menstrual disturbances versus 8% of controls, suggesting a possible endocrine effect. Cord blood analyses have confirmed Co and Cr presence in neonates born to mothers with MoM implants, confirming fetal exposure.
These results highlight the importance of risk assessment and long-term monitoring in women of childbearing potential receiving DMCs.

Conclusion
DMC implants offer significant biomechanical and clinical advantages over fixed-bearing designs in THA. Increased jump distance, improved head-to-neck ratios, and dual articulation mechanisms enhance joint stability, particularly for high-risk and revision patients.
Clinical data demonstrate reduced dislocation rates, improved function, and strong long-term survivorship when combined with modern materials such as HXLPE and advanced surface coatings. Reduced revision rates and dislocation-related complications reinforce their cost-effectiveness.
However, as indications expand to younger and more active populations, emerging risks such as IPD, fretting corrosion, and systemic metal ion release must be considered. These complications carry implications for local tissue damage, systemic toxicity, and potential reproductive harm.
Future innovations in biomaterials, implant interfaces, and risk-stratified patient selection will be crucial to further enhancing safety and durability. With continued research and postmarket surveillance, DMCs are poised to remain a cornerstone of next-generation hip reconstruction.
