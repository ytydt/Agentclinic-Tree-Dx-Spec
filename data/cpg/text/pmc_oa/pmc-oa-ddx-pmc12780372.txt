Potential Role of Imaging in the Evaluation of Adiposity and Approval of Anti-Obesity Drugs
The global increase in obesity highlights the need for accurate tools to assess body composition and monitor treatment efficacy. Traditional metrics, including body mass index and waist circumference, offer limited precision for fat quantification. Imaging-based techniques capable of visualizing internal structures are increasingly being recognized for their ability to provide comprehensive fat assessment. This review outlines the principles, strengths, and limitations of key modalities, including dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), CT, MRI, and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). DXA is cost-effective and accessible for population-level screening, whereas CT and MRI offer higher precision, particularly for visceral fat assessment. BIA is a practical, low-cost alternative, but it is limited by variability and lack of standardization. Regulatory agencies, including the US Food and Drug Administration and the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, are increasingly supporting imaging-based endpoints in clinical trials of anti-obesity drugs because these methods capture fat mass reduction beyond total weight loss. Recent phase III trials of semaglutide and tirzepatide have underscored the utility of DXA and CT in quantifying fat loss and preserving lean mass. Selecting appropriate imaging modalities based on technical capabilities and regulatory considerations can improve the evaluation of obesity treatments and strengthen the design of anti-obesity drug trials.

INTRODUCTION
The global prevalence of obesity has steadily increased over recent decades, emerging as a major public health issue. If the current trends persist, nearly 60% of the world’s population may become overweight or obese by 2030. In obesity, fat distribution, particularly the accumulation of visceral fat, plays a key role in increasing the risk of metabolic disorders, such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, fatty liver, chronic kidney disease, and cancer. Obesity also imposes a significant economic burden, accounting for up to 10% of healthcare expenditures in developed countries. Individual-level interventions alone may be insufficient to address the escalating obesity issue; therefore, more comprehensive measures are required. Among these, the development and clinical application of new pharmaceutical treatments represent a promising approach.
Accurate assessment of adiposity is essential for monitoring obesity and evaluating the efficacy of anti-obesity therapies. Traditional indices such as body weight, body mass index (BMI), and waist circumference are easy to measure; however, these parameters have notable limitations. BMI and body weight cannot distinguish between fat and lean mass and show only a weak correlation with visceral fat volume. Although waist circumference is often used as an indicator of central adiposity, its reliability is limited by measurement variability, site dependence, and lack of standardized measurement protocols.
To address these limitations and enable precise quantification of body fat, imaging-based techniques that visualize internal body structures and enable a more comprehensive assessment of body fat have become increasingly important (Fig. 1). Available imaging modalities include dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), CT, MRI, and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (Table 1). These modalities provide a comprehensive assessment of body fat mass and distribution, aiding both clinical management and drug efficacy evaluation. As the regulatory approval of anti-obesity drugs requires evidence that weight loss is primarily attributable to fat mass reduction, agencies such as the United States Food and Drug Administration (U.S. FDA) and Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) recommend including imaging data in clinical trials.
This review explores the principles, advantages, and limitations of the various imaging modalities used to assess adiposity. It further examines how regulatory agencies, including the U.S. FDA and Korean MFDS, advocate for imaging-based endpoints in clinical trials. Finally, by analyzing recent clinical trials involving newly approved anti-obesity medications, we highlight the clinical and regulatory value of imaging techniques for evaluating the efficacy of anti-obesity drugs.

DUAL-ENERGY X-RAY ABSORPTIOMETRY
DXA, which is widely used to analyze body composition, uses two X-ray beams at different energy levels, typically 40–47 keV and 70–80 keV, which pass through the body to measure the degree of X-ray absorption. Because tissues differ in density and composition, they exhibit distinct attenuation patterns. Bone, being highly dense, strongly attenuates both energy levels, whereas adipose tissue shows a lower overall attenuation, particularly at lower energy levels. Based on these differences, DXA classifies body tissues into three components: bone mineral content, fat mass, and lean mass (fat-free). It also allows for regional analysis, providing detailed measurements of fat and lean mass in specific areas, such as the left arm or right leg.

Body Fat Measurement
Fat and muscle masses can be quantitatively assessed using DXA. A recent advancement in whole-body DXA is its ability to estimate visceral adipose tissue (VAT) using dedicated software developed by manufacturers, such as CoreScan by GE Healthcare (Madison, WI, USA) and InnerCore by Hologic (Marlborough, MA, USA). These tools apply proprietary predictive equations to differentiate VAT from subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) within the android region. Specifically, the software identifies SAT within the android region of interest (ROI), typically defined as the region between the last thoracic rib and the upper part of the iliac wings, and subtracts this estimated SAT from the total fat mass in the ROI to calculate VAT (Fig. 2). Although this method estimates VAT indirectly and is limited to a relatively small portion of theandroid region, studies have demonstrated a high correlation between VAT measurements obtained using DXA and those obtained using CT and MRI.
Currently regarded as the primary imaging modality for body composition assessment in the general population, DXA remains the standard method used in clinical and research settings. Several clinical guidelines, including those of the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People, recommend DXA as the preferred method for assessing body composition. DXA is the most recommended and widely used imaging technique in clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of anti-obesity medications.

Strengths and Limitations
The amount of radiation exposure of DXA is very low, typically between 1 and 10 µSv per scan, which is comparable to or even lower than the average daily background radiation of approximately 6.7 µSv. This minimal radiation exposure allows for safe and repeated use, making DXA suitable for long-term monitoring of body composition. DXA scans are rapid, usually completed within 10 minutes, and the technology is widely accessible at a lower cost than CT or MRI. Because of these advantages, DXA is readily generalizable to both clinical practice and research settings.
Despite its advantages, DXA has several limitations. First, it is sensitive to fluctuations in the hydration status, which may lead to inaccuracies in individuals with dehydration or edema. Measurements can also be affected by gastrointestinal contents, such as recent food intake. In individuals with extreme body compositions, such as those with very low fat mass or cachexia, DXA may not provide accurate estimates. Its accuracy in estimating VAT is limited because the measurements are confined to a predefined android region and can be affected by intra-abdominal organs and bowel contents. Another limitation is the lack of standardization across different DXA devices. Measurement results may vary significantly depending on the machine and software used. Therefore, consistent use of the same device and software version is recommended when monitoring patients over time to ensure measurement reliability.

CT
CT is currently the most widely used cross-sectional imaging modality, and it serves as a standard diagnostic tool in many clinical settings. CT distinguishes various tissues based on their attenuation, measured in Hounsfield units (HU), which reflects tissue density. Typically, muscle tissue exhibits attenuation values between 30 and 100 HU, whereas adipose tissue demonstrates lower attenuation values ranging from -30 to -190 HU (Fig. 3). These properties enable the precise separation of anatomical structures and accurate measurement of their area and volume. Unlike DXA, which categorizes body composition into only three components (bone mineral content, fat mass, and lean mass), CT can accurately differentiate the components within a lean mass, such as intra-abdominal solid organs (liver, spleen, kidney, pancreas, etc.), tumors, bowel, blood vessels, and lymph nodes (Fig. 4). CT also offers high inter-device consistency.

Body Fat Measurement
Fat in any region of the body can be measured using CT, which allows for the precise separation and quantification of visceral and subcutaneous fat by both area and volume. Therefore, along with MRI, CT is considered the gold standard for assessing quantitative changes in fat, especially in the trunk region, where DXA has limitations. CT has been used in phase III trials of recently approved anti-obesity drugs, including the trial of semaglutide (Wegovy®; Novo Nordisk, Bagsværd, Denmark), to assess visceral fat area (VFA) as a secondary endpoint.

Strengths and Limitations
With its high resolution and precision, CT enables detailed evaluation of specific adipose tissue compartments. Its body composition measurements closely correlate with those obtained using MRI and whole-body reference methods. In addition to quantifying VFA and subcutaneous fat area (SFA), CT can also be used to evaluate muscle quality by assessing fat accumulation within the muscle tissue; excessive fat accumulation in the muscle is known as myosteatosis (Fig. 5). Myosteatosis is associated with decreased muscle function and various conditions, such as metabolic diseases, cancer, aging, and obesity. Fat accumulation in the tissues of patients with cancer or chronic diseases serves as a diagnostic indicator of sarcopenic obesity, and it is expected to function as an important biomarker for predicting treatment response and clinical prognosis. In addition, CT provides valuable information on various cardiometabolic diseases, including sarcopenia, osteoporosis, atherosclerosis, and fat accumulation in specific organs.
However, the routine use of CT for adiposity assessment is limited by its relatively high radiation exposure and cost. A typical abdominal CT scan delivers an estimated radiation dose of approximately 5 to 10 mSv, which is significantly higher than that delivered by DXA. To mitigate this, single-slice or low-dose CT protocols have been implemented, reducing exposure to approximately 15–19 µSv while maintaining measurement accuracy.

MRI

Body Fat Measurement
MRI differentiates fat from other tissues by exploiting the differences in radiofrequency pulse sequences. Similar to CT, MRI is a cross-sectional imaging modality that enables accurate and detailed measurement of fat in various regions, including VFA and SFA. It is regarded as the gold standard for evaluating both quantitative and qualitative changes in fat. Regarding the evaluation of myosteatosis, MRI provides the best contrast between adipose and muscle tissues and has recently demonstrated higher sensitivity in detecting early fatty infiltration in muscles, with superior visualization of anatomical details compared with CT.
Several MRI-based techniques are available for the quantitative evaluation of body fat (Fig. 6). Among these, the Dixon method, which is based on the chemical shift between fat and water, generates water- and fat-only images, allowing clear separation of fat and non-fat tissues. Fat-only images improve the detection and assessment of intermuscular and intramuscular fat by eliminating signal cancellation artifacts and enhancing tissue contrast. The Dixon method is relatively simple, fast, and suitable for whole-body evaluations. The proton density fat fraction (PDFF), derived using the Dixon method, provides a quantitative measure of fat content and offers reliable and reproducible measurements comparable to CT-derived muscle density values. MR spectroscopy, although more technically complex, directly measures the fat content on spectral peaks. It can distinguish between saturated and unsaturated fats and detect trace amounts of fat (<5%), making it particularly useful for localized, high-resolution metabolic studies.

Strengths and Limitations
With superior soft tissue resolution, MRI provides high contrast for the qualitative assessment of adipose tissue and muscle, resulting in better qualitative evaluation than CT. Unlike DXA or CT, MRI has the advantage of no radiation exposure, making it suitable for whole-body imaging and long-term follow-up. Additionally, MRI can quantify subtle variations in fat distribution within tissues. MRI is not affected by edema and can also provide information on fluid retention, muscle inflammation, fatty infiltration, and fibrosis.
Despite these advantages, MRI has several limitations, including high cost, limited accessibility, and longer scan times. It also involves greater operational complexity than other modalities.

BIOELECTRICAL IMPEDANCE ANALYSIS

Body Fat Measurement
As a noninvasive method for estimating body composition, BIA measures the impedance to a weak electrical current, which flows easily through water-rich muscle but is resisted by fat tissue. Based on these electrical properties, BIA provides indirect estimates of body composition by interpreting impedance data in relation to established population-based equations.

Strengths and Limitations
BIA has several advantages, such as the absence of radiation exposure, easy device operation, and short measurement time, and is widely applied in large-scale studies, health screenings, athlete management, and nutritional assessments. In addition, the availability of portable devices facilitates their effective use outside clinical settings. However, BIA is less accurate than DXA, CT, and MRI. Its accuracy is also affected by various factors such as hydration status, postprandial state, immediate post-exercise conditions, and temperature. Moreover, BIA has limitations in accurately measuring fat distribution in specific regions such as VAT and generally demonstrates lower accuracy than other imaging modalities.

IMAGING IN CLINICAL TRIALS OF ANTI-OBESITY DRUGS: REGULATORY RECOMMENDATIONS
As body composition has become an important factor in evaluating the clinical benefits of anti-obesity interventions, regulatory agencies have begun to incorporate imaging-based assessments into their guidelines on anti-obesity drug development. The following section summarizes the key recommendations of the U.S. FDA and Korean MFDS regarding the use of imaging modalities in clinical trials (Table 2).

Recommendations From the U.S. FDA
In January 2025, the U.S. FDA released its latest update on obesity drug development guidance since 2007, reflecting the increasing role of imaging in clinical trials. Although weight and BMI remain the primary efficacy endpoints, the updated guidelines emphasize that the therapeutic goal is long-term fat mass reduction rather than weight reduction alone, particularly to mitigate obesity-related morbidity and mortality.
These guidelines encourage the use of imaging-based body composition assessments to better distinguish between fat and lean mass loss during treatment. It explicitly states that imaging should be conducted in a representative sample of participants, both at baseline and at the end of treatment. This recommendation applies to both adult and pediatric populations, with the 2025 revision offering guidance on the use of imaging in children.
Importantly, the guidelines recognize that changes in body composition, such as fat mass reduction, may support regulatory decisions when accompanied by improvements in metabolic risk factors such as glucose or lipid levels. In such cases, imaging data can be used as supporting evidence for product labeling claims, allowing sponsors to note effects such as “fat mass reduction” in official product information. Thus, although not central to drug approval, changes in body composition are considered valuable secondary outcomes that can enhance the therapeutic profile of antiobesity medications.
Additionally, guidelines recommend that pivotal trials be conducted in obese or overweight patients with obesity-related comorbidities, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, impaired glycemic control, cardiovascular disease, heart failure, liver disease, and chronic kidney disease, and that the therapeutic effects of these comorbidities should be evaluated.
U.S. FDA guidance also addresses technical and logistical challenges, such as cost, access to imaging devices, and inter-device variability. Despite these limitations, DXA is the preferred method, although MRI and other validated modalities are also deemed acceptable. This guidance allows flexibility in choosing imaging methods, provided that the approach is scientifically justified and discussed with the agency. This enables sponsors to tailor imaging strategies to suit specific study designs and participant characteristics.

Recommendation by the Korean MFDS
In the 2015 clinical trial guidelines for anti-obesity drugs, the Korean MFDS acknowledges the value of measuring body fat and its distribution as important indicators in assessing the efficacy of anti-obesity drugs. Although body weight and BMI are the primary efficacy parameters, the U.S. FDA guidelines state that validated methods capable of measuring body fat could be used. The guideline recommends demonstrating that weight loss is due to fat reduction rather than muscle or water loss and proposes tools such as waist circumference, subcutaneous fat thickness measurements, or validated body fat imaging techniques. Notably, the guidelines state that CT or MRI can be useful for evaluating changes in abdominal or visceral fat, underscoring the potential role of imaging in supporting mechanistic understanding and efficacy endpoints. Although DXA has not been explicitly mentioned, it is a well-established imaging modality for fat quantification and has been widely used in previous clinical studies; thus, its use as an available imaging method can be reasonably inferred. Although imaging is not mandatory, these statements suggest that CT, MRI, and DXA may be selectively used as tools to quantitatively and reliably demonstrate body fat reduction.

REVIEW OF PIVOTAL CLINICAL TRIALS OF RECENTLY U.S. FDA-APPROVED ANTI-OBESITY DRUGS
Drug development for the treatment of obesity has progressed steadily over the past decades, as summarized in Table 3. Although imaging-based outcomes in pivotal phase III trials of recently approved drugs have been classified as secondary endpoints, their inclusion has enhanced the understanding of drug efficacy beyond body weight alone and has provided evidence aligned with the evolving regulatory expectations of agencies.

Semaglutide
Among the recently U.S. FDA-approved anti-obesity agents, semaglutide (Wegovy®) stands out because of its extensive phase III clinical development program, known as the Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity (STEP) trials. This program not only established the efficacy and safety of semaglutide for chronic weight management but also incorporated imaging modalities such as DXA and CT to evaluate body composition.
Approval by the U.S. FDA was primarily based on the results of STEP 1 through 4, and all randomized phase III trials were conducted involving adults with overweight or obesity, with or without type 2 diabetes. These studies demonstrated consistent and substantial weight loss ranging from 9.6% to 16% over 68 weeks. Importantly, STEP 1 included DXA-based body composition assessments as a secondary endpoint in a subgroup of 140 of the 1,961 participants. After 68 weeks of semaglutide treatment, a 3.48% reduction in total fat mass, a 1.99% reduction in visceral fat mass, and a 3.04% increase in the proportion of lean body mass were observed. In comparison, the placebo group demonstrated only a 0.19% reduction in total fat mass, a 0.01% reduction in visceral fat mass, and a 0.09% increase in lean body mass.
After approval, the STEP trial series was expanded to include additional phase III trials. Notably, STEP 6 investigated semaglutide in East Asian populations, incorporating CT-based VAT measurements as a secondary endpoint in a subset of 180 of 401 participants, assessed at the umbilical level. Participants treated with semaglutide exhibited a 40.1% reduction in VAT, compared with a 6.9% reduction in the placebo group.

Tirzepatide
Another recently U.S. FDA-approved agent for chronic weight management is tirzepatide (Zepbound®; Eli Lilly, Indianapolis, IN, USA), a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist. Its regulatory approval was primarily supported by findings from the phase III trials SURMOUNT-1 and SURMOUNT-2, which demonstrated significant weight loss in adults with obesity or overweight, with or without type 2 diabetes.
Although the primary endpoints of these trials focused on changes in body weight and the proportion of participants achieving ≥5% or ≥10% weight loss, body composition was assessed as a secondary endpoint. In SURMOUNT-1, among 2,539 patients, a prespecified subgroup of 255 participants underwent DXA to assess changes in fat mass and lean mass. In the placebo group, the total fat mass decreased by 8.2%, and the total fat-to-lean mass ratio decreased modestly from 0.95 to 0.88. In contrast, the tirzepatide group experienced a markedly greater 33.9% reduction in total fat mass, accompanied by a substantial decrease in the fat-to-lean mass ratio, from 0.93 to 0.70. These findings suggest a more favorable shift in body composition with tirzepatide, highlighting preferential fat mass loss while preserving lean tissue.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES ON THE USE OF IMAGING IN STUDIES ON OBESITY

Standardization of Imaging-Based Endpoints
Although DXA can measure body fat mass, there is no consensus on the cutoff points for defining obesity. There are no standardized measurement protocols, anatomic landmarks, or cutoff points for CT and MRI in the assessment of body adiposity. The Japanese Society for the Study of Obesity recommends the umbilical level for VFA measurement and defines an abnormal VFA as ≥100 cm2, which has been used in the STEP 6 trial of semaglutide. A study from Korea involving 36,783 health screening participants suggested optimal cutoffs for VFA at the umbilicus level of 134.6 cm2 for male and 91.1 cm2 for female to identify individuals at risk of metabolic syndrome. The normalization of the measured fat area remains an unresolved issue, and potential approaches include adjustments based on height or weight and may also need to incorporate age and sex. Further validation of anatomical landmarks and measurement criteria through large-scale studies is warranted to improve the reliability of imaging-based assessments of obesity and ultimately establish suitable imaging endpoints for the study of obesity.

Cost-Effectiveness and Practicality With the Potential of Opportunistic Imaging
Currently, DXA is considered more cost-effective than CT or MRI in anti-obesity drug trials, which is likely to be due to its relatively lower cost and greater accessibility. However, there is also clear potential for improving the cost-effectiveness of CT and MRI. For example, the use of single-slice scanning, abbreviated protocols, or artificial intelligence (AI) can reduce scan time and resource requirements, thereby potentially improving the overall cost-effectiveness of cross-sectional imaging in large-scale clinical trials.
Commonly used for a range of clinical purposes, CT also allows for body composition analysis on existing scans without additional radiation exposure, which is known as “opportunistic imaging”. This approach is particularly valuable in oncology, where CT is more readily available than DXA at most cancer centers. Therefore, CT may be the preferred and cost-effective method for body composition analysis in patients with cancer. Long-term cancer survivors, who often undergo regular CT follow-up, have a life expectancy comparable to that of the general population, but they have an increased risk of metabolic diseases such as obesity. Accordingly, appropriate screening and management of obesity is warranted, and the value of CT as a tool for body composition analysis in large-scale clinical trials involving this cohort is likely to increase.
Efforts are underway to alleviate the high cost and prolonged scan time of MRI for body composition analysis. When body composition assessment is the primary goal, scan protocols can be simplified to improve feasibility. Studies have shown that abbreviated MRI protocols for specific purposes can significantly lower costs and scan time. Therefore, MRI may be more practical. Further studies are required to assess the cost-effectiveness of CT and MRI for body composition analysis, particularly when optimized protocols and resource-saving strategies are used.

Application of AI
Recent advances in AI, including deep learning, have improved the efficiency of CT- or MRI-based body composition analyses by rapidly automating the quantification of SFA, VFA, and skeletal muscle and the identification of predefined anatomical landmarks (e.g., L3 level). Large-scale acquisition of body composition measures in clinical practice is challenging due to the associated cost, time, and required expertise, and in this context, AI tools can potentially enable rapid and automated body composition analysis by leveraging imaging studies. Many retrospective studies have demonstrated that AI-based segmentation of body fat using CT or MRI can achieve high accuracy, with Dice similarity coefficients exceeding 0.9. The real-time implementation of muscle quality maps for assessing myosteatosis is also possible using deep learning. Although prospective clinical studies on AI-based body composition analysis are limited, the ability of this modality to significantly reduce the labor, time, and cost associated with CT or MRI by eliminating the need for manual segmentation makes it a strong candidate for use along with these imaging modalities in large-scale clinical trials.

Future Role of CT and MRI in Anti-Obesity Drug Trials and Regulatory Considerations
Although DXA is currently more commonly used than CT or MRI in clinical trials investigating anti-obesity drugs owing to its lower cost, greater accessibility, minimal radiation exposure, and ease of use, CT and MRI provide substantially more detailed and comprehensive information, offering clear advantages over DXA. Recent concerns regarding weight-loss–associated muscle loss, as reported in the literature, underscore the limitations of DXA, which cannot assess muscle quality or myosteatosis. CT and MRI address this gap by enabling detailed characterization of muscle composition and quality. Additionally, CT and MRI can simultaneously evaluate fat accumulation in organs, whereas CT can also identify atherosclerotic plaques and vascular calcification. Notably, the effect of retatrutide, a triple agonist (targeting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide, glucagon-like peptide 1, and glucagon receptors) currently awaiting U.S. FDA approval for use as a treatment for obesity, was investigated in a phase IIa trial involving patients with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) using the MRI-PDFF technique. CT and MRI allow the assessment of important cardiometabolic comorbidities, such as MASLD and atherosclerosis, which cannot be evaluated using DXA. Given that the U.S. FDA recommends conducting pivotal trials in obese or overweight patients with weight-related comorbidities and encourages evaluation of the effects of treatment(s) on these comorbidities, cross-sectional imaging is a valuable modality for providing detailed relevant information, shaping future trial designs, and meeting evolving regulatory expectations. Furthermore, ongoing advancements in imaging technology, protocol optimization, and AI-based automated analysis, particularly when repurposing existing health screening scans for opportunistic body composition assessment, are likely to improve the cost-effectiveness and scalability of clinical studies, paving the way for broader integration of cross-sectional imaging into obesity-related clinical studies.

CONCLUSION
Each imaging modality used for body composition analysis has unique advantages and limitations that make it suitable for different populations and research goals. DXA is well-suited for obesity assessment in the general population because of its cost-effectiveness, wide availability, and low radiation exposure. CT and MRI offer more precise body composition analysis and detailed assessment of muscle and fat distribution than DXA. Although CT and MRI are more expensive and less accessible, they can be used opportunistically in patients undergoing periodic scans, such as those with cancer, or in special populations and research settings requiring high-precision analysis. Notably, the applicability of CT is expected to expand with the integration of AI-based analyses and the development of low-dose imaging techniques, whereas MRI offers the unique advantage of quantifying subtle fat infiltration without the risks associated with radiation exposure. Currently, pivotal clinical trials of anti-obesity drugs use imaging as a secondary endpoint. However, regulatory agencies such as the U.S. FDA have increasingly emphasized not only total weight reduction but specifically the reduction of fat mass, as well as improvements in obesity-related morbidity and mortality, and in light of this shift, the application of imaging modalities in clinical trials is expected to grow. As demonstrated in phase III clinical trials of recently approved anti-obesity drugs, incorporating imaging endpoints provides body composition-specific insights that complement traditional clinical outcomes. Such data not only enhance the interpretation of weight loss but also align with evolving regulatory expectations for evaluating both the quantity and quality of weight reduction. Therefore, thoughtful selection of imaging tools guided by study objectives, population characteristics, and regulatory frameworks is essential in the design of clinical trials for anti-obesity therapies.

Figure (A) shows coronal abdominal CT images of a 40-year-old man (A) and a 22-year-old man (B). The BMI at the time of scanning was 22.9 kg/m2 for (A) and 26.5 kg/m2 for (B). Although (B) had a higher BMI, the CT images revealed that he had less visceral fat (red). Notably, the skeletal muscle mass of (B) was also higher than that of (A).
Estimation of VAT using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. The region of interest, between the last thoracic rib and the upper part of the iliac wings, was defined within the android region. Subcutaneous adipose tissue was first measured and then subtracted from the total fat mass in the same region to indirectly estimate VAT. VAT was reported in terms of mass (g), volume (cm3), and area (cm2). VAT = visceral adipose tissue, YN %ile = young normal percentile, AM %ile = age-matched percentile
Tissue segmentation on CT images based on HU values. Different tissue types were visually distinguished based on their HU values on the CT images. Subcutaneous fat (red) and visceral fat (green) were segmented based on a range of -190 to -30 HU. Skeletal muscle (purple) was identified using a threshold range of 30 to 100 HU. HU = Hounsfield unit
Tissue types distinguishable by DXA, CT, and MRI. Although DXA can classify body tissues into only three categories (bone, lean tissue, and fat), CT and MRI allow for a more detailed differentiation between lean tissue and fat. DXA = dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry
Muscle quality maps indicating the degree of myosteatosis. A, B: CT-based muscle quality maps of two the patients with color-coded muscle areas representing attenuation levels. Patient (A) exhibited a large area of normal-attenuation muscle and minimal low-attenuation muscle and intramuscular and intermuscular adipose tissue. In contrast, patient (B) exhibited a greater proportion of low-attenuation muscle and intramuscular and intermuscular fat, indicating a higher degree of myosteatosis than patient (A).
MRI techniques for body fat measurement. Body MRI of a 22-year-old man illustrating various techniques for fat quantification. A-D: Dixon method: opposed- (A) and in-phase (B) images were acquired using a dual-echo technique. Fat- (C) and water-only (D) images were generated by combining opposed- and in-phase data, enabling the visualization of fat and water signals in T1-weighted images at each voxel. (C) High contrast allows fat to appear with strong signal intensity, clearly delineating the intermuscular (arrows) and intramuscular fat (arrowheads). E, F: Proton density fat fraction: the fat fraction in the right hemiliver (E) was calculated as 19.6%, and the fat fraction in the visceral fat (F) was calculated as 99.8% within a 2.0 × 2.0-cm rectangular region of interest. G, H: MR spectroscopy: (G) a 3.0 × 3.0 × 3.0-cm voxel was placed in the right hemiliver for fat quantification. (H) In the upper graph, the spectroscopic frequency spectrum displays a prominent water peak at 4.7 ppm (black arrow) and lipid peaks at lower concentrations (white arrow). The lower graph shows the fat and water signals at different echo times. The T2-corrected fat-signal fraction was calculated to be 19.9%.
