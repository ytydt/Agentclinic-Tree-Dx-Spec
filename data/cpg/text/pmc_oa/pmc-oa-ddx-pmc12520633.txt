Economic Evaluation of Medications in Prevention and Treatment of Obesity: A Systematic Review
Background:
Obesity imposes a heavy burden on healthcare systems and society. Despite various treatment options, choosing cost-effective pharmacological interventions remains a key concern for health policymakers. This study systematically reviews economic evaluations of antiobesity drugs, comparing their costs and outcomes with those of lifestyle interventions.
Methods:
A systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase) were searched for full economic evaluations of FDA-approved antiobesity drugs, including semaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, phentermine-topiramate, and orlistat. Inclusion criteria required studies to report cost-effectiveness, cost-utility, or cost-benefit outcomes. Data on incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), quality-adjusted life years (QALY), or disability-adjusted life years (DALY) were extracted and adjusted to 2024 USD using PPP. Study quality was assessed using CHEERS 2022.
Results:
Fifteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Most used Markov models and cost-effectiveness analyses. The review of articles showed that 60% of the articles were conducted in the United States of America, 33.33% of the articles in Europe, and 6.66% of the articles in Australia. Among the reviewed studies, semaglutide was found to be the most cost-effective and clinically effective drug in most scenarios. Phentermine-topiramate showed acceptable cost-effectiveness, particularly in low-resource settings. Orlistat and naltrexone-bupropion were also considered affordable options, though with lower effectiveness. Tirzepatide, despite its high efficacy, demonstrated a higher ICER, making its cost-effectiveness context-dependent.
Conclusions:
These findings highlight the importance of integrating cost-effective pharmacological treatments into obesity management strategies. Semaglutide appears to be a highly effective and economically favorable option, while phentermine-topiramate and orlistat offer practical alternatives in resource-limited settings. Economic evaluations can inform policy decisions and optimize healthcare resource allocation in combating obesity.

Introduction
Overweight and obesity are characterized by abnormal or excessive fat accumulation, which poses a health risk. A body mass index (BMI) above 25 indicates overweight, while a BMI above 30 indicates obesity. Globally, nearly one-third of the population is obese. Between 1975 and 2016, the prevalence of overweight or obese children and adolescents aged 5-19 has increased more than fourfold, rising from 4% to 18% worldwide.
Obesity causes the occurrence of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, respiratory diseases, as well as gastrointestinal, thyroid, uterine, and ovarian cancer. Psychiatric disorders such as depression and anxiety are also associated with obesity. Proper diet and exercise are the most critical criteria for obtaining an appropriate weight. However, evidence that diet and exercise induce physiological counter-regulatory mechanisms that limit weight loss has led to the understanding that Obesity is a complex and multicomponent metabolic disease of energy homeostasis that involves central and peripheral mechanisms. Surgical intervention is also recommended for people with a BMI equal to or above 40. Obesity surgery causes complications such as severe nutritional deficiencies, for example, dumping syndrome, and other stomach complications, and some patients may return to their previous weight after surgery. Current clinical guidelines recommend antiobesity medications for obesity or overweight.
An adult’s body weight (whether he is obese or not) is stable in the long term on an average of 0.3 to 0.5 kg per year. In obese people, about 75% of the weight loss from a low-calorie diet occurs due to reduced body fat. Medications that have received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the United States and the European Medications Agency (EMEA) and the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) in China approval for weight loss include phentermine plus topiramate (PpT) (Qysmia), orlistat (Zenical), naltrexone bupropion extended release (NB ER) (Cantrio), and the glucagon-like receptor agonist peptide-1 (GLP-1 RA), liraglutide (LIRA) (Saxenda). Also, Lorcaserin (LORA) (Blovik) had previously received FDA approval, but it was recalled at the beginning of 2020 due to the increased risk of malignancies. Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4 mg) also got its approval from the FDA in 2021.
Studies show that using long-acting (GLP-1) agonists makes it possible to target endogenous nutrient-stimulated hormone pathways and achieve greater efficacy with acceptable safety. (GLP-1) agonists are therapeutic agents that improve hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), cause weight loss, and reduce the risk of hypoglycemia. The European Medicines Agency approved LIRA for weight management in 2015. semaglutide (SEMA) has recently been approved and is considered a supplement to diet and exercise for treating obesity. Tirzeptide is a once-weekly subcutaneous peptide (approved by the US (FDA) for type 2 diabetes) engineered from the native GIP sequence. Once-weekly, injectable SEMA, a selective GLP-1 receptor agonist, is approved for the treatment of type 2 diabetes at doses up to 1 mg. In trials related to patients who received SEMA, the average decrease in glycosylated hemoglobin levels of up to 1.8% and the average decrease in body weight of up to 6.5 k/g have been reported.
Obesity has a significant economic burden on the health system and society. Many economic evaluation studies have been conducted on obesity drugs to measure the cost-effectiveness of these drugs. In a US cost-effectiveness analysis study, Semaglutide 2.4 mg was found to be cost-effective compared to other pharmacological interventions in treating adult patients with obesity from a third-party payer perspective. In another study, a cost-effectiveness analysis comparing five anti-obesity pharmacological treatments from a US payer perspective found phentermine-topiramate to be the dominant treatment compared to other drugs.
In the most recent study, Xue et al. conducted a pharmacoeconomic evaluation of antiobesity drugs for chronic weight management through a systematic review. The study focuses on the economic evaluation of antiobesity drugs, with an emphasis on methodological considerations. The results showed that cost-utility analysis based on modeling was the predominant evaluation method, with most studies focusing on Orlistat. Emerging evidence on GLP-1 RA drugs for obesity is also highlighted. The study underscores the importance of simulation models in cost-effectiveness evaluations and the need for further research on the long-term effects of weight loss.
However, a recent analysis showed that using GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) instead of insulin to prevent all-cause mortality and hospitalized hypoglycemia resulted in higher costs. The number needed to treat (NNT) was 57 for mortality and 30 for hypoglycemia, with costs of US$54,851 and US$29,115 per case prevented, respectively, from the payer’s perspective. While the healthcare sector perspective showed savings, sensitivity analyses indicated that the cost-effectiveness of GLP-1RAs ranged from 60% to 100%, suggesting varying outcomes based on conditions.
Despite the growing body of clinical research on the efficacy and safety of antiobesity drugs, there remains a lack of consolidated evidence regarding their cost-benefit and economic efficiency compared to lifestyle modifications or surgical options. Given the financial constraints of healthcare systems and the long-term burden of obesity-related diseases, assessing the economic value of these pharmacological interventions is crucial. This study addresses this gap by systematically reviewing the economic evaluations of antiobesity medications to inform more cost-effective treatment strategies and resource allocation decisions.

Methods
This article presents a systematic review of the economic evaluations of drug interventions for obesity treatment. In this study, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) was used for the systematic review reporting guide.

Literature search
We conducted a focused search to identify suitable studies for analysis. We examined the PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science databases, using only the most relevant keywords. Our search strategy was structured according to the PICO format. For example, the PubMed database focused on patients with a body mass index of more than 30 as the study population. The investigated intervention was a comparison of drug interventions, including Tirzeptide, Semaglutide, Liraglutide, Phentermine-topiramate, Naltrexone-bupropion, and Qysmia with each other or with diet and exercise for obesity treatment.
We also explored additional gray sources to ensure a comprehensive search: Website of Health Technology Assessment International (HTAi), International Society of Pharmacoeconomics and Outcome Research (ISPOR), International Health Economic Association (iHEA),
An initial search was performed to check for sufficient primary study numbers and previous systematic reviews or protocols. Next, the search strategy was written using subject headings (such as MeSH and Emtree). Economic evaluation systematic review guides were used to select the final keywords. The terms and their replacement words were combined and used based on the synonyms of economic evaluation and related therapeutic interventions, using AND/OR connections and, in some cases, NOT. Search strategies can be seen in Table 1. The final search was conducted on 10 May 2024, and studies published between 1990 and 2024 were included. In this study, the results that were evaluated from the comparison of drug interventions used to treat obese people included ICER, DALY, or QALY.

Inclusion criteria
Studies were included if they reported full economic evaluations—specifically cost-effectiveness, cost-utility, or cost-benefit analyses—of antiobesity pharmacological agents in adult populations (≥18 years). The interventions of interest included the following medications: Tirzepatide, Semaglutide, Liraglutide, Phentermine-topiramate, Naltrexone-bupropion, and Qsymia. Only studies that were fully available and published in peer-reviewed journals were considered.

Exclusion criteria
Studies were excluded if they conducted partial economic evaluations (e.g., cost analysis or effectiveness analysis only) or were presented as review articles, conference abstracts, letters to the editor, or study protocols. Additionally, studies that did not report incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) or were published in a language other than English were excluded.
All articles related to the economic evaluation analysis of drug interventions in obese patients were selected according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria mentioned above, and duplicates were removed using Endnote software. In the first stage, the title and abstract of the remaining studies were independently studied and reviewed by two researchers (Beigi and Khosravi) to prevent any bias; if there was a disagreement, the third researcher (Sourasrafil) reviewed the studies. In the next step, the full text of the studies was carefully examined by two researchers (Beigi and Khosravi) separately. The differences of the researchers were reviewed by a third person (Sourasrafil). All studies reported QALY, DALY, or ICER related to the topic. All studies that met the inclusion criteria were included in the review.

Synthesis
Accurate information was independently extracted from each study by two authors (Beigi and Khosravi) using a predesigned data extraction form. The basic information about the entered study includes the name of the author, the year of publication of the article, the type of drug used, the study population, effectiveness index, determining the type of perspective (society, health system, health insurance organization, etc.), results (ICUR or cost per QALY), the type of sensitivity analysis was (one-way, two-way, possible sensitivity analysis, etc.). Also, to compare the costs in different studies, all the costs were converted to US$ in 2024 based on the purchasing power parity index. If an article did not report the year of costing, the year of publication was considered as a basis for adjusting the cost.

Quality assessment
The quality of the studies was assessed using the comprehensive CHEERS checklist. This checklist, designed to evaluate the reporting quality of health economic evaluations, was followed by two researchers, Beigi and Khosravi, meticulously and thoroughly. Their findings were then reviewed by the third researcher, Sourasrafil, to ensure a comprehensive and accurate assessment.
This checklist includes 28 questions: title and abstract, objectives, target community, context of work, study perspective, compared interventions, time horizon, discount rate, desired health outcomes, effectiveness measurement, measurement, and valuation of preferences based on outcomes. Estimation of resources and costs, the currency used and currency conversion rate, type of model selected for the study, assumptions, evaluation methods, approach to interaction with patients and other people affected by the study, study parameters, additional costs, and consequences. It examines identifying uncertainty, identifying heterogeneities, the impact of interaction with patients and other people affected by the study, study findings, limitations and sources of funding, and declaring conflicts of study. Studies that fully met each checklist item were scored as “1,” those that partially met the criteria were scored as 0.5, and 0 when the study did not meet the criteria. A percentage score was then calculated for each study.
The risk of bias in the included nonrandomized studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. Two independent reviewers conducted the assessments, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. No automation tools were employed in this process. Reporting bias was qualitatively evaluated by comparing the reported outcomes with the original study objectives. Studies without registered protocols or with inconsistencies in outcome reporting were flagged as being at risk of reporting bias. Additionally, the potential influence of unpublished or missing data on the overall findings was acknowledged.

Results
The search initially yielded 1711 records in the databases. After removing duplicates, 947 records remained. Subsequently, 694 records were excluded based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study. Of these, 9 articles were removed due to lack of access to the full text, resulting in a final selection of 15 articles based on the study criteria. The findings of the study show that among the 15 articles included in the study, 4 articles were published in 2023 AD, one article in 2022, and two articles in 2021 AD. Also, one article has been published in each of the years 2019, 2018, 2015, and 2011 AD. Also, two articles have been published in each of 2005 and 2008 AD. The geographical distribution of the studies is as follows: 60% of the articles (9 articles) were published in the United States of America, 33.33% of the articles (5 articles) were published in Europe, and 6.66% of the articles (1 article) were published in Australia.
14 articles out of 15 final articles included in the study were done using the cost-effectiveness analysis method. Only one article used the cost-utility analysis method. The review of the final articles showed that 13 articles calculated the ICER per QALY, and one calculated the ICER per DALY. Also, one article only calculated life years and QALY. Also, one study calculated ICFCR and ICFER in addition to calculating the ICER value per QALY.
Three studies had a 1-year time horizon, and five studies had a time horizon of more than 30 years. Only one study had a time horizon of 6 months. Also, one study had a time 10-year horizon. Five studies defined several time horizons, including 1-year, 3-year, 5-year, 6-year, and 10-year horizons. The perspective study defined in 7 articles was only the health system, in 6 studies only the third party (insurance), and 1 study only the society.
The discount rate was 3% in 10 studies, 3.5% in 3, 4% in one study, and 5% in another. Two studies did not mention whether or not to perform a sensitivity analysis. In one study, the one-way method was used; in 6 studies, the PSA method was used. Five studies used one-way and PSA methods for sensitivity analysis. one study did not mention the type of sensitivity analysis method.
In the review of the final articles included in the study, the method of financing the study was not mentioned in three studies. One study was not financed. However, in the majority of the studies (11 in total), the sources of funding were transparently disclosed, providing a comprehensive view of the research’s financial landscape. Mechanism of drug action
In this study, the mechanisms of action of certain drugs were also identified. It was found that sibutramine acts as a norepinephrine and serotonin reuptake inhibitor. This drug aids in weight loss by suppressing appetite and increasing thermogenesis. Orlistat is an oral gastrointestinal lipase inhibitor that binds to lipase enzymes in the intestine, preventing the breakdown of dietary fats into smaller, absorbable molecules. This process blocks fat absorption, reduces calorie intake, and facilitates weight loss. The standard dose of Orlistat is 120 mg, taken three times daily before meals. This dosage can inhibit up to 30% of dietary fat absorption. Patients are advised to use this medication alongside lifestyle modifications for effective weight management. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor analogs (GLP-1RAs) were initially developed for blood sugar control. However, clinical studies revealed their significant effects on weight loss. These drugs work through mechanisms such as delaying gastric emptying, which prolongs the feeling of fullness, and reducing food intake in obese individuals. These features ultimately lead to effective and sustained weight loss. SEMA 2.4 mg is a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analog that aids in weight loss through mechanisms such as increasing satiety, enhancing satisfaction from food, and reducing hunger. Discussion
The results of the current study indicated that out of the 15 articles reviewed, the majority were conducted in the United States and Europe. Most of these studies utilized Markov models and probabilistic sensitivity analysis PSA. Thirteen articles reported ICER per QALY, while only one article employed DALY. Additionally, the time horizons varied significantly among the studies, ranging from 6 months to over 30 years. In most cases, the perspective taken was that of the health system or third-party payer, and a discount rate of 3% was the most commonly used.
This systematic review evaluated drug interventions for weight loss in comparison to lifestyle changes and surgical options. The results showed that LIRA 3 mg had varying costs for patients based on their degree of obesity when compared to the OPTIFAST program and no intervention. Additionally, the OPTIFAST program was identified as a more cost-effective option. The Qysmia drug has been deemed cost-effective for individuals with overweight or obesity and related health issues, particularly when used over an extended period. The SEMA 2.4 mg is more cost-effective than bariatric surgery. In terms of Orlistat, various studies have indicated that its use in conjunction with a diet is more effective for weight loss compared to diet alone. However, the effectiveness of Orlistat largely depends on the patient’s specific condition and the duration of the treatment.
Some studies have indicated that drugs such as LIRA 1.8 mg, SEMA 1 mg, DULA, and Exenatide show variable cost-effectiveness. One particular study found that SEMA was the most effective drug for weight loss. When comparing drugs like PpT, Orlistat, and SEMA against no treatment, PpT was recognized as a cost-effective option for weight loss.
PpT or Qysmia has been evaluated against various strategies for obesity treatment in six studies. One study found that the combination of Qysmia and dietary changes was more effective than a combination of medication and diet alone. In contrast to LIRA, Qysmia works by inhibiting fat absorption in the gut, leading to weight loss. This dual mechanism of phentermine (appetite suppression via sympathetic activation) and topiramate (appetite regulation via GABA modulation) enhances weight loss outcomes, which contributes to its favorable cost-effectiveness profile as demonstrated in multiple studies. These studies emphasize that sustained use improves long-term outcomes, particularly in patients with comorbidities. Previous studies, including a review conducted in 2016, demonstrated that Qysmia could result in at least a 5% weight loss within 6 to 12 months.
Both SEMA and LIRA are GLP-1 agonists that effectively reduce weight in patients with obesity and type 2 diabetes. In one study, SEMA 2.4 mg was found to be more effective and cost-effective when compared to surgery as well as to LIRA, Dola, Axenatide, and no treatment at all. The STEP research in 2021 states that the use of SEMA 2.4 mg has caused a weight loss of about 15% during 68 weeks, which is more than LIRA. SEMA appears to be a better option for patients with more severe weight loss needs due to its higher efficacy and appetite-regulating power. A study by Kim et al. (2022) examining SEMA from a payer perspective in the United States suggests that it is more cost-effective in terms of ICER compared with alternatives such as PpT and NB with a reasonable incremental cost. Its findings are in line with the results of present study and show that SEMA is cost-effective, especially compared to lifestyle interventions. However, factors such as treatment costs and side effects should also be considered in choosing between these two drugs.
Orlistat is a lipase inhibitor that prevents the absorption of fats in the digestive tract and is commonly used to treat obesity. In a review of final articles, Orlistat was compared to lifestyle interventions, specifically adherence to a special diet, such as a low-calorie diet. The findings from all four studies indicated that the combination of Orlistat and dietary changes was more effective for weight loss than diet alone.
In the present review, one study compared Orlistat with Sibutramine, concluding that neither of the two drugs was cost-effective. However, due to its unique mechanism, Orlistat may be beneficial for certain patients seeking a complementary treatment to lower fat absorption. While LIRA demonstrates stronger weight loss effects by influencing appetite and hormonal regulation, Orlistat is mainly effective by reducing fat absorption and may be particularly useful in cases of high fat intake.
Liraglutide, by mimicking GLP-1, delays gastric emptying and enhances satiety, which directly translates to reduced calorie intake. These effects explain the moderate-to-high cost-effectiveness shown in studies that compare LIRA to standard care, particularly in diabetic populations where glycemic control adds further value.
Orlistat has been proposed as an affordable and economical option in numerous studies due to its low cost and easy accessibility. Although it is less effective than medications like SEMA and Tirzeptide, it is considered a viable option for low-income patients and health systems with limited budgets. A study conducted in Ireland indicates that Orlistat has a low ICER compared to some other drugs, making it potentially cost-effective for certain patients and payers. Orlistat’s cost-effectiveness arises from its low unit cost and its mechanism of action—blocking gastrointestinal lipase—making it an attractive option in resource-limited settings or as an adjunct in high-fat diets, despite its moderate efficacy.
PpT is considered more cost-effective compared to other medications like LIRA and SEMA. Some studies suggest it could be an affordable option for overweight patients. Although PpT may not be as effective as GLP-1 receptor agonists, its ICER indicates that it can be a cost-effective choice for a specific group of patients. Its mechanism combines appetite suppression and metabolic rate enhancement, which contributes to its utility in patients unable to tolerate GLP-1 drugs or where budgetary constraints limit access. This pharmacologic profile is reflected in its low ICER values in multiple comparative analyses.
These analyses, based on credible studies, demonstrate the importance of balancing cost, effectiveness, and accessibility when selecting medications for obesity treatment. The choice of drug ultimately depends on the individual patient’s needs, preferences, and potential side effects. The findings of this study hold significant importance in the economic evaluation of drug interventions for obesity treatment, particularly for policymakers, healthcare providers, and patients.
These results can aid physicians and treatment teams in selecting both effective and cost-effective medications. For example, drugs like SEMA emerge as suitable options for patients with severe obesity due to their high cost-effectiveness. Physicians can tailor treatment choices to align with individual patient conditions and needs, ultimately facilitating improved health outcomes.
The findings of this study can assist public health policymakers in making more informed decisions regarding resource allocation and the development of public health programs. Understanding which drugs yield the best economic outcomes enables them to use limited resources more effectively and prioritize interventions that have the greatest impact. This is particularly crucial in situations where health resources are scarce. This study aims to enhance budget transparency within health systems. By conducting a cost-effectiveness analysis of antiobesity drugs, hospital and health system managers can make more informed decisions regarding budget allocation and avoid unnecessary additional treatment costs.
Such improvements can lead to reduced overall treatment expenses and increased efficiency within the health system. Furthermore, the findings from this study can raise patient awareness about various treatment options and their associated costs. With better knowledge of medication expenses and effectiveness, patients can make more informed choices about their treatments and select the most suitable options for managing obesity. Additionally, these results can assist researchers and pharmaceutical companies in guiding future research and development efforts. A more precise understanding of effective and low-cost medications can pave the way for the creation of new drugs that offer superior characteristics and effectiveness.
The findings of this review can also inform the design of future clinical studies to achieve better outcomes in the area of cost-effectiveness. Overall, these insights serve as a valuable resource for clinical decision-making, health policy development, and resource allocation within health systems. Ultimately, improving patient health outcomes and reducing treatment costs are among the positive impacts of applying these findings.

Limitation
One of the main limitations of this study is the variability in the design of the reviewed studies. Different analytical methods and models have been employed, as well as differences in the context of healthcare systems, such as access to treatments and cost structures, which can lead to significant discrepancies in results and interpretations. These differences hinder comparisons between studies and may prevent a unified analysis of the findings.
The time horizons considered in the various studies also vary significantly, impacting the results. For instance, studies that focus on short-term costs may yield different conclusions than those emphasizing long-term costs. This inconsistency can result in disagreements in economic evaluations. Additionally, many studies often overlook cultural, social, and economic factors that can influence patients’ therapeutic behaviors and access to medications. Such variables can significantly affect findings and interpretations and may differ across patient populations in various countries or regions.
Another limitation is the financial support and dependence of the authors on pharmaceutical companies. Data regarding funding and authors’ affiliations indicate that a substantial portion of studies—33%, or 5 articles—was funded by pharmaceutical companies in the United States and Europe. Notably, Novo Nordisk has supported two studies evaluating its drugs, such as SEMA and LIRA. In some cases, all or some authors were employed by or acted as consultants for the company. Additionally, Nestlé Health Science funded two studies in this area, where the lead author served as a cost consultant, and other authors were employees of the company.
Approximately 33% of the studies (5 articles) received support from academic and National institutions, resulting in less dependence on pharmaceutical funding, which helps maintain the independence of these studies. Only 6% of the studies (1 article) reported being conducted independently without financial support, with none of the authors serving as employees or consultants for pharmaceutical companies, two articles did not declare funding situations. Also, one study did not disclose its funding status, but the study author was a consultant for Roche Ireland. Roche Spa in Italy supported one article.
Pharmaceutical company-funded studies often report favorable outcomes for the companies’ products. For instance, research funded by Novo Nordisk often shows its drugs to be more effective than alternative treatments. This bias may stem from study design choices, the selection of specific indicators, or a cost-effectiveness perspective that benefits the funding company. Affiliations with pharmaceutical companies may facilitate the publication of positive articles regarding their products. Sponsors may also influence research outcomes based on corporate policies, and they can control access to and interpretation of the data. Consequently, the research findings may disproportionately benefit the sponsoring company.
In about 33% of the articles, conflicts of interest arise due to the authors’ affiliations with pharmaceutical companies. These affiliations can lead to favorable presentations of the company’s drugs and may significantly impact study outcomes, ultimately affecting clinical recommendations and policies related to obesity treatment. Thus, it is crucial to exercise caution and conduct thorough evaluations when using these findings for clinical or policy decisions to mitigate potential biases stemming from financial support.

Conclusions
This systematic review conducts a comparative analysis of various antiobesity drugs, highlighting the differing levels of cost-effectiveness and health outcomes associated with each. Notably, SEMA emerges as one of the most effective drugs in this category. Research indicates that SEMA is more cost-effective than alternatives such as PpT and naltrexone-bupropion, primarily due to its positive effects on weight loss and blood sugar improvement.
In contrast, Tirzeptide has been identified as an appealing option for its efficacy in weight loss and enhancement of HbA1c levels, despite having a higher ICER. PpT is considered a suitable choice for patients on a limited budget, as it is less expensive than newer medications like LIRA. Additionally, both Orlistat and naltrexone-bupropion have been recognized in some studies as cost-effective choices for health systems and patients, given their lower costs, even if they demonstrate less efficacy compared to GLP-1 drugs.
Given the high costs associated with invasive surgeries for weight loss, the increasing burden of obesity-related chronic diseases, and the broader economic impact of reduced productivity, the findings of this review underscore the importance of integrating cost-effective pharmacological treatments into national obesity management strategies. Facilitating access to medications that promote sustained weight loss could reduce reliance on surgical interventions, decrease the incidence of comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular conditions, and ultimately lower healthcare expenditures. Incorporating these drugs into clinical practice guidelines may improve patient outcomes and quality of life, while also enabling more efficient allocation of healthcare resources. From a public health perspective, prioritizing equitable access and affordability of effective antiobesity therapies is a strategic move towards controlling the obesity epidemic and mitigating its long-term socioeconomic consequences.
