Mitochondrial transfer as a novel therapeutic approach in ischemic stroke treatment: Current challenges and future perspectives Mitochondrial transfer as a novel therapeutic approach in ischemic stroke treatment: current challenges and future perspectives
Abstract
Ischemic stroke, the second leading cause of human mortality, presents a formidable challenge to healthcare. Following ischemic insult, the brain undergoes intricate pathological transformations, prominently marked by mitochondrial damage, including swelling, fission, and mitophagy, collectively termed mitochondrial quality control disorder. Mitochondria, pivotal in energy regulation and oxidative stress modulation, play a critical role in neuronal apoptosis post‐stroke. To solve the problems caused by mitochondrial quality control disorders, mitochondrial transfer has become a new therapeutic strategy for central nervous system diseases. Mitochondrial transfer refers to the process by which certain cell types export their mitochondria and pass them on to other cell types, a process also known as intercellular mitochondrial transfer. Mechanistically, mitochondrial transfer occurs via tunneling nanotubes, extracellular vesicles, and free mitochondrial transfer, exerting multifaceted effects such as anti‐inflammatory, anti‐lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis modulation, and enhancement of mitochondrial metabolism. This review explores the therapeutic efficacy, current obstacles, and future prospects of mitochondrial transfer in ischemic stroke, offering insights to researchers and instilling hope in patients for conquering this debilitating condition.
Cerebral ischemia is a serious neurological disease that mainly leads to pathological changes such as mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress and iron death. Mitochondria play a key role in the regulation of cellular energy metabolism and oxidative stress, and their damage can lead to mitochondrial damage, reduced adenosine triphosphate production, apoptosis and neuroinflammation. Mitochondrial transfer therapy, as an emerging treatment strategy, promotes the transfer of healthy mitochondria, improves energy metabolism, reduces neuroinflammatory response and reactive oxygen species levels, inhibits iron death through the mitochondrial fusion mechanism, and helps to improve ischemic brain injury, showing a good therapeutic prospect, and is expected to become an effective method to improve the recovery of brain tissue after ischemia.
Highlights
The study introduces mitochondrial transfer as a groundbreaking therapeutic strategy for treating ischemic stroke, a condition that remains a significant challenge in healthcare due to its high mortality and disability rates.
The review elucidates the mechanisms by which mitochondrial transfer occurs, including through tunneling nanotubes, extracellular vesicles, and free mitochondrial transfer. These mechanisms are crucial for intercellular communication and material exchange, offering new avenues for therapeutic intervention.
The findings provide a foundation for developing novel therapeutic strategies that could potentially revolutionize the treatment of ischemic stroke and other neurodegenerative diseases by targeting mitochondrial dysfunction.

INTRODUCTION
Ischemic stroke, also known as cerebral infarction, arises from inadequate blood supply to the brain due to circulatory disorders, leading to cerebral hypoxia and ischemia. This condition results in local brain tissue ischemic necrosis or encephalomalacia. With societal development, ischemic stroke incidence rates are rapidly escalating, now ranking as a primary cause of human disability and mortality globally. Pathologically, ischemic stroke induces inflammatory reactions, oxidative stress, cell apoptosis, and neuronal death. Neuronal demise, a profound consequence of ischemic stroke, critically impacts brain function, encompassing cognition, perception, and motor control.
Recent advances in basic and clinical research have highlighted the limited regenerative capacity of neurons, particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress due to the abundance of polyunsaturated fatty acids in neuronal membranes. Mitochondria, pivotal in aerobic respiration and energy metabolism, play a crucial role in maintaining neuronal homeostasis and redox balance. During an ischemic stroke, the disruption of oxygen and nutrient supply adversely affects mitochondrial function, leading to compromised oxidative phosphorylation, diminished adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, and elevated generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Additionally, ischemic insult diminishes mitochondrial calcium handling and overall mitochondrial function, contributing significantly to neuronal demise.
Faced with these challenges, researchers have discovered a treatment called mitochondrial transfer, which transfers healthy mitochondria into damaged cells. This novel methodology employs mechanisms such as tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) or microvesicles to facilitate the transfer of mitochondria from donor to recipient cells. Under physiological conditions, the phenomenon of mitochondrial transfer occurs all the time and involves a variety of cell types and mechanisms. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are one of the main donor cells for mitochondrial transfer, and they can transfer mitochondria to a variety of recipient cells, such as cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells and nerve cells, through tunnel nanotubes. Cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells can carry out bidirectional mitochondrial transfer through TNTs to maintain the normal function of the heart and enhance its energy metabolism. In neuronal cells, astrocytes are able to transfer mitochondria into neurons via extracellular vesicles (EVs) to regulate neuronal function and synaptic formation. In the immune system, myeloid‐derived regulatory cells can transfer mitochondria into T cells through EVs to enhance the metabolic activity and antitumor ability of T cells.
However, mitochondrial transfer is more common in pathological settings and usually occurs under stressful conditions such as hypoxia, oxidative stress, nutrient deficiency or injury. In the study of ischemic stroke, mitochondrial transfer as a new treatment strategy is receiving more and more attention. In 2016, researchers discovered and reported the phenomenon of mitochondria transfer from astrocytes to nerve cells in the oxygen‐glucose deprivation model. This finding reveals the potential of astrocytes to support nerve cells after ischemic stroke through mitochondrial transfer. In 2021, a study by Nasoni et al. found that mitochondrial transfer also existed between mouse hippocampal nerve cells. In 2023, Chakraborty et al. found that microglia could also transfer mitochondria to nerve cells. This suggests that mitochondrial transfer is not limited to astrocytes and nerve cells but may also occur between different types of nerve cells. However, recent studies have further revealed the regulatory mechanism of mitochondrial transfer, providing a new perspective for understanding the complex intracellular communication in the brain after ischemic stroke. Studies have shown that astrocytes can support the survival and functional recovery of damaged neurons by releasing mitochondria. This process is regulated by low‐density lipoprotein‐associated receptor protein 1 (LRP1). By reducing the production of lactic acid and the lactation of ADP‐ribosylation factor 1 (ARF1), LRP1 promotes the transfer of healthy mitochondria from astrocytes to neurons, thereby reducing the damage caused by ischemic stroke. Mitochondrial transplantation is a kind of treatment of mitochondrial transfer, which refers to the technique of injecting isolated mitochondria into damaged parts of tissues and organs or into the blood circulation system to exert a therapeutic effect. This method of isolating mitochondria from MSCs has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in animal models, highlighting the clinical translational potential of mitochondrial transfer. Moreover, intravenous administration of MSCs has shown reductions in cerebral infarct volume and improvements in neurobehavioral outcomes, highlighting the therapeutic benefits of mitochondrial transfer in ischemic stroke.
Despite its therapeutic potential, mitochondrial transfer faces several challenges. Questions persist regarding the efficacy of transferred mitochondria in enhancing ATP production post‐fusion and the optimal selection of donor cells, with MSCs emerging as favorable candidates due to their low immunogenicity and robust stability. Further investigations into MSC‐induced neuronal regeneration, migration, and survival within the ischemic microenvironment are warranted to elucidate long‐term therapeutic benefits.
This review synthesizes current methodologies and therapeutic applications of mitochondrial transfer in ischemic stroke, proposing mitochondrial transfer a promising therapeutic strategy for neuronal disorders within ischemic stroke.

MECHANISM OF MITOCHONDRIAL TRANSFER
Mitochondrial transfer is a complex process in which multiple mechanisms are involved, including mitochondrial transfer pathway, mitochondrial dynamics, mitochondrial quality control (MQC), immune responses and the inflammatory process. Mitochondria or its components can be cell‐to‐cell transmission through these mechanisms. The mechanisms of mitochondrial transfer encompass TNTs, EVs, the transfer of free mitochondria, and various other therapeutic modalities (Figure 1). Mechanisms of mitochondrial transfer play a critical role in maintaining cellular function, response to various stresses and prevent disease. This review enhances the comprehension of the incidence and progression of mitochondria‐targeting therapies and will support endeavors to devise novel strategies for improving clinical treatment outcomes.
Mechanism of mitochondrial transfer. (A) Tunneling nanotubes (TNTs): In mitochondrial transfer therapy, TNTs enable the direct transfer of mitochondria from healthy donor cells to recipient cells that are either damaged or require more energy. Proteins like Miro1 and Myo10 assist in this movement along actin filaments. The formation and function of TNTs are affected by factors such as metabolism, stress, and cytoskeletal changes, with calcium ions being essential for actin and myosin dynamics within TNTs. Mutations in connexin 43 (CX43), a protein that transports calcium, can disrupt this communication. Changes in mitochondrial calcium balance can influence mitochondrial movement towards injured cells. (B) Mitochondrial extracellular vesicles (mitoEVs): mitochondrial‐derived vesicles (MDVs) are composed of various subpopulations, many of which are assisted by proteins dynamin‐related protein 1 (DRP1), PTEN‐induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1), and Parkin, and their formation is associated with mitochondrial quality control. Under stress, damaged mitochondrial components are mainly conveyed to neighboring healthy cells via MDVs to maintain mitochondrial quality and cellular homeostasis. (C) Free mitochondria transfer: studies suggest that cells can release free mitochondria, similar to the mitochondrial excretion mediated by MDVs. The process is dependent on CD38 and its catalyzed cyclic ADP‐ribose (cADPR)‐induced Ca2+ release. TNTs
Recent studies have highlighted TNTs as a significant mode of cell‐to‐cell transfer (Figure 1A). Hans‐Hermann Gerdes' team at the University of Heidelberg first reported TNTs as a unique pathway for direct interaction between cells in 2004. TNTs is composed of filamentous‐actin (F‐actin) regulatory factor, actin, and tubulin filaments, enabling dynamic changes in donor cells through membrane protrusions and the formation of nanosized membrane tubes for intercellular communication. TNTs facilitate the passage of cellular components and organelles, including proteins, RNA, signaling molecules, and mitochondria, with sizes ranging from 700 nm to 100 μm. Consequently, TNTs enhance intercellular communication and material exchange between cells, holding significant implications for biomedical research and therapeutic strategies.
TNTs directly connect the adjacent cells in mitochondrial transfer therapy, which provide a direct pathway for passage between cells. TNTs formed in coculture between a single MSC and multiple neurons can be observed under a microscope. At the end of the physical structure of TNTs, the part where they connect to the cell membrane and form an open channel, syncytin2 and its receptor protein the major facilitator superfamily domain containing protein 2a (MFSD2A) are involved in intercellular connections. Based on this feature, MSCs often as the preferred donor cells. Although TNTs support bidirectional transmission, mitochondria are typically transferred via TNTs from healthy donor cells to damaged or more energy‐demanding recipient cells. Mitochondrial transport via TNTs relies on specific proteins, such as Miro1 (a Rho [ras homology gene family]‐GTPase) and Myo10 (a myosin), which assist the mitochondria in moving along actin filaments. Formation and function of TNTs are regulated by various factors, including the metabolic status of the cell, stress signals, inflammatory response and the dynamic changes in the cytoskeleton. However, the specific mechanisms of TNTs formation and function remain unclear. Some researchers speculate that it may primarily be associated with the changes of calcium ions during the formation of TNTs. Given that TNTs consist of an F‐actin‐based actin bundle, the dynamics of actin and myosin, which are dependent on Ca2+, fundamentally regulate the balance between growth and contraction in TNTs. Mutations in CX43, the adhesion protein responsible for transporting Ca2+ between cells, inhibit intercellular calcium and mitochondrial communication. Alteration of mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis can lead to mitochondrial dynamics in donor cells change and move toward the injured cells. TNTs‐mediated mitochondrial transfer constitutes a highly specific and targeted mechanism that facilitates the restoration of the MQC system in damaged cells. Additionally, this process is integral to intercellular signaling and material exchange, thereby playing a crucial role in cell‐to‐cell interactions and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis. The mediation of mitochondrial transfer by nanotubes presents a potential therapeutic strategy for diseases associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. This approach enhances mitochondrial function through the fusion of healthy mitochondrial fractions and the recycling of damaged components. However, the regulatory mechanism, the efficiency of transfer and the stability in vivo environment still requires further study.

Mitochondrial EVs
EVs are vesicular entities with lipid bilayer membranes that a kind of membrane vesicles released from cells into the extracellular environment and actively used to transport various kinds of biologically active substances, including proteins, mRNA, mt DNA, and other molecules. EVs are vital in intercellular communication. It has been proposed that EVs, including microvesicles and exosomes, are capable of transferring mitochondrial components into recipient cells through mechanisms such as endocytosis or fusion with the plasma membrane. This transfer can subsequently influence the functional state of the recipient cells, impacting processes such as energy metabolism, oxidative stress response, and cell signaling. EVs can selectively deliver mitochondrial constituents in a particular cell type, which may be determined by the specific cell surface receptors and signaling pathway. Compared with other modes of transfer, the membrane structure of EVs provides a protective mechanism for mitochondrial constituents, which enables it to stably exist in the extracellular environment and resist damage from the external environment.
Mitochondrial derived vesicles (MDVs) are a class of tiny vesicles produced by mitochondria through specific biological processes. these vesicles contained mitochondrial constituents such as protein, lipid and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and can be released from the mitochondria to the cytosol. In the selective transport of mitochondrial components to EVs, MDVs is divided into two distinct pathways: sorting nexin‐9 (SNX9)‐dependent MDVs and Parkin‐dependent MDVs. Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial proteins can be selectively ingested into EVs via SNX9‐dependent MDVs. SNX9 is a small GTP enzyme involved in regulating the formation and transport of MDVs. However, when mitochondria are damaged, MDVs carrying damaged mitochondrial components are targeted to lysosomes for degradation, a process that relies on the Parkin protein. Parkin is a protein associated with Parkinson's disease that is involved in mitochondrial autophagy and clearance of damaged mitochondria. After ischemic stroke, a large number of ROS are produced due to mitochondrial dysfunction due to hypoxia and reperfusion of brain tissue. Under the stimulation of ROS, the relevant signaling pathways in cells will undergo abnormal changes, and then activate the PTEN‐induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin signaling pathway. This process is a stress response of cells to mitochondrial dysfunction, and aims to promote mitochondrial autophagy by activating PINK/Parkin signaling pathway, clearing damaged mitochondria to maintain homeostasis and normal function within cells. The increase of Parkin protein will activate the process of mitochondrial autophagy, and promote the formation of MDVs of damaged mitochondrial components, ultimately targeting MDVs to lysosomes, thus blocking the process of mitochondrial components entering EVs. By clearing damaged mitochondria, the Parkin protein reduces oxidative stress, inflammation, and the release of proapoptotic factors. This process reduces the occurrence of oxidative stress and apoptosis, thereby protecting nerve cells from further damage and contributing to the recovery of nerve function after ischemic stroke.
MDVs transfer between organelles is recognized to complement the MQC system. Under stress or stressful conditions, damaged mitochondrial components from cells were mainly conveyed to neighboring healthy cells via MDVs to maintenance mitochondrial quality and cellular homeostasis (Figure 1B). In addition, MDVs exhibits enormous potential for future therapeutic strategies. As with TNT‐mediated cell transfer, transplantation of healthy mitochondria components into cells with dysfunctional or damaged mitochondria can result in the recovery of cell biological functions, Defective mitochondrial dysfunction is eliminated by mitophagy, which can be regarded as exogenous “mitochondrial biogenesis”. However, the MDVs formed from these mitochondria includes only tiny mitochondria and specific mitochondrial components. EVs, as a carrier of mitochondrial components, provide a new perspective for the possibility of developing new treatment strategies, such as mitochondrial diseases and metabolic diseases. In addition to facilitating mitochondrial transfer, MDVs possess a variety of bioactive substances and signaling molecules on their vesicle membranes, which may contribute to enhancing their therapeutic potential.

Free mitochondria transfer
Free mitochondrial transfer refers to the process in which mitochondria are released from the donor cell without being wrapped by additional membrane structures (such as EVs) and directly into the extracellular environment for capture and uptake by the recipient cell. Mitochondria can be released into the extracellular environment through direct budding of the cell membrane or through the breakdown of other cell membrane structures. Recipient cells capture free mitochondria through specific cell surface receptors or molecules (such as heparan sulfate). Free mitochondria were uptaked by the target cell such as macropinocytosis, which may involve the dynamic changes of the cytoskeleton and remodeling of the cell membrane. The mechanism is dependent on CD38 and its catalyzed catalyzed cyclic ADP‐ribose (cADPR)‐induced Ca2+ release. CD38 is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide + (NAD+) glycoside hydrolase that catalyzes the synthesis of intracellular cADPR and breaks down extracellular NAD+. This process of breaking down extracellular NAD+ releases Ca2+, which stimulates the cytoskeleton and plasma membrane of the cell cortex, while mediating the endocytosis of free mitochondria from astrocytes (Figure 1C). Free mitochondria transfer is considered an effective mechanism for the quality control mechanism, which clear damaged mitochondria, and may promote repair of damaged cells. In the event of increased metabolic demand, the recipient cell can use the captured mitochondria to support its energy metabolism and function. For example, in ischemic stroke, astrocytes can release mitochondria for capture and use by damaged neurons to support their survival. Thus, it has potential applications in the treatment of mitochondrial dysfunction and related diseases. However, the process and mechanism of this transfer mode were relatively complicated, which involves a series of signaling pathways and multiple cytokines, and a detailed understanding of this process is still under research.

Other transfer methods
Cells can also directly incorporate mitochondrial networks via cell fusion, which is particularly important when immune cells clear damaged mitochondria. The cell fusion could be either spontaneous or induction of specific signaling. These modes of mitochondrial transfer are important in a variety of physiological and pathological processes, such as tissue repair, immune response, tumor metabolism and neurodegenerative diseases. Cells can modulate the energy metabolism, respond to environmental variations and sharing of resources among cells via through the mechanism mentioned above.

MITOCHONDRIAL TRANSFER PLAYS AN IMPORTANT ROLE IN ISCHEMIC STROKE
MQC is an endogenous cellular protection mechanism and a comprehensive network to detect mitochondrial quality, which plays a crucial role in the maintenance of mitochondrial function, biogenesis, and homeostasis. MQC classical pathway regulates and maintains mitochondrial by coordinating various processes such as mitochondrial biogenesis, mitochondrial fusion and separation, mitochondrial proteolysis and mitochondrial autophagy degradation. Inflammatory factors and immune responses generated under pathological conditions can cause varying degrees of damage to the mitochondrial function of neurons and brain tissue and also interfere with the quality control of mitochondria, which will eventually lead to the imbalance of energy metabolism. Excessive mitochondrial stress can lead to lysosomal dysfunction. In injured neurons, a large number of damaged mitochondrial components accumulate at the axon end. This results in long‐distance transport of autophagosomes and insufficient local translation of proteins, which prevent mitochondrial autophagy. Damaged neurons with mitochondrial dysfunction are unable to quickly compensate themselves due to MQC disturbances, axon and synaptic function and regeneration are also severely affected. These factors prevent endogenous autophagy from removing damaged mitochondria within a single cell. Consequently, cells maintain mitochondrial quality by releasing mitochondrial contents into the extracellular environment. Then the mitochondria began to divide, on the one hand to remove damaged mitochondria, and through the autophagy pathway for degradation and recycling. On the other hand, mitochondria with better functional continue to fusion, a process known as mitochondrial biogenesis. This is a regeneration procedure that maintains mitochondrial numbers through replacing the damaged mitochondria with exogenous healthy mitochondria, resulting in enhanced bioenergetics in recipient cells.
However, we found that mitochondrial biogenesis in neurons in the injured area after ischemic stroke was limited, or mitochondrial biogenesis was insufficient to satisfy the energy supply in the cell. In this case, mitochondrial transfer is an effective method for exogenous mitochondrial supplementation. Due to long‐term ischemia and hypoxia in the ischemic core region, mitochondrial damage is relatively serious, and energy metabolism recovery is slow. Although ischemic penumbra region is also affected by ischemia, due to its relatively good blood supply and metabolic reserve, mitochondrial energy metabolism recovers faster. Ischemic penumbra is an important target area for stroke treatment, and its function can be improved by promoting the transfer of mitochondria from healthy cells to ischemic penumbra cells. For example, the mitochondrial transfer ability of MSCs is utilized to transport healthy mitochondria to the ischemic penumbra region to restore energy metabolism of cells. The hippocampus plays an important role in learning and memory, and the functional recovery of the hippocampus region is particularly critical after ischemic stroke. Studies have shown that the use of specific drugs or gene editing techniques can enhance mitochondrial fusion and reduce mitochondrial division, thereby maintaining the normal function of mitochondria and promoting the recovery of energy metabolism in hippocampal neurons. Based on the above conclusions, the key to the treatment of ischemic stroke is to develop targeted transfer strategies of mitochondria to specific injured brain regions and related neurons. In the central nervous system, both glial cells (astrocytes and microglia) and MSCs are good donors of neurons and can support the energy metabolism of neurons by releasing mitochondria. Astrocytes can also transfer mitochondria to brain endothelial cells and pericytes via exosomes, thereby improving cerebrovascular function. The cells that receive mitochondria are mainly damaged neurons. TNTs and EVs are the main mitochondrial transfer pathways between neuronal cells, which can promote the transfer efficiency by regulating the relevant mechanisms. A recent study found that an iron oxide nanoparticles can promote the formation of gap junction channels between MSCs and damaged cells, thereby improving the targeted transport of healthy mitochondria from stem cells to damaged cells. It provides a potential mitochondrial delivery strategy for achieving mitochondrial replacement therapy for damaged cells in vivo. Studies have also shown that Miro 1 is essential for the formation of TNT and the survival of neurons. On the one hand, Miro 1 improves the efficiency of mitochondrial transfer between cells by regulating the number and stability of TNTs. On the other hand, when Miro 1 is overexpressed in donor cells such as MSCs, the efficiency of mitochondrial transfer is significantly improved. For example, in animal models of experimental stroke, the introduction of pluripotent MSCs with overexpression of Miro 1 can significantly improve the recovery of neurological function. The development of targeted delivery technologies, such as the use of nanocarriers or genetic engineering, can wrap mitochondria in targeted vectors so that they can be specifically taken up by recipient cells, ensuring that mitochondria can be accurately delivered to specific brain regions or cell types. In addition, drugs or other interventions can be used to regulate the receptivity of the recipient cell so that it can better take up and use the transferred mitochondria. For example, mitochondrial fusion and functional recovery are promoted by activating mitochondrial fusion proteins (such as MFN2) of recipient cells. These techniques and strategies offer a variety of possibilities for targeted mitochondrial delivery, helping to improve the precision and effectiveness of therapy, especially in diseases such as ischemic stroke that require specific cell type repair.
Mitochondrial transfer provides healthy mitochondria, which undergo mesh fusion with damaged mitochondria, thereby affecting the dynamics of mitochondria in damaged cells, activating repair and regeneration mechanisms of damaged cells, including stimulating mitochondrial biosynthesis, and promoting autophagy clearance of damaged mitochondria. It also helps to maintain the health of mitochondrial network, improve energy metabolism, reduce oxidative damage and activate cell repair mechanisms. These effects contribute to neurological function recovery after stroke and promote the recovery of impaired tissues in the damaged brain region. Enhancing the role of mitochondrial metabolism
When an ischemic insult occurs, the mitochondria in neurons are no longer enough to support the functional homeostasis in the cells, so we need to supplement them by mitochondrial transfer pathways. Healthy mitochondria are transferred from surrounding cells or transplanted cells (such as stem cells) to damaged cells, and the transferred mitochondria usually accompanies fusion of the mitochondrial network in the damaged cell, which assists to restore the mitochondrial function and biological energy of the damaged cell. At this time, the new mitochondria produced by fusion can promote ATP generation, provide the necessary energy and enhance the mitochondrial metabolism of the damaged area to meet the energy demand in normal and pathological states. The transferred mitochondria can also carry mtDNA to the recipient cell, which can encode 13 proteins that constitute an important part of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC). Healthy exogenous mitochondria can rapidly initiate oxidative phosphorylation to reduce energy restriction of recipient cells during the acute phase of brain injury. In addition, the new mitochondria can rapidly proliferate in the recipient cells and activate mitochondrial biogenesis, continuously restoring the energy supply of the recipient cells.

Anti‐neuroinflammatory effect
Neuronal cell death to varying degrees is one of the main features of the damaged brain area after stroke, and releases damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) factors that cause local inflammation in the damaged brain area. Inflammatory factors trigger secondary brain injury by aggravating blood‐brain barrier breakdown, microvascular failure, brain edema, oxidative stress, and direct induction of neuronal cell death. Therefore, inhibiting the inflammatory response is critical for rescuing the survival state of neurons and maintain cell function by inhibiting inflammatory response for the treatment of post‐stroke injury, and it is also a research hot topic in the treatment of ischemic stroke in recent years.
Mitochondria or their components, such as mtDNA, may be recognized as DAMPs, which trigger inflammatory reactions. Research indicates that specific mitochondrial transfers may exert anti‐inflammatory effects by inhibiting signaling pathways such as the nucleotide‐binding domain, leucine‐rich repeat, and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, toll‐like receptors, and cGAS‐STING. Healthy mitochondria can be transported between cells through nanotubules or EVs. Moreover, mitochondrial mesh fusion can extensively enhance the metabolic reprogramming of recipient cells, such as increasing the levels of glutathione and α‐ketoglutarate, promote mitochondrial respiration, reduce tissue oxidative stress and inflammatory signals in cerebral ischemia reperfusion injury, and improve cell survival and brain function. Simultaneously, the transferred mitochondria can restore mitochondrial dynamics in neurons, mediate the selective degradation of mitochondria from axons to cell bodies, and thus reduce neurotoxicity in local inflammatory areas of the brain. And mitochondrial transfer may also affect the function of immune cells, such as macrophages and microglia, which play a key role in neuroinflammation. Healthy mitochondria enhance the metabolic function of cells and regulate their polarization, thereby reducing the release of inflammatory factors. Furthermore, we found that MSCs also possess anti‐inflammatory, paracrine, and other multidimensional neuroprotective effects. Early application of MSCs after stroke significantly upregulates the expression levels of anti‐inflammatory factors such as interleukin (IL)‐10, IL‐1 receptor antagonist (IL‐1RA), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and 2,3,4',5‐tetrahydroxystilbene 2‐O‐β‐d‐glucoside (TSG‐6), and downregulates the level of the inflammatory factor tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α in brain tissue. These changes are associated with the regulation of many processes, including increased secretion of anti‐inflammatory molecules, decreased pro‐inflammatory cytokines and neutrophils, and repair effects on the blood‐brain barrier. Therefore, MSC‐based mitochondrial transfer may have a stronger neuroprotective effect than exogenous mitochondrial transfer alone.

The role of mitochondria in ferroptosis
Ferroptosis is an iron‐dependent mode of oxidative cell death that has been associated with the development of several neurological diseases. Ferroptosis is considered as one of the major causes for neuronal death in ischemic stroke. Ferroptosis is characterized as mitochondrial dysfunction, including reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, increased ROS, and unbalance of mitochondrial fission and fusion, so MQC is essential for maintaining mitochondrial function and inhibiting ferroptosis. Specifically, on the one hand, excessive mitochondrial autophagy can cause mitochondrial dysfunction and the production of more ROS in areas of brain injury after stroke. On the other hand, the degradation of ferritin in mitochondria and the subsequent increase in the content of free iron in cells, as well as disordered MQC, mediate ferroptosis‐induced neuronal death through mitochondrial dynamic imbalance and excessive autophagy.
Some studies indicated that MSCs are able to transfer healthy mitochondria to damaged neurons via TNTs. The delivered mitochondria restore mitochondrial bioenergy production and calcium homeostasis in the recipient cells by fusing with the neuronal mitochondrial membrane, thereby inhibiting mitochondrial division and autophagy, reducing oxidative stress due to mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately alleviating neuronal death. In addition, MSC‐mediated mitochondrial transfer can not only improve the mitochondrial function of damaged neurons but also play a multi‐dimensional neuroprotective role through its anti‐inflammatory and paracrine effects. In addition, mitochondrial transfer may also affect the balance of cell death pathways, for example, by affecting apoptosis and autophagy processes, which may indirectly regulate sensitivity to ferroptosis and contribute to functional recovery after stroke. Finally, it is important to note that ferroptosis is a relatively newly discovered form of regulated cell death. Its regulatory mechanisms and relationships with other cell death pathways are still under active investigation. Therefore, the exact mechanism of mitochondrial transfer treatment of ferroptosis after ischemic stroke still needs to be further elucidated.

Elevate the level of ATP
In the nervous system, healthy mitochondria are able to efficiently perform oxidative phosphorylation, which is the primary pathway by which cells produce ATP, and bioenergy is essential for establishing electrochemical gradients and synaptic transmission across the plasma membrane. After ischemic stroke, the mitochondria in the brain injury area gradually lose the electrochemical ion gradient, resulting in reduced ATP synthesis, and the damaged mitochondria also produce a large amount of ROS, which not only consumes ATP but also leads to mitochondrial dysfunction. In addition, an imbalance of intracellular Ca2+ in the brain injury area, and Ca2+ enters the mitochondria would lead to calcium overload and ATP production disorder, and long‐term depletion of ATP would lead to plasma membrane depolarization and eventually cell death.
Mitochondrial transfer promotes the integration of mitochondria into the endogenous mitochondrial network of recipient cells, improving the quality and quantity of mitochondria through the process of fission and fusion, which helps to maintain or enhance the energy metabolism of the cell. It can also stimulate mitochondrial biosynthesis of recipient cells, increase mitochondrial DNA replication and expressions of proteins in mitochondria, and thus increase ATP production. Mitochondrial transfer can also enhance the viability of damaged neurons and promote recovery after stroke. For instance, in cortical neurons, mitochondrial transfer mediated by astrocytes can reverse the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and Ca2+ dysregulation. There is increasing evidence that mitochondrial supplementation after ischemic stroke is beneficial to the energy supply of the brain injury area as a basis for restoring subsequent brain function. For that, Norat et al., from the Department of Neurosurgery at the University of Virginia Health System Pedro evaluated the transport efficacy of intra‐arterial autologous mitochondrial transfer and its influence on ischemic brain tissue through animal experiments, and confirmed that damaged neuron cells can not only integrate exogenous mitochondria, but also improve the ATP content of damaged brain tissue and provide exogenous energy support for ischemic tissue. This is essential for the survival, function and overall health of brain cells after stroke.

Reduce the role of ROS
Mitochondria are a major source of ROS production in cells. Intracellular ROS can act as intracellular signaling molecules to regulate the normal physiological functions of the body. After ischemic stroke, ROS levels in mitochondria increase significantly, which damages mitochondria and leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, and can also cause intracellular Ca2+ overload, which further increases ROS levels. This feedback loop maintains Ca2+ overload and increases ROS production. At this time, excess ROS generation can destroy the antioxidant defense mechanism in the brain, oxidization of the surrounding molecules renders these molecules dysfunctional, which in turn triggers a series of pathophysiological reactions, including inflammation, destruction of the blood‐brain barrier, apoptosis and autophagy, ultimately lead to neurodegeneration and neuronal death. Since mitochondria is the main source of ROS in the body, it is critical for inhibiting ROS production in mitochondria after stroke.
Through mitochondrial transfer technology, healthy mitochondria can be transferred to damaged cells, which replaces the dysfunctional mitochondria, removes the fragmented mitochondria that produce more ROS, and reduces the oxidative damage of mtDNA. Increase or preserve mitochondria with highly effective energy metabolism, and disturbed mitochondria may be the source of excessive ROS production. By restoring normal mitochondrial respiratory chain function, electron leakage and subsequent superoxide anion (O2−) production can be reduced, thereby reducing ROS production. And mitochondrial transfer can improve the oxidative phosphorylation efficiency of the recipient cell, which means that the ETC is more tightly coupled, reducing the possibility of electron leakage and thus reducing ROS generated by the direct reaction of electrons with oxygen. Second, healthy mitochondria may contain higher levels of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and catalase (CAT), which help neutralize ROS and reduce oxidative damage. Finally, some investigators have experimentally verified that the mitochondria isolated from the autologous tissue of MCAO mice were directly injected into the lateral ventricle. After injection, it was found that a large number of imported mitochondria were distributed in the ischemic penumbra. A series of oxidative stress experiments were then also performed, including lipid peroxidation levels and ROS clearance. It is confirmed that mitochondrial transfer can improve the oxidative stress response of ischemic penumbra and reduce the intracellular ROS damage under pathological conditions.

LIMITATIONS AND CHALLENGES IN ISCHEMIC STROKE TREATMENT OF MITOCHONDRIAL TRANSFER
Now, while we see hope in treating ischemic stroke with mitochondrial transfer, we still face various difficulties and challenges. For example, the availability of healthy donor mitochondria, technical challenges during transplantation, ethical issues, safety issues, and so on. First of all, the safety issues related to immune rejection caused by exogenous mitochondrial transfer need special attention. Although mitochondrial immunogenicity is relatively low, there is still some risk. In xenotransplantation, exogenous mitochondria can activate the innate immune system, and pattern recognition receptors (such as toll‐like receptors and NOD‐like receptors) recognize DAMPs released by mitochondria, such as mtDNA and N‐formylpeptides. This recognition triggers an inflammatory response, leading to the release of inflammatory factors and the activation of immune cells. In addition, the transplanted mitochondria interact with the mitochondria of the recipient cell, which may also increase autoimmune markers. This will further aggravate the attack of the immune system and increase the risk of transplant rejection. Secondly, studies have shown that isolated mitochondria lack long‐term neuroprotective effects. For example, astrocytes in neural tissue can deliver mitochondria to alleviate neuronal damage after a stroke. However, the neuroprotective effects induced by astrocytes are short‐lived, and researchers are still addressing this issue. Thirdly, we cannot provide precise targeting the mitochondria of damaged neurons in damaged neurons in brain injury areas as much as possible to improve the efficacy of mitochondrial transfer. Finally, studies have shown that high concentrations of purified mitochondria are prone to accumulation and cause additional damage to neurons. In addition, it is also difficult for us to obtain an appreciable amount of purified healthy mitochondria and maintain their activity in clinical practice.
Finally, we have other unresolved issues: (1) Standardized procedures for mitochondrial transplantation (such as transplantation dose, transplantation method, time window, etc.); (2) Mitochondrial pretreatment technology; (3) Mitochondrial uptake rate and distribution accuracy; and (4) the exact mechanism of mitochondrial uptake and brain protection. With the development of mitochondrial extraction technology, the isolation and transplantation of mitochondria can be quickly completed, but there are still doubts about the safety of human mitochondrial transplantation. According to the findings above, there is still considerable research space for exploring the application of mitochondrial transplantation in ischemic stroke.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS FOR THE DEVELOPMENT OF MITOCHONDRIAL‐TRANSFER‐BASED THERAPIES
Mitochondria serve as a potential therapeutic target to treat ischemic stroke. It has been the focus of intense study in recent years. Healthy mitochondria play an extremely important role in neuroprotection and neurological recovery after ischemic stroke. It can be seen that the research and development of mitochondrial transfer technology is also a hot direction for our current and future research.
Firstly, we can focus on the selection and optimization of mitochondria. Msenchymal stem cells as mitochondrial transfer vector carriers, due to their low immunogenicity, ability to cross the blood‐brain barrier, and ability to deliver targeted gene drugs, MSCs and their derived exosomes have broad prospects in the clinical treatment of ischemic stroke. To improve the treatment efficiency of ischemic stroke, it is very important to precisely regulate the differentiation of MSCs to better participate in the mitochondrial transfer process. Studies have shown that mitochondrial transfer between MSCs and other cells can be promoted by activating the expression of gap connexin. By inducing MSCs to differentiate into specific cell types, the functional recovery effect of MSCS in specific brain regions or cell types can be improved. For example, inducing MSCs to differentiate into neurons or glial cells can more efficiently transfer mitochondria into damaged neurons. In addition, epigenetic modifications (such as DNA methylation, histone modification, etc.) also play an important role in stem cell differentiation. By regulating epigenetic marks, the expression pattern of genes can be changed, thus affecting the differentiation pathway of MSCs and the efficiency of mitochondrial transfer. Secondly, we can also study and optimize mitochondrial transfer methods. Currently, there exist several approaches for mitochondrial transfer, such as injection, fusion, and the uptake of isolated mitochondria or mitochondria‐containing particles. Nevertheless, these methods possess limitations in terms of efficiency, specificity, and safety. Future research could concentrate on enhancing these methods to boost the targeting of mitochondria to specific cell types or tissues, improve transfer efficiency, and minimize the risk of adverse reactions. Thirdly, we can also explore whether other methods can be used to improve the efficiency and stability of mitochondrial transfer. Researchers have used focused ultrasound (FUS) to activate microbubbles to evaluate the effect of mitochondrial transport through the blood‐brain barrier. What surprised us was that the FUS method can significantly increase the permeability of the blood‐brain barrier, infusing more mitochondria into ischemic tissue, and the mitochondria transplanted with FUS assistance can be integrated into all studied cell types, effectively improving the efficacy of mitochondrial transfer.
Finally, we conclude with some reflections. Mitochondrial transfer therapy is not only limited to transferring healthy mitochondria to damaged cells for therapeutic purposes, but can also transfer damaged mitochondria out of the cell, reducing the accumulation of damaged mitochondria or their components (such as mtDNA) in the cell, leading to adverse reactions such as inflammation, autophagy, apoptosis, and cell dysfunction. Therefore, we can also focus our research on the translocation of damaged mitochondria. As early as 2014, the team led by Li et al. from Tsinghua University discovered a new organelle—migratome. By 2021, the research team had discovered and named a new phenomenon within cells—mitocytosis (mitochondrial exocytosis). After mild mitochondrial stress, damaged mitochondria are transported to the migrating body and then transported to the extracellular space by the migrating body. The research team believes that this is a quality control process of cells towards mitochondria, which clears out damaged mitochondria. Therefore, functionally, mitochondrial exocytosis plays an important role in maintaining mitochondrial quality. Enhanced mitochondrial exocytosis can protect cells from mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial respiratory loss induced by mitochondrial stressors. The proposal of this method provides a new direction and research idea for us to continue to study mitochondrial transfer therapy. How to enhance mitochondrial exocytosis effect and long‐term effect of this effect in pathological environments is worthy of further study.
Mitochondrial transfer not only plays a good therapeutic role in central nervous system diseases such as stroke and spinal cord injury but also can be applied to diseases such as Parkinson's disease and various organ ischemia‐reperfusion. This treatment method shows great promise for the treatment in clinical treatment. In summary, as research in this field progresses, optimizing transfer methods, expanding therapeutic applications, and understanding potential mechanisms will be the key focus areas for mitochondrial transfer therapy.

CONCLUSION
Ischemic stroke may lead to severe tissue and organ dysfunction, among these, mitochondrial dysfunction has been confirmed as the key factors in post stroke injury. Thus, a brand‐new and intervening approach is mitochondrial transfer. As the name implies, we transfer healthy mitochondria to damaged cells. By replacing damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria with exogenous healthy ones and fusing with damaged mitochondria to restore mitochondrial homeostasis, as well as inhibiting mitochondrial division and autophagy to restore MQC, we can help cells regain vitality. This article documents the latest progress of mitochondrial metastasis as a novel and promising treatment for ischemic stroke. We also emphasized the potential of stem cell mitochondrial transfer as a novel therapeutic strategy for ischemic stroke. An animal ischemic stroke model is used to validate that mitochondrial transfer can improve cellular oxidative stress, calcium overload, ferroptosis, and inflammatory response, reduce cell apoptosis and necrosis, thereby improving the pathological state of the brain microenvironment and the function of tissues and organs in the brain injury area. Our research summarizes that mitochondrial metastasis is involved in the occurrence and development of central nervous system diseases. Creatively combining mitochondrial therapy with stem cell therapy effectively promotes the exploration of stroke mechanisms and clinical treatment.
