Comprehensive evaluation of risk factors for intraventricular hemorrhage in preterm neonates: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Objective
Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality among preterm neonates, making early identification of risk factors critical to improving outcomes. Understanding both clinical conditions and therapeutic interventions associated with IVH can guide prevention and management strategies.
Methods
A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted, incorporating 30 studies that investigated various risk factors for IVH. The studies were categorized based on the clinical conditions and therapeutic interventions involved. The data were analyzed using pooled relative risks (RR) and heterogeneity assessments, including I2 values and P-values.
Results
The analysis revealed several significant clinical risk factors for IVH. Male gender was associated with a 15% increased risk of IVH (pooled RR: 1.15, 95% CI 1.00–1.32), while low birth weight (< 1000g) was linked to a 94% increased risk (pooled RR: 1.94, 95% CI 1.38–2.73). Other significant factors included the presence of Apgar ≤ 5 or 7 (pooled RR: 2.14, 95% CI 1.69–2.72), asphyxia (pooled RR: 1.70, 95% CI 1.26–2.28), and thrombocytopenia (pooled RR: 1.34, 95% CI 1.10–1.62). Neonatal conditions such as respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) and hyaline membrane disease (HMD) were also found to increase IVH risk significantly. In terms of therapeutic interventions, antenatal steroids were associated with a reduced risk of IVH (pooled RR: 0.79, 95% CI 0.70–0.89), while mechanical ventilation, the use of catecholamines, surfactant administration increased IVH risk significantly (pooled RR for mechanical ventilation: 3.27, 95% CI 2.77–3.86; pooled RR for surfactant: 2.32, 95% CI 1.61–3.35).
Conclusion
Several clinical factors and therapeutic interventions are associated with the risk of IVH in neonates. These findings emphasize the importance of early identification and management of high-risk neonates, as well as the need for further research to refine prevention strategies.

Introduction
Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) is a critical condition primarily observed in preterm neonates, posing significant risks of mortality and long-term complications. This condition occurs due to the immaturity of the central nervous system and vascular structures, which makes the fragile vessels in the germinal matrix prone to rupture. The hemorrhage often extends into the lateral ventricles, resulting in IVH.
In the United States, nearly 25% of very preterm neonates with very low birth weight (VLBW; birth weight less than 1500 g) experience IVH, with 10% to 15% suffering from severe cases (grade 3 or 4). Severe IVH is often associated with neurological complications, including altered consciousness, seizures, cerebral palsy, and intellectual disabilities.
Advancements in neonatal intensive care have significantly improved the survival rates of preterm infants. However, this progress has also led to a relative increase in the number of neonates at a higher risk of developing IVH. The pathophysiology of IVH is complex and involves multiple contributing factors. Common risk factors include prematurity, low birth weight (LBW), delayed cord clamping, infections, quality of antenatal care, prolonged mechanical ventilation, low Apgar scores, hypoxic injuries, twin gestation, thrombocytopenia, maternal bleeding during pregnancy, hypotension, sepsis, hypoxia, hypercapnia, seizures, patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), respiratory distress syndrome, and genetic predispositions.
Most IVH cases occur within the first 72 h of life (early-onset IVH) and can rapidly progress within the first week. Identifying potential risk factors is crucial for predicting hemorrhages, enabling timely diagnosis, and implementing appropriate interventions to prevent neural damage and adverse neurological outcomes in neonates.
Regarding delayed cord clamping (DCC), some studies have suggested that while DCC generally benefits neonatal outcomes by improving blood volume and reducing anemia, it may also transiently increase cerebral blood flow fluctuations, potentially raising IVH risk in very preterm neonates. However, evidence remains inconclusive, with other research demonstrating no increased risk or even protective effects. Thus, the relationship between DCC and IVH requires further clarification. To enhance transparency, this review explicitly cites studies supporting both perspectives to provide a balanced understanding.
While numerous studies have explored specific risk factors for IVH in preterm neonates, a comprehensive analysis covering the majority of these elements is missing. The systematic review and meta-analysis aim to bridge this gap by offering a thorough evaluation of these risk factors, with the goal to enhance prediction capabilities, refine preventive strategies, and contribute to better neonatal care protocols. By categorizing and analyzing both clinical and therapeutic variables this review could lead to updated protocols for managing VLBW infants, potentially reducing IVH occurrences and improving outcomes, while also pointing out areas for future research, particularly focusing on modifiable risk factors and their interactions. Additionally, it stratifies risk based on maternal conditions, infant status, and interventions, which provides a more exhaustive understanding of the etiology of IVH. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to comprehensively evaluate the risk factors for IVH in preterm neonates.

Materials and methods
This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the guidelines set out in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020. The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42024620894.

Studies included in this review were those that examined the relationship between IVH and its associated risk factors, reporting outcomes such as frequency, odds ratios (ORs), or risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Eligible studies involved premature neonates (preterm infants) born at less than 37 weeks of gestation, including those with VLBW (birth weight < 1500 g). These studies investigated risk factors for IVH, such as prematurity, low birth weight, mechanical ventilation, infections, low Apgar score, thrombocytopenia, and maternal bleeding, and compared neonates with IVH to those without it. The study types eligible for inclusion were analytical observational studies, such as case–control or cohort studies, with IVH as the primary outcome. Also, all included studies were published before September 2024 with a sample size of ≥ 80 participants (Table 1).
Exclusion criteria included individual case reports, reviews, meta-analyses, brief communications, and letters to the editor. Studies that focused on populations other than premature neonates or those that did not investigate the risk factors for IVH were excluded. Additionally, studies that did not report the occurrence of IVH using consistent outcomes such as frequency, OR, RR, and 95% CI, or those where IVH was not the primary outcome or was poorly defined, were not considered (Table 1).

Search strategy and selection process
A comprehensive search was conducted in Medline (PubMed), Web of Science, and Scopus databases without time restrictions. The search was based on keywords such as "Risk factor", "Cerebral intraventricular hemorrhage", "Premature infant", and their MeSH synonyms (detailed in Table 1). All search results were imported into Endnote (version 20) for organization, and duplicates were removed. Afterward, two authors (MGH, BP) independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the retrieved studies. Disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third author (YM) (Table 1).

Data extraction
Data extraction was performed independently by two authors (AR, BP) using a standardized checklist that included details such as the first author’s name, year of publication, country of the study, study design, sample size, average age of participants, the number of patients with and without risk factors, investigated risk factors, and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) score.

Quality assessment
The quality of each included study was assessed using the NOS, which evaluates three main domains: participant selection (4 points), comparability of groups (2 points), and outcome assessment (3 points), for a total maximum score of 9. The quality assessment was performed by two authors (AR, BP), and any discrepancies were resolved by a third author (YM).

Statistical analysis
For quantitative synthesis, relative risks (RRs) and their corresponding 95% CIs were transformed into logarithmic scales, and standard errors (SEs) of the log RRs were calculated to stabilize variance. Pooled effect estimates were derived using the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model, which accounts for both within-study and between-study variability. This approach was prioritized over fixed-effects models due to significant heterogeneity identified in preliminary analyses. Heterogeneity across studies was quantified using Cochran’s Q test (significance threshold: P < 0.10) and the I2 statistic, where values of 25–50%, 50–75%, and > 75% were interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, meta-regression analyses were conducted, adjusting for covariates such as study design, sample size, and geographic region. Subgroup analyses were further performed based on predefined clinical and methodological factors (e.g., birth weight strata, ventilation protocols). Publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots for asymmetry and statistically confirmed using Egger’s linear regression test (P < 0.05 indicating significant bias). All analyses were executed in STATA 18.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA), with statistical significance for effect estimates defined as a two-tailed P < 0.05.

As a result of the search in electronic databases, 2216 studies were identified, of which 946 were found to be duplicates. After screening the articles based on title and abstract, 116 studies remained, and after reviewing the full texts and applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 30 studies were selected for the final analysis. Of the 30 eligible studies, 24 were cohort studies and 6 were case–control studies (Supplementary Table File). The screening and selection process of studies is illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart in Fig. 1.

Twenty-one studies investigated the effect of cesarean delivery on the occurrence of IVH, with the pooled effect size found to be 1.19 (pooled RR: 1.19; 95% CI 1.08–1.30; P-value < 0.0001; I2: 94.26%). In contrast, no significant association was found in the five studies that examined the effect of vaginal delivery (Fig. 2). Five maternal risk factors—gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, amnionitis, chorioamnionitis, and placental abruption—were reported as non-significant due to P-values greater than 0.05. Regarding premature rupture of membranes (PROM), a total of 9 studies were included, with a pooled RR of 1.17 (pooled RR: 1.26; 95% CI 1.05–1.51; P-value: 0.03; I2: 56.38%), suggesting a moderate association with IVH. Cohort studies (7 studies) showed a stronger effect (RR = 1.26, 95% CI 1.05–1.51), while case–control studies (2 studies) showed a weaker effect (RR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.32–1.06). Regional variation was also observed, with studies from America showing a higher pooled RR of 1.84 (95% CI 1.22–2.76), while those from Asia and Europe reported weaker associations (RR = 1.15, 95% CI 0.89–1.49 and RR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.71–1.28, respectively). These findings indicate that both cesarean section and PROM are important risk factors for IVH in preterm neonates, with variability based on study design and region (Table 2).

Regarding neonatal risk factors at birth, 17 studies investigated the association between male gender and IVH, but no significant correlation was found in any of these studies, including the meta-analysis (Fig. 3A). Similarly, multiple studies investigating female sex showed no significant link with the occurrence of IVH (pooled RR: 1.15; 95% CI 1.00–1.32; P: 0.98; I2 = 0.00%). Furthermore, three studies assessing the risk of twin pregnancies did not find any significant relationship with IVH (Table 3).
Studies exploring birth weight revealed that in three studies, a birth weight ≤ 1000g increased the risk of IVH by 94% (pooled RR: 1.94; 95% CI 1.38–2.73; P-value > 0.0001; I2 = 84.36%). The pooled result from three studies in neonates with a birth weight > 1000g showed a significantly lower risk of IVH (pooled RR: 0.41; 95% CI 0.29–0.57; P-value < 0.0001; I2 = 85.33%), suggesting an inverse association between higher birth weight and IVH occurrence (Table 3). Four studies showed that an Apgar score ≤ 5 or 7 significantly increased the risk of IVH (pooled RR: 2.14; 95% CI 1.69–2.72; P-value > 0.0001; I2 = 89.11%) (Table 3). Additionally, the analysis of four studies investigating asphyxia showed a significant association with increased IVH occurrence (pooled RR: 1.70; 95% CI 1.26–2.28; P-value = 0.01; I2 = 76.07%) (Table 3). Finally, nine studies exploring the association between resuscitation and IVH found no significant correlation (P-value: 0.29). Multiple birth was not significantly associated with an increased risk of IVH, with a pooled RR of 1.07 (95% CI 0.86–1.33) across three studies, with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 57.93%, P-value = 0.09) (Table 3).
The clinical risk factors for IVH after birth, based on several studies, highlight significant associations with conditions like PDA, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), thrombocytopenia, hyaline membrane disease (HMD), and hypercapnia. Studies examining PDA (12 studies) showed a significant increased risk of IVH (pooled RR: 1.65, 95% CI 1.43–1.91, P-value < 0.0001) (Fig. 3), while RDS (11 studies) was found to increase the occurrence of IVH by 92% (pooled RR: 1.92, 95% CI 1.58–2.33, P-value < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). Thrombocytopenia, according to 6 studies, increased IVH risk by 34% (pooled RR: 1.34, 95% CI 1.10–1.62, P-value < 0.0001). Additionally, HMD and hypercapnia both showed significant associations, increasing IVH risk by 2.09 (pooled RR: 2.09, 95% CI 1.15–3.80, P-value = 0.03) and 2.33 times (pooled RR: 2.33, 95% CI 1.64–3.33, P-value < 0.0001), respectively (Table 3).
In contrast, other clinical factors, including pneumothorax, sepsis, hypertension, necrotizing enterocolitis, pulmonary hemorrhage, and elevated CRP levels, did not show significant relationships with IVH occurrence. Pneumothorax (10 studies) had no significant effect (pooled RR: 2.15, 95% CI 1.73–2.66, P-value = 0.25), and sepsis (8 studies) was also not significantly linked to IVH (pooled RR: 1.65, 95% CI 1.31–2.07, P-value = 0.12). Hypertension, necrotizing enterocolitis, pulmonary hemorrhage, and CRP > 10 or 20, as reviewed in 3 studies each, showed no meaningful impact on the risk of IVH (P-value > 0.05) (Table 3).

The association between therapeutic interventions and the risk of IVH has been studied in various clinical trials. The use of antenatal steroids, as reviewed in 17 studies, showed a significant reduction in the risk of IVH, with a pooled relative risk (pooled RR) of 0.79 (95% CI 0.70–0.89, P-value < 0.0001) (Fig. 4). In contrast, mechanical ventilation, as analyzed in 12 studies, was found to significantly increase the risk of IVH, with a pooled RR of 3.27 (95% CI 2.77–3.86, P-value < 0.0001) (Fig. 4 and Table 4).
Furthermore, the use of catecholamines and surfactants was associated with an increased likelihood of IVH, with pooled RRs of 1.99 and 2.32, respectively. It is important to note that the term catecholamines in this analysis refers to the class of vasoactive agents (e.g., dopamine, dobutamine, epinephrine), although most original studies did not specify the exact drug administered. Similarly, inotropes were assessed at the class level due to inconsistent reporting. No significant association was observed between the use of inotropes or tocolytics and the risk of IVH. These findings highlight the heterogeneous effects of different pharmacological interventions on IVH occurrence in neonates, though further drug-specific research is needed to refine these associations (Table 4).

Discussion
The systematic review process for understanding the risk factors of IVH in neonates involved a thorough search of electronic databases, resulting in 2216 studies initially identified. After removing duplicates and screening based on titles, abstracts, and full texts, 30 studies were selected for the final analysis. However, the high heterogeneity observed in many analyses, as indicated by elevated I2 values, was likely attributable to variations in study design (e.g., prospective vs. retrospective), clinical protocols (such as timing of surfactant or steroid administration), outcome definitions (e.g., inclusion of different Papile grades for IVH), and population characteristics (such as gestational age and birth weight thresholds). Regional disparities in neonatal care practices may have further contributed to this variability.
Among maternal risk factors, cesarean delivery emerged as a significant predictor of IVH with a pooled RR of 1.19, suggesting that surgical delivery might predispose neonates to IVH, possibly due to the stresses of surgery or different management protocols post-delivery. Improvements in modern vaginal delivery techniques compared to traditional practices may contribute to the observed lack of increased IVH risk. The use of vacuum and forceps, when appropriately indicated, can reduce delivery-related trauma and shorten the duration of labor, thereby lowering the likelihood of complications. Furthermore, stricter clinical guidelines for selecting candidates for vaginal delivery, enhanced operator skills—particularly among midwives—and meticulous neonatal care have collectively contributed to the improved safety profile of vaginal birth in recent years.
Conversely, no significant association was found with vaginal delivery, highlighting the importance of mode of delivery in neonatal outcomes. PROM showed a moderate association with IVH, with an RR of 1.26, although this varied by region, indicating potential influences from clinical practices or genetic factors. The highest risk was observed in American studies, which may reflect differences in clinical practices such as delivery time, infection management protocols, or access to prenatal care. Further, a study by Eisha A. Christian et al. indicated that length of stay and hospital costs in the United States increased significantly between 2000 and 2010. Other maternal conditions like gestational diabetes and preeclampsia did not significantly correlate with IVH, possibly due to the studies' lack of power to detect subtle effects or due to effective management reducing their impact.
Neonatal factors, particularly birth weight, had a profound impact on IVH risk. Very low birth weight neonates (≤ 1000 g) were found to have a 94% increased risk of IVH, underscoring the vulnerability of extremely preterm infants. In contrast, higher birth weights were protective, which might be linked to better physiological development at birth. Low Apgar scores and asphyxia were also significantly associated with IVH, emphasizing the critical role of neonatal resuscitation and immediate post-birth care. However, factors like male gender and multiple births did not show a consistent association with IVH, suggesting that gender or plurality might not be as significant as previously thought in the context of IVH. However, some findings, including the study by Shah et al., suggest a trend toward a higher link among men, which could be affected by biological differences in vascular development and inflammatory response between females and males. Furthermore, findings of a study demonstrated that multiple pregnancies cannot be directly linked to an increased risk of IVH.
Postnatal clinical conditions were also key contributors to IVH risk. Conditions like PDA, RDS, thrombocytopenia, HMD, and hypercapnia were all significantly associated with an increased risk of IVH. These findings point to a complex interaction where systemic diseases or treatments can alter cerebral blood flow or vascular integrity, leading to hemorrhage. Conversely, conditions like pneumothorax and sepsis did not show a significant link, possibly due to their management or the timing of their occurrence relative to when IVH is most likely to happen.
Therapeutic interventions had a mixed impact on IVH risk. Antenatal steroid administration was beneficial, reducing IVH risk with an RR of 0.79, supporting the practice of using steroids for lung maturation in high-risk pregnancies. Antenatal steroids, particularly two doses of betamethasone 24 h to 7 days before delivery, reduce the risk of IVH in VLBW preterm infants by facilitating lung maturation, reducing the need for mechanical ventilation, strengthening cerebral vasculature, and modulating inflammatory responses. However, mechanical ventilation was strongly associated with increased IVH risk (RR 3.27), which it increases positive chest pressure and reduces venous return to the heart. In addition, alterations in cerebral blood flow and lung injury caused by prolonged mechanical ventilation, through the release of inflammatory cytokines, increase the risk of IVH and chronic lung disease. Therefore, careful management of mechanical ventilation in these infants is essential. The utilization of catecholamines and surfactants was found to be essentially related with an expanded hazard of IVH in preterm neonates; be that as it may, these affiliations likely reflect the basic seriousness of sickness instead of coordinate causal impacts. Additionally, surfactant treatment is routinely utilized in neonates with respiratory trouble disorder, a condition freely related with IVH. Due to conflicting detailing over ponders, investigations were conducted at the pharmacological course level instead of by person medicate. No critical affiliation was noted between inotrope or tocolytic use and IVH, in spite of the fact that changeability in definitions and restricted announcing may have impacted these discoveries. These findings underscore the challenge of distinguishing treatment effects from disease severity and emphasize the need for cautious interpretation of associations observed in observational data.

Clinical implications and future research
These findings highlight the critical need for targeted management and early identification of preterm infants at high risk of IVH. Prenatal administration of corticosteroids can significantly improve outcomes by supporting fetal lung development and reducing the risk of postnatal complications, which is consistent with current medical recommendations for high-risk pregnancies. In contrast, mechanical ventilation was strongly associated with an increased risk of IVH, suggesting that milder forms of respiratory support—such as noninvasive ventilation or volume-controlled settings—should be considered to reduce abrupt changes in cerebral blood flow and pressure.
The different geographic regions observed in the association between PROM and IVH reflect differences in prenatal care practices around the world. In high-income countries, reduction of inflammation associated with PROM can be achieved with early recognition and appropriate management to reduce the risk of IVH. However, in low-resource settings, improvements in neonatal outcomes can occur with improved maternal infection control and access to antenatal care. Furthermore, the strong association between VLBW and IVH highlights the vulnerability of very preterm infants and requires personalized care plans tailored to the unique needs of the infants and specialized monitoring. High heterogeneity across studies suggests that important confounding variables such as Apgar scores, gestational age, or specific ventilator strategies may not be consistently controlled for and should be more carefully examined in future research.

Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, due to the limited number of available studies and inconsistent reporting of key variables, subgroup analyses based on additional covariates were not feasible. Second, substantial heterogeneity observed in several meta-analyses may reflect variations in study design, outcome definitions, and population characteristics.

Conclusion
In conclusion, this meta-analysis offers a nuanced view of IVH risk factors, indicating that both maternal and neonatal health, as well as clinical management strategies, play pivotal roles. The findings suggest areas for clinical practice improvements, particularly in delivery methods, neonatal resuscitation, and careful monitoring during therapeutic interventions. Future research should focus on reducing the heterogeneity in study outcomes by standardizing definitions and protocols for IVH. Moreover, longitudinal studies could help in understanding the long-term neurodevelopmental implications of these risk factors. This comprehensive approach to understanding IVH not only aids in prevention, but also in tailoring neonatal care for better outcomes.
