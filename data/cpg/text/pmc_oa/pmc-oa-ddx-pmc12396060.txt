Evaluation Strategies for Tissue-engineered Tracheas: From In Vitro Characterization to In Vivo Assessment
The trachea plays a critical role in maintaining airway patency, ventilation, and mucociliary clearance, supported by its unique anatomical and structural features. Tracheal defects resulting from congenital anomalies, malignancies, trauma, or prolonged intubation present significant clinical challenges. Traditional reconstruction methods, such as end-to-end anastomosis and patch grafts, are often limited by technical feasibility and suboptimal outcomes. Recently, tissue-engineered tracheal scaffolds (TETs), particularly those fabricated using 3D bioprinting technologies, have emerged as promising alternatives due to their ability to mimic natural structures and integrate functional components. However, despite technological progress, no long-term successful clinical applications have been established to date, highlighting the need for robust and standardized preclinical evaluation frameworks. This review systematically analyzes current in vitro and in vivo methodologies used to assess the safety, biocompatibility, mechanical integrity, and functional performance of 3D printing-based TETs. By introducing a variety of analysis methods to evaluate the mechanical, physicochemical, and biocompatibility properties of TETs, this study aims to propose essential components for the evaluation of 3D-printed TETs.

Introduction
The airway, consisting of the trachea and bronchi, is an important component of the respiratory system, characterized by a specialized anatomical structure that supports its essential physiological functions. The trachea is a semi-flexible tube, composed of C-shaped, incomplete rings of hyaline cartilage along the anterior and lateral walls, with the posterior aspect enclosed by the trachealis smooth muscle. It plays a vital role in respiratory physiology by conducting air between the larynx to bronchi, while also facilitating heat and moisture exchange and particle elimination.
Tracheal defects are frequently caused by congenital disorders, cancers, traumatic injuries, and complications arising from prolonged intubation or tracheostomy. These defects are categorized as circumferential (total) or partial based on their extent, with treatment options varying accordingly. Small defects are typically managed by primary closure, which is a straightforward procedure. Medium-sized defects are usually treated using patch-type grafts utilizing tissues such as costal cartilage or pericardium. However, these grafts have several limitations, including donor site morbidity, suboptimal biomechanical properties, and postoperative resorption. For large or circumferential defects, end-to-end anastomosis (ETEA) remains the standard treatment of choice. However, ETEA is technically demanding and is impractical when the defect exceeds 6 cm in length.
To overcome these limitations, various artificial tracheal substitutes have been developed. Recent advances in tissue engineering have shown promising potential in creating artificial tracheas with both functional and structural integrity. In particular, a variety of 3D printing technologies have emerged over the past few decades, each offering distinct advantages in terms of precision, fabrication efficiency, and the ability to create complex, biomimetic structures suitable for engineering living tissue constructs. Among them, 3D bioprinting stands at the forefront. In 3D bioprinting, biological materials, biochemicals, and living cells are precisely deposited layer by layer to fabricate three-dimensional structures, with spatial control over the placement of functional components.
Despite these advances, no clinically successful, long-term functional applications have been reported to date, underscoring the need for more refined and standardized preclinical evaluation systems. Since the trachea serves not only as an airway conduit but also plays essential roles in mucociliary clearance and ventilation, functional reconstruction requires more than structural replacement. Preclinical evaluation is therefore critical and must comprehensively assess safety, biocompatibility, immune response, mechanical properties, and functional performance. Recent studies have employed diverse evaluation approaches, including in vitro assays and in vivo models.
This study systematically reviews the literature to identify and analyze the evaluation parameters, methodologies, and objectives used in the preclinical assessment of tissue-engineered tracheas, with the aim of contributing to the development of standardized evaluation protocols for future clinical translation.

Methods
A non-systematic literature review was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar to analyze the various analytical techniques employed in artificial trachea research utilizing 3D printing and 3D bioprinting methodologies. Relevant source material was identified and refined using various combinations of keywords and phrases. These keywords encompassed, but were not limited to: trachea reconstruction, 3D printing, 3D bioprinting, tissue engineering, scaffold, and cartilage regeneration. Only articles published in English and deemed relevant to these keywords were included in this review.

Types of Tissue-engineered Tracheas (TETs)
3D printed artificial tracheas can be classified into four types based on the materials used in their fabrication (Table I). First, some artificial tracheas are constructed solely from synthetic thermoplastic polymers such as polycaprolactone (PCL), polylactic acid (PLA), polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA), and polyurethane (PU). Second, a co-printing approach is used to fabricate constructs by combining thermoplastic polymers with cell-laden hydrogels. The third type involves the use of only cell-laden hydrogels, without incorporating any polymers. Finally, the most recent approach utilizes hydrogels embedded with bioactive substances, such as exosomes or cytokines, instead of living cells.

Artificial trachea using thermoplastic polymer. Various thermoplastic, absorbable polymers such as polyglycolic acid (PGA), PLGA, PLA, and PCL exhibit mechanical properties comparable to those of native tracheal cartilage. Accordingly, numerous studies have explored their potential applications in the development of artificial trachea. Among these, PCL is widely utilized in 3D printing due to its approval by the Food and Drug Administration for multiple biomedical applications. PCL is a biocompatible and flexible polymer with a relatively low melting temperature of approximately 60˚C enabling its use in co-printing processes alongside cell-laden hydrogels. In tracheal tissue fabrication, PCL is commonly engineered into fibrous networks to provide mechanical stability to the hollow construct while introducing microscale porosity for effective nutrient exchange. Although PCL tracheal scaffolds have been successfully reported in various studies, long-term follow-up data in large animal models for clinical application remain limited. Recently, Shai et al. reported long-term outcomes up to three months in a porcine model. This study successfully fabricated a 20×20×1.5 mm PCL artificial trachea using a 3D bioprinter, which was implanted into a porcine model following a 2 cm circumferential resection of the trachea. The graft accurately replicated the native tracheal structure and was successfully implanted without immediate complications. Out of a total of eight pigs, only two survived for more than three months. Regenerative tissue growth, neo-tissue formation, and partial epithelialization were observed at the implantation site. With the rapid advancement of 3D bioprinting technologies, recent trends indicate a decreasing reliance on polymer-only approaches for the fabrication of TETs, favoring more complex, multi-material systems incorporating bioactive or cellular components.
Artificial trachea using thermoplastic polymer and cells. With advancements in bioprinting technology, it has become feasible to incorporate living cells, such as chondrocytes, respiratory epithelial cells, and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), into hydrogels and co-print them during the fabrication of artificial tracheal constructs. The airway is composed of various cell types, primarily categorized into functional and stromal cells. Functional cells mainly include epithelial cells and chondrocytes. The respiratory epithelium lining the trachea is a pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium and essential for regulating airway biology and functions as a physical barrier between the airway and the host. Chondrocytes, the sole resident cell type in cartilage, are primarily responsible for the synthesis and maintenance of the cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM). For tissue engineering applications, chondrocytes can be harvested from autologous sources such as auricular or nasal cartilage and subsequently utilized to promote chondrogenesis. In tracheal tissue engineering, chondrocytes are critical for generating neocartilage structures that confer mechanical integrity and structural support to the engineered airway. Although the native airway performs multiple essential physiological functions, its roles in maintaining ventilation and facilitating mucociliary clearance are of particular importance. Accordingly, the reconstruction of a biomimetic airway presents key challenges, including the regeneration of a functional epithelial lining, restoration of cartilage architecture, and promotion of neovascularization to ensure long-term viability and integration.
To address these multifaceted requirements, recent studies have employed multi-cellular bioprinting strategies to fabricate biomimetic tracheal constructs. Park et al. reported the development of a 3D-bioprinted tracheal scaffold composed of PCL, into which autologous nasal epithelial cells and chondrocyte-laden hydrogel were sequentially printed in a layer-by-layer manner using an extrusion-based bioprinting system. Similarly, Ke et al. fabricated a tracheal construct with anatomically distinct cartilage and smooth muscle regions by combining PCL with a hydrogel matrix encapsulating human MSCs. Shan et al. developed a multifunctional tracheal substitute comprising a composite hydrogel of hyaluronic acid methacryloyl and decellularized cartilaginous matrix (HAMA-DCM) loaded with autologous chondrocytes to mimic the outer cartilaginous layer, and a 3D printed PCL mesh scaffold coated with silk fibroin methacryloyl (SilMA) hydrogel loaded with autologous epithelial cells to replicate the inner airway epithelial layer.
Artificial trachea using cells (thermoplastic polymer-free). Polymer-free approaches are being explored as potential alternatives to address the limitations of biodegradable polymer-based constructs, which are often associated with granulation tissue formation on the luminal surface due to foreign body reactions, inflammation, and infection. Taniguchi et al. reported the fabrication of a polymer-free artificial trachea using 3D bioprinting techniques that incorporated chondrocyte and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). Chondrocytes and MSCs were first cultured in cell culture plates to form multicellular spheroids. These spheroids were then aspirated using a robotically controlled nozzle and precisely positioned onto a fine needle array under computer control. Following spheroid placement, the assembled 3D cellular constructs were matured in a media-perfused bioreactor. This approach, known as the Kenzan methods, enables the fabrication of polymer-free TETs by precisely inserting spheroids into a microneedle array using a bioprinting system. Sun et al. proposed a polymer-free TETs fabricated using 3D bioprinting with two tissue-specific matrix hydrogels (e.g., cartilage-specific matrix gels and fibrous tissue-specific matrix gels). Polymer-free TETs have shown promising results in tissue regeneration; however, many studies involving animal experiments require silicone supportive stents to ensure the structural stability of the graft. In clinical practice, the process of removing the silicone tube could become an invasive procedure for patients, highlighting the need for further research into mechanically stable polymer-free TETs that can maintain the lumen without the need for supportive stents during tissue regeneration.
Cell-free artificial trachea functionalized with exosomes or cytokines (cell free). Recently, studies on the development of TETs utilizing exosome technology have been introduced. Cell seeding is one of the widely used strategies in tissue engineering, including trachea regeneration. However, direct cell transplantation presents various risks, such as tumorigenicity, ethical issues, and immune rejection. Moreover, in 3D bioprinted organs, high post-printing cell viability is considered a critical factor for successful tissue regeneration; nevertheless, technical limitations in maintain cell viability after printing still remain. Exosomes, as cell-to-cell messengers, facilitate the transport of important functional substances from donor cells to recipient cells and modulate the specific functions of target cells through paracrine effects. Therefore, by utilizing exosomes, it is possible to harness the functional capabilities of their parent cells while mitigating the risks associated with direct cell transplantation. Shen et al. successfully fabricated TETs by incorporating endothelial progenitor cell-derived exosomes into methacrylate gelatin (GelMA) hydrogel, which was then co-printed with PCL. Liu et al. reported cell-free TETs, which is based on a 3D-printed PCL scaffold coated with a transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) and stromal cell-derived factor 1α (SDF-1α) embedded GelMA hydrogel to facilitate cell recruitment and differentiation toward chondrocyte-phenotype.

In Vitro Characterization of the TETs
Mechanical aspects of tissue-engineered artificial trachea. The adult human trachea averages 1.8 cm in diameter and 12 cm in length. Its dimensions, however, are dynamic: expanding in both length and diameter during inhalation, and narrowing and shortening during exhalation. Therefore, an artificial trachea should exhibit sufficient radial compressive strength and longitudinal flexibility in response to intraluminal pressure changes, thereby ensuring the maintenance of effective respiratory function. As a result, to achieve appropriate therapeutic outcomes in the clinical application of artificial tracheal scaffolds, it is essential to evaluate their mechanical properties. Consequently, numerous studies have systematically evaluated the mechanical properties of artificial tracheal scaffolds to ensure functional performance under physiological conditions.
Most studies have primarily evaluated the longitudinal tensile strength of TETs. For tensile strength measurements, all specimens were fixed in the vertical direction and tested using a universal testing machine. Subsequently, compression tests were conducted to assess both radial and axial (vertical) compressive strength, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the mechanical robustness of the scaffolds. In addition, three-point bending tests were commonly performed to evaluate the flexibility of the fabricated TETs. To assess durability and determine whether the constructs could endure the repetitive mechanical loading and unloading that occurs during respiration, Khalid et al. conducted cyclical tests. Using a universal mechanical testing machine equipped with a 50 N load cell, the strain rate was adjusted to simulate a fast adult breathing rate of 20 breaths per minute, applied for 250 cycles. Tracheal scaffolds were placed vertically, and peak loads at cycles 1 and 250 were recorded to calculate the percentage of cyclical strain recovery. Additionally, Yu et al. performed a torsion (rotation) test to simulate the movement of the human neck and assess the motility performance of the TETs (Table II, Figure 1).

Biocompatibility of tissue-engineered artificial trachea. To ensure effective tissue regeneration and safety during human implantation, TETs must exhibit biocompatibility and non-toxicity (Table II). In most studies, biocompatibility has been assessed in vitro using CCK-8 assay or live/dead assays. To assess and compare cellular viability of co-culture cells on scaffolds, Khalid et al. performed a series of tests over a 14-day period, including the alamarBlue™ metabolic assay, the PicoGreen dsDNA assay, and fluorescent imagining of live and dead cells using the LIVE/DEAD® Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit. In a preliminary biocompatibility study, Guedes et al. evaluated the optimal gamma irradiation conditions for sterilizing PCL-based scaffolds. To assess cytocompatibility between the scaffolds and cells, human dental pulp stem/stromal cells (hDPSCs) were seeded onto the PCL scaffolds, and cell viability was analyzed using the Presto Blue™ assay. Subsequently, samples were processed for Scanning Electronic Microscopy (SEM) to observe the morphology of cells adhered to the PCL scaffolds. In addition, following the in vitro biocompatibility evaluation, an in vivo biocompatibility test was conducted in accordance with ISO 10993-6:2016 guidelines (Biological evaluation of medical devices, Part 6: Tests for local effects after implantation). Scaffolds were subcutaneously implanted in the dorsal region of Sasco Sprague-Dawley rats, and analyses were conducted at 7 and 15 days post-implantation. Samples were observed using SEM, and the biological response was evaluated through histological analysis with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The evaluation followed the guidelines outlined in Annex E of ISO 10993-6:2016, assessing parameters such as inflammatory cell infiltration, fibrosis, neovascularization, and necrosis around the implant site. A semi-quantitative scoring system was applied, classifying tissue responses as follows: “minimal or no reaction” (score 0.0-2.9), “slight reaction” (score 3.0-8.9), “moderate reaction” (score 9.0-15.0), and “severe reaction” (score >15).
Biodegradability of tissue-engineered artificial trachea. Several studies have evaluated and reported the in vitro degradation characteristics of 3D printed tracheal scaffolds (Table II). Paunović et al. 3D printed tubular structures as prototypes of their customized bioresorbable airway stent and incubated them in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (pH 7.4) at 37˚C and 50˚C. At designated time point, the weight of the specimens was measured to calculate water uptake. Subsequently, to assess changes in the mechanical performance of the stents during the degradation process, all stents underwent uniaxial compression testing at the corresponding time points. Similarly, Zhang et al. immersed PCL scaffolds in PBS at 37˚C to mimic in vivo physiological environment and assessed their mechanical properties after different degradation periods (3 months and 6months).
Physicochemical aspects of tissue-engineered artificial trachea. Several studies have employed a range of analytical techniques, including Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), to evaluate the physicochemical properties of TETs fabricated via 3D printing. To assess the chemical and material properties of the poly (L-lactic acid) (PLLA)-based TETs they developed, Gao et al. conducted FTIR and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analyses on raw PLLA, 3D-printed PLLA scaffolds and irradiation-sterilized PLLA scaffolds. In their study, FTIR analysis demonstrated that the absorption spectra corresponding to backbone C-C and C-O stretching modes remained unchanged across all groups, indicating that neither the 3D printing nor the sterilization process induced structural alterations in the PLLA. Furthermore, DSC analysis revealed no significant thermal decomposition within the examined temperature range, suggesting that the crystalline structure of PLLA was preserved following 3D printing and sterilization. Since fused deposition modeling (FDM)-based 3D printing involves the thermal melting of polymer, evaluating the thermal stability of the synthesized polymer is essential. In this context, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) can be utilized to assess the thermal stability and degradation behavior of TETs. Furthermore, in several studies, the water contact angle of scaffolds has been measured to evaluate surface wettability characteristics-specifically hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity- and to infer their potential to support cell adhesion (Table II).

In Vivo Animal Studies
Animal models. Animal models play a crucial role in the study of airway diseases and in translational research. Rodent models have been widely used to understand fundamental biological mechanisms and to develop new therapeutic strategies for the trachea. In contrast, large animal models are essential for evaluating the clinical applicability, safety, and feasibility of newly developed tracheal substitutes. Compared to smaller animals, large mammals such as dogs, pigs, sheep, and goats have airway structures that are more similar to those of humans in terms of size, morphology, and physiology, making them more suitable for studies aiming for clinical translation. In fact, a variety of studies have reported TETs transplantation using a wide range of animal models, from small animals such as mice, rats, and rabbits to large animals including dogs, pigs and sheep.
Tracheal defect animal models are generally classified into circumferential and segmental defect models. Reconstruction of a tubular-shaped whole trachea presents greater challenges compared to segmental defect repair, primarily due to the extensive anastomotic area involved. Chan et al. fabricated 2-cm-long 3D-printed circumferential tracheal grafts using PCL and applied them to a rabbit circumferential tracheal defect model. Shai et al. performed a long-term evaluation of the post-implantation response to their 3D-printed PCL scaffold. A 2 cm segment of the porcine trachea was resected and a PCL construct measuring 20 mm in length, 20 mm in diameter and 1.5 mm in thickness was implanted. The animals were monitored for up to 90 days following implantation.
Animal experiments on segmental grafts are typically conducted using partial tracheal defect models, in which the anterior portion of the trachea is surgically excised. Most of these investigations have employed rabbit models to assess graft performance and regeneration outcomes (Figure 2). However, Liu et al. resected a 3-4 mm segment of the trachea in C57BL/6J mice, implanted their manufactured trachea graft using 9-0 nylon sutures, and monitored the animals for up to 3 months post-transplantation. Torsello et al. employed an ovine model to evaluate a 3D-printed PCL laryngeal and tracheal graft for anterior laryngotracheal reconstruction, a surgical procedure for congenital subglottic stenosis. In their study, scaffold fabrication was guided by a detailed anatomical analysis of the ovine larynx and trachea, performed using 3D reconstruction CT scan. Key anatomical parameters, including the thickness of the cartilaginous rings, the membranous portion, the inter-ring distance, and the cranio-caudal length of the cricoid cartilage, were measured. Based on these measurements, the laryngeal and tracheal scaffold were designed using 3D CAD software.

Assessments in Animal Studies
Functional assessments. In animal studies, the efficacy and safety of the TETs transplantation are evaluated through various parameters following transplantation. In most studies, bronchoscopy is performed over a defined period to monitor tissue regeneration and the degree of stenosis at the implantation site. Park et al. performed bronchoscopy using a rigid 4 mm 0° endoscope at 2, 4, 6 and 8 weeks post-transplantation. The inner surface of the scaffold-implanted area was examined, and the following parameters were evaluated: narrowing of the lumen, re-epithelization, formation of granulation tissue around the scaffold grafted area and integration of the implanted scaffolds. The degree of stenosis was classified using a four-point scale: 0-10% as no stenosis, 11-50% as mild stenosis, 51-70% as moderate stenosis, and >71% as severe stenosis.
Computed tomography (CT) scanning can also be utilized to assess the patency of the TETs following transplantation. Notably, Kim et al. quantified the regenerated cartilage area using Image J software (NIH, USA), based on 3D reconstructed images obtained from micro-CT scans. Liu et al. also reported their findings based on micro-CT analysis. Using sagittal images from CT scans, the minimal luminal diameter of the host and graft airways was measured. The CT images were reconstructed and segmented, and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis using Amira software (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was performed to evaluate graft function during the respiratory process. CFD analysis simulates airflow to assess whether regenerated tissue maintains adequate respiratory function. In addition, the observation of cross-sectional gross images and the measurement of the cross-sectional area of the harvested trachea can be used to evaluate the luminal patency of TETs.
In several studies, SEM was employed to evaluate the maturation status of epithelial cells that had migrated into the lumen of the TETs using harvested tissue samples, as well as to examine morphological changes in TETs (e.g. surface structures, pore size) during in vivo degradation.
Tissue assessments. Histological analysis is one of the critical methods for evaluating the outcomes of in vivo experiments, as it enables the assessment of tissue regeneration, inflammation or foreign body reactions, and the extent of fibrosis (Table III). Park et al. implanted a 3D printed PCL tracheal scaffold into a rabbit model with a partial tracheal defect after a two-week prevascularization period in the omentum. Safranin O staining revealed the regeneration of newly formed cartilage tissue (red coloration) at the scaffold implantation site. In a separate study, Park et al. reported a tracheal construct in which the epithelial lining and cartilage rings were printed on the inner and outer surfaces, respectively, of a PCL-based framework. The presence of sulfated glycosaminoglycans (sGAGs) within the regenerated cartilage tissue was confirmed using AB and Safranin O staining. Alternatively, AB staining has also been used to visualize the mucous components of regenerated respiratory epithelium. Kim et al. fabricated a PCL-based artificial trachea coated with human bronchial epithelial cells (hBECs), iPSC-derived MSCs, and iPSC-derived chondrocytes, and performed animal implantation experiments. In their study, AB staining of tissues harvested 4 weeks after animal implantation revealed mucus production (blue coloration) within the regenerated epithelium. To assess the extent of epithelial regeneration, the thickness of the regenerated epithelium on AB-stained slides was quantitatively measured using Image J software.
Furthermore, IHC and IF staining with specific markers have been employed to evaluate the regeneration of cartilage and epithelial structure within the newly formed tissue. For instance, IF staining for keratin 5 (a basal cell marker) and β-tubulin (a marker of cilia) was conducted to identify mucosal cells within the regenerated tracheal epithelium. Shai et al. implanted a 3D printed PCL tracheal scaffold into a porcine model and performed IHC staining for chondrocyte markers, including Sox9, type II collagen, and aggrecan, to evaluate neocartilage formation in tissues harvested on postoperative day 19. Additionally, angiogenesis within the newly formed cartilage was assessed using vascular markers such as CD31 and α-SMA, and cellular proliferation was evaluated by proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) staining. IHC staining for CD68 can be used to assess whether inflammatory granulation in engineered chondrocyte-scaffold constructs induces luminal stenosis of the airway.
The regenerative potential of TETs toward cartilage has also been confirmed through qRT-PCR-based analysis of chondrogenic gene expression (Table IV). During chondrogenesis, chondroprogenitor cells differentiate into mature chondrocytes characteristic of hyaline cartilage, synthesizing key cartilage-specific extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, most notably type II collagen (COL2) and aggrecan (ACAN). Other ECM components, including type IX (COL9) and type XI collagen (COL11), are also expressed at comparatively lower levels.

In contrast, hypertrophic and fibrocartilage chondrocyte phenotypes are associated with the development of osteoarthritis (OA). In the late stages of OA progression, chondrocytes undergo phenotypic transition, acquiring hypertrophic and fibrocartilage-like characteristics that contribute to the degradation of articular cartilage. Hypertrophic differentiation is associated with the secretion of specific ECM molecules, with type X collagen (COL10) serving as a hallmark marker of this phenotype. MMP13 and RUNX2, similar to COL10, serve as key markers of hypertrophic differentiation.
The fibrocartilage chondrocyte phenotype is primarily observed in joint tissues during the late stages of OA. This phenotype is characterized by elevated expression of type I collagen (COL1), α-SMA, fibroblast-specific protein 1 (FSP1 or S100A4), tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1 (TIMP1), and cell migration-inducing hyaluronidase-1 (CEMIP). In most cases, the absence of COL1 protein is sufficient to exclude the presence of fibrocartilage. Classical markers confirming the hyaline cartilage phenotype include SOX9, COL2, and ACAN, while COL10 and RUNX2 are commonly analyzed to evaluate hypertrophy. Other markers have been less frequently reported in the literature.

Conclusion
This study provides a comprehensive reference for the safety and functional evaluation of 3D printing-based TETs. By systematically reviewing current preclinical methodologies, we identified key evaluation parameters and proposed guiding principles to support their safe clinical translation. Given the complex physiological roles of the native tracheas, including airway patency, ventilation, and mucociliary clearance, successful TET reconstruction demands integrated regeneration of functional epithelium, biomechanically robust cartilage structures, and a supporting vascular network to ensure long-term tissue viability.
A wide range of in vitro and in vivo approaches have been employed to evaluate these key functional components. However, due to the considerable variability in design strategies, biomaterials, and intended clinical applications of TETs, a universal evaluation protocol remains impractical. Therefore, it is critical to adopt a flexible and context-specific assessment framework tailored to the unique attributes of each scaffold. The insights presented in this review may contribute to the establishment of standardized yet adaptable preclinical testing strategies, ultimately facilitating the regulatory approval and clinical implementation of 3D printing-based tracheal constructs.
