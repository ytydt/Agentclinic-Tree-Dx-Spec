The clinical outcomes of robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty through the direct anterior approach: A meta-analysis and systematic review
Background:
Existing evidence on the effectiveness of robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA) via the direct anterior approach compared to conventional THA is still scarce within evidence-based medicine. This meta-analysis seeks to bridge this gap by consolidating the available data.
Methods:
In this meta-analysis, we conducted searches across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, CQVIP, and CBM databases, covering records from their inception until January 2024. The included studies reported on the differences in clinical outcomes between robotic-assisted THA via the direct anterior approach and conventional THA. We excluded duplicate publications, studies lacking full text, incomplete data, animal experiments, literature reviews, and systematic studies from the analysis. All data were analyzed using STATA 15.1 statistical software.
Results:
This meta-analysis included 5 cohort studies with a total of 634 patients, with sample sizes ranging from 48 to 208. Of these, 290 patients were in the robotic-assisted group, while 344 were in the conventional group. The combined findings indicated a notable increase in acetabular inclination in robot-assisted THA when compared to that in conventional THA (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.75, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.02–1.47, P = .043). Moreover, robot-assisted THA demonstrated a significantly lower acetabular anteversion compared to that in the conventional THA (SMD = –0.34, 95% CI: –0.67 to –0.02, P = .039). Robot-assisted THA displayed a significantly diminished limb-length discrepancy in contrast to that in conventional THA (SMD = –0.24, 95% CI: –0.44 to –0.05, P = .015).
Conclusion:
In comparison to conventional THA, robotic-assisted THA through the direct anterior approach results in increased acetabular inclination, reduced acetabular anteversion, and improved limb-length consistency. The Harris Hip Score, however, did not significantly differ between the 2 approaches.

Introduction
Owing to the increase in the aging population and the rise in life expectancy, a growing number of older adults are experiencing hip osteoarthritis and other degenerative diseases. Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a widely performed surgical procedure for treating hip joint disorders, like osteoarthritis, to relieve pain and enhance joint function. By 2030, over 500,000 individuals in the United States are projected to undergo THA. Consequently, minimizing complications and revisions while enhancing the long-term survival of implants during THA has become crucial.
Owing to progress in surgical technology, enhancements in prosthesis design, and refined perioperative patient care, the quality of life and longevity of prostheses in individuals undergoing conventional THA have substantially improved. Nevertheless, the management of complications and revisions remains a challenge that has not yet been effectively addressed. As advancements in surgical techniques continue to evolve, robotic-assisted procedures have emerged as a promising innovation in orthopedic surgery. Robotic-assisted THA offers potential benefits, including increased precision, improved component positioning, and enhanced functional outcomes.
In traditional robotic-assisted THA with a posterior approach, surgeons navigate through the back of the hip to reach the joint, necessitating the dissection of muscles and tendons to expose the joint. Although this approach has proven effective and is widely utilized, the technique comes with inherent challenges related to tissue damage, recovery time, and postoperative pain. In recent times, the direct anterior approach (DAA) has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative, allowing access to the hip joint from the front. The DAA, on the contrary, offers a departure from the conventional posterior approach. By accessing the hip joint from the front, this approach avoids major muscle dissection and aims to provide a direct path to the joint. This innovation has gained traction owing to its potential benefits, including reduced tissue damage, rapid recovery, and improved postoperative pain management.
A growing body of literature has compared the clinical outcomes of DAA to other surgical approaches in THA. For instance, recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that DAA is associated with improved early functional recovery, shorter hospital stays, and reduced postoperative pain when compared to the posterior or lateral approaches, though often at the cost of longer operative times and a steeper learning curve. Furthermore, comparisons between DAA on a traction table versus a standard table suggest no significant differences in overall complication rates or implant positioning accuracy. These findings underscore the evolving role of DAA in contemporary THA and its increasing adoption in clinical practice.
Despite these advances, current evidence on robotic-assisted DAA remains limited. To our knowledge, no meta-analysis has systematically evaluated the outcomes of robotic-assisted DAA in comparison with conventional THA, whether performed via posterior, lateral, or non-robotic anterior approaches. This gap highlights the need for a comprehensive synthesis of the available data. Therefore, this meta-analysis and systematic review aim to assess the clinical outcomes of robotic-assisted DAA versus conventional THA. Through quantitative synthesis of existing studies, we seek to provide evidence-based guidance to inform surgical decision-making and improve patient outcomes in hip arthroplasty.

2. Methods

2.1. Protocol registration
This meta-analysis was registered with the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols under registration number INPLASY202440074 (doi:10.37766/inplasy2024.4.0074).

2.2. Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria
The inclusion criteria were as follows:
Study population: Patients requiring hip replacement.
Intervention: Robotic-assisted THA via the direct anterior approach.
Control: Conventional THA (non-robotic-assisted).
Outcome measures: Acetabular inclination, acetabular anteversion, acetabular offset, limb-length discrepancy, and the Harris Hip Score.
Study design: Cohort studies or randomized controlled trials (RCTs), with language restricted to English and Chinese.
Exclusion criteria included duplicate publications, incomplete studies, missing or unavailable data, animal experiments, reviews, and systematic reviews.

2.3. Search strategy
In this meta-analysis, we searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, CQVIP, and CBM databases from the day of establishment of these databases to January 2024. The mesh terms used were: (((((Robot[Title/Abstract]) OR (Robotic[Title/Abstract])) OR (robotic-assisted[Title/Abstract])) OR (Robot-enhanced[Title/Abstract])) AND (((((((((“Arthroplasty, Replacement, Hip”[Mesh]) OR (Hip Replacement Arthroplasties[Title/Abstract])) OR (Hip Prosthesis Implantation[Title/Abstract])) OR (Hip Prosthesis Implantations[Title/Abstract])) OR (Hip Replacement Arthroplasty[Title/Abstract])) OR (Total Hip Replacements[Title/Abstract])) OR (Total Hip Replacement[Title/Abstract])) OR (Total Hip Arthroplasty[Title/Abstract])) OR (Total Hip Arthroplasties[Title/Abstract]))) AND (anterior[Title/Abstract]).

2.4. Literature screening and data extraction
Two researchers were responsible for conducting the literature searches, data screening, and data extraction. Any disagreements or queries were resolved through consultation with a third party. The study data included information on the author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and outcome measures such as acetabular inclination, acetabular anteversion, acetabular offset, limb-length discrepancy, and the Harris Hip Score.

2.5. Literature quality assessment
Two researchers independently assessed the quality of the studies. The Cochrane risk assessment tool was utilized to evaluate the quality of the RCTs, whereas the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was used to evaluate cohort studies. Disagreements were resolved through consultation or discussion with a third party. The meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses checklist, emphasizing reported and relevant criteria crucial for systematic evaluation.

2.5. Data synthesis and statistical analysis
All data were processed with the statistical software STATA 15.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). Continuous variables were analyzed using the standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI), while categorical variables were assessed using an odds ratio with a 95% CI. A heterogeneity test with P > .1 and I2 < 50% indicated homogeneity among the studies, permitting pooled analysis with a fixed-effects model. Furthermore, P < .1 and I2 > 50% indicated heterogeneity among the studies; a sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify the sources of variation. The between-study variance (T2) was estimated using the DerSimonian–Laird method, which is the default estimator in STATA 15.1 for random-effects models. If significant differences were found, a random-effects model was used, or a descriptive analysis was performed instead of pooling the results. Funnel plots and Egger test were employed to assess publication bias.

3. Results

3.1. Literature search results
A total of 119 articles were initially collected for this study. After excluding duplicates, 48 articles remained. Following a review of titles and abstracts, 43 articles were further evaluated, and ultimately, 5 studies were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).
Flow diagram for selection of studies.

3.2. Baseline characteristics and quality assessment of the included studies
Five cohort studies were incorporated into this meta-analysis, encompassing a total of 634 patients with sample sizes ranging from 48 to 208. Among these 634 patients, 290 patients were included in the robotic group, while 344 were assigned to the conventional group. The study participants hailed from diverse locations, including France, Turkey, the United States of America, Romania, and China. In terms of demographics, the mean age for patients in the robotic group ranged from 54.0 to 66.5, while in the conventional group, the age ranged from 53.0 to 68.0. The mean BMI in the robotic group varied from 25.4 to 29.8, and in the conventional group, the BMI was within the range of 24.8 to 29.3. Quality assessment, as measured using Newcastle–Ottawa Scale scores, consistently exceeded 7, meeting the required standards (Table 1). Notably, in all included studies, the conventional THA procedures were performed via the DAA without robotic assistance.

3.3. Results of the meta-analysis

3.3.1. Acetabular inclination
Five studies were included in the acetabular inclination analysis. The reported mean acetabular inclination values ranged from 39.23° to 54.72° in the robotic-assisted group and 36.9° to 41.8° in the conventional group. Most values fell within the Lewinnek safe zone (30°–50°). During this analysis, the acetabular inclination after robotic-assisted THA was compared with that after conventional THA. Owing to significant heterogeneity (I2 = 94.2%, P = .000), a meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model. We identified that the acetabular inclination following robotic-assisted THA is significantly higher than that observed after conventional THA (SMD = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.02–1.47, P = .043) (Fig. 2).
Differences in acetabular inclination after robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA) and conventional THA.

3.3.2. Acetabular anteversion
Five studies were included in the acetabular anteversion analysis. The reported mean acetabular anteversion values ranged from 14.19° to 23.4° in the robotic-assisted group and 15.62° to 25.3° in the conventional group. All values fell within the generally accepted safe zone of 5° to 25°. During this analysis, the acetabular anteversion after robotic-assisted THA was compared with that following conventional THA. Owing to significant heterogeneity (I2 = 73.1%, P = .005), a meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model. We discovered that the acetabular anteversion following robotic-assisted THA was significantly lower than that observed after conventional THA (SMD = –0.34, 95% CI: –0.67 to –0.02, P = .039) (Fig. 3).
Differences in acetabular anteversion after robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA) and conventional THA.

3.3.3. Acetabular offset
Two studies were included in the acetabular offset analysis. The reported mean acetabular offset values ranged from –4.9 to –2.9 mm in the robotic-assisted group and –3.83to –3.3 mm in the conventional group. During this analysis, the acetabular offset following robotic-assisted THA was compared to the acetabular offset after conventional THA. As no significant heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 18.9%, P = .267), a fixed-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis. No significant difference in acetabular offset after robotic-assisted THA compared to conventional THA was observed (SMD = 0.00, 95% CI: –0.28 to 0.28, P = .964) (Fig. 4).
Differences in acetabular offset following robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA) and conventional THA.

3.3.4. Limb-length discrepancy
Four studies were included in the analysis of limb-length discrepancy. The limb-length discrepancy after robotic-assisted THA was compared to that after conventional THA. Since no significant heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 18.9%, P = .267), a fixed-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The results showed that limb-length discrepancy following robotic-assisted THA was significantly lower than that observed after conventional THA (SMD = –0.24, 95% CI: –0.44 to –0.05, P = .015) (Fig. 5).
Differences in limb-length discrepancy after robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA) and conventional THA.

3.3.5. Harris hip score
Two studies were included in the Harris Hip Score analysis. During this analysis, the Harris Hip Score after robotic-assisted THA was compared to the score after conventional THA. No significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 67.9%, P = .078). Therefore, a fixed-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. No significant difference in the Harris Hip Score was observed after robotic-assisted THA compared to that following conventional THA (SMD = –0.16, 95% CI: –0.72 to 0.41, P = .746) (Fig. 6).
Differences in Harris Hip Score after robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA) and conventional THA.

3.4. Sensitivity analysis
We conducted a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of each individual trial, followed by a combined analysis of the remaining studies. The meta-analysis revealed that the cumulative impact of all meta-analyses had minimal influence on the overall results, indicating a robust and reliable outcome.

3.5. Publication bias
The funnel plot generated in this study appears symmetrical, and Egger test yielded a P-value of .373, indicating that no significant publication bias was detected in this analysis (Fig. 7).
Funnel plot for evaluating the publication bias of the meta-analysis.

4. Discussion
Following the inception of robotic-assisted THA in the 1990s, surgeons have sought solutions to the aforementioned challenges. Robotic-assisted THA is widely regarded as a safer and more precise alternative to conventional THA for implant placement, exhibiting a reduced risk of complications and revisions, and demonstrating enhanced long-term prosthetic survival. Despite these advancements, studies focusing specifically on robotic-assisted THA via the DAA remain limited. Our meta-analysis addresses this gap by concentrating on robotic-assisted DAA, distinguishing it from prior analyses that pooled results from multiple surgical approaches.
A key finding of this meta-analysis is that robotic-assisted DAA results in significantly greater acetabular inclination than conventional THA. Although most reported values fall within the Lewinnek safe zone (30°–50°), the upper range in the robotic group approached or exceeded this limit in some studies (e.g., 54.72°). Higher inclination angles have been associated with increased joint stability and lower risk of dislocation but may also raise concerns about edge loading and accelerated polyethylene wear. The consistency in achieving target inclination angles suggests that robotic guidance enables fine-tuned adjustments based on preoperative planning and intraoperative feedback, particularly in the supine position characteristic of DAA. The potential trade-off between stability and wear risk highlights the importance of individualized cup orientation strategies. This may be particularly relevant in younger or more active patients, where component longevity is critical despite early postoperative success.
Similarly, our analysis demonstrated significantly reduced acetabular anteversion in the robotic-assisted group compared to the conventional group, with all values remaining within the accepted safe range of 5° to 25°. Lower anteversion may be beneficial in minimizing anterior instability, especially in DAA where the anterior capsule and surrounding musculature are preserved. Moreover, the narrower variation in anteversion observed in the robotic group reflects superior reproducibility and control during cup placement. Notably, previous studies such as Wang et al failed to detect a significant difference in anteversion between robotic and conventional techniques, likely due to the inclusion of posterior approaches that rely more heavily on intraoperative landmarks. Our findings suggest that the combination of DAA and robotic assistance may synergistically enhance accuracy and reduce variability in component orientation. The preservation of anterior soft tissue structures in DAA may complement the controlled trajectories enabled by robotic platforms, further minimizing human-dependent variation.
In contrast, no significant difference was observed in acetabular offset between groups. Restoration of native offset is essential for maintaining hip biomechanics, including abductor muscle tension, range of motion, and joint reaction forces. The comparable offset values indicate that both robotic and conventional DAA techniques are capable of achieving satisfactory restoration. However, the limited number of studies reporting offset data may have constrained the statistical power to detect subtle but clinically relevant differences. Future trials should place more emphasis on standardizing offset measurement techniques and reporting norms.
Taken together, these radiographic outcomes underscore the potential of robotic-assisted DAA to improve surgical precision in key aspects of cup positioning. While no significant differences were noted in patient-reported outcomes such as Harris Hip Score, these radiographic improvements may have long-term implications for implant survivorship, complication rates, and patient function. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to determine whether the observed advantages in inclination and anteversion translate into meaningful clinical benefits over time.
Other secondary endpoints, such as limb-length discrepancy and functional recovery scores, were not the primary focus of our analysis and are presented concisely. While some studies, such as Zhang et al, have shown that robotic assistance may reduce limb-length inequality, our analysis did not demonstrate consistent findings, potentially due to variability in measurement methods, surgical protocols, or sample sizes.
Despite the valuable insights gained, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, all included studies were observational cohort studies, introducing potential selection bias and confounding. Second, significant heterogeneity existed in patient demographics, surgical protocols, and outcome reporting. Third, the small number of studies limited the scope of subgroup analysis and reduced the robustness of pooled estimates. Fourth, the absence of RCTs precludes causal inference. Future high-quality RCTs comparing robotic-assisted and conventional DAA, with standardized radiographic and functional endpoints, are essential to validate our findings and guide clinical practice. Finally, when interpreting the results, it should be considered that the DerSimonian and Laird method was used without Hartung–Knapp adjustment. Recent literature indicates that this estimator may produce overly optimistic confidence intervals, potentially detecting significance where more robust methods do not.

5. Conclusion
In conclusion, in comparison to the conventional THA, robotic-assisted THA through the direct anterior approach results in increased acetabular inclination, reduced acetabular anteversion, and improved limb-length consistency. The Harris Hip Score, however, did not significantly differ between the 2 approaches. Future research should be conducted to explore these outcomes and further validate the effectiveness of robotic-assisted THA in diverse patient populations.
