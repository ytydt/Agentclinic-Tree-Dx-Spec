EndoCompass Project: Research Roadmap for Endocrine Causes and Consequences of Cancers
Abstract
Background
Endocrine science remains underrepresented in European Union research programmes despite the fundamental role of hormone health in human wellbeing. Analysis of the CORDIS database reveals a persistent gap between the societal impact of endocrine disorders and their research prioritisation. At the national funding level, endocrine societies report limited or little attention of national research funding towards endocrinology. The EndoCompass project – a joint initiative between the European Society of Endocrinology and the European Society of Paediatric Endocrinology – aimed to identify and promote strategic research priorities in endocrine science to address critical hormone-related health challenges.
Methods
Research priorities were established through comprehensive analysis of the EU CORDIS database covering the Horizon 2020 framework period (2014–2020). Expert consultation was conducted to identify key research priorities, followed by broader stakeholder engagement including society members and patient advocacy groups.
Results
Research priorities encompass re-evaluation of progestins in breast cancer treatment; strategies to disrupt AR signalling in prostate cancer; surveillance for endocrine sequelae in childhood cancer survivors; understanding genotoxic effects on fertility; and management of checkpoint inhibitor endocrinopathies and familial cancer syndromes. Emphasis is placed on precision medicine and metabolic factors in cancer.
Conclusions
This component of the EndoCompass project provides an evidence-based roadmap for strategic research investment. This framework identifies crucial investigation areas into cancer-endocrine pathophysiology, prevention, and treatment strategies, ultimately aimed at reducing the burden of these disorders on individuals and society. The findings support the broader EndoCompass objective of aligning research funding with areas of highest potential impact on endocrine health.

Introduction
There are unambiguous links between endocrine agents and various cancers. The two most common cancers in Europe are the hormone-dependent cancers, namely, oestrogen receptor (ER)-positive breast cancer, which comprises 75% of all breast cancer cases, and prostate cancer, the most common cancer in men. These two major cancers are mediated by endogenous endocrine pathways, but their initiation and progression can be influenced by a myriad of other variables. The association between physiological and environmental endocrine agents and cancer initiation is complex and varied, but what is also apparent are endocrine-related health issues that are a direct consequence of cancer (or the treatments used for cancer patients). As such, malignant tumour growth is both a consequence of endocrine agents, as well as the cause of endocrine disorders and diseases. These complex, but critical, issues will be discussed here, with a focus on their impact on society and the requirements needed to address them clinically. Specific recommendations for future research will be provided to tackle these health-related problems.

Endocrine-Related Cancers: ER-Positive Breast Cancer

Epidemiology, Societal Impact, Research State of the Art
Breast cancer accounts for 30% of all newly diagnosed cancers in women in Europe, and ER-positive breast cancers represent 3 out of every 4 cases. In these hormone-dependent breast cancers, the developmental ER transcriptional pathway re-emerges later in life to initiate tumour formation and disease progression. It is known that other endocrine pathways can impinge on ER activity, in particular pregnancy-associated hormones, although the link with cancer risk has been a complex and controversial topic for many years (Fig. 1).
ER-positive breast cancer and prostate cancer – disease-modifying variables.
The use of synthetic progesterone-based compounds called progestins in various female physiological contexts, such as the contraceptive pill and hormone replacement therapy (HRT), has been transformative. Progestins mimic the endogenous hormone progesterone and activate an intracellular receptor called the progesterone receptor (PR). Historical epidemiological data from HRT trials suggested that the inclusion of a progestin in HRT could increase a woman’s risk of breast cancer by 2- to 3-fold. The immediate impact was substantial, with HRT usage declining by 40–70% in European countries, as a result of the purported link between progestins and breast cancer risk. This meant that many women dealt with the debilitating menopausal symptoms, to avoid the breast cancer risk associated with HRT use. Preclinical work supported these findings and suggested that progestins could be pro-tumourigenic and could promote breast cancer.
The 18-year follow-up on the HRT trials has suggested that the initial conclusions were incorrect, that overall survival is not affected by the use of progestins, and that the perceived risk of getting breast cancer, and the implied increased mortality, were not accurate. In addition, recent preclinical data have suggested that any progestin-mediated cell growth is a consequence of promiscuous compounds that activate the androgen receptor (AR), and that pure PR ligands pose no risk. Epidemiological data from France have supported this concept for years: namely that pure PR agonists are protective, whereas mixed ligands that can also activate other nuclear receptors (such as the AR) can be associated with increased breast cancer risk.
The perception of the PR and its agonist ligands as cancer-promoting agents is not accurate, and paradigms in the field need to be re-evaluated, particularly as PR agonists have been shown to be effective cancer treatments, but have fallen out of favour because of the inaccurate perception around the risks associated with these ligands.

Future Research Priorities
A better understanding of the role of these ligands is required. Critically, future work needs to not group progestins as one class of molecules and, instead, separate them based on those that are pure PR agonists and those that cross-react with other nuclear receptors. The reclassification of progestins into those that are specific to the PR and those that can activate other hormonal receptors (such as the AR) means that they need to be considered separate entities and their utility should be re-evaluated and exploited for physiological and pathological use, where appropriate.
There is a need to re-evaluate different classes of progestins, to assess pure PR ligands as a separate entity, when compared with “progestins” that can also regulate other hormone receptors, particularly the AR and the glucocorticoid receptor. These agents should be reclassified and studied (biologically and clinically) as distinct entities. Re-education of the public around genuine breast cancer risks is needed, when using these different agents in HRT formulations.
Investment is required in fundamental research, to assess the contradictions observed with hormone receptor ligands for the treatment of ER-positive breast cancer. A consortium-based approach should identify the most physiologically relevant models for studying hormonal cross talk in ER-positive breast cancer. These agreed models should be used to directly assess PR agonists versus antagonists as treatments, as well as AR agonists versus antagonists as treatments, in ER-positive breast cancer. The clinical context that is most appropriate for these specific endocrine ligands should be evaluated. A specific emphasis needs to be placed on the potential clinical utility of these agents in metastatic disease, which represents the unmet clinical need.
There should be investment into new approaches for treating ER-positive breast cancer that do not involve additional approaches to block the ER itself (i.e., selective ER modulators, selective ER degraders, and proteolysis targeting chimeras [PROTACs]) or downstream effectors of the ER pathway (i.e., CDK4/6 inhibitors). This would involve a better understanding of the cross talk between the ER and other nuclear receptors, so that existing ligands for other nuclear receptors can be effectively exploited to indirectly modulate ER activity. This work would also involve the identification of upstream regulators of the ER that can be therapeutically targeted. These would probably include ER-associated pioneer factors and epigenetic proteins that contribute to cis-regulatory elements that can ultimately be used by the ER for transcriptional activity. Therapeutic interventions that target proteins functionally upstream of the ER will probably bypass known treatment-resistance mechanisms.

Endocrine-Related Cancers: Prostate Cancer

Epidemiology, Societal Impact, Research State of the Art
Prostate cancer represents the tumour type with the highest incidence among the European male population. At an early stage, most of these tumours (>95%) are localised (prostate-confined) and AR-positive adenocarcinomas, arising from the luminal epithelial prostatic cells.
Given the AR-driven nature of this disease, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), consisting of the systemic inhibition of androgen production, represents the main pharmacological approach for treating prostate cancer. Androgen deprivation therapy is especially effective when combined with radical therapeutic approaches, such as surgery and/or radiotherapy.
Unfortunately, approximately 20% of the patients undergoing ADT eventually develop resistance, progressing to a phenotype known as castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC). Despite the development of therapeutic strategies in recent years, such as AR signalling inhibitors (ARSI), which include the androgen precursor synthesis inhibitor abiraterone and the second-generation AR inhibitors enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide, CRPC remains a lethal disease, due to its progression to these drugs.
Notably, a substantial body of data from published clinical trials demonstrates that intensified inhibition of the AR-driven oncogenic programme, using ADT plus novel ARSI, yields clinical benefits when compared with ADT alone. This is observed across patients in both castration-sensitive settings (e.g., LATITUDE, STAMPEDE, ENZAMET, ARCHES, TITAN, and EMBARK) and castration-resistant settings (e.g., AFFIRM, PREVAIL, PROSPER, SPARTAN, and ARAMIS).
Molecular data obtained from these and various other clinical trials, along with findings from experimental approaches, highlight that the main mechanisms of resistance to ADT and ARSI involve the restoration of AR signalling through gain-of-function AR gene amplification, AR gain-of-function mutations, upregulation of constitutively active AR splice variants, increased intratumoural androgen biosynthesis, or the bypassing of the AR by switching to other receptors (e.g., the glucocorticoid receptor) to drive transcription of a subset of AR-regulated genes. However, there is also a subset of tumours which get transdifferentiated into an AR-negative or AR-independent status, a phenotype also called neuroendocrine prostate cancer, which is associated with a pronouncedly poor prognosis. While AR-negative neuroendocrine prostate cancer plays a crucial role in ARSI resistance since this subset does not express the ARSI target, there remains a lack of consensus regarding its prevalence.
For instance, a study of 150 patients with CRPC reported that over 96% of them exhibited adenocarcinoma histology, with only 2.9% showing adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation. Conversely, another study revealed a different landscape, with RNA-sequencing analyses of biopsies from patients with abiraterone- or enzalutamide-resistant CRPC showing that only 33% displayed adenocarcinoma phenotype, while 12% had neuroendocrine features and 27% exhibited an intermediate type, distinct from either neuroendocrine or adenocarcinoma.
Considering the challenge in targeting the aggressive phenotypes that emerge in response to ARSI, it has been suggested that reducing treatment selective pressure may offer a potential therapeutic approach. Strategies such as intermittent versus continuous ADT for patients with castration-sensitive prostate cancer have been proposed, with reported inconclusive results. Therefore, despite the demonstrated clinical benefit of hormonal therapy for patients with prostate cancer, there is still a need for optimisation to address the associated, progressive, therapy-resistant phenomena.

Future Research Priorities
The AR continues to be the primary therapeutic target for prostate cancer. However, a deeper understanding of the resistance mechanisms driving persistent AR signalling is essential, to identify novel therapeutic approaches aimed at preventing or overcoming ARSI resistance. Additionally, further research efforts are warranted to elucidate the biology of transdifferentiated neuroendocrine prostate cancer, with the aim of uncovering effective therapeutic targets for this phenotype. Addressing these 2 objectives is hampered by the considerable challenge posed by intratumour and intra-/interpatient heterogeneity. Therefore, additional research, including single-cell analyses across various settings, and taking into accord clinical and patient-related factors (e.g., metabolic status), is imperative.
Investment is needed in innovative strategies to disrupt persistent AR signalling and treat advanced prostate cancer. Delving deeper into our understanding of AR splicing, particularly the biology of AR-V7 (recognised as the most clinically relevant AR splicing variant since it lacks the ligand-binding domain targeted by AR inhibitors), emerges as a critical research focus. Therefore, comprehensive research efforts are warranted to identify novel approaches that prevent the emergence of or suppress constitutively active AR splicing variants. For example, inhibitors of the AR and AR-V7 common N-terminal domain (e.g., EPI-7170) and PROTACs have been recently developed, and their clinical efficiency is being evaluated in ongoing clinical trials. Additionally, exploration of other promising areas of interest should prioritise the discovery of novel regulators of upstream AR transcriptional activity, such as pioneer factors and AR/AR-V7 co-regulators. These approaches hold promise for influencing AR activity regardless of its copy number, protein levels and/or mutation status.
Considering clinical factors, particularly metabolic status, is crucial for clinical decision making in prostate cancer management. Prostate cancer predominantly affects elderly males (>60 years old), often coinciding with comorbidities such as metabolic conditions (e.g., diabetes) and concomitant medication use, which can impact the prostate cancer microenvironment and treatment outcomes. Given the pronounced regulatory endocrine component of this tumour type, understanding how the metabolic status of the patients influences prostate cancer biology is paramount, and should be considered for clinical decisions. In addition, while some studies suggest that drugs related to metabolism may exert antitumour effects on prostate cancer, further data from fundamental investigations and clinical studies are necessary to fully understand the underlying molecular mechanisms and elucidate their clinical efficacy. Bridging this gap between fundamental research and clinical translation is essential for integrating metabolic interventions into prostate cancer treatment strategies effectively.
Identifying novel targets for translation into the development of therapeutic strategies to effectively address CRPC remains a critical unmet clinical need. Despite recent advancements, such as the approval of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors for tumours with DNA repair deficiencies, and the promise of AKT inhibitors for PTEN-loss tumours, substantial research is still required to identify better predictive biomarkers for selecting sensitive populations and guiding clinical decision making. Moreover, it is imperative to explore alternative oncogenic pathways beyond the AR in prostate cancer. Certain tumours, like neuroendocrine prostate cancer, circumvent AR signalling, emphasising the need for innovative, long-lasting approaches and the discovery of predictive biomarkers to benefit patients with prostate cancer.
Overcoming the immunosuppressive microenvironment in prostate cancer remains a significant challenge. While immunotherapy has revolutionised cancer treatment in certain tumour types, its efficacy in prostate cancer is hampered by factors such as poor expression of immune checkpoint proteins and the presence of a strongly immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment. Although sipuleucel-T has been approved for prostate cancer treatment, its clinical utility is limited according to the IMPACT clinical trial. Current perspectives in this field focus on 2 main strategies: (1) identifying global and effective immune targets for prostate cancer treatment and (2) turning an immunosuppressive prostate cancer microenvironment into an inflamed one.

Late Endocrine Complications after Cancer Treatment in Childhood or Young Adulthood

Epidemiology, Societal Impact, Research State of the Art
Improved survival following childhood cancer has resulted in a new population of childhood cancer survivors (CCSs), currently 500,000 in Europe, with a survival rate of ∼81%. Childhood cancer survivors are at increased risk of treatment-related late complications, with a cumulative burden of 4.7 severe complications per survivor at age 50, including endocrine complications, cognitive dysfunction, and cardiovascular disease. Endocrine sequelae are among the most common late complications, and up to 50% of children and young adults treated for childhood cancer experience at least 1 endocrine disorder.
The risk of long-term complications depends on the tumour type and location, age at diagnosis, oncological treatment, and genetic polymorphism. The highest risk is seen in survivors of tumours of the central nervous system (CNS), leukaemias, bone tumours, and Hodgkin’s disease. The most common endocrine complications are hypothalamic-pituitary insufficiency, hypothyroidism, thyroid cancer, gonadal dysfunction, low bone mineral density (BMD), and metabolic syndrome.
New directions in research include novel risk-prediction strategies, genetic predisposition, the development of improved early-detection strategies (<5 years after diagnosis), and new interventions to prevent the long-term consequences of endocrine complications, such as short stature, obesity, cardiovascular disease, and psychological impairment. The lack of randomised controlled trials for surveillance and treatment of endocrine complications in CCSs is challenging as the treatment protocols are changing over time and comparison between different treatment eras is complicated.
Future research should include identifying survivors at high risk of late toxicity, through the use of a panel of biomarkers. This will develop precision medicine, which will enhance survivors’ outcomes and minimise unnecessary treatment. Childhood cancer is a rare disease, including many different diagnoses and treatments, which calls for action to gather European researchers to build an infrastructure with large cohorts of CCSs for research.

Future Research Priorities

Hypothalamic-Pituitary Disorders after Cancer Treatment
Hypothalamic-pituitary disorders are frequently reported in CCSs and are associated with impaired growth, pubertal development, and reproductive function. Cranial radiotherapy is the primary cause of hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction as a late complication. Tumour location and surgery involving the hypothalamic-pituitary region result in early onset of hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction. Radiation-induced hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction may appear months to many years after radiotherapy, with growth hormone (GH) in most cases being the first pituitary hormone to be deficient and adrenocorticotrophin being the last.
Arginine vasopressin deficiency (AVP-D), formerly known as diabetes insipidus, is not a late effect of cancer treatment, but may occur during cancer treatment due to the tumour or neurosurgery. If AVP-D newly occurs in the late effects clinic, it may be a sign of tumour recurrence, warranting additional investigations.
Exposure to cranial radiotherapy is less common today in survivors of non-CNS cancers, and prophylactic CNS radiotherapy in survivors of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia is no longer a routine treatment. Furthermore, therapy with protons instead of photons may affect the prevalence of hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction by reducing the scatter to normal tissue.
A few studies have reported chemotherapy as a cause of hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction, but this was not confirmed in larger cohorts. However, targeted chemotherapy such as imatinib, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), and immune system modulators may cause pituitary dysfunction, for which surveillance is advocated. Hypothalamic dysfunction, resulting in pituitary dysfunction plus obesity, sleep disturbance, temperature dysregulation, and behavioural problems, may be severe in children after treatment for suprasellar tumours and may aggravate in time. Known risk factors are tumour location and extent of neurosurgery. The effect of radiotherapy on the hypothalamus is very unknown. Hypothalamic obesity will be discussed in more detail in the chapter on neuroendocrinology and pituitary diseases.
To study the prevalence of hypothalamic dysfunction following radiotherapy (photons or protons) during childhood;
To investigate the need for prolonged follow-up and when to stop screening for hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction;
To determine the effect of targeted therapies during childhood on hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction;
To study the consequences of hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction in CCSs on metabolic health, bone health, psychosocial development, sexual performance, and fatigue; and
To perform collaborative (European) studies collecting CCSs.

Thyroid Disorders after Cancer Treatment
Thyroid disorders, such as hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, thyroid nodules, and thyroid cancer, are frequent after treatment for childhood cancer. Primary hypothyroidism is one of the most frequently reported complications after radiotherapy to the neck, especially in Hodgkin’s lymphoma, with a prevalence of up to 50%. Treatment with meta-iodobenzylguanidine for childhood neuroblastoma may result in hypothyroidism, thyroid nodules, and thyroid cancer. Chemotherapy, such as busulphan and cyclophosphamide, has been associated with hypothyroidism. Furthermore, TKIs have been shown to cause hypothyroidism. Hyperthyroidism can present after neck or craniospinal radiotherapy.
Thyroid cancer that occurs as secondary cancer after childhood cancer treatment may require a different approach from primary thyroid cancer, due to previous exposure to toxic treatment, differences in genetic background or the individual experiences of the patient. A shared decision model has been proposed for screening for secondary thyroid cancer.
To study the mechanism of TKI-induced hypothyroidism;
To understand the long-term TKI-induced complications;
To investigate the effect of shared decision making on surveillance for secondary thyroid cancer;
To develop an International Guideline Harmonization Group (IGHG) recommendation for surveillance of thyroid dysfunction in CCSs; and
To update the IGHG recommendation for surveillance of thyroid cancer in CCSs.

Safety of GH Therapy
Concerns have been raised regarding the influence of hormone replacement on cell growth and metabolism in CCSs. However, current consensus guidelines have stated that GH treatment should be considered safe in disease-free CCSs who are GH-deficient. Published clinical guidelines on hormone replacement are not specific to CCSs as they need to take into consideration risks of tumour recurrence and secondary malignancy.
So far, no association has been found between tumour recurrence or secondary neoplasia and GH therapy in CCSs who have GH deficiency. It was agreed by consensus that GH should start after 3 months in children with benign disease such as craniopharyngioma, and after 1 year for treatment after malignant disease.
Research gaps include when to start GH therapy after completion of cancer treatment in children with GH deficiency, which dose of GH to aim for, the effect of long-acting GH on growth and tumour recurrence in CCSs, and GH therapy in children who receive maintenance treatment, such as with a TKI. The beneficial effects of GH therapy in adult CCSs on body composition, bone health, metabolic syndrome, and quality of life remain to be studied.
To determine the time to start GH treatment after or during treatment for childhood cancer in children with chronic low-grade disease.
To study the effect of GH treatment on final height, body composition, and tumour outcome in children with GH deficiency, directly after completion of cancer treatment.
To determine the effect of long-acting GH on growth and tumour recurrence in CCSs.
To perform a randomised controlled trial on the effect of GH therapy on body composition, bone health, metabolic syndrome, and quality of life in adult CCSs.

Bone Health
Low BMD is reported in up to 36% of CCSs, with the highest risk among survivors of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia and tumours of the CNS, and those who have received stem cell transplants. The standardised incidence ratio of any first fracture has been shown to be 3.53 (95% CI, 3.06–4.06) for males and 5.35 (95% CI, 4.46–6.52) for females.
Suggested mechanisms for low BMD include the impact of the cancer diagnosis on the bone (acute lymphoblastic leukaemia), glucocorticoid treatment, chemotherapy (methotrexate), cranial radiotherapy, nutritional status/vitamin D deficiency, lifestyle, and endocrine conditions, such as GH deficiency and hypogonadism. New cancer treatment protocols, including TKIs, may result in deficits in BMD, but more research is needed to improve the understanding of the negative effects of these agents on bone health.
To study the effect of TKIs on bone health;
To determine the effects of treatment with GH, thyroid hormone, and sex steroids on bone health in CCSs;
To examine the indications for and effects of bisphosphonates regarding bone health outcomes in CCSs; and
To discover ways to prevent toxicity of chemotherapy on bone development.

Obesity and Metabolic Syndrome
Overweight, obesity, and metabolic syndrome are prevalent in CCSs. In a recent analysis of the Dutch CCS cohort, a prevalence of overweight of 46.3% was found in men and 44.3% in women (obesity 11.2% and 15.9%, morbid obesity 2.4% and 5.4%).
In particular, CCSs have a significantly higher risk for obesity and metabolic syndrome when compared with siblings and the general population, which is associated with pituitary dysfunction. Survivors with hypothalamic involvement of their tumour are at the highest risk for morbid obesity, due to hypothalamic dysfunction (see the chapter on neuroendocrinology and pituitary diseases).
In addition, survivors of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, treated with prophylactic cranial radiotherapy, are at high risk for obesity. Survivors treated with total body irradiation (TBI) have an increased risk for diabetes mellitus and insulin resistance, with an even higher risk among those treated with cranial radiotherapy in addition to TBI. One suggested explanation is a decreased lean mass and different body fat distribution in CCSs treated with TBI.
To determine the impact on cardiovascular and psychosocial later outcomes of surveillance for and timely referral of obesity in the late effects clinic;
To study the effect of standardised controlled interventions with physical activity and lifestyle factors on obesity in CCSs; and
To improve the management of acquired hypothalamic obesity (described in the chapter on neuroendocrinology and pituitary diseases).

Anticipated Impact of Future Research
Increased knowledge regarding endocrine late complications after childhood cancer, including the development of precision medicine, will allow clinicians to better identify survivors at high risk for late toxicity. This will enhance survivors’ outcomes and minimise unnecessary treatment. We will know more about targeted therapies during childhood and the effect on endocrine dysfunction, including the need for prolonged follow-up.

Oncofertility: Effect of Oncological Treatment on Spermatogenesis and Health of Offspring
Survivors of childhood cancer are at increased risk for gonadal insufficiency with impaired fertility after treatment with radiotherapy and after exposure to alkylating agents which, for both modalities, is dose-dependent. Testicular radiation doses greater than 4 Gy, ovarian radiation doses greater than 5 Gy, hypothalamic-pituitary radiotherapy greater than 22–30 Gy in girls, and TBI in both boys and girls are known risks for premature gonadal failure and infertility.

Epidemiology, Societal Impact, Research State of the Art in Men
Thanks to the availability of highly efficient cancer treatments, the number of young men who recover from cancer is steadily increasing, exceeding 95% for testicular cancer and 80% for childhood cancers and many types of lymphoma. The most frequent cancers in postpubertal subjects are testicular cancer and haematological malignancies.
While fertility preservation based on sperm cryopreservation is highly recommended, in the majority of cases, spermatogenesis recovers after a few months or years from the end of treatment, implying that natural conception after genotoxic treatment is a viable alternative to the use of cryopreserved spermatozoa. Data from the current literature are relatively scarce concerning the effect of these therapies on the integrity of sperm DNA and epigenetic changes; data mainly refer to up to 2 years from the end of therapies. Recently, high sperm DNA fragmentation, persisting even after 3 years from the end of therapies, was reported in both testicular cancer and haematological malignancies. These studies questioned the safety of natural conception after the canonical 24 months of waiting period, underlying the need for personalised preconception counselling. However, it is still unclear if these genetic changes in the spermatozoa of cancer survivors are related to therapy or malignant disease per se.
Epidemiological studies comparing the frequency of malformations in children born to cancer survivors versus cancer-free fathers are scarce. The largest study to date reports a significant 17% increase in major malformations, but the cohort is extremely heterogeneous in terms of type of cancers, treatments, and the time for conception after the oncological therapies.
Apart from the malformation rate, no other health conditions have been studied so far. In this regard, it is relevant to note that epigenetic changes in spermatozoa may lead to diseases which are not always detectable at birth, and they may even lead to a transgenerational effect. Finally, the de novo mutation rate after oncological treatments was addressed only by a single, small study, leaving open the question about the risk estimate for monogenic and complex diseases.

Future Research Priorities
Studies must identify genetic and clinical markers indicative of a higher susceptibility to chemotherapy- and radiotherapy-induced gonadal toxicity (in males and females). There is an urgent need to better understand the genotoxic effects of cancer as a disease, and also of oncological therapies, on sperm DNA, along with their consequences and persistency over time. Objectives include the analysis of large cohorts of subjects prior to and after being treated with different regimens of chemo-/radiotherapies, in order to define (1) the type and entity of the genotoxic effect (sperm DNA fragmentation and DNA methylation) and (2) its persistency over time in function of the cancer disease as well as the treatment type and dosage. Such parameters should be evaluated prior to and after treatment, with follow-up for at least 5 years (or over 5 years if pathological values are detected).
Development of reliable technology to detect the de novo mutation rate in sperm suspension before and after therapy;
Very large epidemiological studies on birth defects in children born to cancer survivors, which are able to provide risk estimates for specific cancer types/treatment groups, rather than providing a general estimate for all cancers; and
Large prospective epidemiological studies focusing not only on malformation rate but also on other health outcomes of children.

A deeper knowledge of the genotoxic effect of oncological treatments on spermatozoa and the development of diagnostic tests would allow informed decision making regarding the timing of conception and the choice between natural versus cryopreserved spermatozoa in the context of assisted reproductive technologies. Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors

Epidemiology, Societal Impact, Research State of the Art
Immune checkpoints are molecules that modulate the duration and amplitude of the physiological immune function, playing important roles in maintaining immune homoeostasis. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are antibodies that target certain immune checkpoints, such as programmed death-1, its ligand, or cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4.
Immune checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as a powerful new tool for various types of cancers, including lymphoma, melanoma, lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and urothelial carcinoma. The indications for ICIs are currently increasing. As immune checkpoints are crucial for immunological self-tolerance, such therapies can trigger autoimmune adverse effects, affecting a number of organs.
Endocrinopathies are among the most common complications, including thyroid dysfunction, hypophysitis and, more rarely, diabetes mellitus, and primary adrenal insufficiency. The time of onset of endocrinopathies varies widely (weeks to months after the initial dose). Furthermore, the severity of endocrinopathies spans a great range and grading systems are used in clinical practice. Combinations of ICIs appear to increase the risk of endocrine complications. It has been hypothesised that autoimmune complications after ICIs may be a positive predictor of immunotherapy response. Therapy with ICIs can be continued in most cases, high-dose glucocorticoids are rarely required for management, while endocrine deficiency usually persists, necessitating lifelong replacement, except for cases with transient thyroiditis.

Future Research Priorities
Immune checkpoint inhibitors have significant endocrine side effects. Physicians treating patients should be aware of these effects since they are relatively frequent, and their management differs from other immune-related adverse events in 3 key ways: (1) ICI therapy can be continued in most cases, (2) high-dose glucocorticoids are rarely required for management, and (3) endocrine deficiency usually persists, necessitating lifelong replacement, except for cases with transient thyroiditis.
Investment into international big data registries is needed. There is a surprising lack of reports on endocrinopathies related to ICI therapy in large patient databases that include data on adequately diagnosed ICI endocrinopathies in patients who are followed up over time.
As the range of ICIs expands, there is a need to explore not only side effects but also the outcome of oncologic and endocrine treatments. It has been hypothesised that autoimmune complications after ICIs may be a positive predictor of the response to immunotherapy.
The grading systems should be evaluated and standardised. ICI therapy can be continued in most cases and high-dose glucocorticoids are rarely required for management.
Exact dosing of hormone replacement and dose adaptation through the lifespan should be studied. Endocrine deficiency usually persists, necessitating lifelong replacement, except for cases with transient thyroiditis.

International efforts through big data registries and collaborative studies will help improve screening, early diagnosis, grading systems and individualised management (in both the short term and the long term) of endocrinopathies related to ICI therapy. Familial Endocrine Cancer Syndromes

Epidemiology, Societal Impact, Research State of the Art

Epidemiology
Familial endocrine cancer syndromes are a heterogeneous group of rare hereditary endocrine disorders, usually transmitted in an autosomal dominant pattern. In each of these syndromes, the germline genetic alteration predisposes carriers to a specific set of endocrine and non-endocrine tumours and conditions. These conditions vary in expressivity and penetrance at the individual level: The mutation does not necessarily lead to manifestations or to the full pattern of manifestations that define the syndrome. The most common of these familial endocrine cancer syndromes are described below.
Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1. Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1, OMIM #131100, ORPHA 625) is caused by germline inactivating variants in the MEN1 tumour suppressor gene. It is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. The syndrome is characterised by 3 major components: parathyroid tumours, duodenopancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (dpNETs) and pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNETs), and minor associations, such as lung neuroendocrine tumours, thymic neuroendocrine tumours, and adrenal tumours. Non-endocrine features include cutaneous lesions and breast cancer. The estimated prevalence is 1/20,000 to 1/40,000. The overall penetrance is 76–99% by age 50, and 99% by age 88 (including primary hyperparathyroidism in >95%, dpNET in 80% and PitNET in 50–80%).
Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 2. Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 2. Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2A, OMIM #171400, ORPHA 247698; MEN2B, OMIM #162300, ORPHA 247709) is due to mutations of the RET proto-oncogene, leading to medullary thyroid carcinoma in approximately 100% of cases, phaeochromocytoma in about 50% and parathyroid tumours in 10–20% (the last only in MEN2A, whereas MEN2B, the more aggressive and rare form, usually due to M918TRET mutations, may also be associated with additional endocrine and non-endocrine features). Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. The estimated prevalence is 1/40,000.
Von Hippel-Lindau Syndrome. Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome (VHL, OMIM #193300, ORPHA 892) results from mutations in the VHL tumour suppressor gene, resulting in an inconsistent association with retinal, brain, and medullary haemangioblastoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours, renal cell carcinoma, and/or phaeochromocytoma. Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. Its prevalence is estimated to be 1/53,000. The penetrance is >80% for at least 1 disease after the age of 60.
Phaeochromocytoma/Paraganglioma Syndrome. Phaeochromocytoma/paraganglioma syndrome (PPGL, OMIM #115310, #168000, #601650, #605373, #614165, ORPHA 29072) is mainly due to mutations in genes encoding succinyl dehydrogenase subunits (SDHx), leading to an increased risk of developing adrenal (also called phaeochromocytomas) or extra-adrenal (autonomic nervous system) paragangliomas. Some rare cases of pituitary tumours have been described in the 3PA syndrome. Phaeochromocytomas are found in 0.1–0.6% of patients with hypertension. The penetrance is variable, depending on the gene.

Societal Impact
Access to comprehensive genetic testing is important, for both diagnosis and management. For example, in MEN2, predictive genetic testing is recommended for first-degree relatives of mutation carriers, often as soon as in early childhood. This allows identification of people who are not at risk for syndrome-related tumours, as well as timely initiation of screening in identified mutation carriers and, in the case of MEN2, determination of the optimal age for thyroidectomy. However, in some countries, the diagnosis of a genetic disorder may have an impact on the ability to obtain health insurance, life insurance or a mortgage, which may lead to reluctance to undergo genetic testing.
Patients with these familial endocrine cancer syndromes can experience a long delay in diagnosis, leading to an additional psychosocial burden as well as increased morbidity and mortality, both in patients and family members. Given the rarity of these disorders, blanket population screening is undesirable (apart from, debatably, in MEN2B). This means that there is a need to identify easy to use, highly specific clues for generalists, to aid early diagnosis.
Patients with familial endocrine cancer syndromes are advised to follow a lifelong, disorder-specific, screening programme, which is aimed at early detection of manifestations to allow timely intervention, hence preventing subsequent morbidity and mortality. While this has improved outcomes, concerns have been raised (for example, in MEN1) about the burden of these screening programmes in terms of radiation exposure, financial aspects, toxicity, psychological burden and the increased detection of small indolent tumours that may or may not become clinically relevant. Thus, there is a need for biomarkers that may allow a more personalised and risk-based approach.
Morbidity and mortality in patients with familial endocrine cancer syndromes are caused by the tumours that arise as part of the syndrome and also by the sequelae of (surgical) treatment, such as hypoparathyroidism and endocrine/exocrine pancreatic insufficiency in MEN1, hypothyroidism in MEN2, adrenal insufficiency in MEN2, and familial PPGL syndrome and dialysis in VHL. There is a need for targeted, non-invasive or minimally invasive early interventions, and more optimal, risk-based timing of interventions.

Future Research Priorities

International Epidemiological Registries Coupled with Biobanking
It is important to define the modifying factors of tumourigenesis, to look at the genomic and epigenomic background beyond the gene of interest, in particular trying to define some protective or exacerbating genetic or epigenetic factors. This would require whole genome and omics approaches, for example comparing patients from the same family but with different disease profiles, or specifically evaluating patients with the more aggressive forms of endocrine cancer syndromes. Although epigenetic/environmental triggers may be involved in accelerating tumourigenesis in endocrine cancer syndromes, no specific large-scale studies have been conducted to identify them.
There is a need to improve early diagnosis of rare diseases for early care and to reduce delays in diagnosis. Some rare diseases may start with non-endocrine or non-specific signs. Research should focus on identifying these early, specific signs, in order to improve the education of non-endocrine specialists to help them recognise the early signs of each disease. For example, MEN2B, the most aggressive and rarest form of MEN2, can lead to medullary thyroid cancer early in life. However, clinical signs of this cancer will be detectable at a stage when the disease is already aggressive. Some authors have shown that severe gastrointestinal symptoms should be a warning signal that allows early detection of MEN2B.
Optimisation of genetic screening programmes is required, based on large studies with high levels of evidence to improve the cost-effectiveness of genetic testing in patients with suspected hereditary disease. This work must include the future neonatal genomic screening programmes.
There is a need to optimise screening protocols and develop shared decision-making tools for the detection and management of disease manifestations. For example, in MEN1, this would help define the optimal strategy for mild hypercalcaemia, or for small non-secreting neuroendocrine tumours.
Identification of novel approaches for individual risk prediction will enable personalised screening, monitoring, and timing of intervention. These may include blood-based biomarkers, functional imaging, dynamic prediction models based on clinical data or models based on artificial intelligence. For example, there is a variable penetrance among patients with SDHx mutations. However, all patients are followed in the same way as there is no way to determine which patients are at a low or high risk of developing PPGL.
Phenotype-genotype correlations should be used to optimise and personalise treatment. Although thousands of mutations have been identified worldwide in all of these endocrine cancer syndromes, genotype-phenotype correlations have not yet been identified in all of them because of the lack of large enough registries, or the lack of harmonisation of epidemiological, genetic, and clinical data to draw firm conclusions about a type of mutation or set of mutations in a given syndrome. For example, while VHL missense mutations appear to confer an increased risk of phaeochromocytoma compared with truncating variants or gene deletions, the follow-up of phaeochromocytoma is the same for every patient with VHL, regardless of the mutation, but may differ between the guidelines of different societies.
It is important to characterise the psychosocial consequences of lifelong surveillance and survivorship care. Endocrine cancer syndromes are lifelong conditions that require regular follow-up, even after successful treatment of the tumour. This has psychosocial consequences for patients and families, and appropriate support cannot be provided by physicians alone. Patient advocacy groups are a cornerstone in the management of these patients. Psychosocial research is needed to determine the impact on the quality of life of patients and their carers/families, to improve the long-term consequences of these endocrine cancer syndromes. Furthermore, issues regarding counselling about advanced family planning deserve attention.

Preclinical Models of Tumourigenesis
Identification of new specific therapeutic and theranostic targets, focusing on the gene or specific signalling pathways, will improve outcomes in patients with unresectable and/or metastatic tumours. The recent clinical results with RET-targeting TKIs in patients with RET-positive medullary thyroid cancer, or with belzutifan, an inhibitor of the HIF-2α pathway (HIF is involved in the VHL oxygen-sensing pathway), show that there is room for further therapeutic research focusing on the direct effects of mutations on the targets (as opposed to non-specific treatments aimed at destroying metastases).
Identification of new preventative and therapeutic strategies will allow early targeted interventions before tumours reach an advanced stage. In these familial endocrine cancer syndromes where we are now screening patients prospectively and identifying associated tumours early, there is a unique window of opportunity for cancer interception that, if exploited, could improve prognosis immensely.
Elucidation of tissue-restricted tumour development and progression.
Enabling high-throughput pharmacological screening.
Testing combined specific and non-specific treatments that could dramatically change the prognosis of endocrine cancer syndromes.
Further goals can be met by developing advanced preclinical models that recapitulate tumourigenesis in the human context.

Anticipated Impact of Future Research
Coordinating national and international efforts through registries and collaborative preclinical studies will help improve the early diagnosis, management, and monitoring of these rare diseases, through the prism of an individualised approach. This will also reduce the burden of this group of syndromes, in terms of cost to patients, by avoiding long-term treatments or sequelae, and society.
