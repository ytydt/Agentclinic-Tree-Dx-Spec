Engineering non-coding DNA Elements in Rice: an Elegant Approach To fine-tune Agronomical Advantageous Traits
Rice serves as a fundamental staple crop, supporting the dietary needs of nearly one-third of the global population. This critical role necessitates immediate and strategic efforts to develop multi-attribute genotypes to ensure sustainable food and nutritional security for the burgeoning population. The augmentation of rice production is not only vital for addressing immediate food demands but also crucial for fostering long-term sustainability, thereby supporting livelihoods and driving economic development. Achieving this transformation necessitates a holistic and systems-level understanding of the molecular and regulatory networks that govern phenotypic plasticity and agronomic performance. While coding regions are pivotal for expression, non-coding elements play an even more prominent role in regulating transcriptional activity and orchestrating essential biological processes. Natural allelic variation within these non-coding elements serves as an evolutionary substrate for regulatory rewiring, contributing to adaptive plasticity, domestication traits, and intraspecific diversification. Therefore, the precise modulation of desirable agronomic traits could be facilitated by targeted engineering of such elements, which often allows for the fine-tuning of allelic effects in terms of the attenuation and partial restoration of alleles to impact desirable traits over coding components, which often results in complete exclusion or lethality. Therefore, we attempted to provide a comprehensive synthesis of functionally characterized non-coding elements exclusively for rice, highlight their functional roles, and emphasize how natural variation within these elements is critical for selecting traits associated with domestication and the breeding of elite genotypes. Notably, the potential of engineered non-coding RNA elements for the enhancement of agronomically advantageous traits is critically discussed. The future roadmap of non-coding element editing in rice is expected to be significantly shaped by continuous technological innovations in the editing toolbox, coupled with breakthrough discoveries for non-coding elements influencing agronomical traits. These advances have the potential to revolutionize the development of superior rice genotypes, ultimately contributing to the global effort to ensure food and nutritional security.

Introduction
Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is one of the most widely consumed staple crops, providing food and nutritional security for approximately one-third of the global population (Rezvi et al.). It plays a pivotal role in ensuring global food security, economic stability, and cultural heritage, serving as a major source of macro- and micronutrients, vitamins, and minerals (Birla et al.). However, increasingly erratic climatic conditions and the progressive depletion of natural resources have resulted in plateauing or even declining rice yields, which are insufficient to meet the growing demand (Chandra et al.; Rezvi et al.). Consequently, addressing the challenge of feeding and nourishing the expanding global population requires urgent and strategic interventions to ensure sustained and resilient rice productivity. To meet these demands, the development of high-yielding and climate-resilient genotypes with diverse beneficial traits is imperative. Owing to its small genome and status as a model crop, rice is an ideal system for advanced genetic engineering, enabling the creation of elite genotypes with enhanced agronomic and nutritional traits (Duy et al.; Saeed et al.). As a result, these genetically improved genotypes are better equipped to cope with climatic variability and pathogen-associated stresses, and to ensure increasing demands for food and nutritional security (Saeed et al.). The Green Revolution, which integrated traditional breeding methods with breeding-assisted and technological innovations, led to significant increases in rice yields during the latter half of the late 20th century, thereby fostering the adoption of molecular breeding strategies techniques in rice farming and improvements (Chen et al.). Recently, the advent of the CRISPR/Cas genome-editing system, a groundbreaking molecular tool, has revolutionized rice research by dramatically expanding opportunities for improving both rice cultivation and commercialization (Tabassum et al.). CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) and Cas (CRISPR-associated) proteins represent repetitive genomic elements found in prokaryotes, which evolved as part of an adaptive immune system that enables bacteria to defend against viral invasions (Ferreira and Reis). This technology is now extensively employed in rice genome editing, facilitating high-precision breeding for a wide range of applications (Tabassum et al.; Tang and Zhang). While the integration of multi-omics data is essential for understanding the functional networks underlying agronomic traits, non-coding regulatory elements also play a critical role in fine-tuning traits of agricultural significance (Tang and Zhang; Bravo-Vázquez et al.). Although conventional gene editing targeting coding elements dissects numerous advantageous agronomical and complex traits in rice (Mishra et al.; Tabassum et al.), alternatively, the precise manipulation of non-coding regulatory regions holds great potential for creating novel alleles of key genes, thereby advancing elite rice breeding programs. Significant efforts have been made to understand the role of non-coding DNA elements, such as cis-regulatory elements, in plant development and adaptation (Marand et al.; Shi et al.), moreover, emerging recent advances highlighted their persistence in natural variation-mediated transcriptional regulation in crops (Saeed et al.; Cui et al.; Chen et al.). Accordingly, the restoration and fine-tuning of allelic effects in rice have been successfully demonstrated through advances in promoter editing for precise transcriptional regulation (Tang and Zhang), targeted manipulation of diverse non-coding RNAs (Deng et al.; Vázquez et al., 2024), and the strategic engineering of upstream regulatory elements to facilitate gene silencing mechanisms (Li and Wei, 2025). Owing to their critical involvement in rice growth and development, it is worth discussing the role of functionally characterized non-coding regulatory element editing in rice agronomical advantageous trait improvement. Therefore, we emphasize and discuss the pivotal role of natural allelic variation in non-coding elements for rice adaptation and demonstrate how their targeted engineering offers a powerful route to achieving sustained genetic gain. We also critically discuss effective developed editing technologies, existing bottlenecks, and potential solutions for rice improvement through non–coding regulatory element engineering for sustainability. In conclusion, we assert that allelic variation in non-coding regulatory DNA elements is crucial for the adaptive agronomic traits, and harnessing them by utilizing cutting-edge editing technologies could enable the creation of targeted alleles of key genes for elite breeding programs.

Non-coding Elements in Rice: Functional Classification and Regulation
In plant genomes, approximately 1% of DNA encodes proteins, while the remaining non-coding regions were historically regarded as functionally irrelevant. This perception led to the limited functional investigations compared to coding sequences (Chen et al.), However, over the past decade, advancements in RNA sequencing technologies and sophisticated computational methods have facilitated the rapid identification and characterization of functional non-coding elements, thereby revealing their crucial role in regulating plant growth and development (Cui et al.; Chen et al.). Non-coding DNA elements are classified into two major categories (Fig. 1). These categories are primarily distinguished by their positional relationship to coding regions, either upstream or downstream, and their functional significance. The first group includes elements which contribute to the regulation of gene expression, such as promoters, 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs), upstream open reading frames (uORFs), introns, enhancers, insulators, transposons, retrotransposons, and silencers. The second group consists of genetic elements that are transcribed into RNAs including transfer RNAs (tRNAs), ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and microRNAs (miRNAs), which primarily considered as functional RNA molecules and are generally not translated into proteins (Cui et al.; Chen et al.; Marand et al.). However, emerging evidence suggests that certain lncRNAs, circRNAs, and even some miRNAs can give rise to short regulatory peptides under specific conditions (Bhar and Roy). Promoters play a pivotal role in determining the spatial and temporal expression patterns of target genes, thereby directly influencing phenotypic traits in rice (Wu et al.). The 5’ and 3’ UTRs are key regulatory sequences that modulate gene expression through the formation of complex post-transcriptional regulatory networks (Lu et al.; Park et al.). uORFs function as essential cis-regulatory elements that fine-tune gene expression in a graded manner in response to diverse physiological and environmental cues (Liu et al.; Xue et al.). Introns play a specialized role in enhancing the adaptability, functional complexity, and preferential gene expression of the plant genome (Jeon et al.; Fiume et al.). Transposons and retrotransposons influence gene expression, alternative splicing, and epigenetic memory at insertion or excision sites, with the capacity to modulate the graded expression of adjacent genes, thereby contributing to the adaptation (Saika et al.; Luo et al.). Other critical regulatory elements, including enhancers, insulators, and silencers, have not been extensively characterized in rice; however, their coordinated regulatory functions in plant systems have been documented (Bilas et al., 2016). A few well-characterized, transcribed but not translated elements include tRNAs, which are essential for protein synthesis; rRNAs, which function as structural and catalytic functional components of ribosomes; lncRNAs, which regulate gene expression by interacting with chromatin, transcription factors, or other RNA molecules; circRNAs, which participate in complex regulatory networks by acting as sponges; and siRNAs and miRNAs, which mediate post-transcriptional regulation and fine-tune gene expression across a broad range of biological processes (Zhou et al.; Zheng et al.; Chao et al.; Chandra et al.). Decisively, both classes of regulatory elements, transcribed and non-transcribed, contribute to the fine-tuning of gene expression in response to internal developmental cues and external environmental stimuli. Therefore, elucidating the advanced molecular mechanisms underlying their function is essential to enhancing rice breeding and crop improvement strategies.

Why non-coding Elements: Drives Trait-specific Natural Variation
Genetic variation is a fundamental driver of sustainability in agriculture, and nature provides a complex regulatory framework that governs these variations (Duan et al.; Cui et al.). One of the longstanding questions in crop breeding is how genetic diversity translates into phenotypic variation. Current research indicates that the remarkable diversity observed in plant phenotypes is largely attributable to the regulation of gene expression, rather than direct changes in gene products. This supports the hypothesis that the mutations in the regulatory elements have played a pivotal role in crop domestication, with agronomic traits selected over generations (Cui et al.). Recent genomic studies have revealed that many trait-related changes in cultivated crops are predominantly located in the promoter regions, in contrast to their wild relatives (Wu et al.). These differences often arise from subtle modifications in gene expression primarily mediated by non-coding regulatory elements. The growing focus on such elements stems from the advantages of weak regulatory mutations over null mutations. While null mutations, such as complete gene knockouts or overexpression, often lead to severe or abnormal phenotypes due to disrupted protein levels, weak mutations tend to fine-tune gene expression more precisely, enabling desirable trait modifications without compromising overall plant viability (Wang et al.; Li et al.; Wu et al.). Variations in gene promoters play a crucial role in the evolution of key adaptive traits in rice (Wu et al.). A single nucleotide change in promoter regions during the domestication process can have significant advantages, contributing to the development of cereal crops suited for human consumption. Seed-related traits, such as seed size, germination rate, and vigor, are closely linked to crop yield and grain quality. As such, these traits were frequently selected during rice domestication, driving improvements in both the quantity and quality of gross produce. For example, a single-nucleotide polymorphism in the 5′ regulatory region of the qSH1 gene has been shown to reduce seed shattering by preventing the formation of the abscission layer (Konishi et al.). Additionally, CRISPR-Cas9-based mutagenesis of non-coding sequences offers a powerful tool for generating native overexpression alleles, leading to increased RNA and protein abundance, which can accelerate agronomic improvements (Patel-Tupper et al., 2024). These findings suggest that the integration of advanced gene-editing technologies with naturally occurring genetic variation can effectively fine-tune desirable phenotypic traits, thereby optimizing rice performance in a more targeted and efficient manner. This integrated approach highlights new insights into the role of regulatory element mutations and their restoration, providing a deeper understanding of rice evolution and domestication. It offers potential for more precise and controlled breeding strategies that enhance beneficial traits without introducing pleiotropic effects.

Natural Allelic Variation Within Non-coding Elements: Determining Growth and Development
Understanding the regulatory functions of non-coding elements unlocks exciting new opportunities to accelerate rice improvement with impactful discoveries. Comprehensive examples of natural allelic variation influencing phenotypic traits in rice are provided in Table 1. The grain size and yield are governed by a major trait locus known as GW6, which is regulated via the gibberellin signaling pathway. Remarkably, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockouts of GW6 cause dramatic reductions in plant height, grain length, and grain width, yet intriguingly do not affect the number of grains per panicle (Shi et al.). Another key locus influencing rice grain quality arises from allelic variation in the promoter region of GW7, a gene encoding a TONNEAU-recruiting motif protein. Upregulation of GW7 expression is closely linked to the formation of slender grains, a desirable trait in many rice varieties (Wang et al.). Further genetic analysis revealed that Gn1a, a gene regulating spikelet number and thus grain yield, exhibits limited sequence variation among cultivated rice but harbors extensive nucleotide diversity in wild rice populations, highlighting untapped potential for breeding (Wang et al.). Additionally, natural polymorphisms in the promoter of the Rice Flowering LOCUS T1 (RFT1) gene significantly influence flowering time variation, contributing to the adaptive divergence of rice populations (Ogiso-Tanaka et al.). Promoter variations in the Stay-Green (SGR) gene, which encodes Mg²⁺-dechelatase, drive earlier and more intense senescence in indica rice cultivars, affecting grain filling and yield (Shin et al.). The black rice trait is a result of the ectopic expression of Kala4, a bHLH transcription factor gene, caused by rearrangements in its promoter region. This alteration regulates flavonol biosynthesis and downstream genes responsible for pigment production (Oikawa et al.). GSE5 controls grain size by modulating spikelet hull cell proliferation, and natural variations in its promoter region contribute significantly to the observed diversity in grain size among rice varieties (Duan et al.). Investigations revealed that 18% of indica and 48% of aus accessions harbor small insertions in the promoter of ARE1, which reduce its expression and consequently enhance grain yield under nitrogen-limiting conditions, underscoring the challenges of improving nitrogen utilization efficiency (Wang et al.). The SNB gene, located within the qGW7 locus, contains an insertion in its promoter region that acts as a negative regulator of grain size, and knockout mutants exhibiting this promoter variation show increased grain length, width, and weight, while overexpression leads to the opposite phenotype (Ma et al.). Rice roots can absorb aspartate at soil-relevant concentrations, with the japonica subspecies demonstrating approximately 1.5-fold higher uptake efficiency compared to indica. This difference is closely linked to single-nucleotide polymorphisms in rice amino acid transporter genes, observed across accessions from both japonica and indica backgrounds (Guo et al.).
A new quantitative trait locus for grain width and weight is associated with a single nucleotide polymorphism located upstream of the TGW2 gene, which encodes CELL NUMBER REGULATOR 1 (CNR) and regulates cell proliferation and expansion in glumes (Ruan et al.). The WOX11 genomic sequence reveals the highest level of genetic diversity within its promoter region, which harbors a non-canonical miniature inverted-repeat transposable element (ncMITE) sequences, which plays a crucial role in tissue and organ formation in multicellular organisms (Zhang et al.). The DEP1 locus, which governs erect panicles, is widely utilized in rice breeding, and a single nucleotide polymorphism in the promoter region causes a shift in the core sequence, affecting both primary and secondary branches, as well as the number of grains per panicle (Zhao et al.). Sucrose synthase 3 (Susy 3) is a critical enzyme involved in starch accumulation and shows multiple sequence variants, including single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and insertion/deletions (indel), across promoters, exons, introns, and the 3′ downstream non-transcribed region. These changes particularly affect seed-specific expression by altering promoter activity (Lestari et al.). The number of secondary branches per panicle, a key determinant of grain number, is influenced by natural variation in the distal promoter region, which directly impacts yield (Wang et al.). Nitrogen fertilizers are commonly used to enhance productivity, but they pose significant environmental and health impacts. Natural variation in an NPF6.1-driven promoter element boosts nitrogen use efficiency, offering a sustainable solution that reduces fertilizer dependency (Tang et al.). Additionally, a major sugar-responsive sequence (SRS) located upstream of the transcription start site represses the expression of α-amylase genes in rice suspension cells and germinating embryos through transcriptional regulation (Lu et al.). Promoter region sequence variation in GNP1, encoding GA20ox1, leads to significant increases in grain number and overall yield in near-isogenic lines (NILs) compared to controls (Wu et al.; Wang et al.). SNPs and Indels in the Hd8 promoter regulate heading date under long-day conditions by modulating expression of the gene encoding the HAP3 subunit of the CCAAT-box-binding transcription factor (Yuan et al.).
α-Tocopherol, a potent antioxidant with well-documented health benefits, has been linked to nucleotide sequence variations in the promoter region of the TMT gene, which are significantly correlated with differences in α-tocopherol content across diverse rice accessions (Wang et al.). Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have uncovered the genetic basis of ionomic variation in rice, identifying significant SNPs predominantly located within the promoter region of MOT1;1, especially in indica subpopulations (Yang et al.). Amino acid transporters are essential for plant growth and nutrient distribution, and sequence variations in the promoter and UTR of AAP3, AAP4, AAP5, and AAP6 between japonica and indica rice varieties contribute to phenotypic differences, particularly in tiller number and grain yield, by regulating amino acid allocation (Peng et al.; Lu et al.; Wang et al.; Fang et al.). ACBP2 is specifically expressed in the embryos and endosperm of germinating seeds, and deletion of its 5′-flanking region leads to a marked reduction in expression, highlighting key regulatory mechanisms governing its seed-specific activity (Guo et al.). Yield potential, plant height, and heading date are pivotal agronomic traits influencing rice productivity, and the quantitative trait locus Ghd7, identified in elite hybrid cultivars, exerts a significant impact on these agronomically advantageous traits. A functional C-to-T mutation in its promoter region has been shown to modulate gene expression, thereby influencing plant height (Xue et al.; Lu et al.). Precise editing of promoter and distal regulatory regions in pleiotropic genes such as Ghd7 and PRR37 has been shown to reduce their repressive effects on heading date, thereby enhancing rice yield (Guo et al.). The quantitative trait locus Rice Big Grain 1 (qRBG1), identified from a single-segment substitution line, involves natural variation in its promoter region, which contributes to increased cell number and size, ultimately resulting in larger grains (Zhang et al.).

Nucleotide variations in the promoter region of the transcription factor Grain number, plant height, and heading date 8 (Ghd8) are involved in regulating key agronomic traits in rice (Wang et al.). The rice mutant GRA117 exhibits seedling albinism and delayed chloroplast development, and an insertion within such a promoter reduces their transcriptional activity, impairing carbon assimilation and resulting in abnormal phenotypes (Wang et al.). Grain size 5 (GS5) regulates grain size by modulating grain width, rate of filling, and weight by encoding a serine carboxypeptidase, which functions as a positive regulator of grain development. Two SNPs in the upstream region of this gene, which distinguish between the wide-grain and narrow-grain alleles, have been identified as a molecular basis for differential expression in developing young panicles (Li et al.; Xu et al.). Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) are essential amino acids that play a pivotal role in rice growth and development, and subsequent variations in these amino concentrations are linked to differential transcriptional regulation of the transcription factor bZIP18, driven by promoter region polymorphisms (Sun et al.). A high degree of polymorphism in the promoter of the heading date protein gene influences flowering time variation and diversity, offering insights into facultative short-day photoperiodic responses (Takahashi et al.). Tiller Inclined Growth 1 (TIG1) positively regulates the expression of Expansin A (EXPA) and Small Auxin-Up RNA (SAUR) genes, thereby promoting cell elongation. Promoter variation in TIG1 among indica rice cultivars has led to reduced expression, shorter cell length, and altered tiller angles, contributing to the evolutionary shift from prostrate tillers in wild rice to erect tillers in domesticated varieties (Zhang et al.).
Natural variations in the MIR528 promoter region contribute to differences in miR528 expression among rice varieties, which are linked to differential binding affinities with the transcription factor SQUAMOSA Promoter Binding Protein-Like 9 (SPL9) (Yang et al.). Allelic variation in the promoter region and a non-synonymous substitution (Ser33Leu) in the CYP71P6 gene may influence traits associated with rice yield potential (Sahoo et al.). The dominant mutant Big Grain2 (bg2), derived from an enhancer-trap population, encodes for cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP78A13, and Indels within its promoter and SNPs in its coding region are responsible for variation in grain yield (Xu et al.). Grain Width 8 (GW8), a quantitative trait locus associated with cell proliferation, exhibits a strong correlation with grain size. Allelic differences in its promoter sequence represent a valuable resource for marker-assisted selection in rice breeding programs (Wang et al.). Indels in both the promoter and coding regions of the SLG7 gene regulate grain shape by encoding a LONGIFOLIA-like protein, which promotes organ elongation similar to LONGIFOLIA 1 and 2 in Arabidopsis, thereby facilitating the formation of desirable grain morphology (Zhou et al.). The dominant dwarf mutant Elongated uppermost internode1 (Eui1) was identified through the deletion of a cis-regulatory silencing element containing an RY motif, which fine-tunes its expression and maintains endogenous gibberellin (GA) homeostasis critical for plant height regulation (Xie et al.). Pollen development and fertility are critical for reproductive success, and a 31-bp Indel in the promoter of Sucrose Transporter 3 (SUT3) was found to mediate its preferential expression, potentially playing an essential role in pollen development within rice panicles (Li et al.). The impact of 5′-UTRs on the expression of seed storage protein genes was examined in transgenic rice lines, and all resulting tested 5′-UTRs significantly enhanced promoter activity without altering spatial or temporal expression patterns (Yang et al.; Liu et al.; Li et al.).
The MT2b gene is highly expressed in germinated rice embryos during seed germination and seedling development signifying critical cis-regulatory elements were identified within its promoter elements. The 5′-UTR is indispensable for achieving high-level promoter activity in transgenic rice embryos (Wu et al.). Meiosis Arrested at Leptotene2 (MEL2), a regulatory gene essential for the proper timing of meiotic entry in rice, mediates translational regulation of key meiotic genes through preferential recognition of U-rich RNA consensus sequence within their 3′-UTRs (Miyazaki et al.). Dissection of the Mac1 mRNA 5′-UTR revealed specific regulatory elements responsible for its efficient translation, underscoring the crucial role of specified regions in modulating post-transcriptional gene expression (Mutsuro-Aoki et al.). Translational repression is regulated by a stowaway-like MITE embedded within the 3′-UTR of Ghd2, a gene that controls various agronomic traits such as grain number, plant height, and heading date (Shen et al.). A rice genomic clone, TubA1, encoding α-tubulin, has been isolated, demonstrating that intron 1 is essential for robust gene expression in actively dividing tissues (Jeon et al.).

Underlying Natural Variations Governing Abiotic Constrains
Abiotic constraints, such as temperature extremes, water stress, soil salinity, and nutrient deficiencies, pose significant limitations to rice production by adversely affecting growth, development, and yield through the disruption of physiological processes, reduction of photosynthetic efficiency, and impairment of nutrients uptake (Chandra et al.; Cui et al.). Given the extensive natural genetic variation in coding and non-coding elements in rice, targeted induction using advanced genome editing techniques to restore desirable traits represents a promising strategy to mitigate climate change impacts by enhancing regional adaptation (Hu et al.). The ZIP23 promoter displayed diversity in stress-responsive cis-regulatory elements, and a 35-nucleotide deletion in the IR20 accession has been associated with enhanced drought tolerance and increased grain yield (Dey et al.). In addition to essential nutrients, unlikely, rice is a significant source of toxic elements such as cadmium for a considerable population in Asia, with indica rice accumulating more cadmium in shoots and grains than japonica. This variation in cadmium accumulation is attributed to natural variation in the HMA3 promoter region, which is critical for allele-specific expression differences (Liu et al.). Low-temperature germination is critical for rice, and analysis of natural variation revealed a transposon insertion in the UBC12 promoter that negatively regulates germination via multiple downstream signaling pathways (Zhang et al., 2023). A polymorphism in the CTB4a promoter region has been identified, which may facilitate rice cultivation in cold-adapted environments to help meet increasing global demand by crop diversification towards colder climates (Zhang et al.). A functional nucleotide polymorphism in the HAN1 promoter increases the presence of a putative MYB cis-element in the temperate japonica rice allele, enhancing chilling tolerance and supporting local environmental adaptation (Mao et al.). STL1, a candidate gene for salt tolerance in rice, contains a non-synonymous SNP in its coding region and three SNPs in its promoter region, which collectively contribute to improved salt tolerance (Yuan et al.). Rice ecotypes, such as upland and lowland varieties, exhibit differing levels of flooding tolerance, potentially due to sequence divergence in the CBL10 promoter between cultivars (Ye et al.). A set of significant SNPs are found in the promoter region of GNA1, which is crucial for regulating plant height and root length under drought conditions (Ma et al.). LG3, a gene associated with osmotic stress tolerance, is more commonly found in upland genotypes than in lowland, with natural variation in its promoter region is linked to enhanced osmotic stress tolerance during rice seed germination (Xiong et al.). SLG1, which encodes the cytosolic tRNA 2-thiolation protein, is crucial for rice’s response to high-temperature stress, with variations in both the promoter and coding regions differentiating indica and japonica subspecies, contributing to increased thermotolerance in indica varieties (Xu et al.). A C-to-T single-nucleotide variation in the promoter of the DROUGHT1 gene, which encodes a COBRA-like protein, improves drought resistance in rice by altering cell wall architecture, increasing cellulose content, maintaining crystallinity, and enhancing gene expression in upland rice (Xu et al.). Natural variation in the promoter and coding regions of the rice aluminum transporter gene (NRAT1), which encodes a root plasma membrane aluminum uptake transporter, confers enhanced aluminum tolerance in Arabidopsis, even in the absence of a native homolog (Li et al.). Expression of PTB1, which influences the setting rate of rice panicles, is modulated by promoter haplotypes and environmental temperature (Li et al.). To investigate the role of UTRs in salt stress response, two transgenic events of the NHX1 gene were developed: one incorporating the 5′ UTR and coding sequence (CDS), and the other including the 5′ UTR, CDS, and 3′ UTR. The resulting former set of transgenic lines displayed superior salt tolerance compared to the latter (Amin et al.). Functional characterization of the PHD13 and PHD52 promoters, which contain DRE/CRT cis-regulatory elements, revealed significant upregulation under low-temperature stress, offering a theoretical framework for improving cold tolerance (Wang et al.). Further, exploring and dissecting novel genetic variations in non-coding elements via advanced engineering tools could furnish significant insights into local climate adaptation for sustainable rice production.

Natural Variations Underpinning Responses To Biotic Stressors
Addressing biotic stresses is essential for enhancing rice productivity and overall plant performance, as these factors directly influence plant health and net yield (Chandra et al.). Rice disease resistance has been linked to promoter mutations in a key gene involved in pollen development, despite the distinct molecular mechanisms that underlie disease resistance and sexual reproduction (Chu et al.). Xanthomonas oryzae, a major pathogen affecting rice production in the Asian subcontinent, exhibits increased virulence due to natural variations in the promoters of SWEET13 and SWEET14, which modulate host susceptibility and broaden the resistance spectrum (Zaka et al.). Significant variations in jasmonate levels regulating leaf senescence were observed among rice accessions, with genomic polymorphisms in the promoter and ORFs of the candidate genes PME1 and TSD2 being strongly associated with these variations (Fang et al.). LOC_Os11g46250, a protein-coding gene of unknown function, has been proposed as a candidate for Xa22, with significant SNPs clustered in both its promoter and coding regions, demonstrating strong correlations with resistance to multiple Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) races (Zhang et al.). Allelic variants driven by promoter-region SNPs enhance binding to MYB transcription factors, thereby suppressing bsr-d1 expression, which leads to reduced resistance to rice blast by increased peroxidase activity (Li et al.). Additionally, regulatory changes within these elements may also indirectly modulate salicylic acid biosynthesis, metabolism, and signal transduction pathways downstream of H₂O₂-mediated signaling in the bsr-d1-dependent immune response (Li et al.; Zhu et al.). The selection of polyadenylation sites (PAS) in the rice blast fungus is crucial for pathogenicity regulation and rice colonization. This process increases the utilization of distally located pathogenicity-related genes, particularly those involved in signaling pathways such as 14-3-3B, which requires an extended 3′-UTR for effective infection establishment (Rodríguez-Romero et al., 2019). The bacterial avirulence protein AvrXa23 activates the executor R gene Xa23 by binding to a 28-bp DNA element in its promoter region. PCR amplification and sequencing of promoter fragments from diverse germplasm accessions identified nine SNPs in the Xa23/xa23 promoter alleles (Cui et al.). This expanding knowledge base supports the development of precision breeding strategies, enabling targeted enhancement of disease resistance in rice without compromising agronomically important traits.

Natural Variations Regulating Quality Traits
Besides being one of the most consumed staple foods, rice has the potential to provide a sustainable and affordable solution for nutrition security. Addressing grain quality traits is crucial for optimizing rice performance and enhancing its market potential, as attributes such as texture, nutritional content, and appearance directly influence consumer preferences and market demand (Wu et al.). Novel cis-acting regulatory elements identified in wild Oryza species have been shown to enhance rice bran quality by downregulating the expression of the phospholipase D alpha1 enzyme (Kaur et al.). The nutritional quality of rice is largely influenced by grain protein content (GPC), with an SNP in the GluA2 promoter region linked to variations in transcript expression and GPC diversity (Yang et al.). The 3′-UTR of the rice α-amylase gene is a key regulator of transcript abundance in response to sugar-dependent accumulation, providing insights into the sugar repression mechanism in eukaryotes (Chan and Yu). Grain chalkiness, a highly undesirable trait affecting rice marketability, is regulated by the quantitative trait locus (QTL) Chalk5, which significantly impacts head rice yield and other quality attributes. Two conserved nucleotide polymorphisms in the Chalk5 promoter across different rice varieties may account for variations in mRNA expression, contributing to phenotypic differences in grain chalkiness (Li et al.). In contrast, a functional A/G polymorphism in the promoter region of the WHITE-CORE RATE 1 (WCR1) gene leads to allelic diversity that modulates chalkiness levels and enhances grain quality (Wu et al.). A comprehensive understanding of endosperm starch biosynthesis regulation is fundamental for improving rice digestibility and grain quality. Sequence variants within the promoter region of granule-bound starch synthase I (GBSSI) have been identified, forming haplotypes that differentiate samples based on their amylose content phenotypes (Butardo et al., 2017). These promoter variants offer a strategic opportunity for the targeted engineering of regulatory elements to enhance rice quality traits without modifying core genomic regions, thereby boosting market value and consumer satisfaction.

Non-coding Elements Editing: Advance Tools and Technologies
Several prominent genome editing technologies, including CRISPR-Cas9, transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), and zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs), have been developed for crop improvement. Each of these methods offers distinct advantages and inherent limitations when applied to genome editing in crops (Ali et al.). To overcome the limitations of these existing methodologies, several breakthrough modifications and tools have been developed to achieve more efficient and effective protocols in rice. Although the core genome editing tools are made of basal components, factors like codon usage, kinds of promoters, vectors, and delivery systems are often optimized for specific crops like rice to improve editing efficiency and specificity and are widely applicable to make them feasible for individual crops. For instance, targeting TA-rich sequences in core promoters can be challenging for conventional Cas9 variants. To address this, a unique FrCas9 system derived from Faecalibaculum rodentium has been developed for rice genome editing. This system demonstrated activity against NNTA PAMs, thus overcoming challenges in targeting TA-rich sequences of core promoters (Wang et al.). A nuclease-mediated tiling deletion (NTD) method was applied to the non-coding regions of the Green Revolution gene SD1, efficiently inducing numerous nucleotide deletions and generating six mutants with quantitative variations in plant height (Ma et al.). Although Cas9 nuclease is effective for knocking out miRNA genes, it often introduces small insertions and deletions, making it unsuitable for complete disruption of these targets. To address this issue, Cas12a nuclease was developed, generating larger deletions that disrupt the secondary structure of pre-miRNAs and prevent the production of mature miRNAs (Zheng et al.). This approach has proven to be more effective than Cas9 in generating knockout mutants of miRNA genes (Zheng et al., 2020). A recent advancement in rice-specific editing has developed a newly developed CRISPR/Cas13b-mediated system to target endogenous transcripts, effectively inhibiting multiple mRNAs and lncRNAs with over 50% inhibition in stable transgenic lines (Shuman et al.). A more recently developed MiRKD toolbox provides a genome-editing strategy that uses endogenous miRNAs to regulate gene expression in crops (Liu et al.). It has successfully achieved conditional knockdown of a target gene in rice, demonstrating its potential for controlling gene expression based on specific tissue type and environmental conditions. Despite these advancements, current tools and technologies do not fully meet the needs for efficient engineering of non-coding elements in crops such as rice. Future investigations should focus on developing specific, efficient editing tools for other non-coding elements to enable more effective and precise applications for translational research.

Applications of Non-coding Elements Engineering
CRISPR, a prokaryotic adaptive immune system, functions as a defense mechanism evolved by bacteria to recognize and degrade viral genetic material. This system has been repurposed into a powerful genome editing tool, with its efficiency demonstrated across various agronomically advantageous traits in various crops through modulation of multiple regulatory pathways and mechanisms (Cui et al.). The CRISPR/Cas9 system has emerged as a cornerstone of precision breeding, enabling rapid and targeted improvement of agronomic traits. Numerous elite genes associated with rice yield and stress tolerance have been successfully edited using this platform (Shaheen et al.). A comprehensive tabulation of functionally dissected non-coding elements in rice, achieved through advanced genome editing technologies, is presented in Table 2. Before CRISPR’s emergence, researchers relied on natural or induced mutations, such as insertions, deletions, and SNPs within coding and non-coding regulatory regions, to uncover the genetic basis of key agronomical traits. However, CRISPR has revolutionized functional genomics by allowing precise dissection and restoration of these regulatory elements. One such example is grain number per panicle (GNP), a pivotal yield component in rice, which is intricately regulated by the non-coding element REM20, a major modulator of GNP. Natural variation in the inverted repeat sequence containing the CArG box within the REM20 promoter significantly alters its expression, thereby contributing to the observed differences in grain number per panicle (GNP) among diverse rice cultivars (Wu et al.). Similarly, mutations within the promoter region of CARE, a cis-element crucial for gibberellin (GA)-induced expression of proteinases in rice seeds, result in the loss of GA responsiveness and disrupted transactivation by the transcription factor GAMyb (Sutoh and Yamauchi).
Amylose content in starch is a key determinant of cooked rice texture, and precise genome editing was employed to generate six novel alleles of the Waxy (Wx) gene by targeting the region near the TATA box of its promoter, enabling fine-tuning of grain amylose content (Huang et al.). Suppressing the expression of the granule-bound starch synthase I (GBSSI) gene by targeting its 3′-UTR leads to reduced amylose levels in the rice endosperm compared to wild type (Park et al.), which is beneficial for achieving a soft grain with stickier texture, shorter cooking time, and enhanced palatability. Targeted editing of the grain size gene WIDE GRAIN 7 (WG7), identified from a T-DNA insertion mutant, revealed its role as a positive regulator of grain size which encodes for a cysteine–tryptophan-rich transcriptional activator (Huang et al., 2019). CRISPR/Cas9 has also been applied to develop beneficial alleles in key rice yield-related QTL, such as TEOSINTE BRANCHED1 (TB1), which holds promise for generating high-yield cultivars with improved agronomic traits (Cui et al.). In disease resistance breeding, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing of the effector-binding elements in the promoter of SWEET14 conferred broad-spectrum resistance to bacterial blight in Super Basmati rice (Zafar et al.). An efficient CRISPR/Cas12a-based promoter editing system was developed and applied to the D18 gene, which encodes GA3-oxidase in the gibberellin biosynthesis pathways resulting in a semi-dwarf phenotype without negatively affecting grain size (Zhou et al.). Furthermore, a 54-base pair cis-regulatory element within the Ideal Plant Architecture 1 (IPA1) gene was functionally dissected using a CRISPR/Cas9 screen, allowing resolution of the trade-off between grain number per panicle and tiller number, ultimately boosting grain yield (Song et al.).
Efficient promoter engineering strategies facilitate the creation of quantitative trait alleles with finely tuned gene expression, leading to improved rice lines with optimal amylose content (AC) and gel consistency (GC) (Zeng et al.). Glucan water-dikinase 1 (GWD1), a crucial enzyme in transitory starch degradation, was targeted using CRISPR/Cas9 to generate hypomorphic (weak) mutants that did not impair plant growth and development but subtly altered grain transparency and seed germination behavior (Wang et al.). CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of nucleotides at exon–intron junction has been shown to induce exon skipping during pre-mRNA splicing, thereby enhancing several agronomically important traits (Tang et al.). To improve grain appearance quality, a CRISPR/Cas9-based strategy was employed to edit the AC II element-containing region of the SLG7 promoter, resulting in the creation of novel beneficial alleles (Tan et al.). CRISPR/Cas9 editing of promoter regions in three SWEET genes conferred robust and broad-spectrum disease resistance in rice without compromising yield traits (Oliva et al.). A multiplex genome editing approach was applied to simultaneously engineer promoter alleles of IPA1 and Bsr-d1, leading to substantial gains in both yield and disease resistance (Hsieh-Feng, 2022). A novel transgene-free breeding strategy was developed by editing the Xa13 promoter using CRISPR/Cas9, producing rice lines resistant to bacterial blight (Li et al.). In parallel, targeted modification of effector-binding elements (EBEs) in the promoter of three major susceptible genes enabled the development of broad-spectrum blight-resistant rice lines that retain their original agronomic performance (Ni et al.). A specific core nucleotide deletion in the UPT box of the Xa13 promoter not only conferred resistance to rice blight but also facilitated the selection of transgene-free lines (Yu et al.). A high-efficiency multiplex promoter-targeting (HMP) strategy, leveraging CRISPR/Cas9, was utilized to generate novel cis-regulatory alleles in three key heading date genes, resulting from a germplasm collection with quantitatively diverse heading date phenotypes (Zhou et al., 2024). Comparative analysis of the promoter regions of orthologous genes between inbred parental lines revealed substantial sequence variation, especially small insertions and deletions, leading to the formation of unique cis-regulatory elements responsible for heterotic gene expression (Zhang et al.).

An allelic library of the SWEET14 promoter in rice was developed using TALE-nuclease constructs, providing evidence that TALE-mediated susceptibility may arise from the induction of multiple genetically redundant host susceptibility genes by a single effector (Blanvillain-Baufumé et al.). The transport of essential micronutrients, such as zinc (Zn), is regulated by the nicotianamine synthase (NAS) gene family. Precise editing of the NAS2 promoter significantly enhanced zinc accumulation, resulting in increased zinc concentration per plant (Ludwig et al., 2024). Often, despite their enormous genetic potential, high-yielding rice cultivars underperform under disease pressure. For instance, TBR225, a popular variety in Northern Vietnam, is susceptible to bacterial leaf blight. However, promoter editing of SWEET14 conferred enhanced resistance without compromising yield potential (Duy et al.). Strikingly, a susceptible rice variety was successfully converted into disease-resistant lines by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in of a promoter element from the Xa23 susceptibility allele, demonstrating the potential for broad-spectrum resistance engineering through promoter modification (Wei et al.).
CRISPR/Cas9 was effectively employed to insert two transcriptions activator-like effector binding elements (EBEs) into the promoter region of a rice bacterial blight resistance gene, achieving precise, directional targeted insertions with an efficiency of up to 24% (Kumar et al.). Using CRISPR/Cas9, targeted mutations in the EBE region of the SULTR3;6 promoters successfully generated rice lines with broad-spectrum resistance to bacterial leaf streak (Xu et al.). The Wx gene, which regulates amylose content and determines grain texture, was edited in its 5′ UTR to modulate amylose levels, resulting in the development of soft-textured rice lines (Yang et al.). The salt-inducible RAV2 promoter, known for its exclusive role in salt stress response, was functionally validated through precise promoter engineering (Duan et al.). A naturally occurring deletion within the first intron of the Waxy gene gave rise to a novel allele critical for seed amylose content (Liu et al.), while a 9.5-kb deletion in the first intron of MADS51 altered heading date by producing a non-coding RNA, offering insights into temperature sensitivity governed by intronic regulatory elements (Zhao et al.). Precise deletion of the Xa13 promoter in transgenic rice leaves inhibited its expression, enabling the development of disease-resistant rice lines suitable for practical deployment (Li et al.). Editing the promoter region of Ehd1 to create weak alleles in elite japonica backgrounds resulted in enhanced breeding yields without compromising plant development (Li et al.). Additionally, eight distinct targets were designed in the Wxa background using CRISPR/Cas9, and transgenic lines were screened for phenotypic variation, leading to the generation of eight novel Waxy alleles with varying amylose content (Zhang et al.).
uORFs are known to repress the translation of downstream primary ORFs; therefore, editing or mutating uORFs can be an effective strategy to enhance protein expression (Si et al., Zhang et al.). CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing has been utilized to delay heading dates in rice by subtly increasing the expression of flowering repressors (Liu et al.). Advanced tools like base editing and prime editing have enabled the de novo creation or extension of existing uORFs by mutating their stop codons, resulting in synthetic uORFs that incrementally downregulate translation of the primary ORFs (Xue et al.). A highly flexible method has also been proposed wherein uORFs in key genes such as DEP1 and GIF1 are disrupted or deleted, effectively manipulating protein levels to enhance desirable yield traits (Yang et al.). The 3′ UTR of the HPPD gene was precisely edited using CRISPR/Cas9, generating herbicide-resistant rice lines that outperformed wild-type varieties, opening new possibilities for herbicide tolerance breeding (Wu et al.). Similarly, editing the 3′ UTR of the cold tolerance gene LT7 yielded allelic variants with enhanced low-temperature resistance, demonstrating the potential of 3′ UTR manipulation in stress resilience (Wang et al.). Polymorphisms within the 3′ UTRs of the RNG1 and RNG3 genes have been shown to influence resistance to rice blast disease, and editing these regulatory regions improved disease resistance without affecting key agronomic traits (Xu et al.). Transposable elements can influence gene expression, alternative splicing, and epigenetic regulation and deletion of the Tos17 retrotransposon via CRISPR/Cas9-modulated graded expression patterns of neighboring genes, providing critical insight into their regulatory roles in rice improvement (Saika et al.; Luo et al.). A large-scale enhancer-mining approach led to the identification of short transcriptional enhancers (STEs) capable of significantly boosting gene expression in rice protoplasts. Integration of these STEs into target loci enabled robust transcriptional upregulation, presenting a promising strategy for multiplexed gene activation via CRISPR/Cas-mediated enhancer knock-ins (Yao et al.).
Resistant starch (RS) has emerged as a valuable dietary component for enhancing satiety and managing diabetes, and the SBEIIb gene is a key regulator governing RS content in rice. Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated precise multi-target promoter editing, SBEIIb expression was downregulated, resulting in a remarkable improvement compared to wild-type (Sang et al.). Promoter editing of the Wx gene has also led to the development of superior alleles with temperature-responsive regulation of amylose content, unveiling new transcriptional control mechanisms underlying rice grain quality (Yan et al.). CRISPR/Cas9 technology has further been used to remove repressor motifs from the NF-YC4 promoter, reducing repressor binding and significantly enhancing NF-YC4 expression, thereby increasing seed protein content (Wang et al.). Similarly, excision of a transcriptional inhibitor motif from the phosphate transporter PHO1;2 promoter led to its enhanced expression, improved root-to-shoot phosphate transport, and ultimately, increased grain yield (Mourya et al., 2025). Collectively, these studies underscore that increasing the level of resistant starch and enhancing other quality traits can be effectively achieved through multiplex engineering of non-coding regulatory elements in rice, offering a transformative avenue for crop improvement.

Leveraging Traits Modulation: Manipulation of Non-coding RNA
Rice genomes harbor a vast array of functional non-coding regulatory variants that are transcribed but not translated. Variations in these elements play a pivotal role in shaping complex traits during rice domestication (Zheng et al.; Chandra et al.). Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, have emerged as key regulators of numerous biological functions underlying trait modulation. With recent advancements in genome editing, particularly CRISPR/Cas9 technologies, there is growing potential to target these regulatory elements, although significant challenges remain to fully realize their potential (Basak and Nithin; Zhou et al.; Bravo-Vázquez et al.). A detailed tabulation of ncRNAs dissected through editing technologies is presented in Table 3. Most ncRNA-editing studies in rice have focused on miRNAs, with limited but growing interest in lncRNAs and circRNAs. While the approach of editing miRNA genes to disrupt their regulatory networks has drawn criticism for off-target complexities and pleiotropic effects (Ferreira and Reis), conversely, it remains one of the most promising strategies for functional dissection and precise modulation of gene expression via ncRNAs (Basak and Nithin). This strategy allows targeted interference with repressor elements, enabling fine-tuned gene expression switches to influence key agronomic traits. For instance, editing members of the miR156 subfamily enhanced seed dormancy and mitigated pre-harvest sprouting (PHS) without reducing productivity, demonstrating the value of ncRNA manipulation for trait improvement (Miao et al.). A CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout study uncovered a functional small peptide encoded by a precursor miRNA that regulates the expression of both its parent miRNA and downstream target genes, thereby modulating cadmium tolerance (Lu et al.). Using a clean knockout strategy, MIR168a-edited rice lines exhibited accelerated growth, increased tiller numbers, and earlier maturity, alongside broad pleiotropic effects (Zhou et al.). MIR171f, predominantly expressed in rice, was found to regulate the transcript levels of scarecrow-like6-I and II, and precise editing of this miRNA demonstrated its essential role in drought tolerance through the regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis (Um et al.). Deletions in miR171f and miR818b significantly downregulated their target transcripts, thereby enhancing drought stress response (Chung et al.). In another approach, TALEN-mediated targeting of mature miRNA sequences produced heritable mutations, leading to defective miRNA biogenesis and a shootless phenotype (Bi et al.). Knocking out MIR396ef specifically led to enhanced grain size and increased panicle branching, thereby improving grain yield under nitrogen-deficient conditions (Zhang et al.). Similarly, targeted editing of the MIR396 gene family modified plant architecture, yielding longer leaves and shorter internodes, while simultaneously boosting grain size and productivity (Miao et al.). CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of miR535 conferred improved tolerance to multiple abiotic stresses, including salinity, abscisic acid, dehydration, and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-induced drought stress (Yue et al.).

Precise editing of miR535, which targets the SQUAMOSA Promoter-Binding Protein-Like 7 (SPL7) transcription factor, was found to negatively regulate Nramp5, thereby reducing cadmium (Cd) accumulation in rice (Yue et al.). CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing has also been employed to reduce susceptibility to blast disease by targeting miR812w, a transposable element-derived miRNA involved in trans-methylation, thus opening novel avenues for engineering disease-resistant plants (Campo et al., 2024). CRISPR/Cas9 has proven effective for knocking out plant miRNAs by introducing single base-pair insertions or deletions, which generate functionally redundant alleles (Zhou et al.). A multiplex CRISPR/Cas9 approach was used to generate null alleles for circRNAs, producing rice lines that displayed enhanced salt tolerance during seed germination (Zhou et al.). A seed vigor-associated lncRNA identified under cold stress conditions was shown to interact with the SAUR gene family, and its engineering led to delayed seed germination (Gao et al.). Editing of lncRNA57811 in autotetraploid rice via CRISPR/Cas9 resulted in plants with similar morphology to the wild type, but transcriptomic analysis revealed differentially expressed lncRNAs involved in meiosis and reduced fertility (Li et al.). In a striking example, precise genome editing revealed the role of the parent-of-origin lncRNA MISSEN, which regulates endosperm development by hijacking a helicase complex to control tubulin dynamics during cellularization, ultimately causing dented seed phenotypes (Zhou et al.). Another ncRNA, Ef-cd, transcribed from the antisense strand of the flowering activator SOC1, was shown to delay flowering when its promoter was deleted via CRISPR/Cas9 without compromising plant vigor or fertility (Fang et al.). Collectively, these studies underscore the vast potential of editing ncRNAs in rice, offering innovative and precise strategies for improving agronomic traits, stress tolerance, and reproductive fitness, marking a significant leap forward for next-generation breeding.

Challenges and Opportunities
The targeted editing of both coding and non-coding genomic elements in crops like rice holds immense promise for next-generation agricultural biotechnology, yet remains constrained by several intrinsic and technical bottlenecks. A major impediment is the limited functional annotation and epigenomic mapping of non-coding regulatory landscapes, including cis-regulatory elements (CREs) such as enhancers, silencers, insulators, promoters, UTRs, and diverse classes of regulatory RNAs (e.g., miRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs), which hampers the systematic identification and manipulation of functionally relevant loci (Chen et al.). Furthermore, the spatiotemporal plasticity and conditional responsiveness of these elements, coupled with redundant or compensatory regulatory circuits, obscure the establishment of clear genotype-to-phenotype relationships (Cui et al.; Chen et al.). The repetitive nature and chromatin complexity of intergenic and intronic regulatory DNA add layers of difficulty, increasing the risk of off-target mutagenesis, particularly with first-generation CRISPR systems. Additionally, spatiotemporally controlled and tissue-specific delivery of editing reagents, especially for base editors (BEs), prime editors (PEs), and epigenome editors, remains a critical challenge in rice due to cellular barriers and genotype-dependent transformation inefficiencies. Recent advances in precision genome engineering have significantly expanded the editing toolbox. CRISPR/Cas12a (Cpf1) has demonstrated efficacy in generating large-scale deletions and multiplexed editing of gene promoters. Meanwhile, base editing and prime editing technologies also enable precise, programmable nucleotide conversions and targeted insertions at single-base resolution without inducing double-stranded breaks, ideal for fine-tuning regulatory sequences (Molla et al.; Zhou et al.). Beyond technical limitations, regulatory uncertainty remains a formidable barrier. Non-coding edits, though devoid of transgenic sequences, do not always align neatly with existing biosafety and regulatory frameworks. Moreover, the quantitative and polygenic nature of phenotypic traits governed by non-coding regions necessitates the use of high-throughput phenomics, single-cell transcriptomics, and multi-omics integration to resolve subtle molecular and developmental effects. In summary, unlocking the full potential of non-coding genome engineering in rice will require a systems biology-driven approach that integrates functional epigenomics, next-generation editing platforms, spatiotemporally optimized delivery systems, and phenotype-to-genotype machine learning pipelines to enable predictive trait design and sustainable crop enhancement.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives
Rice stands as a cornerstone of global food systems, underpinning the caloric and economic stability of over half the world’s population. Amid escalating demands driven by climate volatility, resource limitations, and population growth, strategic genetic enhancement of rice is imperative for ensuring global food and nutritional security. Beyond protein-coding regions, natural genetic variation within cis-regulatory elements, UTRs, introns, and other non-coding domains is increasingly recognized as a powerful driver of phenotypic diversity, adaptive evolution, and domestication traits. These regulatory elements orchestrate spatial and temporal gene expression, influence epigenetic landscapes, and integrate environmental cues into transcriptional outputs. SNPs, small indels, transposable element insertions, and structural rearrangements within non-coding elements are often associated with key agronomic traits, such as stress tolerance, flowering time, grain yield, and nutrient composition. Functional dissection of these allelic variants, particularly within promoters, enhancers, lncRNAs, and miRNAs is uncovering novel regulatory hierarchies that control complex traits (Fig. 2).
The conclusive overview of non-coding element editing through precise and advanced engineering approaches highlights its potential to dissect the functionalities of non-coding elements mainstreaming key biological processes in rice. These include regulating vegetative growth, influencing caryopsis development, ensuring pre- and post-harvest quality, refining yield by altering panicle and spikelet architecture, reducing abiotic stress constraints, enhancing resilience against biotic threats, and improving nutrient security through macro- and micronutrient biofortification. These advancements are crucial for acquiring novel agronomic traits in rice, enabling their streamlined function and paving the way for future interventions in rice breeding and improvement
The advent of next-generation genome editing platforms, such as CRISPR/Cas9, base editors, prime editors, and epigenome modulators, has revolutionized our capacity to precisely interrogate and engineer non-coding genomic loci. These tools facilitate allele-specific editing, promoter reprogramming, and uORF tuning, thereby enabling rational rewiring of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) to achieve tailored phenotypic outcomes. Despite this progress, editing non-coding elements poses unique technical and biological challenges. The pleiotropic nature of many regulatory elements, their fragmented genomic organization, and their potential to form redundant or overlapping regulatory architectures complicate functional predictions. Moreover, perturbation of a single non-coding element can cascade into genome-wide transcriptional dysregulation, necessitating high-resolution functional genomics, transcriptomics, and chromatin accessibility mapping to minimize off-target effects. Integrative multi-omics approaches, combined with machine learning-guided regulatory element annotation and predictive modeling, are crucial for overcoming these barriers. By leveraging synthetic biology, high-throughput reporter assays, and multiplexed editing strategies, it is now feasible to fine-tune transcriptional activity, simulate natural allelic diversity, and accelerate the de novo design of elite rice cultivars. Ultimately, the systematic and precise engineering of non-coding regulatory elements represents a paradigm shift in functional genomics and crop improvement. It offers unprecedented potential to enhance agronomic performance, abiotic and biotic stress resilience, and grain quality traits, laying the foundation for next-generation, climate-resilient rice breeding programs.
