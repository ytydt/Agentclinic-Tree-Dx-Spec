Evaluation of CNN-based deep learning models for auto-contouring in glioblastoma radiotherapy: a review
Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary brain tumor. Radiation therapy (RT) plays a main role in GBM treatment, with precise tumor volume delineation. Tumor contouring is important in RT treatment planning, followed by successful treatment. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL) techniques, have introduced automated contouring methods to improve accuracy and reduce interobserver variability. This review considers different architectures such as CNN, U-Net, nnU-Net, and PKMI-Net. In addition, a comparison was made between recent advances in DL models for the automatic segmentation of GBM target volumes, focusing on more applicable architectures and their characteristics, challenges, and limitations. This review study was performed on the recent literature from scientific citation websites such as Google Scholar, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science by the end of January 2025. The results of this review study among the analyzed models, nnU-Net architecture emerged as the strongest, offering superior segmentation accuracy due to its self-configuring capabilities and adaptability to different imaging modalities.

Introduction
Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common primary malignant brain tumor with an incidence of nearly 5–10 cases per 100,000 each year. This is the most aggressive brain tumor type with rapid progression and resistance to conventional treatment methods, which leads to a poor prognosis. Despite significant advances in combined treatment strategies including surgical intervention, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and supportive care, the median survival of patients with GBM is only 13 months. Therefore, detecting and treating GBM remains a significant public health challenge.
Radiation therapy (RT) is one of the most commonly used methods for treating glioblastoma (GBM). The tissue contouring process plays a vital role in RT treatment planning and is essential for effective GBM management. The primary principle of the contouring process in RT involves accurately determining the treatment target volume. This process includes identifying the gross tumor volume (GTV), clinical target volume (CTV), and planning target volume (PTV) while distinguishing these from adjacent healthy tissues. The accuracy of tumor delineation is crucial, as it directly impacts the precise delivery of radiation doses while minimizing potential adverse effects on surrounding healthy vital tissue. Therefore, this process demands advanced imaging techniques and the expertise of radiation oncologists skilled in interpretation and decision-making.
Historically, contouring processes have been identified manually by radiation oncologists. However, the conventional manual determination of these volumes presents significant challenges. It is often time-consuming and labor-intensive, leading to fatigue and increasing the likelihood of errors. Such inaccuracies can significantly undermine the effectiveness of the treatment. Recent advancements in computer science and medical imaging have introduced an automated method for the segmentation of various tissues.
Over the past decade, most research in RT has emphasized using artificial intelligence (AI), especially deep learning (DL) methods, for auto-contouring in RT treatment planning as a safe, fast, and accurate method. The advent of auto-contouring tools using AI has introduced a transformative paradigm in the segmentation of target volumes for brain tumors. These tools are designed to increase the accuracy and efficiency of delineating tumor RT boundaries, and several studies have been conducted on their ability to simplify the segmentation process while significantly reducing interobserver variability, which is an inherent challenge in manual contouring.
In recent years, there has been a growing interest in using and introducing various deep learning architectures, such as U-Net, V-Net, and SegNet, for tissue and tumor segmentation. However, significant uncertainty remains about the performance and outcomes of different deep learning architectures for GBM contouring and segmentation. This aspect, often neglected in current studies, requires thorough investigation to identify the most effective methods for clinical application.
This review aims to explore various applicable DL models and architectures used in GBM auto-contouring and compare their functionalities, thereby contributing to the expanding knowledge in this field. It also emphasizes the importance of continuous innovation in employing AI and DL for GBM segmentation in radiotherapy practices. To achieve this aim, it examines the literature on different DL architectures and introduces the most effective practical architecture utilized in radiation therapy for auto-contouring.
While various deep learning paradigms have been proposed for medical image segmentation, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and their derivatives, particularly U-Net and its variants, remain the most widely adopted and effective frameworks in GBM radiotherapy auto-contouring. Therefore, this review primarily focuses on CNN-based architectures, while briefly discussing other deep learning branches such as RNNs, GANs, and Transformers as potential future directions.

Materials and methods
In this narrative review study, a recent survey of literature on advances in AI, particularly deep learning techniques, has introduced automated segmentation of the target volume in glioblastoma using various network architectures, with their results compared. To this end, the search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, SCOPUS, EMBASE, Google Scholar, and ScienceDirect. The search strategy employed MeSH terms and keywords such as “brain cancer, automatic segmentation, deep learning, and glioblastoma.” The inclusion criteria for this review comprised articles published in English from January 2015 up to the end of January 2025.
Included studies were English-language articles focusing on deep learning approaches for glioblastoma segmentation with quantitative performance evaluation. Excluded were non-English studies, conference abstracts without full text, studies unrelated to glioblastoma, or those not using deep learning for segmentation.
The initial search across all databases retrieved 310 records. After removal of duplicates, 214 records remained for title and abstract screening. Following exclusion of irrelevant articles and those not meeting the inclusion criteria, 35 full-text articles were reviewed in detail. Finally, 12 studies met the criteria and were included in this review. A schematic overview of the selection process is provided in Fig. 1.

Results
The effectiveness of DL in automatic contouring lies in its ability to learn complex patterns and features directly from raw data, eliminating the need for hand-crafted features or explicit segmentation algorithms. By utilizing large datasets of annotated MR images, DL models can be trained to automatically identify and delineate tumor boundaries, leading to a significant improvement in the speed and consistency of target volume delineation. By leveraging large datasets of annotated MR images, DL models can generalize better across diverse patient populations, making them highly effective in improving both the speed and accuracy of target volume delineation in GM patients.
Deep learning is a powerful tool in medical image analysis, particularly for the automatic contouring of target volumes in GBMs. Deep learning algorithms, especially conventional neural networks (CNNs) and U-Net, have demonstrated remarkable performance in segmenting tumor volumes from multimodal MR images. These models are recognized for their high accuracy and efficiency. In reviewing the literature, various AI architectures used in glioma tumor contouring were examined. This study categorizes different AI architectures into two main groups: CNNs and U-Net.
In this review, CNN-based models are categorized according to their architectural principles and application domains, following a systematic framework similar to that described by Diana-Albelda et al. (2025). The architectures are grouped into two major categories: conventional CNN-based models such as SegNet, DeepLabv3+, DenseNet, and DeepMedic, which primarily rely on convolutional feature extraction; and encoder–decoder–based CNN variants such as U-Net and its derivatives (nnU-Net, NV-Net, and PKMI-Net), which employ skip connections and hierarchical feature fusion for more precise segmentation. This structure provides a coherent framework for comparing model functionality, performance metrics, and clinical applicability across different studies.

Convolutional neural networks
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), characterized by their multi-layer structure of convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers, facilitate effective feature extraction from training data. This architecture supports automated feature learning and prediction, establishing CNNs as key players in automating GBM target volume contouring. In recent years, there has been a growing body of literature on employing various CNNs for GBM tumor region segmentation. A 2020 study by Shusharina et al. concentrated on utilizing CNNs to aid clinicians in delineating the CTV, achieving a mean Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 0.79. They employed a CT image and two diagnostic MR images: contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1-CE) and T2-weighted FLAIR sequences. The CNNs were able to identify barrier structures that impede disease spread, and the results indicated strong agreement between automated and manual CTV delineation.
Semantic segmentation network (SegNet) is another kind of CNN architecture introduced and developed by Badrinarayanan et al. in 2015. This architecture stands out for its ability to encoder-decoder structure among CNN architectures, which is especially useful in tumor segmentation. In 2023, Sadeghi et al. developed and validated the SegNet model for automatic segmentation of GBM using multimodal MRI data from the BraTS 2019 dataset. The primary aim of their study was to improve the accuracy and consistency of tumor volume delineation, which is essential for radiotherapy planning. The model was evaluated using 89.60% DSC and 1.49 mm Hausdorff Distance (HD) metrics.
Semantic segmentation network (SegNet) is another CNN architecture introduced and developed by Badrinarayanan et al. in 2015. This model is characterized by its encoder–decoder design: the encoder extracts hierarchical features using convolution and pooling operations, while the decoder upsamples feature maps by reusing the max-pooling indices to reconstruct spatial details and precisely locate object boundaries. This mechanism allows SegNet to preserve fine structures that are particularly important in tumor segmentation. In 2023, Sadeghi et al. developed and validated the SegNet model for automatic segmentation of GBM using multimodal MRI data from the BraTS 2019 dataset. Their study aimed to improve the accuracy and consistency of tumor volume delineation, essential for radiotherapy planning. The model achieved strong results with a DSC of 89.60% and a Hausdorff Distance (HD) of 1.49 mm, demonstrating the potential of SegNet for accurate clinical contouring.
Mask R-CNN (Region-based (R) R-CNN), another variant of CNN, has been applied in several studies. Mask R-CNN can detect a target in an image and simultaneously create a mask for each target, making it suitable for segmentation tasks. In 2021, Breto et al. developed a Mask R-CNN for the automatic volume delineation of GBM from daily MR images during RT. They reported promising results, with DSC values (DSC = 0.8) strongly correlating with manually contoured volumes, suggesting potential for real-time tumor monitoring. In another study, they introduced a novel DL technique for the automatic segmentation of GBM on daily low-field MR images. This model, trained on patient data from daily imaging sessions, achieved DSC values of 0.67 for tumors and 0.84 for resection cavities, demonstrating its potential for clinical application.

DeepLabv3 + is a deep architecture that builds on the strengths of CNNs while focusing on semantic segmentation. DeepLabv3 + employs Atrous convolution to effectively capture multi-scale contextual information, allowing it to segment objects at various scales without sacrificing resolution. This architecture enhances feature extraction by combining features from multiple layers, and it also incorporates a simple yet effective decoder module to refine segmentation results, resulting in more precise boundary delineation. Khodadadi Shoushtari et al. developed a DeepLabv3 + architecture with pre-trained ResNet18 weights for the automatic segmentation of GBM in MR images. Trained on the BraTS 2020 dataset using T1-CE and FLAIR sequences, the model achieved impressive results with DSC scores of 78% for the enhanced tumor, 85% for the tumor core, and 87% for the whole tumor. This model demonstrated the effectiveness of combining multiple MRI modalities and extensive hyperparameter optimization to achieve accurate and efficient tumor segmentation. The study underscores the potential of advanced DL architectures to enhance clinical segmentation practices, particularly in high-grade gliomas.
Another DL architecture applied is DeepMedic, which utilizes a dual-path 3D CNN. This feature in this architecture allows the 3D analysis of tumor volumes, which provides more accuracy in segmenting tumors with irregular and complex shapes compared to 2D networks. In a study, Eijgelaar et al. have improved GBM segmentation accuracy using DeepMedic with sparse datasets, with acceptable results such as DSC (0.83). Their study showed that sparse training, which simulates missing sequences during training, improves model robustness, especially when site-specific data is included with a DSC of 0.83.
A Dense Convolutional Network (DenseNet) is a specific CNN architecture introduced by Huang et al. It is the backbone for models like Mask R-CNN in contouring tasks. As shown in Fig. 1, each layer in DenseNet receives inputs from all previous layers, allowing for feature reuse and reducing the required number of parameters. In a 2020 study, Ermiş et al. developed a DenseNet architecture-based segmentation method for resection cavities (RCs) and GTVs utilizing postoperative MRI scans of GBM patients. While the automated method showed promise, further refinements are necessary to compare favorably with human expert delineation. Meanwhile, Bangalore Yogananda et al. utilized multi-parametric MRI data from the BraTS2018 dataset to create three 3D DenseNet CNNs for tumor segmentation. The networks achieved mean Dice values of 0.92, 0.84, and 0.80 for whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC), and enhancing tumor (ET) segmentation, respectively, demonstrating strong agreement with manual segmentation. Fu et al. also introduced a 3D Multipath DenseNet for segmenting GBM tumors from multimodal preoperative MR images. This model outperformed single-path designs, achieving a higher DSC (0.92) and a lower HD 95% (3.9 ± 3.3), indicating improved accuracy in GBM delineation.
Dense Convolutional Network (DenseNet) introduced by Huang et al. is a CNN architecture widely applied in medical image analysis and also used as the backbone for models such as Mask R-CNN in contouring tasks. In this architecture, each layer receives inputs from all previous layers, enabling effective feature reuse while reducing the number of parameters. In a 2020 study, Ermiş et al. applied a DenseNet-based segmentation method to postoperative MRI scans of GBM patients for delineating resection cavities (RCs) and GTVs. Although the results were promising, further improvements were needed to reach expert-level performance. Similarly, Bangalore Yogananda et al. employed multi-parametric MRI data from the BraTS2018 dataset to train three 3D DenseNet CNNs, achieving mean Dice values of 0.92, 0.84, and 0.80 for whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC), and enhancing tumor (ET), respectively, indicating strong agreement with manual annotations. Fu et al. further proposed a 3D Multipath DenseNet for multimodal preoperative MR images, which outperformed single-path designs by achieving a higher DSC (0.92) and lower HD95 (3.9 ± 3.3), confirming its superior accuracy in GBM segmentation.

U-Net
Recent developments in computer science and medical image analysis have led to the introduction of new DL architectures. Current studies have demonstrated the efficacy of developed DL architectures in automatic contouring of GBM target volumes. The U-Net architecture has gained widespread acceptance due to its effectiveness in segmenting medical images. U-Net’s architecture includes a contracting path for context detection and a symmetric expanding path for precise localization. This design has been enhanced for GBM segmentation through modifications such as skip connections, residual blocks, and attention mechanisms, which further increase performance and robustness. U-Net, with its encoder-decoder structure and skip connections, maintains spatial information, thereby increasing the accuracy of target volume detection. These features set U-Net apart from conventional CNN architectures, particularly in the automatic contouring of glioblastoma target volumes. Tian et al. explored the use of transfer learning to enhance the auto-segmentation of glioblastoma GTV for RT planning. Their 3D CNN model, based on U-Net and fine-tuned with a separate dataset, outperformed directly trained models, demonstrating the potential of transfer learning in improving auto segmentation efficiency and accuracy.
The nn-Unet, which employs an encoder-decoder architecture similar to U-Net, incorporates nested skip connections for improved information exchange and feature extraction. However, the main innovation of nn-Unet is its automated configuration. It adjusts hyperparameters such as the number of layers, filter sizes, and learning rates based on the dataset, allowing for adaptability across various segmentation tasks without the need for manual hyperparameter tuning. This design enhances nn-Unet’s ability to capture hierarchical features, further improving segmentation performance. This architecture was introduced by Isensee et al.. The framework optimizes several network parameters, including filter sizes and the number of layers, depending on the size and characteristics of the dataset. This impressive performance was demonstrated in a study conducted by Pemberton et al.. The performance of three brain tumor segmentation models, DeepMedic, nn-Unet, and NV-Net, was evaluated using a diverse dataset from multiple centers. The nn-Unet achieved the highest DSC (WT = 0.97 in the internal test). Furthermore, nn-Unet consistently showed HD values (WT = 7.34 mm in the internal test), indicating superior spatial accuracy.
PKMI-Net is a prior knowledge multimodal imaging model based 3D U-net structure that utilizes multiple imaging data, such as MRI and CT, to obtain more comprehensive information on various tumor characteristics. In this structure, high-level and low-level features of images are used to increase the accuracy and generalizability of the model. With this method, PKMI-Net accurately and efficiently segments the areas required for RT and reduces the need for manual interventions. In 2024, Tian et al. introduced a new method for automatic volume segmentation of GBM tumors using PKMI-Net deep network with DSC 0.94 and HD 2.07 for GTV. The proposed method accurately segments tumor volumes and CTV using prior knowledge from multimodal imaging.
Nested V-Net, or NV-Net, extends the V-Net architecture with nested skip connections similar to nn-Unet. This design effectively captures multi-scale features and contextual information, addressing the challenges of semantic segmentation. In the study by Pemberton et al., while NV-Net performed relatively well, it did not surpass nnU-Net in either DSC or HD, particularly on datasets with incomplete MRI sequences. Each of these models has its advantages and disadvantages. Conventional neural networks and their derivatives have been notably successful in data processing and complex feature detection (Fig. 2). In contrast, U-Net and its derivatives have garnered more attention due to their superior accuracy and local and global capabilities, especially in medical imaging. Discussion
As mentioned in the introduction, target volume segmentation of GBM is a crucial step in RT treatment planning. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of automatic methods, such as artificial intelligence, for segmenting the target volume in GBM tumors. This study aims to determine the effect of different DL architectures used for segmenting the target volume of GBM. The most significant outcomes of applying DL models include reduced inter-observer variability (IOV), enhanced accuracy, and time savings. A high number of IOVs is a common issue in manual contouring that can directly impact treatment outcomes. Several studies have indicated that models, particularly CNN and U-Net, along with their derivatives, can achieve significant results. The major advantages of DL algorithms over manual contouring include detecting various tumor shapes pixel-by-pixel across different slides, saving time, and decreasing disagreement among physicians.
In the current study, a review of the literature indicated that CNN architectures such as SegNet, DeepMedic, Deeplabv3+, and DenseNet, trained with MR images, are the most commonly used for segmenting target volumes in GBM tumors. While the CNN architectures reviewed in this study have shown superior performance in GBM contouring, they have certain limitations when segmenting small tumors. According to the results, the SegNet model generated a smaller set of auto-mined volumes than manual volumes drawn by experts, reducing accuracy in complex and small regions. In this review study, U-Net emerges as a significant AI architecture for contouring glioblastoma (GBM). We compare the performance of four U-Net architectures: 3D U-Net, nn-Unet, NV-Net, and PKMI-Net. These U-Net variants, feature encoder-decoder structures and skip connections, particularly excel when training datasets are limited. However, one downside of these architectures is their slower processing speed compared to simpler architectures like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), as well as the requirement for powerful GPUs and hardware for effective training. Nevertheless, U-Net consistently delivers superior segmentation results and remains a preferred choice in many clinical applications compared to CNN.
The results of this study indicate that, among all U-Net architectures, nnU-Net exhibits the highest performance for contouring the glioblastoma target volume. nnU-Net is based on the U-Net structure. With its auto-configuring capabilities, this architecture automatically adjusts various parameters, such as the process shown in Fig. 3, to optimize and adapt to any image data according to the computer system’s hardware. Additionally, incorporating nested skip connections has significantly improved segmentation accuracy. In a study conducted by Pemberton et al., nnU-Net achieved the highest DSC score for WT and the best spatial accuracy HD when evaluating different models (NV-net, DeepMedic). However, while this architecture employed sparsified training techniques to compensate for some missing MRI sequences, it still shows limited performance, especially for WT, which requires more image data for accurate detection. PKMI-Net may be a suitable alternative to nnU-Net as a multimodal version of 3D U-Net, constructed using masks obtained from GTV, CT scans, and MR images. PKMI-Net requires two separate steps to delineate GTV, CTV1, and CTV2, increasing preprocessing time. In contrast, NV-Net, which employs nested skip connections similar to nnU-Net, has successfully extracted multi-scale features to enhance segmentation accuracy. However, in Pemberton et al.‘s study, NV-Net performed worse than nnU-Net regarding DSC and HD accuracy for WT, particularly with data that included incomplete MRI sequences.
Finally, this review highlights nnU-Net as the optimal architecture for GBM segmentation due to its high accuracy and adaptability. However, PKMI-Net presents a valuable alternative when multimodal data is available and higher accuracy in specific tumor volumes is necessary.
Each of these architectures has its strengths and weaknesses. U-Net remains widely used due to its simplicity and effectiveness, particularly in scenarios with smaller datasets. However, nnU-Net stands out as the most robust model, featuring self-configuring capabilities and superior performance across various types of image data, making it the standard choice for accurate tumor segmentation. PKMI-Net, with its integration of multimodal imaging, shows strong results in complex datasets but requires more preprocessing and may face challenges in distinguishing structurally similar tissues. NV-Net, while offering enhancements through multi-scale feature extraction, does not surpass nnU-Net in cases involving incomplete or heterogeneous MRI sequences.
A growing body of literature has examined the impact of various MR image weights on AI training and performance. The absence of specific MRI sequences, particularly FLAIR, as noted by Eijgelaar et al., significantly lowered the mean Dice Similarity Coefficient (mean DSC = 0.13). This underscores FLAIR’s essential role in outcomes, as it suppresses the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signal, allowing the detection of abnormalities obscured by the bright CSF signal in sequences like T2-weighted images.
The T2-MRI sequence used for network training has achieved notable results in accurately obtaining CTV volumes, particularly with the PKMI-NET model. This efficacy arises because the T2-MRI sequence emphasizes differences in the water content of tissues, yielding clear images of pathological areas, such as edema. The high contrast between normal and abnormal tissues allows architectures like PKMI-NET to extract meaningful patterns, leading to precise segmentation of CTV. However, it is important to note that the PKMI-NET model can sometimes misidentify brain tissues that are structurally similar to tumors, such as the cerebral peduncles, as tumors. This misidentification is particularly evident in T2-FLAIR images, indicating that the model may struggle with accuracy in distinguishing similar tissues.
To address these challenges, employing comprehensive sequences and combining image data, such as perfusion and diffusion MR images (dMRI), during model training has produced improved outcomes. Perfusion imaging significantly helps overcome the challenges of misdiagnosing tissues, such as cerebral peduncles, as tumors, particularly in T2 and FLAIR sequences. Perfusion MRI measures blood flow in tissues, offering additional physiological information that is vital for differentiating normal from abnormal tissues. This capability can enhance architectures like PKMI-NET in detecting brain tissues that resemble tumors.
Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging is an effective method for assessing molecular diffusion within biological tissues. It provides essential information regarding tissue microstructure, including data on edema and necrosis. Consequently, insights from multimodal images can enhance the quality of architecture training.
Despite these advancements, each architecture has its limitations and faces common challenges. To address these issues, gathering clinical data from all imaging modalities can be time-consuming. Additionally, applying data augmentation techniques to the input data can enhance model training performance. By increasing the variability and diversity of the training dataset, these architectures are better equipped to generalize and remain effective across different scenarios, resulting in improved segmentation outcomes.
Another significant challenge is the difficulty that many architectures face in accurately delineating small tumors. Utilizing multimodal images along with advanced training methods can help capture finer details, thereby enhancing segmentation accuracy, particularly for small and complex tumor regions. Finally, a comparison of various DL architectures reveals that these models present both advantages and challenges; no network architecture has perfect parameters. By considering factors such as functional accuracy measured by DSC and HD, network adaptation to various types of input data, especially in cases of missing MRI sequences, and the ability to detect and contour tumors of different sizes and shapes, particularly small tumors, as well as performance speed, nnU-Net has demonstrated competitive results. While it may not be definitively the most accurate model, its self-configuring nature and flexibility make it a strong candidate for GBM tumor contouring.

CNN vs. U-Net comparisons
Target volume segmentation of GBM is critical for radiotherapy planning. CNN architectures such as SegNet, DeepMedic, DeepLabv3+, DenseNet, and Mask R-CNN reduce inter-observer variability, improve accuracy, and save time. Among these, 3D multi-path DenseNet consistently performed well in both large and small tumors. SegNet, with its encoder-decoder structure and fewer parameters, demonstrated acceptable accuracy and shorter training time, making it practical when speed is essential. Mask R-CNN showed promising results for real-time tumor monitoring but faced challenges with small tumors (< 10 ml).
U-Net variants, including 3D U-Net, nnU-Net, NV-Net, and PKMI-Net, generally outperformed CNNs, particularly in scenarios with limited datasets. They require more computational resources but achieve higher segmentation accuracy and improved boundary delineation.

Performance and advantages of nnU-Net
Among U-Net architectures, nnU-Net achieved the highest performance due to self-configuring capabilities and nested skip connections, which enhance multi-scale feature extraction. Pemberton et al. reported superior DSC and HD values for WT compared to NV-Net and DeepMedic. PKMI-Net offers advantages when multimodal data are available but requires additional preprocessing. NV-Net, while improving multi-scale feature extraction, did not surpass nnU-Net in cases with incomplete or heterogeneous MRI sequences.

SegNet, mask R-CNN, and CNN findings
SegNet provided high DSC and low HD, performing well in precise contouring and offering a practical alternative to DenseNet for clinical workflows where speed and computational efficiency are priorities. Mask R-CNN achieved high DSC in real-time applications but struggled with small target volumes. DeepLabv3+ and DeepMedic delivered acceptable results but encountered difficulties with irregular or thin slices, which are critical for accurate tumor delineation.

Added value of multimodality (CT, PET, MRI)
Combining multiple imaging modalities significantly improves segmentation accuracy. FLAIR sequences are crucial for detecting abnormalities otherwise masked by CSF signals. T2-MRI emphasizes tissue water content, highlighting edema and pathological areas. Perfusion and diffusion MRI provide physiological and microstructural information, helping differentiate tumor from normal tissue and enhancing segmentation of small or complex regions. Multimodal input improves model training, generalizability, and clinical applicability.

Limitations of current models
Despite their advantages, DL models have limitations. Small tumors, irregular slices, and missing MRI sequences reduce segmentation accuracy. SegNet may underestimate target volumes in complex regions, and DeepLabv3+, DeepMedic, and Mask R-CNN encounter challenges with thin or irregular slices. Data acquisition, preprocessing, and multimodal integration are time-consuming, which can limit routine clinical implementation.
Beyond these technical issues, broader challenges remain. Data scarcity continues to restrict the development of robust models, as large annotated GBM datasets are rare and labor-intensive to produce. Privacy concerns also hinder multi-institutional data sharing, which is essential for external validation and generalizability. Moreover, multimodal data fusion (MRI, CT, PET) presents difficulties in harmonizing image resolution, acquisition parameters, and co-registration.
To address these limitations, approaches such as transfer learning can reduce dependence on large annotated datasets by leveraging pretrained models, while federated learning enables multi-center collaborations without compromising patient privacy. Looking forward, the emergence of vision foundation models and large transformers (e.g., Vision Transformers, SAM, Swin-UNETR) holds promise to enhance generalizability and adaptability of GBM auto-contouring, though their clinical translation requires rigorous multi-center validation.

Future directions and emerging models
Although most studies in GBM radiotherapy auto-contouring have focused on CNN-based architectures, other branches of deep learning also show potential. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), with their ability to analyze sequential data, may support longitudinal assessment of tumor changes during radiotherapy. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have been applied in brain tumor segmentation to improve delineation of blurred or invasive tumor boundaries through adversarial training. Diffusion models are emerging techniques that enhance image denoising and synthesis, potentially improving image quality for segmentation tasks. Transformer-based models, such as Vision Transformer (ViT) and Swin-UNETR, have achieved superior results in glioma segmentation by capturing long-range dependencies beyond the capabilities of CNNs. While these models have not yet been widely adopted for GBM radiotherapy contouring, their methodological advantages indicate promising directions for future research.

Clinical translatability
For clinical adoption, multi-center validation and adherence to international standards are essential. Integration into hospital workflows requires consideration of computational resources, training time, and data variability. nnU-Net’s adaptability and high performance make it suitable for clinical deployment, while PKMI-Net is advantageous when multimodal data are available. Optimized workflows combining these models can enhance treatment planning, reduce inter-observer variability, and improve patient outcomes.

Strengths and limitations
The current review has several strengths, including its emphasis on the practical advantages of DL models, particularly nnU-Net, for the automatic segmentation and contouring of GBM target volumes. Additionally, the use of multiple MR images enhances segmentation accuracy and aids in the detection of small tumors.
However, this review also has limitations. There may be potential bias in the selection of the reviewed articles. Furthermore, the authors might not have access to all newly published papers or may not cover every aspect of recent findings on GBM tumor contouring, which could impact the generalizability of the results.

Conclusions
This study reviews and compares various DL architectures in contouring GBM target volumes. Among them, nnU-Net showed the highest accuracy and adaptability, excelling at segmenting complex shapes and small tumors. Its self-configuring capability optimizes hyperparameters based on input data, enhancing segmentation accuracy significantly. The study investigated how multiple MRI sequences improve segmentation accuracy, as supported by existing literature. Future research could integrate multimodal imaging techniques like CT, PET, PET-CT, and PET-MRI to boost contouring precision.
In summary, while no model is flawless, nnU-Net consistently achieves high accuracy and efficiency in GBM segmentation, making it the top choice for RT treatment planning. Future implementations of advanced training techniques and diverse image datasets may further improve DL model outcomes in contouring GBM tumors, ultimately benefiting treatment planning and patient outcomes.
