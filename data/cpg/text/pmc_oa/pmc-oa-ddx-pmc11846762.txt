Enhancing Soil Health Through Biocrusts: A Microbial Ecosystem Approach for Degradation Control and Restoration
Escalating global concerns about soil degradation, driven by erosion, salinization, compaction, pollution, and organic matter loss, highlights the critical need for sustainable remediation. Biocrusts—complex communities of cyanobacteria, algae, lichens, bryophytes, and fungi—play a pivotal role in soil stabilization, erosion prevention, and nutrient cycling. This study presents recent advancements in biocrust application for soil management and restoration, focusing on artificial biocrusts as a nature-based solution biotechnology. It emphasizes their effectiveness in enhancing soil quality, biodiversity, and ecosystem functionality. Researchers are leveraging these microbial communities to develop strategies that improve soil health and rehabilitate degraded landscapes. The review concludes that biocrusts are a viable strategy for boosting soil resilience and enhancing soil health against environmental stressors. It recommends future research on their long-term ecological impacts and methods to enhance their functionality.

Introduction
Global drylands are experiencing soil degradation due to land-use practices and climate change, which affects ecosystems and human livelihoods. Various concerns have been raised about the possibility of using biocrusts, a prevalent component of dryland ecosystems, to improve soil fertility and stability. As estimated, approximately 12% of terrestrial landscapes and 25–30% of dry and semi-arid regions are covered by biocrusts. Biocrusts, a complex assemblage of microorganisms, play a significant role in maintaining soil health, preventing degradation, and promoting restoration. They create a thin layer on the soil’s surface and are made up of cyanobacteria, algae, lichens, bryophytes, and fungi.
Biocrusts are crucial for soil stability and operation in dryland ecosystems, supporting soil fertility, water availability, and carbon sequestration. Notably, some species of cyanobacteria have the unique ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen into a form that plants can utilize. This process enriches the soil with nitrogen, which is essential for plant growth. Heindel et al. further demonstrated that soils harboring cyanobacteria-rich biocrusts exhibited marked improvements in nitrogen availability, which substantially benefited plant development. Moreover, biocrusts contribute to enhanced soil porosity and reduced evaporation by forming a protective layer on the soil surface, a critical function in arid regions where water scarcity is a limiting factor for vegetation. Comparative studies, including those by Antoninka et al. and Aqeel et al. (2023), have shown that biocrusted soils retain moisture more effectively than bare soils, primarily due to their improved structural and organic matter composition, thereby maintaining higher moisture levels post-rainfall events.

It should be noted that through photosynthesis and organic matter accumulation, biocrusts contribute to carbon sequestration in soils because cyanobacteria convert carbon dioxide into organic compounds during photosynthesis, which can then be incorporated into the soil matrix as organic matter. Their disappearance may have significant ecological implications, making them especially important in dry and semi-arid areas. The filamentous structures of cyanobacteria and other microorganisms help bind soil particles together, reducing erosion caused by wind and water, which is vital in stabilizing and maintaining soil integrity in fragile ecosystems. A study by Valentin demonstrated that biocrusts can reduce soil erosion rates by up to 90% compared to bare soils. They provide habitat for various organisms including microfauna and macrofauna that depend on these communities for food and shelter (this biodiversity contributes to overall ecosystem resilience, and they create microhabitats that support a variety of life forms which can enhance ecosystem functionality. Plant ecologists have developed strategies to reduce habitat loss due to disturbance or climate change, such as a controversial method of aided migration, which involves relocating seeds or plants northward in anticipation of suitable habitat movement. Another approach is “pre-storation,” which promotes restoration using species acclimated to both present and future conditions. Young et al. suggest assisted migration is a potential biocrust control tactic. A study by Thomazo et al. indicated that biocrusts significantly contribute to the nitrogen cycle in arid and semi-arid ecosystems by facilitating biological fixation, undergoing intense internal N transformation processes, and directly causing some losses through dissolved, gaseous, and erosional processes (Fig. 1a, b). Due to low denitrification rates and limited respiration, biocrusts function as net exporters of ammonium, nitrate, and organic N. However, research on biocrust inoculation primarily focuses on laboratory or plot scales. Duran et al. proposed three perspectives for effective biocrust application and monitoring to understand ecosystem functions and services which aims to scale up the processes (Fig. 1c). However, biocrusts in Antarctica can operate as ecosystem engineers, boosting soil fertility and nutrient availability, which positively improves the ecophysiological performance of native Antarctic vascular plants (Fig. 1d). This is especially important in cold climates where vascular plant growth is restricted by limited water availability, which is essential to establishing and growth of these plants.

Global soil deterioration is a significant issue that affects ecosystem health, environmental sustainability, and agricultural output. It results from degrading nearly one-third of the world’s soil resources, impacting biodiversity, water quality, agricultural production, and climate resilience. Causes include unsustainable farming methods, deforestation, urbanization, industry, overgrazing, and climate change, even though climate change may also positively affect the distribution of biocrusts by removing the upper vegetation that competes with them for space and light. Similarly, certain levels of overgrazing, if not severe enough to degrade the soil, may have comparable effects (Fig. 2). The primary effects of soil degradation include compaction, pollution, nutrient depletion, soil erosion, and loss of organic matter.
Agricultural production is directly affected by soil degradation, lowering crop yields and food security. Degraded soils make it harder for plants and animals to thrive, further reducing biodiversity and can lead to increased poverty, hunger, and food insecurity in vulnerable areas. Additionally, erosion and fertilizer runoff from deteriorated soils can cause contamination of water bodies and aquatic habitats. To address this issue, a comprehensive strategy incorporating soil conservation methods, organic farming, reforestation, agroforestry, sustainable land management practices, and restoration of degraded areas is required. Soil degradation releases stored carbon into the atmosphere, resulting in greenhouse gas emissions and soil organic matter loss. Addressing these challenges necessitates a collaborative effort among governments, non-governmental organizations, researchers, and local communities to advocate for and implement sustainable soil management strategies. Increased public awareness and engagement with international frameworks such as the World Soil Day campaign, the Global Soil Partnership, and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals are critical.
Although it is commonly believed that biocrusts have a slow recovery time of hundreds or thousands of years, and the long-term viability of biocrust restoration initiatives is frequently hampered by severe abiotic conditions, recent research suggests that they can recover relatively quickly with some assistant approaches; cyanobacteria-dominated biocrusts are likely to recover within 5–10 years, while lichen- and moss-dominated crusts take 10–20 years.
Biocrusts play a crucial role in soil stabilization, improving soil health and reducing erosion by binding soil particles together to form aggregating structures,Xiao et al., 2024). Biocrusts not only enhance plant growth and water infiltration but also restore ecosystem functionality through carbon sequestration, nutrient cycling, and the creation of microhabitats for diverse species. Despite their benefits, the effective implementation of biocrust restoration faces several challenges, including the need for suitable environmental conditions, species selection, and integration with existing land management practices. Additionally, the slow growth rates of certain biocrust organisms and their vulnerability to disturbances complicate restoration efforts. Effective biocrust restoration requires collaboration among scientists, land managers, and policymakers, coupled with further research to elucidate the mechanisms of biocrust formation and their interactions within microbial ecosystems in degraded soils. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of biocrusts’ role in enhancing soil health, highlighting their potential in mitigating land degradation and restoring degraded landscapes, and identifying gaps in current knowledge for future research.

Ecological Functions of Biocrusts
Biocrusts are essential for soil stabilization and erosion control in ecosystems. These communities of cyanobacteria, lichens, bryophytes, and fungi form a living cover on the soil surface, providing ecological functions that contribute to ecosystem health and resilience. Biocrusts play a primary role in preventing wind and water erosion by binding soil particles together and stabilizing the soil surface through an intricate network of filaments and hyphae produced by the organisms. They also play a significant role in preventing dust emissions in arid and semi-arid regions by covering the soil surface with a living layer, acting as a barrier that traps soil particles and prevents them from being lifted into the air as dust. This function is particularly important in desert ecosystems where wind erosion can lead to significant dust storms with far-reaching environmental impacts. In addition, biocrusts have been found to retard soil degradation in crop fields, favor forest recovery after land abandonment, and increase old-growth forest productivity, thereby contributing to higher forest resilience.
The mechanism behind soil stabilization and erosion control provided by biocrusts involves several key processes: physical binding, surface roughness, water infiltration, and nutrient cycle. Physical binding involves the exopolysaccharides produced by cyanobacteria and algae within biocrusts, which act as a “glue” that binds soil particles together, increasing soil stability and resistance to erosion. Meanwhile, filamentous cyanobacteria, as well as the rhizoids of lichens and mosses (Belnap, 2003), also play a role in binding soil particles together. Research by Xiao et al. (2024) confirms that cyanobacterial filaments interweave with each other to form a complex “3D” network structure, firmly capturing and stabilizing soil particles to form an aggregating structure. Surface roughness is increased by lichens, bryophytes, and other organisms on the soil surface, reducing the velocity of wind or water flow over the surface and minimizing erosive forces.
Biocrusts enhance water infiltration and retention in arid ecosystems by forming micro-topography on the soil surface, which promotes moisture retention, reduces runoff, and contributes to groundwater recharge, thereby supporting sustainable water availability (Eldridge and Delgado-Baquerizo 2019). They also trap dust particles and fix carbon and nitrogen, directly enhancing soil fertility and increasing organic matter in underlying soils. Additionally, biocrusts stabilize topsoil and regulate the settlement and distribution of soil microorganisms, soil fauna, and vascular plants, thus influencing the biogeochemical cycles of nutrients and water within soil ecosystems (Song et al. 2022b,).
Biocrust organisms often engage in symbiotic relationships with vascular plants. For example, mycorrhizal fungi associated with plant roots can extend their hyphal networks into the biocrust layer, facilitating nutrient exchange between the fungi and plants while benefiting from the nutrients fixed by cyanobacteria. Biocrusts provide a protective barrier for seeds against harsh environmental conditions such as desiccation and extreme temperatures. It has been found that seeds buried under biocrust layers had higher germination rates than those exposed to direct sunlight or harsh winds. Generally, biocrusts create microhabitats that support diverse microbial communities and small invertebrates by providing a stable substrate for other organisms. The interactions among different species within biocrusts can enhance overall ecosystem resilience and productivity, as studies have shown that diverse microbial communities can improve nutrient cycling efficiency compared to monocultures.
Biocrusts also contribute to the carbon and nitrogen cycle in terrestrial ecosystems. They are a significant part of the global carbon budget, characterized by their role in CO2 uptake through photosynthesis (Lopez-Canfin et al. 2021). To this end, research that represented the biological succession of biocrusts over 2 years in 21 locations examined the topsoil CO2 molar fraction (Lopez-Canfin et al. 2021). Cyanobacteria, algae, bryophytes, and lichens in biocrusts capture atmospheric carbon dioxide and accumulate in organic compounds within the soil matrix making it available for decomposers. However, in cold regions, the standing biomass has a very slow turnover where the carbon accumulates in the dead parts and slowly decomposes. Their nitrogen-fixing abilities contribute to nitrogen inputs in soils, supporting plant growth and ecosystem productivity. The physical depositional crust, lichen community, mature and incipient cyanobacteria, and Lepraria isidiata lichen community were all measured in front of plants. The dynamics of CO2 molar fraction are shown to be strongly influenced by biocrust type, soil water content, and temperature; the effect of soil water content is found to be most important and is reliant on temperature and soil moisture conditions.

Despite the harsh climatic conditions and lack of life, biocrust nonvascular colonies effectively colonized desert settings. For instance, since 2000, the eco-physiological reactions of biocrusts in China to alterations in anthropological disturbances, abiotic pressures, and species composition have been the research subject. Studies have also looked at their contributions to ecological and hydrological processes in deserts (Table 1), as well as their geographic distributions, succession, and connections with vascular plants and soil biomes.
The study by Chowaniec et al. discovered a considerable increase in photosynthetic biomass and microbial activity in inland shifting sands, implying that chlorophyll content in dunes might predict the existence of photoautotrophs and soil crust growth in warm-summer humid continental climates. The lowest chl a/b ratio was found in the early phases of succession, demonstrating the importance of eukaryotic microorganisms with chl b. The dune blowouts provide environmental niches for microorganisms that create biocrusts, with high biomass and optimal moisture conditions in dune blowouts—Later phases of biocrusts exhibited increased microbial mutual exclusion and more sophisticated networks. Aridity, salinity, and total phosphorus may affect the balance between deterministic and stochastic processes, with bacteria and eukaryotes responding differently. Depending on the kind of biocrust and disturbance, the impact of the disturbance changed as the soils covered with moss retained more water at high-pressure heads, but soils tramped on retained more water (Guan and Cao 2021). In addition, soils coated with moss exhibited less hydraulic conductivity than soils covered with lichen comprehending that the hydrological characteristics of biocrusts forecast eco-hydrological processes in arid and semi-arid environments (Guan and Cao 2021).
In research that simulated warming and decreased precipitation over 10 years in open-top chambers, it was discovered that moss in crustal communities will be more heavily impacted by these two factors than lichens and cyanobacteria where the hydrological performance of biological soil crusts will be directly affected. Alongside biocrust burial, research carried out by Kidron et al. during 2 years of severe drought revealed that biocrust flaking and rupture occurred. The study discovered that wind removal of coppice dunes was responsible for biocrust disintegration and erosion of the previously concealed crust. Substantial alterations in the characteristics of the extracellular polymeric substances that make up the biocrust were the reason for its flaking and rupture. According to the study, droughts may have impacted the extracellular polymeric substance’s ability to maintain crust coherence and flexibility. Even if up to 25% of the interdunal surface was damaged by crust flaking, additional flaking will probably happen if the dry surface is continuously driven by drought. This positive feedback cycle may jeopardize the integrity of the dune field, resulting in dust storms, dune encroachment, and deteriorating air quality, which might have disastrous effects on ecosystems vulnerable to drought.
Overall, based on the information provided in the papers, biocrusts play a vital role in plant growth, biodiversity, and the carbon and nitrogen cycle, as well as in controlling soil degradation through their multifaceted functions in stabilizing soil surfaces, enhancing soil fertility, and promoting ecosystem resilience in dryland environments. They provide habitat and resources for diverse microorganisms and invertebrates, creating ecological niches that support various species. Their intricate interactions within biocrust communities create ecological niches that contribute to overall biodiversity in arid ecosystems.
In the Baltic Sea and an inland dune in northern Germany, the community structure of biocrust phototrophic organisms was examined along a dune chronosequence. Sediment analyses, species identification, and a vegetation survey were carried out. In an attempt to examine the community structure of biocrust phototrophic organism, Kammann et al. confirmed that in later successional dune types, the biocrusts were dominated by lichens and bryophytes instead of algae. Less than 15% of the detected species were shared by the moss and lichen group, compared to 50% by the algal population. Along with advanced biocrust formation, the research also observed an increase in organic matter and moisture content, which may have resulted from biomass growth and possible microbial nutrient mobilization. Understanding the contributions of biocrust to plant productivity and community assembly processes in global ecosystems will be greatly impacted because plant performance was enhanced by moss-dominated biocrusts and hindered by lichen-dominated biocrusts. Also, the responses from plants differed among functional categories as well,C4 grasses benefited more from biocrusts than C3 grasses did. Compared to plants with N-fixing symbionts, those lacking symbionts reacted more favorably to biocrusts while biocrusts promoted the development of non-native plant species, they reduced germination.

On the surface of the earth, biocrusts are vital because they have a direct impact on soil temperature and other factors. The findings further ascertained that biocrusts in the drylands of central-western Argentina lower soil temperatures, with crusted and uncrusted zones showing significantly different mean temperatures. To maintain soil stability, maintain nutrient and hydrological cycles, and support primary production, biocrust are essential, particularly in the coastal region (Table 2). The cyanobacterial and algal community structure in coastal dunes was strongly impacted by the total phosphorus concentration of biocrusts-free sand. Also, artificial planting in semi-arid deserts can boost sand fixation and succession in biocrusts, potentially affecting the carbon cycle in desert ecosystems. Biocrust organisms, which primarily inhabit topsoil for photosynthesis, can compete with vascular plants for resources like water and nutrients which can lead to reduced organic matter input, as biocrusts may limit plant growth and reduce organic matter inputs into the soil. Additionally, biocrusts can alter decomposition rates by affecting microbial communities and their activities. As a result, soil organic carbon tends to decrease with depth due to limited organic matter inputs from aboveground biomass and reduced biological activity in deeper layers. However, photosynthesis is effectively blocked in deeper soil layers due to lack of light penetration, reduced root systems, and microbial activity decline as sunlight cannot penetrate deep into the soil profile, limiting photosynthesis because plant roots are concentrated near the surface, allowing for easier access to nutrients and water. As depth increases, microbial populations contributing to soil organic carbon formation tend to decline due to lower oxygen availability. These factors collectively contribute to a decrease in SOC levels.
Wu et al. found that the sand-consolidating cyanobacterium Scytonema hyalinum is well-suited for cultivation in municipal wastewater, capable of accumulating substantial biomass while achieving high nutrient removal efficiency, including COD, total dissolved nitrogen, and total dissolved phosphorus. This shows a great potential for producing sand-consolidating cyanobacterium using wastewater, with temperature being an important regulating element.
In an experiment by Guan et al. to test the reaction of the soil micro-food web to biocrust in a microcosm by varying the water content and air temperature, it was confirmed that, in comparison to bare soil, biocrusts greatly enhanced the abundance of nematodes and soil microbes. The soil micro-food web, however, was not significantly impacted by temperature changes and was further discovered that the primary variables influencing micro-food pathways under biocrust were soil pH and organic carbon. The best stable environment for the development of a complex and reticulated soil micro-food web was found in soil with 8% water content. The study also found support for different pathways regulating the warming-induced reduction in soil respiration at low and high initial biocrust cover levels with emphasis on the need to explicitly include biocrusts in modeling efforts to quantify soil C-climate feedback in drylands.

Relationship Between Biocrusts and Soil Biodiversity
Research on biocrust communities in dry or semi-arid environments has predominantly focused on temperate coastal dunes, as evidenced by studies such as those conducted by Samolov et al. and Li and Hu. These investigations have shed light on how increasing biotic cover and aridity influence soil properties and ecosystem functions in the coastal dune environments of the Iberian Peninsula, a topic further explored by Fernández-Alonso et al. (2021). In a seminal biodiversity study, Schulz et al. highlighted the ecological significance of biocrusts on the Baltic Sea coast, noting their impact on soil characteristics and the considerable diversity of site-specific microorganisms. Schaub et al. focused on the initial stages of biocrust succession in the Baltic Sea’s yellow dunes, where they documented how environmental factors such as sandblasting and the burial of phototrophic microbes affect the growth of dominant filamentous algae and cyanobacteria. Furthermore, Roncero-Ramos et al. demonstrated that biocrusts, especially those in sheltered landward sites, enhance soil fertility even in early successional stages by promoting the accumulation of soil organic matter, total nitrogen, and phosphate activity.
It has been found that biodiversity in biocrusts is predominantly driven by turnover, with generalist bacteria and specialists in eukaryotes contributing to variations in α diversity with succession. Microenvironments and macroclimates contribute to high turnover where bacterial α diversity grows during convergent succession, while distance decay reduces β diversity. Environmental restrictions impact successional α and β diversity variations, with edaphic influences at local and climatic ones at continental levels. Although they can also be found in other temperate zones with scarce vegetation, biocrusts are distinct biocoenoses containing microorganisms adapted for dry or semiarid climates. Biocrusts comprise lichens, bryophytes, microalgae, microfungi, cyanobacteria, and heterotrophic bacteria living in and on the soil surface and can withstand high quantities of radiation, prolonged desiccation times, and severe temperatures. When available moisture triggers metabolism, they expeditiously initiate photosynthesis. Green algae, lichens, bryophytes, heterotrophic bacteria, and filamentous cyanobacteria are the next in line to stabilize movable sand grains. Decreases in biomass, species diversity, and surface cover may arise from frequent disruptions. However, the findings from Chowaniec et al. when examining the photosynthetic pigment concentration and dehydrogenase activity in biocrust at various inland sandy grassland succession phases demonstrate that as inland shifting sands advance, photosynthetic biomass and total microbial activity in biocrust significantly increase.
In warm, humid continental regions, the concentration of chl a in biocrust can serve as a valuable indicator of the presence of photoautotrophs and the establishment of biocrust. Additionally, the study discovers a strong correlation between elevated microbial activity and photosynthetic biomass, indicating that photoautotrophs play a significant role in biocrusts. It should be noted that dune blowouts are important settings for biocrust development because they include a high biomass of microorganisms, which may be linked to organic matter and ideal moisture conditions and the deflation fields can aid with the continued settlement of dune slopes via biocrust. In another study by Karsten et al., a lineage of Klebsormidium (green algae) frequently found in biocrusts was discovered, originating in dry southern Africa. Using three different African strains of Klebsormidium subtilissimum from cold desert environments, all Klebsormidium strains produced the maximum amount of photosynthetic oxygen at low light speed and showed no signs of photo-inhibition under high light. At lower temperatures, photosynthesis was more efficient than respiration; at higher temperatures, the converse was true. According to a study by Holzinger et al., streptophyte green algae show molecular processes during desiccation stress that are comparable to those of land plants, indicating ancestor mechanisms for successfully colonizing terrestrial habitats. To gain a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms, future research should not only examine a single model organism but also consider strain- and clade-specific characteristics.

Constructing Artificial Biocrusts for Degradation Control
Artificial biocrusts are biocrusts that serve the same functions as natural biocrusts in damaged ecosystems which can be built to reduce soil erosion, increase soil fertility, and aid in ecosystem restoration efforts. The procedure entails choosing and inoculating microorganisms onto an appropriate substrate to encourage the formation of a stable biocrust community. Inoculants generally consist of genera from the cyanobacteria, including Microcoleus, Scytonema, Nostoc, among others. Some researchers also experiment with co-inoculating cyanobacteria and various functional bacteria. The substrate, which is usually sandy or clay soil, provides a favorable environment for microbial development and colonization while preserving moisture. The inoculation method entails spreading microbial inoculants carrying the desired species over the substrate surface.
A microbial biocrust schematic workflow cultivation and application of artificial biocrusts on nutrient-poor areas or degraded land
Artificial biocrusts must be properly maintained and monitored to ensure their success. Artificial biocrusts can greatly increase the robustness and sustainability of ecosystems by carefully choosing the microorganisms and substrates, as well as by using the right inoculation strategies. Also, Zhao et al. investigated the impact of various covers—such as nonwoven fabric, dust-proof net, and sun-shading net—on the colonization and growth of artificial cyanobacteria crusts in order to control dust and stabilize sand surfaces and revealed that fresh cyanobacteria coated in two layers of nonwoven fabric and one layer of sun-shading net produced the best results, with a 50.0% biocrust cover and a friction velocity that was noticeably higher than that of sand. Artificial biocrusts have an important role in soil stabilization, and preventing soil degradation in degraded environments. By soil stability, artificial biocrusts in their capacity immediately form a protective layer on the soil surface, limiting further degradation due to wind and water erosion and can also withstand natural stressors such as drought and severe heat (Fig. 3).

Artificial biocrusts have been demonstrated in studies to be effective in restoring degraded soils in a variety of settings, including mining sites, range lands, and agricultural fields. Researchers and land managers can work together to restore degraded soils and promote ecosystem resilience in these sensitive settings by tapping into the ecological activities of natural biocrust communities. Also, the physiological characteristics of biocrusts and the makeup of their microbial communities show that photoautotrophs have a major impact on the physiological characteristics and composition of heterotrophic microorganisms, presumably by affecting the physicochemical habitat parameters (Fig. 4). The physiological characteristics of various biocrust successional phases are probably determined by the composition of the communities and particular ecological conditions.
The study from Li et al. focuses on the functions that biocrusts play in the self-organization of desert ecosystems as well as soil ecological, hydrological, landscape, and biogeochemical processes. Using large-scale model simulations and molecular biology to investigate underlying systems, the research has progressed from local to regional to landscape sizes. Understanding the spatiotemporal distributions of biocrusts has been superseded by an understanding of the multi-scale ecosystem and landscape-level processes and response mechanisms experiencing climate change. However, subsequent investigations ought to concentrate on describing the poisons generated in arid areas and their existence in medicinal and ecological components as well as to concentrate on the selection of species and inoculation methods for artificial biocrusts, such as the use of network-structured nanocomposite for the colonization and growth of lichen, moss, and cyanobacteria on sand surfaces in grasslands that have been desertified.

Long-term Monitoring and Assessment of Biocrust Functions and Ecosystem Services
Following their role in maintaining ecosystem function in dryland settings, biocrusts have emerged as an essential tool for ecological restoration. It has been emphasized by Berkowitz et al. on how crucial it is to monitor and evaluate biocrust functions and ecosystem services over an extended period of time in order to comprehend the advantages that dredged material-supported habitat enhancement initiatives have for ecology and engineering. It implies that these initiatives safeguard shorelines, store carbon, and offer important habitat for a variety of animals. Through project design and operational management, particular ecological functions should be the main focus. Despite advancements in biocrust restoration technology, there is a lack of information on monitoring biocrust functioning and setting appropriate restoration goals. The effects of climate change on global dryland ecosystems’ C and N cycling and water balance have important ramifications for both earth system dynamics and dryland restoration. Contributions of biocrust to ecosystem function differ depending on the species, stage of growth, and successional condition.
Long-term monitoring of some other ecosystems (e.g., wetlands) has been made possible by the Landsat Earth Observation Program, which has been vital in providing important insights about the state of conservation and changes in these ecosystems. Stress factors have the potential to impact soil microbes and tree roots, hence reducing tree vigor and productivity in urban areas. Research on soil microbial activity and mycorrhizal symbiosis are crucial for tracking how trees adapt to urban environments. Furthermore, the study of Rodríguez-Caballero et al. highlighted that in order to comprehend the ecological and engineering benefits of habitat development efforts, long-term monitoring and assessment of biocrust functions are necessary. Through project design and operational management, particular ecological functions should be the main focus, and delivering ecosystem services will greatly aided by biocrusts, which are essential ecosystem elements in semiarid areas.
In conclusion, understanding environmental changes and putting into practice successful restoration plans depend on the monitoring and assessment of ecosystem functioning across a variety of habitats. To improve our understanding of these ecosystems and advance sustainable management techniques, more investigation and observation are required. Degraded biocrust communities can recover more quickly when restoration approaches including soil stabilization, habitat restoration, and inoculation with native biocrust species are used. Monitoring procedures can be used to assess the influence on biocrust populations and track progress, while long-term planning incorporates biocrust conservation into more comprehensive ecosystem management methods.

The selection, cultivation, and inoculation of strains for degradation control in biocrusts involve a complex process that requires careful selection of suitable strains, provision of optimal growth conditions, and strategic inoculation techniques (Table 3). These techniques are critical for enhancing the degradation control capabilities of biocrusts. The selection of a desiccation-tolerant strain is crucial as it must survive and thrive in degraded soils, especially in dryland regions. In the initial selection stage, it is crucial to identify suitable strains that exhibit strong degradation control capabilities. This is typically achieved through a process of screening a wide variety of strains under controlled laboratory conditions to assess their degradation control potential. Following the selection, cultivation and inoculation of cyanobacterial strains and their consortia are performed, resulting in the formation of biocrusts in various soil types. The cultivation of the chosen strains involves the creation of suitable growth environments which can be achieved by providing the strains with optimal growth conditions, such as appropriate temperature, pH, moisture levels, and nutrient availability. The strains are then allowed to multiply and grow over a period of time.
In the inoculation stage, the cultivated strains are introduced into the biocrusts. This is typically achieved using a variety of techniques such as spraying, injection, or manual mixing. The inoculated biocrusts are then left to establish and proliferate, thereby enhancing the degradation control capabilities of the biocrust. Studies have highlighted the significant contribution of biocrusts to ecological and hydrological processes in deserts, as well as their applications in cultivating and inoculating non-vascular plants for controlling land degradation and facilitating ecological restoration.
Artificially produced biocrusts using cyanobacterial inoculation technology are regarded as one of the most promising ways for large-scale and long-term repair of damaged dryland soils. It is important to note that the success of these techniques relies heavily on the specific characteristics of the selected strains, as well as the specific environmental conditions of the biocrusts such as temperature and precipitation. Long-term monitoring of sand-binding vegetation in some desert environments has shown that physical crusts with high clay and silt concentrations play an important role in biocrust formation. Therefore, ongoing monitoring and adjustment of the cultivation and inoculation techniques may be necessary to ensure optimal degradation control.

Success Stories in Biocrust Restoration Applications
In the realm of biocrust restoration applications, success stories serve as valuable insights into the effectiveness and feasibility of various restoration techniques. The restoration of biocrusts has demonstrated encouraging outcomes in several applications, as scientists and environmentalists are employing inventive methods to promote the resuscitation of biocrusts in arid environments. An ongoing groundbreaking project from the USA aims to cultivate whole biocrust communities on a large scale, with the possibility that this initiative could lead to significant advancements in restoring biocrusts in the face of climate change, aiding in ecological restoration and protecting dryland ecosystems globally. A study conducted by Bowker et al. documented a remarkable success story in the restoration of biocrust in a degraded ecosystem. Through the implementation of targeted restoration techniques such as inoculation with native biocrust species and soil stabilization measures, the researchers were able to witness a significant recovery of biocrust cover and diversity over a relatively short period of time. This success story underscores the importance of careful planning and execution in biocrust restoration efforts.
Also, slurry inoculation techniques have also shown promise in speeding up the re-establishment of soil cryptogamic biocrust in damaged locations such as mining sites. Additionally, efforts to raise public awareness about the importance of biocrusts have been successful in promoting conservation practices that protect these fragile ecosystems. These success stories demonstrate how creative restoration methods and teamwork in research can lead to beneficial outcomes in the conservation of biocrust and the restoration of ecosystems. Studies on slurry and psyllium supplements are confirmed to help restore the arid zone biocrust where the performance of diluted slurry facilitates larger-scale applications without jeopardizing already-existing biocrust regions. Although psyllium has the potential to improve biocrust coherence, more research is needed to determine its long-term impacts. Cyanobacterial inoculation can be improved by including polysaccharides (xanthan gum, alginate), hunmic substances, silicates, and biochar into the final inoculum formulation, which can significantly enhance their effectiveness in improving soil aggregate stability. Another study by Chi et al. effectively combined chemical and cyanobacterial inoculation with a network-structured nanocomposite, which was shown biosafe and effective in supporting the rapid production of cyanobacteria-induced biocrusts. Selecting the appropriate chemical assistance agents based on the inoculated species and inoculation circumstances is critical for creating this technology.
Although biocrust inoculants offer potential as materials for rehabilitation, in field settings they frequently do not develop. According to the research, adding biocrust inoculum improved biocrust establishment over a 1.5-year period but did not result in complete recovery. The application of jute net to the soil surface promoted biocrust establishment, suggesting that it stabilizes the soil surface, reduces abiotic stress, and enhances resource availability. However, while the methods for improving habitat are successful, hardening strategies are still difficult to implement. Furthermore, as highlighted by Kidron et al. and Raggio et al., a comprehensive review of various biocrust restoration projects revealed several key success stories across different geographical regions. These success stories showcased the resilience of biocrust communities and the positive impact of restoration efforts on ecosystem health and function.
The study of Maggioli et al. built upon these findings by investigating innovative techniques to improve cyanobacterial survival and growth in inoculated dryland soils. Their study identified several plant-based amelioration strategies, such as the use of organic amendments and protective mesh covers, which significantly enhanced the growth of cyanobacteria. These techniques reduced abiotic stresses and improved field application outcomes, suggesting practical pathways for enhancing biocrust restoration efforts in degraded drylands. The emphasis on practical techniques that can be implemented in the field highlights a critical direction for future research in biocrust restoration.
Chiquoine et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of biocrust inoculation in rapidly restoring ecosystem functions in severely disturbed desert ecosystems. Their study underscores the importance of targeted inoculation strategies in restoration practices, revealing that inoculated plots achieved lichen and moss compositions comparable to those of undisturbed controls and facilitated significant recovery of cyanobacteria density. Notably, their findings suggest that while topsoil application can partially restore cyanobacteria communities, inoculation alone is more effective in promoting lichen and moss cover. In a contrasting environment, Ficko et al. investigated biocrust restoration techniques in Arctic regions, where industrial activities have led to significant biocrust degradation. Their research demonstrated that various inoculant dispersal and habitat amelioration techniques successfully established lichens, highlighting that restoration strategies must be tailored to specific environmental conditions. These findings underscore the critical need to understand local ecological dynamics and species interactions when implementing biocrust restoration in challenging environments.
Despite the progress made in biocrust restoration research, several challenges remain. A study by Zhou et al. pointed out that while laboratory cultivation and field inoculations of biocrust materials have shown promise, there is still a need for further research to assess the long-term impacts of biocrust restoration on vegetation diversity and ecological processes. The necessity for sustained investigations into the ecological implications of biocrust restoration is paramount for understanding the broader impacts of these initiatives. Moreover, ongoing research must focus on scaling successful laboratory techniques to field applications, as many studies have highlighted discrepancies between controlled experiments and real-world outcomes. As highlighted by Young et al. and Chamizo et al., the establishment of biocrusts in natural conditions can be hindered by various abiotic factors, necessitating the development of robust field techniques that can effectively mitigate these challenges.

Field Management Strategies for Implementing Biocrust Restoration
Biocrust restoration is a crucial process that requires careful planning and management strategies to ensure its successful establishment and long-term benefits. Climate change and human activities both contribute to land deterioration in drylands which have been restored using techniques such as inoculating soil with cyanobacteria. The capacity of cyanobacteria to manufacture exopolysaccharides (EPS) enhances soil properties, although these effects vary among species and under different environmental conditions. A comprehensive site assessment is essential to select the most appropriate biocrust species based on the specific characteristics of the site. Further steps include the introduction of biocrust fragments, seeds, or inocula, management of water availability, disturbance control, and the monitoring and maintenance of restoration progress. Engaging stakeholders, land managers, and local communities in the restoration process, along with adopting an adaptive management approach, is crucial for success.
Cyanobacteria inoculation in the soil creates EPS, which alters soil characteristics and helps to reduce soil erosion. The study by Chamizo et al. reveals that inoculation with Microcoleus vaginatus and Stylidium javanicum improves soil stability, encourages soil colonization, and accelerates biocrust succession. Synthesized EPS also improves water retention and helps to keep moisture availability. However, on sandy soils, EPS can slow water flow and reduce hydraulic conductivity. Soil hydrophobicity rose in sandy soils inoculated with S. javanicum, probably due to enhanced growth and EPS release. This increase in hydrophobicity may shorten the time it takes for runoff to begin and enhance runoff output, supplying excess water to downslope vegetation patches and promoting plant survival in drylands. However, a larger EPS level may impair hydraulic conductivity, thus changing the time of runoff. The study discovered that the monosaccharidic properties of the released EPS and sheath may not always give an advantage for survival in sandy settings, as seen by Nostoc commune’s limited development. The decreased size of the produced polymers may have inhibited N. commune development in sandy soils. Studies has shown that inoculating soil with cyanobacterial biomass and released EPS can bring additional benefits, such as low MW molecules for soil heterotrophic microbes and a varied monosaccharidic media. The study stresses the need of taking into account the features of EPS generated during culture growth for developing successful soil restoration approaches.
The restoration of degraded soils is a long-term process, and it is crucial to focus on their long-term effects. As demonstrated in the studies mentioned above, the construction of artificial biocrusts significantly influences the nutrient cycling, hydraulic conductivity, and other properties of the inoculated soils. However, we currently lack long-term monitoring of artificial biocrusts in the field and the management strategies developed based on such monitoring.
Adaptive management allows for flexibility and adjustment based on monitoring data and feedback from stakeholders. This approach allows for continuous evaluation of the effectiveness of restoration strategies and makes informed decisions to optimize outcomes. By following these strategies, land managers and restoration practitioners can enhance the success of biocrust restoration efforts and contribute to the conservation and restoration of healthy soils and ecosystems.

Estimates of the biocrust recovery period can be inaccurate, particularly when made shortly after a disturbance. Recovery times, often based on biocrust cover and species composition, are frequently underestimated. The restoration of biocrusts faces several challenges that require comprehensive solutions (Table 4). In dry, fine-grained soils, soil stripping exposes an underlying horizon with distinct edaphic characteristics, underscoring the importance of abiotic factors. The recovery time for biocrusts in mesic environments may be comparable to that in arid settings. While biocrust species exhibit a robust capacity for development, with a typical recovery period of less than a year in laboratory conditions, field regeneration rates vary significantly: cyanobacteria crusts may regenerate within five to ten years, whereas lichen and moss crusts often require ten to twenty years for full recovery.
Climate, soil properties, disturbance intensity, and timing are some of the variables that affect the biocrusts’ natural recovery. Recuperation is frequently a dynamic process that is impacted by nonlinear external factors like unusual weather and the process proceeds in a succession pattern, with lichens and bryophytes coming after cyanobacteria and algae. However, variables such as fire, grazing trampling, and topographic conditions can affect the timing of this developmental process. These factors may cause biocrusts to remain perpetually in the early stages dominated by cyanobacteria, preventing further development into lichen- or moss-dominated biocrusts.

Potential for Innovations and Strategies
Biocrusts are essential for sustaining dryland environments by stabilizing soils, boosting fertility, retaining moisture, and storing carbon. Investigating novel approaches to augment biocrust formation and facilitate their regeneration indicates that to promote the formation of biocrust and increase soil fertility, one strategy with high feasibility is to inoculate the soil with cyanobacteria that can withstand desiccation. For example, cyanobacteria possess specialized cells (heterocysts) that create an anaerobic environment conducive for the enzyme nitrogenase to convert nitrogen (N2) into ammonia (NH3). This process enriches the soil with bioavailable nitrogen, which is essential for plant growth. Research by has indicated that the introduction of cyanobacteria, such as Nostoc calcicola BOT1 and Scytonema spp. BOT2, into substrates poor in nutrients and water, such as coarse sand, fine sand, and loamy soil, can lead to a notable increase in soil fertility. The collaboration produced the best results, encouraging the growth of rice seedlings on all kinds of soil. The EPS content was more significantly impacted by the introduction of N. calcicola BOT1. A year after being desiccated, biocrusts were resurrected with the significance of choosing cyanobacterial species according to their functional roles and suitability for particular soil conditions. The findings imply that a cyanobacterial consortium may provide a viable and effective means of encouraging plant development in difficult settings.
It has been shown that using cyanobacterial contributes significantly to the organic matter contents in the soil through their biomass production. As they grow and proliferate, they produce extracellular polysaccharides and other organic compounds that enhance soil structure and stability. However, when cyanobacterial cells die or are decomposed by soil microorganism, they release nutrients back into the soil, enriching it further. This increase in organic matter not only improves nutrient retention but also enhances water-holding capacity and aeration within the soil. Improved soil structure also reduces erosion risk and helps maintain moisture levels during dry periods because the polysaccharides produced by cyanobacteria during their growth help in binding soil particles together, leading to improved aggregation. Well-aggregated soils have better porosity and permeability, which facilitate root penetration and enhance water infiltration. Studies as showed that cyanobacterial species has solubilize inorganic phosphates from mineral sources in the soil through secretion of organic acids as citric and oxalic acid. This process makes phosphorus more available to plants, addressing common nutrient limitations in soil and enhanced energy transfer within plants (a key role in photosynthesis).
Furthermore, some cyanobacteria produce various phytohormones (auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins) can help promoting plant growth directly or indirectly by enhancing root development or increasing nutrient uptake efficiency. These substances can stimulate seed germination, root elongation, and overall plant vigor. Also, some cyanobacterial strains exhibit biocontrol properties against pathogenic microorganisms in the soil due to their ability to produce antimicrobial compounds. By suppressing harmful pathogens, these beneficial cyanobacteria help maintain a healthier rhizosphere environment for plants. Future directions and implications include expanding cyanobacterial consortia for large-scale land rehabilitation projects, investigating the long-term effects of cyanobacterial inoculation on soil health and ecosystem resilience, and working with scientists, conservation organizations, and policymakers to develop creative approaches to biocrust restoration and sustainable land management techniques.

Action and Policy Support for Biocrust Restoration Projects
The success of biocrust restoration projects is contingent upon public support and efficient policy implementation; choice experiments (CE) surveys provide essential evidence that local residents value environmental quality improvements over flood control, highlighting the need for policymakers to align restoration objectives with community preferences. Technology, including social media and smartphone apps, can also positively impact community engagement and awareness-raising for policy changes related to habitat restoration,land managers can effectively communicate with the public and increase community resilience in the face of environmental challenges by leveraging technology. When implementing biocrust restoration projects, it is essential to consider the broader context of climate change and sustainability because the impact of air traffic on climate change is substantial, and lowering emissions can help lessen biocrust effects.
Adequate regulations, modifications in conduct, research, monitoring, cooperation, policy development, education, restoration methodologies, long-term planning, and monitoring are all necessary to support biocrust restoration programs. Developing successful restoration techniques requires a thorough understanding of biocrust ecology, biodiversity, and reactions to environmental stressors. Developing collaborative restoration initiatives requires cooperation with a wide range of stakeholders, including scientists, land managers, legislators, local populations, and non-profit groups. The preservation of biocrust habitats has to be given top priority when developing policies, which should include financial sources, incentives for restoration, and restrictions on land use. Outreach and education can encourage community involvement and increase knowledge of the significance of biocrusts.

Conclusions and Perspectives
Soil health is an integral component of ecosystem sustainability, agricultural productivity, and environmental conservation. Biocrusts—communities of cyanobacteria, algae, lichens, bryophytes, and fungi that colonize the soil surface in arid and semi-arid regions—play a pivotal role in enhancing soil health through their complex microbial interactions. Understanding the microbial ecosystems within biocrusts provides essential insights into strategies for controlling degradation and restoring degraded soils. This review examines the mechanisms by which biocrusts improve soil health, focusing on nutrient cycling, soil stabilization, carbon sequestration, and water retention. It synthesizes current research on the ecological functions of biocrusts, highlighting their potential as a sustainable solution to mitigate soil degradation and enhance ecosystem resilience. Biocrusts have shown promising results in enhancing soil health, stabilizing soils, increasing water infiltration, fixing nitrogen, and sequestering carbon, offering a sustainable approach to land rehabilitation that aligns with conservation objectives. The preservation and promotion of biocrusts in both natural and managed landscapes can significantly enhance soil fertility and resilience. Nonetheless, further research is required to elucidate the mechanisms underlying biocrust functionality, optimize their application across different environmental contexts, and evaluate their long-term impacts on soil quality and biodiversity. Future studies should investigate the roles of specific microbial taxa within biocrust communities, assess the resilience of biocrusts to changing environmental conditions, develop practical guidelines for integrating biocrusts into restoration strategies, evaluate the economic benefits of biocrust applications for soil health, and explore the interactions between biocrusts and aboveground vegetation in ecosystem processes.
