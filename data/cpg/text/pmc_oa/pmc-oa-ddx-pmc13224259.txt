The Wiltse paraspinal approach in lumbar surgery: A systematic review
Background:
The Wiltse paraspinal approach utilizes the natural cleavage plane between the multifidus and longissimus muscles to minimize tissue trauma. Despite its integration into minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS), standardized evidence across anatomical and clinical domains remains fragmented.
Methods:
A Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses-compliant systematic review was conducted across major medical databases through June 2024.
Results:
A total of 15 studies met the inclusion criteria. Anatomical evidence confirmed a reproducible intermuscular plane, most identifiable at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels. Technically, the approach provided effective exposure for decompressions and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusions. Clinical comparisons demonstrated significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss and lower early postoperative pain versus conventional midline approaches, with equivalent long-term outcomes.
Conclusion:
The Wiltse approach is a reliable, muscle-sparing technique and remains a cornerstone of contemporary MISS.

INTRODUCTION
Wiltse’s paraspinal corridor exploits the natural intermuscular cleavage plane between the multifidus and longissimus muscles. This approach represents a fundamental shift toward tissue-sparing spinal surgery, preserving the medial branch of the posterior primary rami and the segmental blood supply. In contemporary lumbar minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS), the Wiltse corridor is versatile for decompression, pedicle screw fixation, and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusions (TLIFs). Hydrodissection and specialized retractors further enhance reproducibility and surgical exposure. In this systematic review, we evaluated the anatomical validity, technical evolution, and clinical outcomes of the Wiltse approach as applied to contemporary lumbar MISS versus open surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, we assessed heterogeneous anatomical, technical, and clinical outcome measures using the Wiltse approach in MISS versus open lumbar procedures. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase through June 2024 using terms “Wiltse approach,” “paraspinal approach,” and “sacrospinalis-splitting approach.” Two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Inclusion criteria included anatomical characterizations and clinical comparative studies in adult lumbar surgery. Methodological quality was assessed using ROBINS-I, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, and the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist.

RESULTS
A total of 15 studies met the inclusion criteria: Anatomical (33.3%), technical (26.7%), and clinical (40.0%) [Figure 1]. Evidence was of moderate-to-high quality [Table 1]. A summary of included studies is also presented in Table 2. Anatomical studies confirmed a reproducible intermuscular cleavage plane providing a clear corridor to the posterior lumbar elements while preserving midline structures, most reliably at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels [Figure 2]. Technically, the approach evolved from blunt dissection to minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS) adaptations using hydrodissection and tubular or muscle-splitting retractor systems, which facilitate decompressions and TLIFs with reduced soft tissue disruption [Figure 3]. Clinical outcomes demonstrated that Wiltse MISS significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss and early pain versus conventional open midline techniques, particularly in limited multilevel procedures. Long-term fusion rates and functional scores were comparable for both techniques.

DISCUSSION
The corridor’s validity is a principal strength, particularly at the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels [Figure 2]. It preserves the multifidus muscle to maintain segmental spinal stability and prevent the atrophy typically associated with open midline subperiosteal dissection. MIS adaptations, including hydrodissection and tubular retractors, have further expanded its utility for MIS-TLIF (Wiltse and Spencer 1988) [Figure 3]. Primary perioperative advantages include reduced intraoperative blood loss, lower pain scores, and faster recovery. Crucially, these early benefits do not compromise long-term efficacy, as fusion rates and functional outcomes remain comparable for both procedures. Despite biological advantages, underutilization persists in some centers, requiring standardized anatomical education and technical guidelines [Table 1].

CONCLUSION
The Wiltse paraspinal approach represents a biologically sound, anatomically validated corridor for lumbar surgery. Its major advantages include reduced blood loss and pain while maintaining long-term clinical outcomes comparable to traditional open midline techniques.
