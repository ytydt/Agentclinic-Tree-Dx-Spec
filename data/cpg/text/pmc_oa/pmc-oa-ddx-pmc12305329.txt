The genetic puzzle of rheumatoid arthritis: Causes, progression, and treatment
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a multifaceted autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic inflammation and progressive joint destruction, influenced by a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors. Substantial evidence highlights a significant genetic contribution to RA pathogenesis, with key genetic risk factors including human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes and non-HLA variants. These genetic elements are intricately involved in immune dysregulation, antigen presentation, and signaling pathways. The genetic heterogeneity of RA is further accentuated by gene-gene and gene-environment interactions, while biomarkers and genetic profiles associated with disease progression and joint damage continue to be rigorously explored. Additionally, the evolving field of pharmacogenomics sheds light on the challenges and prospects of developing personalized therapeutic approaches for RA. Genetic markers are being explored to predict response to various RA therapies, including DMARDs and biologics. Understanding genetic risk factors and pharmacogenomic insights can support early diagnosis, predict disease severity and progression, and improve therapeutic decisions in RA patients. The main objective of this review is to comprehensively explore current knowledge regarding the genetic factors contributing to RA susceptibility, progression, and treatment response. Furthermore, by addressing genetic risk factors, gene-environment interactions, and emerging pharmacogenomic insights, the review aims to highlight critical gaps and future directions in genetic researches related to RA.
Highlights
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease marked by persistent inflammation and progressive joint damage.
Genetic factors are critical in determining RA susceptibility, disease progression, and therapeutic responses.
Variants in HLA genes and non-HLA genes significantly influence RA risk, severity, and immune dysfunction.
Genetic research could enhance diagnostic accuracy, develop targeted therapies, and improve patient’s outcome in RA.

Introduction
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by systemic, long-lasting inflammation, swelling, pain, and pannus formation in synovial tissues. Due to inflammatory signals, various immune cells migrate into synovial tissues and interact with other resident immune and non-immune cells in synovial tissues. These interactions, along with the inflammatory medium of synovium, lead to the activation of immune and non-immune cells, which cause chronic inflammation in synovial tissues of RA patients. Chronic inflammation in RA can eventually lead to bone and cartilage destruction. The immune system plays a critical role in RA development and gives us a good insight into RA pathogenesis. RA can manifest in any period of life, but the most prevalent age for RA development ranges from 30 to 50. RA is 2–3 times more frequent in females compared with males. The mortality rate is 2.5 times higher in RA patients in comparison to healthy control. Management in RA patients increased their survival rate. Nonetheless, RA patients have a lifespan of 5–10 years lower than healthy people.
Generally, the incidence rate of RA remains almost consistent across various populations, ranging from 0.5 to 1 %. Although the highest prevalence (5.3–6.8 %) is reported in the natives of North America, the lowest prevalence (0.2–0.3 %) is reported in Japan and China. The prevalence rate of RA in families with an RA patient is approximately 2–4 %. Furthermore, this rate is reported at 5–10 % for dizygotic twins and 12–30 % for monozygotic twins. These reports indicate that genetic factors play a significant role in RA development, with an estimated genetic contribution ranging from 50 to 60 %. Over 100 loci have been found to play a role in RA development. Some of the most specific genes that are linked with an increased RA susceptibility, including HLA-DRB1, PTPN22, PADI-4, the signal transducer and activator of transcription 4 (STAT4), FCγR2A, CCL21, CTLA-4, IRF5, TRAF1, CD40, CCR6, and IL2RA. Variations in genes within the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region, such as HLA-DRB1 alleles, and non-HLA genes like PTPN22, STAT4, and TNFAIP3, are strongly linked to RA susceptibility, disease severity, disease progression, and treatment response. Variants within the HLA-DRB1 gene, known as shared epitope alleles, increase susceptibility to RA by influencing antigen presentation and T-cell activation, which leads to autoimmune responses against self-antigens. Genetic factors also influence the production of autoantibodies, such as anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs) and rheumatoid factor (RF), contributing to the development and progression of RA by targeting joint tissues and promoting inflammation and tissue damage. Additionally, variations in genes involved in immune regulation, such as PTPN22, CTLA4, and TNFAIP3, disrupt immune homeostasis and tolerance, leading to aberrant immune responses and chronic inflammation in RA. Genetic variations in cytokine genes, including the tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1 (IL-1), further influence cytokine production and signaling pathways involved in inflammation and joint destruction in RA. Moreover, genetic factors affecting the regulatory T cells, which play a crucial role in maintaining immune tolerance and preventing autoimmunity, are implicated in RA pathogenesis. Genetic interactions with epigenetic mechanisms, such as histone modifications and DNA methylation, regulate gene expression patterns in RA-relevant cells, which causes disease progression.
Understanding the genetic basis of RA offers insights into the disease mechanisms and facilitates personalized medicine approaches. Genetic research is crucial for unraveling the causes of RA by pinpointing genetic markers that contribute to the disease's progression. This research could shed light on the underlying mechanisms of RA, deepening our understanding of its etiology. Furthermore, it paves the way for personalized medicine, enabling the development of targeted therapies and preventive strategies tailored to individual genetic profiles. Ultimately, these insights can improve diagnostic tools and enhance treatment efficacy, improving outcomes for patients with RA. We discuss RA pathogenesis's major genetic risk factors. Furthermore, the genetic heterogeneity of RA, considering the influence of gene-environment and gene-gene interactions, is explored. It is also discussed the genetic determinants of disease progression and their associations with clinical presentations. We present current knowledge about the role of genetics in RA pathogenesis and foster future research directions in this field. Indeed, our review article aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current knowledge on the genetic factors contributing to RA susceptibility, disease severity, and the potential use of genetics for RA management.

The contribution of HLA genes in RA susceptibility (Table 1) is estimated to be around 50 %, and some studies suggest that the HLA genes are the most important genetic risk factors in RA development. Therefore, it is essential to take a closer look at these genes. The HLA genes have three classes, and each class has its own set of genes: Class I (HLA-A, B, C), Class II (HLA-DP, DQ, DR), and Class III (HLA-C4, C2, TNF, HSP-70). Class II alleles are the most significant genetic risk factors for RA development (almost 30 %). These alleles encode molecules that express on the surface of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and play a critical role in various immune responses by presenting antigens to CD4+ T cells. The HLA class II has classical (HLA-DR, DQ, and DP), and nonclassical (DM, DO) genes.
Variations in individual amino acid positions, mainly within HLA-DR molecules, form antigen-binding grooves that significantly contribute to RA susceptibility. The significance of these positions within HLA-DR molecules is that these positions could lead to abnormal immune responses of T cells in RA disease. Among HLA-DR genes, the HLA-DRB1 locus, containing conserved amino acid sequences (RRRAA, QKRAA, QRRAA) at positions 70–74 in the third hypervariable region, strongly associates with RA. Various alleles of the HLA-DRB1 have been associated with RA susceptibility in different ethnicities. For instance, the HLA-DRB1∗04/01 and -∗04/04 alleles were associated with an increased risk of RA development in the European population. Furthermore, the HLA-DRB1∗04/05 allele in Asia and the HLA-DRB1∗14/02 allele in the USA were reported as a susceptible risk factor for RA development. In the Sudanese population, the HLA-DRB1∗04 and ∗01 alleles were associated with ACPA(+) RA patients, while the HLA-DRB1∗08 allele was the most frequent in ACPA(−) RA patients. The HLA-DRB1∗13 and ∗01 alleles, characterized by the amino acid sequence DERAA at 70–74 positions, showed a protective effect against the development of ACPA+ RA patients. Interestingly, previous research has documented that all HLA-DRB1 alleles with the DERAA sequence protect against RA development. Variations in amino acid at 11/13/71/74 positions in HLA-DRB1 showed an increased risk of RA development. RA patients with RF and ACPA autoantibodies exhibited a strong association with these variations.
It is shown that the HLA-DPB1∗04/01 and 06/01 alleles have a higher frequency in RA patients compared to the healthy control group, indicating a susceptible genetic risk factor for RA development. Conversely, the HLA-DPB1∗01/01, ∗04/02, and ∗05/01 alleles showed lower frequency in RA patients than in healthy control subjects, suggesting a protective role for RA development. HLA-DPB1 alleles play a significant role in RA development, influencing both susceptibility and severity of the disease. Several studies have documented that the HLA-DPB1∗04/01 alleles are more frequent in RA patients than in healthy controls. HLA-DPB1∗02/01 alleles are significantly associated with RA susceptibility in the Japanese. The HLA-DPB1∗03/01 alleles were linked with RF-negative RA patients in the European population. In the Japanese population, the HLA-DPB1∗02 and ∗01 alleles showed susceptible risk factors, while the HLA-DPB1∗05 and ∗01 alleles showed a protective effect for ACPA(+) RA development.
The HLA-DQB1∗03 allele showed a susceptible risk factor for RA development, while the HLA-DRB1∗07 allele showed a protective effect against RA development. The HLA-DQB1∗04 allele showed a susceptible risk factor, while DQB1∗02 and ∗06 alleles confer protection against RA development.

Genes that are involved in immune dysregulation
The protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor 22 (PTPN22) gene is the most influential non-HLA genetic predisposition factor in RA development. This gene produces a 110-KDa protein known as lymphoid tyrosine phosphatase (LYP), widely expressed in innate and adaptive immune cells. This protein is critical in immune response processes, including TCR, BCR, and TLR signaling pathways. The rs2476601 (R620W) single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 22 (PTPN22) gene, which replaces the cytosine with thymidine (1858 C/T), is associated with an increased risk of RA development in Caucasian ethnicity. This SNP is linked with loss of tolerance against self-antigens and overproduction of autoantibodies such as ACPA and RF in RA patients. Moreover, this SNP also could be associated with high levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ cytokines and low levels of IL-4 and IL-10 cytokines in RA patients. The R620W polymorphism could shift the balance of Th1/Th2 lymphocytes towards Th1.
Furthermore, the SNP could also reduce the binding capacity of LYP to the C-terminal Src kinases (Csk's), thereby inhibiting Csk's inhibitory function. Therefore, T-lymphocytes could be overactivated, which is associated with increased susceptibility to RA. In contrast to the rs2476601, the rs33996649 (R263Q) SNP, which replaces the arginine with glutamine, could alter the structure of the active site in the PTPN22 protein. This change could decrease the catalytic activity of PTPN22, which is associated with a reduced risk of RA development in Mexican and European populations.
The peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PADI-4), a calcium-dependent enzyme, is encoded by the PADI-4 gene. The physiologic function of PADI-4 is the conversion of arginine to citrulline, a post-translational modification. In RA patients, this citrullination occurs in various proteins such as fibrinogen, filaggrin, vimentin, collagen, and actin. Citrullination can induce monocyte extracellular traps (METs) and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formations. Furthermore, citrullination can trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-α, IL-12, and TNF-α, which play roles in RA pathogenesis. Citrullination is linked with the production of anti-citrullinated peptide/protein auto-antibodies (ACPAs), detected in 60–70 % of RA patients. It is also noteworthy that 29 % of RA patients in North America exhibit autoantibodies directed against the PADI-4 enzyme.. The PADI4-94 G/A polymorphism was associated with RA susceptibility in the Asian population. The PADI4-92C/G polymorphism was associated with RA susceptibility in an African population. An association was observed between the PADI4-90C/T polymorphism and RA susceptibility in the Latin American population. The PADI-4 rs2240340 polymorphism was associated with RA susceptibility in the Nordic population. The PADI4-104C/T polymorphism was associated with RA susceptibility in Chinese and Japanese populations. These studies demonstrated that polymorphisms in the PADI-4 gene can influence the functioning of the PADI-4 protein and, thereby, could be an essential factor in RA development.
The autoimmune regulator (AIRE) gene is responsible for encoding a pivotal transcription factor in immune tolerance and susceptibility to RA. Within the thymic medulla, this transcription factor is actively expressed, and its primary function is to present self-antigens to T cells, a critical step in immunologic tolerance. This mechanism ensures the immune system discriminates self-antigens from non-self-antigens. Some polymorphisms in this gene were linked with a reduced expression of the AIRE protein. Low levels of AIRE expression could decrease the presentation of self-antigens, and thereby, adverse selection will be dysregulated. This could be linked with the escape of autoimmune T-cells into the peripheral and cause autoimmune diseases such as RA. Different SNPs in the AIRE gene were reported to be associated with RA susceptibility in various populations. For instance, the rs2075786 polymorphism was reported to increase susceptibility risk in RA patients in Asian populations but not Caucasians. The rs760426 polymorphism showed a strong association with RA in the Japanese population. The rs878081 polymorphism in AIRE increased susceptibility risk in RA patients in the Chinese Han population. These studies demonstrated that polymorphisms in the AIRE gene can influence the functioning of the AIRE protein and could be an essential factor in RA development.
The cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) gene encodes cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 and acts as an antagonist for the CD28 receptor. The binding of CTLA-4 with CD80 or CD86 on the surface of APCs is linked with inhibitory signals that suppress T-cell activation. This action generally inhibits the immune system. Various autoimmune diseases, including RA, have been linked with polymorphisms in the CTLA-4 gene. Variations in the CTLA-4 gene could be associated with a reduced surface expression of CTLA-4 in immune cells. Reduced expression of CTLA-4 diminishes its inhibitory capacity, thereby promoting T-cell activation and proliferation.. Some SNPs in CTLA4 are reported in RA patients. For instance, the rs231775 polymorphism was associated with an increased risk of RA development in the Asian population but did not show the same effect on Caucasian and African populations. The rs3087243 polymorphism was found to be associated with a lower risk of RA development in both Caucasian and Asian populations. A meta-analysis confirmed that rs231775 and rs3087243 polymorphism are associated with an increased risk of RA development in the general population and ethnic-specific analysis. This study did not show a significant association between rs5742909 polymorphism and RA susceptibility. The rs5742909 has been reported to be associated with RA susceptibility in Egyptians and Canadians. These studies demonstrated that polymorphisms in the CTLA4 gene can influence the functioning of CTLA4 protein and could be an essential factor in RA development.

Genes that are involved in signaling pathways
The NF-κB signaling pathway plays a significant role in RA susceptibility through its effects in inflammation. Signaling through the NF-κB pathway could trigger the production of various proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, thereby increasing inflammatory conditions. These cytokines play a critical role in inflammation and the release of enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and collagenases, which break down cartilage and the extracellular matrix, resulting in bone and cartilage damage. Tumor necrosis factor alpha-inducible protein 3 (TNFAIP3), which produces the ubiquitin-editing protein (A20), suppresses NF-κB signaling and decreases susceptibility to RA. Accordingly, polymorphisms in NF-κB and TNFAIP3 could affect RA susceptibility. The rs2230926 and rs5029937 polymorphisms in TNFAIP3 showed a susceptible risk factor for RA development in European and Asian populations. The rs11574851 polymorphism in the nuclear factor kappa B subunit 2 (NFκB2) gene was associated with an increased risk of RA development in ACPA(+) RA patients. The results of these studies and the important role of the NF-κB signaling pathway in inflammation indicate that this pathway could be implicated in RA pathogenesis.
STAT4 protein participates in RA susceptibility through its involvement in the proliferation and differentiation of both Th1 and Th17 cells. IL17, primarily produced by Th17 cells, enhances the expression of various inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1 and TNFα in fibroblasts and macrophages. As a result, these cytokines could initiate inflammation in the joints of RA patients. Moreover, STAT4 is a transcription factor that plays a crucial role in signaling pathways of IL-12, IL-23, and type 1 interferon (IFN), essential for the differentiation and proliferation of Th1 and Th17 cells. The elevated protein levels of STAT4 in dendritic cells of the synovial membrane were correlated with the RF levels in the plasma of RA patients. The rs7574865 polymorphism in the STAT4 has been associated with RA susceptibility in Caucasians and Asians.
CD40 signaling pathway was overexpressed in T cells and monocytes in the synovial fluid of RA patients. It has been documented that the CD40 signaling pathway contributed to autoantibody production and overexpression of molecules such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa beta (RANKL) implicated in RA severity. Furthermore, CD40 signaling is involved in RA development through NF-κB activation, resulting in pro-inflammatory cytokine production and T-cell differentiation. CD40 is expressed on synovial cells, which can overexpress pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNFα and IFN-γ. In turn, a high level of CD40 expression may further be involved in the inflammatory response, leading to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, upregulation of adhesion molecules, proliferation of fibroblast, and finally, causing the development and progression of RA patients. Regarding the role of CD40 in inflammatory conditions, polymorphisms in this locus may affect RA susceptibility. Investigations have indicated a correlation between the rs4810485 polymorphism in the CD40 gene and the severity of joint damage in ACPA-positive RA patients.. The rs1569723, rs1883832, and rs4810485 polymorphisms showed an increased susceptibility in RA patients. Furthermore the rs13040307 and rs73115010 SNPs were associated with the increased number of swollen joints in RA patients.

Genes that are involved in antigen presentation
CD28, a 44-KDa type I transmembrane protein found on T cells, interacts with ligands such as CD80 (B7-1) or CD86 (B7-2), providing a crucial secondary signal to activate naïve T cells. The YMNM and PYAP motifs of CD28 play essential roles in activating nuclear factor of activated T-cells (NFAT), activator protein 1(AP1), and NF-κB, which lead to IL-2 expression. The IL-2 secretion is linked with T cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation. Additionally, CD28 plays a crucial role in producing pro-inflammatory cytokines/chemokines and supports T-cell survival. The critical function of CD28 in RA progression was first shown in a mouse model of collagen-induced arthritis. In this arthritis model, the recombinant CTLA-4 Ig is effectively bound to CD80 and CD86, preventing their interactions with CD28, thereby inhibiting lymphocyte proliferation and cytokine production. Therefore, the CD28 could play an essential role in inflammation and might be involved in RA development. GWAS revealed that CD28 polymorphisms are linked to increased susceptibility to RA. Rs3181096 and rs733618 polymorphisms in the CD28 gene were linked with high levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), which is connected with the severity of RA. The C-allele of rs1980422 polymorphism in the CD28 was significantly associated with RA susceptibility. Additionally, both CC and CT genotypes were associated with the increased risk of RA development, and the recessive model of CC further increased RA susceptibility. Furthermore, it is shown that the rs1980422 can be used as a predictor of methotrexate (MTX) therapy response in RA patients.
The interleukin 23 receptor (IL-23R), encoded by the IL-23R gene, is expressed by APCs such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and memory T cells. This receptor, consisting of IL-23Rα in complex with IL-12R β1, plays a crucial role in innate and adaptive immunity. Stimulated macrophages and dendritic cells primarily produce IL-23. This cytokine plays an essential role in autoimmunity by promoting inflammation through the Th17 cell activation. Th17 cells produce IL-17, which induces the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, MMPs, and chemokines. These inflammatory mediators facilitate the recruitment of neutrophils to the site of infection. This cascade ultimately leads to chronic inflammation and the destruction of joints and organs in RA patients. IL-23 was found in the serum and synovial fluid of RA patients, and numerous studies have shown a robust link between IL-23R polymorphisms and susceptibility to RA. The rs11209026 (G > A) polymorphism in IL-23R associated with RA susceptibility in is Egyptians. Further evaluations revealed that this SNP could modify the binding capacity between IL-23 and its receptor. Therefore, this SNP could be involved in RA pathogenesis through its effects on IL-23/IL-23R interactions. The rs2201841 SNP in the IL-23R gene showed an increased risk of RA susceptibility in Caucasians. The rs1004819(AA), rs10889677(AA), rs11805303(TT), and rs7530511(CT) genotypes in IL-23R polymorphisms had a higher frequency in RA patients than healthy controls. The rs1004819(AA) and rs7530511 (CT) genotypes in IL-23R polymorphisms showed an increased risk of RA development. The rs11805303(CC), rs10889677(CC), and rs2201841(TT) in IL-23R polymorphisms had a higher level of CRP and ESR than other genotypes. Furthermore, the rs11805303(CC) and rs1004819(GG) genotypes in IL-23R polymorphisms had more active RA disease. A meta-analysis revealed a significant association between the rs11209026 and rs1343151 polymorphisms and RA susceptibility in Caucasians. Furthermore, this study showed that the G allele of rs10489629 SNP has a higher frequency in RA patients of Asians and Caucasians. According to previous studies and this meta-analysis study, it could be concluded that the IL23R gene might be treated as a susceptible risk factor for RA development.
The IL-2 receptor consists of three subunits, α, β, and γ, expressed on immune cells such as B, T, and natural killer (NK) cells. Interaction of IL-2 with its receptors could have various vital effects, such as the production of inflammatory cytokines and fostering the differentiation of T lymphocytes to CD4+ and CD8+, boosting the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ cells, supporting the expansion of memory CD8+ cells, as well as differentiation of naïve T cells into Th1 and Th2 subsets. Among IL-2 receptors, the α subunit (IL-2RA/CD25) exhibits the strongest affinity for IL-2. Variations in the IL-2RA gene are linked to autoimmune and inflammatory conditions such as RA. The rs791588 genetic variant in the IL-2RA gene was associated with a lower risk of RA development. Indeed, it showed a protective effect on RA disease. Further analysis based on gender and age revealed that both rs791588 and rs2281089 SNPs were linked to decreased risk of RA development. Additionally, rs2104286 was the sole known genetic variant associated with both joint destruction and severity of RA. Additionally, the rs2104286 polymorphism and the soluble level of IL2Rα were associated with RA persistency. These data indicate the significance of the IL-2RA gene in RA pathogenesis.

Genetic heterogeneity in RA

Gene-gene interactions and their impact on RA risk
Recent advancements in human genetics have uncovered genetic associations in RA disease. Interactions between genes could significantly influence the development of complex diseases such as RA. The study of genetic variants might potentially lead to the identification of susceptibility genes and their interactions in RA pathogenesis. Gene-gene interaction occurs when the effects of a gene on disease susceptibility depend on another gene. Numerous gene interactions have been documented in RA disease.
Interactions between genetic variations in the mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinase signal transduction pathways and HLA-DRB1 SE are investigated in ACPA-positive RA patients. It is found that the rs10468473 variant from the MAP2K4 locus has a strong interaction with HLA-DRB1 SE, suggesting its involvement in the development of ACPA-positive RA. Specifically, this interaction proposes the potential role of the MAP2K4 gene in increasing RA susceptibility in individuals with HLA-DRB1 SE alleles. This interaction pattern could be a helpful marker in predicting the prognosis of ACPA-positive RA patients. Interactions between HLA-DRB1 SE alleles and PTPN22 rs2476601 (R620W) alleles were evaluated. A study from Swedish showed that participants with at least one A allele in PTPN22 rs2476601 along with at least one HLA-DRB1 SE allele exhibited an elevated odds ratio (OR 9.9) for developing ACPA-positive RA compared to those with GG genotype of PTPN22 and no HLA-DRB1 SE alleles.
Further analysis revealed that individuals with at least one A allele in PTPN22 rs2476601 without the HLA-DRB1 SE alleles showed no significantly increased risk for RA development. Similar findings were reported in the American NARAC study and the Dutch Leiden EAC study, proposing the existence of statistically significant interactions between HLA-DRB1 SE and PTPN22 across all three studies. Another gene-gene interaction, which is reported by So-young Bang et al., was interactions between the peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PADI4) and SE alleles in various subtypes of RA, including anti-CCP-positive/negative and erosive/non-erosive forms. Multiple studies have identified gene-gene interactions, such as interactions involving the BANK1 and BLK genes in RA disease. Interaction of deletion polymorphism in GSTM1 (glutathione S-transferase Mu-1) with SE alleles increased the risk of ACPA-positive RA development. Interactions between IL6 and IL4I-1 polymorphisms were associated with the aggressive phenotype of synovial fibroblasts during RA development. Recently, it has been demonstrated that interactions between CD86 and CD40L were also associated with an increased risk of RA development. It is reported that interactions between HLA genes (rs9275376 in HLA-DQB1 and rs4947332 in HLA-DRB1), between miRNAs and HLA (MIR3928 and HLA-DRB1), and between MIR5695 and TNFAIP3 are associated with an increased risk of RA development. HLA-DRB1 (rs4947332) and MIR3928 (rs5997893) detected a significant positive interaction. In this case, patients with both risk alleles significantly increased the risk of RA development in comparison to those without the risk alleles. These reports underscore the significance of interactions and complex interplay between HLA and non-HLA genetic variations in RA susceptibility.

Gene-environment interactions in RA development
Research on gene-environment interactions aims to explain how genetic and environmental factors affect the risk of developing human diseases. This involves examining how environmental exposures may alter disease risk in individuals with different genetic backgrounds and how genetic differences may modify the impact of environmental factors. Complex diseases such as RA are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. Studying the interactions between these elements in a disease helps to identify the mechanisms driving disease progression. External factors have been shown to affect high-risk individuals, considerably elevating the likelihood of developing RA. These environmental influences can be classified into four categories: airborne exposures, especially smoking; microbiota and infectious agents; dietary factors; and socioeconomic influences, including occupational and recreational exposures. A significant amount of evidence directly connects these diverse factors to RA development.
Smoking is the most substantial and most extensively studied environmental risk factor linked to RA. It dramatically affects immune responses, elevates oxidative stress in the body, disrupts apoptosis, triggers proinflammatory processes, and leads to citrullination and epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation. Compared to never smokers, individuals with 1–10 pack-years of smoking had a 26 % higher risk of developing RA, while those with over 20 pack-years faced double the risk. Additionally, smoking-related risks of RA were more significant in men than in women. Many studies have examined the interaction between smoking and the well-established genetic risk factor HLA-DRB1 shared epitope (SE) in the development of both antibody-positive and antibody-negative RA. Smokers with two SE copies were shown to have a 21-fold greater risk of developing ACPA-positive RA compared to non-smokers without SE copies. Furthermore, the risk of ACPA-positive RA associated with both SE and smoking increased with smoking intensity and the number of SE alleles. Additionally, the study by van der Helm-van Mil et al. indicated that the interaction between cigarette smoking and SE alleles varied across SE subsets, with the most robust interactions observed for the HLA-DRB1∗01:01, 01:02, and HLA-DRB1 10:01 alleles.
On the other hand, Lundström et al. reported that all SE alleles tested (∗01, ∗04:01, ∗04:04, ∗04:05, ∗04:08, and ∗10:01) showed a strong interaction with smoking, significantly increasing the risk of ACPA-positive RA. Regarding the interaction between cigarette smoking and gene polymorphisms on the RA epigenome, Meng et al. discovered a notable interaction between the rs6933349 variant of mucin 22 (MUC22) and smoking about DNA methylation. This interaction was linked to an increased risk of developing ACPA-positive RA in both Caucasian and Asian populations. Moreover, Kochi et al. identified a significant association between the PADI4 polymorphism (rs1748033) and ACPA status in smokers using a recessive model. This finding implies that the rs1748033 polymorphism could contribute to the formation of ACPA in cigarette smokers. In addition to smoking, various airborne exposures have been linked to Gene-Environment interactions. For instance, exposure to silica and other inorganic dust, common in occupations like construction or electronics, has been associated with an increased risk of RA; working in com has been connected to a heightened risk of RA. However, there is limited research on how these environmental factors interact with gene polymorphisms.
Several studies have shown a link between vitamin D levels and the risk of developing RA. Low vitamin D levels are associated with a higher incidence of RA, and high vitamin D levels potentially lower the risk of autoimmune diseases like RA. Research on the role of vitamin D in the immune system, especially in RA, focuses on the vitamin D receptor (VDR) and its polymorphisms. VDR, a nuclear transcription factor, regulates genes involved in inflammation and immune responses, thereby playing a crucial role in autoimmune diseases. Various immune cells, such as macrophages, monocytes, T lymphocytes, chondrocytes, and synovial cells, express the VDR gene. In RA, joint inflammation is caused by infiltrating various immune cells and proinflammatory cytokine production, leading to bone and cartilage damage. VDR gene polymorphisms on chromosome 12q13.11 are thought to affect immune regulation and joint inflammation in RA. Several polymorphisms of the VDR gene have been identified, with the most studied being Cdx2 (rs11568820), ApaI (rs7975232), TaqI (rs731236), FokI (rs2228570), and BsmI (rs1544410). Research suggests that these variations in the VDR gene can lead to receptor dysfunction and changes in immune responses, affecting the processes regulated by the receptor. The FokI (rs2228570) polymorphism shows a notable association with RA risk. In genetic models, the AA genotype of FokI polymorphism is linked to a lower RA risk, while the AG genotype is linked to an increased risk. The FokI-A variant produces a full-length vitamin D receptor, which protects RA development. In contrast, the FokI-G variant produces a short receptor, which may increase RA susceptibility.
The gut microbiota is another environmental factor that may play a role in RA development. Although the impact of gut microbiota on RA remains a subject of debate, some studies suggest that gut microbiota and genetic factors could be involved in disease development. For instance, a study by Gomez et al. first demonstrated that HLA genes and intestinal microbiome can influence susceptibility to arthritis. This study evaluated the fecal microbiomes of genetically predisposed mice to RA (HLA-DRB1∗0401) and those with a protective genetic background (HLA-DRB1∗0402). Notably, DRB1∗0401 female mice had distinct gut microbes compared to their DRB1∗0402 counterparts. Further analysis revealed that the arthritis-prone mice had more Clostridium species. In contrast, the resistant mice had a more significant presence of Porphyromonadaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae, which are linked to anti-inflammatory responses. These findings suggest that MHC genes may influence intestinal microbiota composition, potentially impacting the relationship between gut bacteria and immune responses.
In addition to the mentioned environmental factors and their effects on genetic markers, more environmental factors may affect genes and predispose individuals to RA. These include obesity, which increases the risk of RA; fatty fish and omega-3 fatty acids, which have protective effects; alcohol, which exhibits a dose-dependent protective effect against RA; and bacteria such as porphyromonas gingivalis, which is linked to an increased risk of ACPA-positive RA. Additionally, high sodium intake is associated with an increased risk of RA, particularly when combined with smoking. Finally, certain virus infections, including Parvovirus B19, hepatitis C virus (HCV), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) have been implicated in increasing the risk of RA development.

Subtypes of RA based on genetic profiles and their clinical implications
Autoantibodies, including RF and ACPA, are present in 70–80 % of RA patients. The presence of RF, ACPA, or both refers to seropositive RA (SPRA). Notably, elevated levels of both autoantibodies are associated with joint and bone erosion even in the early stages of RA. On the other hand, those who fulfill classification criteria (2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria) without ACPA and RF are categorized as seronegative RA (SNRA) patients. Despite the common belief that the SNRA group has less severity, its response to treatment is somewhat slower, and its radiographic progression is on par with SPRA. This implies that SNRA is not as mild as imagined. The SNRA development is complex, shaped by genetic and environmental factors, microbiota, and hormones. In contrast, the pathogenic processes in SPRA seem more standardized, characterized by a more significant impact of genetic factors and a prominent role of adaptive immunity. These autoantibodies offer a potent means of identifying subgroups of RA, which helps characterize RA populations and conduct genetic analysis. The conditions are also immunologically different in SNRA and SPRA patients. In SNRA, synovial lining CD68+ cells, synovial CD4+ T cells, synovial vessel CD31+ cells, and PD-1+ T cells are rare, while these cells are frequently present in SPRA. Conversely, synovial monocytes, macrophages, and plasma cells are common in SNRA but rare in SPRA. Clinically, SNRA and SPRA exhibit distinct patterns of joint and tissue involvement. Damages of metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints II, III, and V are low in SNRA, but their damages are high in SPRA. Synovitis is uncommon in SNRA but frequent in SPRA, while tenosynovitis and extra-synovial involvement are frequent in SNRA and rare in SPRA.
Research studies have reported that both HLA and non-HLA genes play roles in developing SNRA and SPRA. The HLA-B∗08/DRB1∗03 haplotype is commonly associated with SNRA, while HLA-DRB1∗04 and HLA-DRB1∗10 alleles are distinct risk factors exclusively tied to SPRA. Non-HLA genes, particularly those related to Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) proteins, contribute to the risk of developing SPRA but did not show a similar association with SNRA. SNPs in ANKRD55 and CLYBL are strongly related to SNRA, but these SNPs did not show the same effect in SPRA. Additionally, microRNAs like has-miR-362-5p and has-miR-708-3p are more connected to SNRA, whereas microRNAs, such as has-miR-24 and has-miR-125a, are related to both conditions. Some microRNAs, like has-miR-22, has-miR-361-5p, and has-miR-233-3p, showed stronger associations with SPRA. These results indicate that the SNRA and SPRA patients differ entirely in their immunologic profiles, clinical presentations, and genetic backgrounds. Therefore, to achieve the best outcome in treatment, these two groups of RA patients need distinctive approaches to management.

Genetic factors are responsible for 45 %–58 % of radiological damage variations in RA patients (Fig. 2). Monozygotic twins exhibit fewer variations in radiological damage than dizygotic twins or unrelated individuals. These results highlight the significant influence of genetics on disease severity. Although the genetic impact on RA severity is well-documented, only a few severity markers have been discovered so far. Recently, extensive genetic studies on RA patients are expected to uncover new markers related to disease severity and radiological progression. Current research on candidate genes has primarily concentrated on RA susceptibility loci and factors contributing to tissue damage. Some known loci associated with radiographic progression and joint damage include HLA-DRB1, PTGER4, TNF-α, C5-TRAF1, LRP5, C5orf30, IL-6, and IL-10.
Research has found that specific amino acids, Val 11 and Leu 11 in the HLA-DRB1 shared epitope (SE), are associated with more aggressive RA progression, leading to earlier and faster bone erosions. Conversely, the DERAA motif is linked to a lower risk of radiological deterioration. Val 11 correlates with higher clinical activity and increased inflammation, while serine at this position is associated with lower inflammation. A recent meta-analysis identified the SNP rs112112734, with strong linkage disequilibrium with the shared epitope with proximity to HLA-DRB1, showed a significant association with radiological damages, but the association was less critical when adjusted for the presence of the RF. Furthermore, the rs660895 SNP in HLA-DRB1 is strongly linked to radiographic progression in ACPA-positive RA patients. Although ACPA has no direct effects on RA outcomes, it can predict joint damage in RA patients with positive ACPA. Prostaglandin E receptor 4 (PTGER4, EP4) is a G protein-coupled receptor located at 5p13.1 and is one of four receptors for prostaglandin E2. It is present in various immune cells and is crucial in immune regulation and bone metabolism. EP4 and EP2 regulate proinflammatory factors in response to IL-1β and promote the differentiation of Th1 and the expansion of Th17 cells by inducing IL-23 secretion. Given its involvement in RA, Rodriguez-Rodriguez et al. studied PTGER4 polymorphisms. They found that the rs76523431 may be linked to increased radiographic progression in Caucasian RA patients, highlighting the need for further research on the effects of this and other SNPs on PTGER4 expression. Another gene that can play a role in radiographic progression is TNF-α, a major cytokine in RA, often found at high levels in RA patients. It causes inflammation by attracting various immune cells and activating synovial fibroblasts. This activation, influenced by cytokines like IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, produces enzymes like MMP-9 that break down collagen and proteoglycans, thereby leading to joint erosion. Previous studies have indicated that the A allele of the TNF-α-308 G/A is linked to increased TNF-α production. The TNF-α level in plasma has a positive correlation with MMP-9 levels. Stojanović et al. examined how the TNF-α-308A allele could affect MMP-9 activity in RA patients' plasma and synovial fluid. Findings revealed that MMP-9 activity was elevated in plasma and, notably, in the synovial fluid of RA patients carrying genotypes with the TNF-α-308A allele (GA and AA). Specifically, the presence of the A allele in this gene polymorphism was associated with the onset of early erosive joint changes and a rapid progression in radiological assessments of the patients. In addition, various studies have explored the relationship between gene polymorphisms and radiographic progression. For instance, the rs2900180 variant in C5-TRAF1 was linked to increased radiographic progression in ACPA-negative individuals, with the minor allele correlating to more remarkable advancement. Bernardes et al. showed notable links between three genetic polymorphisms of LRP5 (A1330V, V667 M, and N740 N), which are responsible for producing an essential protein in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and the extent of radiographic joint damages in RA. Additionally, C5orf30 acts as a negative regulator of tissue damage in RA by modulating the self-destructive tendencies of rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (RASFs). A key finding is that the T allele of the rs26232 SNP in C5orf30 protects against radiologic damage, a benefit that remains consistent over time. Patients carrying one T allele showed less severe radiologic joint destruction compared to those patients without the allele. Furthermore, homozygous individuals for the T allele experienced even less radiologic joint damage as time progressed. There was a correlation between the IL-6 174G allele and more significant radiographic damage, but this was only evident in patients who were positive for RF or anti-CCP. Additionally, patients with the IL-10 592 CC genotype exhibited more significant radiographic damage than those with the AC or AA genotypes in patients who were negative for RF or anti-CCP. These studies indicated that genetic background could affect clinical presentations, especially bone destruction in SNRA and SPRA patients. Therefore, identifying the susceptible genetic markers could be a helpful tool in understanding the disease condition and lead us to better management.

Genetic signatures predicting treatment response and disease outcomes
Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of drugs allow us to decide precise, more viable, and more secure drug that meets the patient's particular needs. Biomarkers such as the ESR and CRP levels give data about disease activity, whereas prescient biomarkers permit clinicians to survey the probability of response to treatment before a particular treatment. According to research, not all patients proceed to joint damage. It is critical to distinguish patients vulnerable to joint annihilation to start treatment as soon as possible and possibly avoid irreversible joint damage.

Studies have been documented that there are associations between genetic factors and response to various drugs in RA patients (Fig. 3). There are reports of an association between the treatment efficacy of leflunomide and polymorphisms (rs9340799 and rs2234693) in the estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) in RA patients. It has been demonstrated that the AA genotype of the reduced folate carrier 1 (RFC-1) gene has been related to a better response to MTX. Further evaluation revealed that the AA genotype of the RFC-1 gene is implicated in transporting MTX into cells. RA patients with T allele in the ABCB1 gene (3435(C > T)) showed a better response to MTX. Further evaluation revealed that the T allele is involved in transporting the MTX out of cells. They showed that the haplotypes and genotypes harboring 6bp2 allele were associated with non-response to MTX. Haplotypes of the TYMS gene that harbored simultaneously 6bp2 and 3R alleles seem to be predictors of non-response to MTX. The study concluded that TYMS polymorphisms significantly predict clinical response to MTX in RA patients. The MTHFR enzyme synthesizes 5-methyl-tetrahydrofolate (5-MTHF), acting as a methyl donor to convert homocysteine to methionine. Mutations in the MTHFR gene can disrupt these processes, potentially leading to adverse effects related to MTX toxicity. The most extensively studied SNPs in the MTHFR gene are 1298A > C and 677C > T polymorphisms. The 677C > T polymorphism has been linked to an increased risk of MTX toxicity, although findings have been inconsistent. On the other hand, the 1298A > C SNP, despite causing hyper-homocysteinemia, does not appear to raise the likelihood or severity of MTX toxicity. Interestingly, the 1298A > C SNP has been associated with more significant cardiovascular morbidity in RA. Due to a lack of pharmacogenetic data, additional research is necessary to examine the impact of MTHFR polymorphisms on the toxicity and effectiveness of MTX. The C > G polymorphism at the 347 position in the ATIC gene is related to an enhanced efficacy and toxicity of MTX.

Role of genetic factors in comorbidities and extra-articular manifestations of RA
The figure shows the associations of genetic risk factors with extra-articular manifestations (EAMs) and comorbidities in RA.
Fig. 4
Extra-articular manifestations (EAMs) and comorbidities are commonly observed in RA and associated with escalated morbidity and premature mortality rates (Fig. 4). RA-related EAMs can manifest in all organs, displaying diverse clinical presentations and sometimes remaining asymptomatic. Common EAMs and comorbidities in RA patients include vasculitis, cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction and stroke), solid-organ malignancies, and pulmonary, neurological, gastrointestinal, renal, and hematologic diseases. Research indicates that EAMs and comorbidities affect approximately 40 % of RA patients. All RA patients must undergo screening for EAMs and comorbidities to prevent and manage their complications effectively.
There is a link between endothelial dysfunction and the HLA-DRB1∗04 shared epitope, a significant genetic risk factor for RA development. Certain genetic variations have been linked to an elevated risk of developing RA-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD). One notable example is the rs35705950 (G > T) polymorphism in the promoter region of MUC5B, responsible for encoding mucin 5B. This polymorphism has been strongly associated with familial interstitial pneumonia and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), increasing the risk of ILD three times more in RA patients. Mucins are glycosylated proteins that form a crucial part of the mucus-coating ciliated lung epithelium. This layer is a protective barrier and aids in clearing inhaled particles and pathogens. According to Juge et al., MUC5B protein expression occurs in the bronchiolar epithelium and metaplastic epithelia found in honeycomb cysts in RA-ILD. Overexpression of MUC5B due to genetic factors could lead to excessive protein production, potentially impairing ciliary clearance and disrupting normal lung repair mechanisms. Interestingly, despite its high level of expression associated with an increased risk of disease development, the MUC5B polymorphism in the promoter region paradoxically related to a better outcome in IPF patients. The widespread presence of this polymorphism across various populations suggests that it may be preserved due to selection pressure, possibly to resist infection or improve tissue repair capabilities. While other genes have also been implicated in RA-ILD, none of the MUC5B promoter variants have shown an association with RA susceptibility. Recently, an association between RA-ILD and the RPA3-UMAD1 locus has been observed in Japanese patients. The rs12702634 RPA3-UMAD1 variant located at 7p21 has been identified as a new risk locus in the Japanese population, influencing the expression levels of RPA3. It was suggested that RPA3 may be a potential risk gene in this area. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies indicate that RPA3 is predominantly expressed in blood cells, including erythroid cells and proliferating T cells. RPA3 is a heterotrimeric replication protein, A complex (RPA) component crucial for DNA replication and the cellular response to DNA damage. Additionally, RPA has been shown to influence telomere elongation by regulating telomerase activity. Dysfunction of telomeres can lead to DNA damage, initiate senescence, and result in adverse effects on various organs, with pulmonary fibrosis being an expected outcome of telomere-related diseases. Furthermore, studies have found that patients with RA-ILD tend to have shorter telomeres compared to healthy individuals. Genetic variants in the RTEL1 and TERT coding region, linked with telomere shortening, have been associated with initiating RA-associated ILD at a younger age. Telomeres are repetitive DNA-protein structures at the ends of chromosomes that protect them from degradation. Diseases with short telomeres often have mutations in the telomerase complex, which generally help to maintain telomere length. Telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) mutations and telomerase RNA are associated with telomere shortening. Recent findings indicate that telomeres in RA-ILD are shorter than the other connective tissue diseases (CTD)-associated ILDs. The RA-ILD exhibits worse outcomes in terms of survival and lung function, suggesting that telomere length might be a prognostic factor in RA-ILD. There are also higher rates of mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance (such as TERT, RTEL1, and PARN) in RA-ILD patients compared to controls. Further evaluation revealed that those patients with these mutations have shorter leukocyte telomeres. Shortened telomeres are generally thought to contribute to the increased age-related susceptibility to RA. CD4 T-cells in these RA patients showed malfunction in TERT, leading to increased cell death and reduced clonal expansion. Immune senescence is a well-known aspect of RA, and some researchers suggest that inadequate telomere maintenance due to TERT-mediated telomerase downregulation may contribute to immune dysregulation in RA. Finally, several HLA allele variants, including HLA-DR4, HLA-DRB1∗15, HLA-DRB1∗16, HLA-B40, HLA-B54, and HLA-DQB1∗0601 have been associated with ILD in RA patients.
The appearance of rheumatoid nodules is linked to specific genetic factors, particularly the HLA-DR4 haplotype and the DRB1 alleles. RA patients with heterozygosity for specific HLADRB1 alleles, such as ∗0401 with B1∗0404/8 or ∗0101, have an increased risk of nodular disease and potentially more severe RA outcomes. Homozygosity for HLA-DRB1∗0401 is associated with susceptibility to severe organ involvement, including conditions like lung disease, vasculitis, Felty's syndrome, and vasculitis mononeuritis. In addition to the genetic risk factors mentioned for EAMs, genetic research is needed to determine the role of genetics in other EAMs, such as gastrointestinal manifestations, neurologic involvement, renal disorders, ocular involvement, hematologic manifestations, and osteoporosis. These studies indicate that there are genetically susceptible risk factors that may be associated with various extra-articular manifestations and comorbidities in RA patients. Therefore, evaluating specific genetic markers in RA patients will help rheumatologists to manage them effectively.

In the last decade, notable advancements have been made in understanding the pathogenesis of RA development. Despite this progress, treatment outcomes differ from person to person, and identifying the best therapy often requires experimentation. Genetic background significantly influences how individuals respond to medications (Table 2). These data highlight the potential of genomic research to reveal insights that could pave the way for personalized and efficient treatment strategies for each RA patient. Glucocorticoids (GCs) and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are efficient drugs in relieving the pain, inflammation, and stiffness associated with RA. RA treatment typically involves various medications, such as conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), biological DMARDs (bDMARDs), and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs).

csDMARDs
While treatment approaches for RA are different among nations, the primary therapy typically involves csDMARDs such as MTX, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, and leflunomide, either alone or in combination with corticosteroids. MTX is the most prescribed csDMARD due to its effectiveness and straightforward approach to a once-weekly dosing schedule. MTX's structure closely resembles folate, but it exhibits a strong binding affinity to dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR). As a competitive inhibitor, MTX targets multiple folate-dependent enzymes and could hinder DNA and RNA synthesis while increasing extracellular adenosine levels. MTX serves as a folic acid antagonist and competitively inhibits DHFR enzyme activity, which impedes the conversion of dihydrofolate to tetrahydrofolate, a critical step in synthesizing purine and thymine nucleotides.
Consequently, MTX significantly disrupts DNA and RNA synthesis, exerting an anti-proliferative effect. Furthermore, inhibition of 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide formyltransferase/IMP cyclohydrolase (ATIC) by MTX polyglutamate (MTXPGs) enhances the accumulation of intracellular 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR) and lead to adenosine release. Through binding to their cell surface receptors, adenosine diminishes inflammation and suppresses immune responses.
Researchers have recently investigated numerous genetic variations that may impact our bodies' processing of MTX. These studies may shed light on why there are differences in how individuals differently metabolize and respond to MTX. This variability is mainly due to genetic differences among people, especially genes responsible for encoding membrane transporter proteins. MTX is absorbed into the body through active transport facilitated by two proteins: the reduced folate carrier protein 1 (RFC1) and the proton-coupled folate transporter (PCFT), which are encoded by the human solute carrier family 19 and 46 member 1 genes (SLC19A1 and SLC46A1), respectively. Variations in these genes could impact MTX function and lead to different treatment responses in RA patients. In some RA patients with active disease, MTX alone or in combination with hydroxychloroquine or a TNF inhibitor does not lead to low disease activity. These patients may require an adjustment in therapy. Evidence suggests that the initial strategy for MTX non-responders should be triple therapy (MTX, sulfasalazine, and hydroxychloroquine). If disease activity remains high despite triple therapy, switching to a TNF inhibitor such as etanercept in combination with MTX is a recommended next step.
The RFC1-80G > A (rs1051266) polymorphism could affect the MTX-PGs levels within cells, thereby influencing MTX treatment's effectiveness. Studies have demonstrated that patients with the AA genotype in the RFC1 gene exhibit a more favorable response to treatment than those with AG and GG genotypes. Further analysis revealed that patients with the AA genotype in the RFC1 gene showed a 3.32-fold higher likelihood of remission from RA symptoms than individuals with the GG genotype. Moreover, three variations in the dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) gene, which is the target of MTX, specifically rs12517451, rs10072026, and rs1643657, have been associated with a higher adverse effect during MTX therapy. The DHFR -317A > G (rs70991108) polymorphism was associated with MTX response, and the AA genotype of the SNP had a less favorable response to MTX treatment. Leflunomide blocks the creation of new pyrimidines by targeting a crucial enzyme called dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH). Despite DHODH being broadly consistent across individuals, a single missense variation in its coding region (rs3213422) has been linked to response to leflunomide treatment.

bDMARDs
The bDMARDs have demonstrated their effectiveness in RA patients who have not responded sufficiently to csDMARDs therapy. The most potent bDMARDs that have been approved and clinically validated include IL-1 inhibitors (anakinra), IL-6 inhibitors (tocilizumab and sarilumab), TNFα inhibitors (infliximab, etanercept, and adalimumab), T cell co-stimulation modulators (abatacept), and anti-CD20 (rituximab).
Growing evidence shows different responses to treatment for this group of medications. There are various bDMARDs currently on the market with high prices. Identifying the predictive factors for response to treatment is vital before starting therapy. Multiple studies have indicated that variations in the TNFα and TNF receptor (TNFR) genes could impact treatment outcomes. Specifically, three studies have examined the relationship between the TNFα 308A > G (rs1800629) SNP and response to adalimumab, etanercept, and infliximab. All these studies found that RA patients with a TNFα-308 GG genotype responded more favorably to the biological treatments than those with −308 AG or -AA genotypes. This suggests that genotyping the TNFα-308A > G SNP could be a valuable tool for predicting how individuals will respond to anti-TNF agents.
Morales et al. examined how genetic polymorphisms in the Fc gamma receptor IIa (FCGR2A) and Fc gamma receptor IIIa (FCGR3A) genes, which encode surface receptors, impact the response to tocilizumab (TCZ) and rituximab in RA patients. These variants affected the affinity of FCRs for the Fc region of biological drugs (bDMARDs). Patients with the lower-affinity FCGR3A rs396991-TT genotype, possibly leading to reduced plasma clearance of tocilizumab, showed a more robust response to the drug. In contrast, those with the high-affinity FCGR3A rs396991-G allele responded better to rituximab, likely due to enhanced immune cell activity. Additionally, patients carrying the FCGR2A rs1801274-TT genotype had higher EULAR (European League Against Rheumatism) responses and greater DAS28 (28-joint Disease Activity Score) improvement after rituximab treatment at multiple time points including 6, 12, and 18 months. Moreover, a meta-analysis found that RA patients with the homozygous AA genotype in rs12083537 polymorphism of the IL-6R gene responded more favorably to TCZ than those with the G allele. Lastly, the HLA-DRB1∗04:05 shared-epitope allele was significantly associated with better SDAI (Simplified Disease Activity Index) improvement after abatacept treatment but showed no significant association with other therapies.

tsDMARDs
Unlike traditional synthetic DMARDs, the tsDMARDs primarily target various kinases within cytokine-mediated signaling pathways. These pathways encompass the Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, and NF-κB pathway. When a pro-inflammatory cytokine binds to its receptors, it initiates JAK recruitment and subsequent STAT phosphorylation. This phosphorylation initiates a cascade that prompts the expression of pro-inflammatory genes, thereby contributing to joint inflammation and tissue damage. The dysregulated activation of the JAK/STAT pathway is now recognized as a critical factor in perpetuating RA pathology by increasing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines. Activation of the JAK/STAT pathway can also trigger activation of the MAPK pathway, contributing to neo-angiogenesis and upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Hyperactivation of NF-κB and overstimulation of the PI3K/Akt/PKB/mTOR cell survival pathway likely contribute to apoptosis resistance in the inflamed synovial tissue in RA. Consequently, molecules involved in these signaling pathways are attractive targets for developing new therapies based on tsDMARDs.
Research has identified several genes that may influence how individuals respond to tsDMARDs when treating RA. These genes are primarily involved in immune function, inflammation, and drug metabolism, affecting the efficacy and safety of tsDMARDs like JAK inhibitors (e.g., tofacitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib). PADI4 and PTPN22 are genes associated with increased susceptibility to RA; variations in these genes may also affect the response to RA treatments. Polymorphisms in these genes have been linked to inflammation regulation and immune responses, influencing the efficacy of tsDMARDs. The JAK (Janus kinase) family genes play a direct role in the mechanism of JAK inhibitors, which are common tsDMARDs. Variants in JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) can influence how effectively JAK inhibitors work for RA patients. Specific polymorphisms in JAK3 might impact a patient's response to drugs like tofacitinib. STAT1 and STAT3 are downstream molecules in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, crucial in immune regulation. Polymorphisms in these genes may affect how well patients respond to tsDMARDs, especially those targeting the JAK-STAT pathway. The multidrug resistance 1 (MDR1) gene encodes for a protein that affects drug transport and metabolism. Variants in this gene can affect drug levels in the blood and, therefore, impact the efficacy and side effects of tsDMARDs in RA treatment. Cytochrome P450 enzymes, particularly the cytochrome P450 family 2 subfamily C member 19 (CYP2C19) and cytochrome P450 family 3 subfamily A member 4 (CYP3A4), are involved in the metabolism of tsDMARDs. Variations in these genes can influence how drugs are processed in the body, affecting drug efficacy and the risk of adverse effects. While biologic DMARDs primarily target IL-6 and TNF, they are also involved in the inflammatory process in RA. Genetic variations in IL6 and TNF genes may play a role in modulating the inflammatory response and, by extension, impact the reaction to tsDMARDs significantly when these drugs affect cytokine signaling indirectly.
Several other genes, like HLA-DRB1 (associated with the shared epitope in RA) and FCGR2A, which encodes a receptor involved in immune response, are also under investigation. They may have a role in treatment response and disease prognosis, although their direct impact on tsDMARD efficacy is still being studied. Pharmacogenomics is an evolving field, and research on these genetic markers could lead to more personalized treatments for RA in the future. Genetic testing may help predict who would benefit most from specific tsDMARDs, reducing trial and error in finding effective treatment options.

Challenges and prospects in implementing novel therapies in RA
NSAIDs, GCs, and DMARDs are commonly used in the clinical management of RA. These medications aim to slow disease progression and alleviate inflammation. However, their long-term use is associated with unavoidable side effects such as nausea, immunosuppression, osteoporosis, fatigue, liver damage, gastrointestinal ulcers, cytopenia, infections, rashes, and psoriasis. There has a critical need for innovative therapeutic approaches that can enhance effectiveness while minimizing adverse reactions. Advanced technologies such as Mesenchymal Stem Cell (MSCs) Drug Delivery, PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras), CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing, and artificial intelligence (AI)-based drug design enable the exploration of therapeutic options that show encouraging prospects for RA management. These innovative tools facilitate comprehensive screening for potential treatments. MSCs, which are multipotent cells, can be obtained from various sources like bone marrow, umbilical cord, or adipose tissue. They possess inherent anti-inflammatory abilities through the secretion of exosomes, cytokines, and growth factors, making them promising candidates for treating inflammatory conditions. Also, MSCs can remarkably migrate to inflamed areas, suggesting potential carriers for drug delivery. One approach could be load anti-inflammatory drugs, such as MTX, in slow-release nanoparticle formulations to the surface of MSCs before injection. Furthermore, both animal model investigations and clinical trials involving RA patients have demonstrated that administering MSCs reduces the proinflammatory condition and improves the clinical presentations of RA patients. This was shown with low levels of proinflammatory mediators, such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α.
A team of researchers in St. Louis, USA, utilized CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing technology to modify induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), creating cartilage stem cells named "SMART" (Stem cells Modified for Autonomous Regenerative Therapy). These cells are equipped with a synthetic gene circuit responsive to IL-1, regulating the production of an IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra). IL-1 triggers inflammation in arthritis by activating inflammatory cells within the joints. The implanted cells sense changes in IL-1 levels and activate intracellular gene circuits to release therapeutic amounts of IL-1Ra during inflammation. This approach offers personalized therapies through reprogramming chondrocytes in RA patients. Employing Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTAC) technology provides a novel therapeutic avenue for combating diseases caused by abnormal expression of harmful mediators. This technology offers a novel and hopeful approach to treating RA. This technology facilitates JAK degradation through the ability of the PROTAC molecule, which can attach to both the E3-ubiquitin ligase and the JAK protein, leading to the ubiquitination and breakdown of the JAK protein. However, there are challenges regarding the effective delivery and availability of PROTAC in clinical settings. In addition to the technologies mentioned above, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) hold promise in revolutionizing therapeutic approaches by customizing treatments to individual patient characteristics. The vast amount of data from digital sources enables AI to identify crucial patterns for diagnosis and treatment. AI shows potential abilities in predicting therapy response and disease outcomes and detecting health issues with high precision. ML algorithms will enhance and provide precise individualized predictions in medicine's digitalization. AI-supported updates will significantly improve the complexity and frequency of updates in disease therapy guidelines. Integrating 'omics' data and digital biomarkers, stored digitally or implanted in microchips, will enable tailored risk profiles and preventive strategies for RA patients. As our comprehension of RA advances and the reasons behind the varying symptoms and treatment outcomes among RA patients, the precision of RA treatments will further improve, whether through better administration techniques or personalized therapies tailored to individuals.

Conclusion
In summary, the intricate genetic landscape of RA highlights the critical role of genetic factors in its susceptibility, progression, and treatment response (Table 1). Mainly, HLA-DRB1 alleles, which significantly influence susceptibility through the shared epitope hypothesis, are involved in RA pathogenesis. Variants in other HLA genes, such as HLA-DPB1, also contribute to disease risk and severity. Genetic variations such as PTPN22, PADI-4, TNFAIP3, and STAT4 are essential in immune dysregulation, signaling pathways, and antigen presentation. These variants affect immune responses and inflammation, central to RA pathogenesis.
Despite significant progress, several challenges still need to be addressed in genetic research on RA. The wide variability in genetic risk factors and their interactions complicates the development of universal predictive models and therapies. Further research is needed to unravel how environmental factors interact with genetic predispositions to influence RA development and progression. Variability in drug responses based on genetic differences necessitates more extensive studies to refine personalized treatment approaches and reduce adverse effects. Treatment choices are often guided by a combination of clinical presentation, disease severity, comorbidities, and predictive biomarkers such as rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-CCP antibodies, and inflammatory markers. Additionally, pharmacogenetic studies have suggested that genetic variations, such as polymorphisms in various genes, may influence response to certain therapies.
Continued validation of genetic markers across diverse populations will enhance their clinical applicability and utility in predicting disease outcomes. Identifying novel genetic targets and understanding their roles in RA pathogenesis will aid in developing targeted therapies. Advancements in genome editing, artificial intelligence, and machine learning can be harnessed to enhance personalized medicine and therapeutic approaches in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis.
The potential implications of these genetic discoveries are profound. By improving our understanding of the genetic basis of RA, we can develop more precise diagnostic tools, tailor treatments to individual genetic profiles, and ultimately enhance patient care through targeted therapies. This comprehensive approach promises to improve outcomes and quality of life for individuals affected by RA.
