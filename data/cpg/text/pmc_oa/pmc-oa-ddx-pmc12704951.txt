Unmasking anemia in the elderly: A systematic review of prevalence, causes, and consequences
ABSTRACT
Background:
Anemia is a prevalent condition in the elderly (aged ≥65 years), associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and reduced quality of life. Despite its impact, comprehensive data on its prevalence, etiology, and outcomes in this population are limited.
Objective:
To synthesize evidence on the prevalence, etiology, and clinical outcomes of anemia in individuals aged 65 years and older, informing clinical practice and future research.
Methods:
A systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar (2004–2024), identifying 47 studies (observational, cohort, cross-sectional, and review). Inclusion criteria focused on anemia in the elderly, reporting prevalence, etiology, or outcomes. Quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and AMSTAR 2. Narrative synthesis, supported by biostatistical analyses (pooled prevalence, hazard ratios [HRs], odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals [CIs]), was performed, with heterogeneity assessed via I² statistics.
Results:
Anemia prevalence ranged from 10% to 50% (pooled prevalence 27.8%, 95% CI: 22.1–33.5, I² =92%), higher in hospitalized patients (37.4%, 95% CI: 29.8–45.0) than community-dwelling elderly (23.1%, 95% CI: 18.2–28.0). Iron deficiency anemia (27.5%, 95% CI: 21.3–33.7), anemia of chronic disease (20.1%, 95% CI: 15.6–24.6), and unexplained anemia (34.2%, 95% CI: 28.0–40.4) were predominant. Anemia increased all-cause mortality (pooled HR: 2.85, 95% CI: 2.10–3.87, I² =78%) and cardiovascular events (pooled HR: 3.12, 95% CI: 2.34–4.16, I² =65%).
Conclusion:
Anemia in the elderly, particularly unexplained anemia, is a significant health burden, necessitating targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Introduction
Anemia, defined by the World Health Organization as hemoglobin levels below 13 g/dL in men and 12 g/dL in women, is widespread among older adults (aged ≥65 years), and its prevalence rises with age. It triggers severe outcomes, including increased mortality, cardiovascular events, functional decline, and diminished quality of life (QoL). Frequently undetected due to its complex causes and the absence of tailored diagnostics, a critical research gap, the impact of anemia is heightened by age-related declines in bone marrow function, renal capacity, and growing comorbidities.
Causes encompass iron deficiency anemia from gastrointestinal bleeding or poor nutrition, anemia of chronic disease tied to inflammation in conditions like cancer or heart failure, and unexplained anemia, whose mechanisms remain elusive, necessitating further study. Unexplained anemia, linked to reduced haematopoiesis, low-grade inflammation, or suboptimal erythropoietin response, poses diagnostic challenges. Nutritional deficiencies (e.g., vitamin B12) and rare disorders like myelodysplastic syndromes also contribute.
Anemia predicts hospitalization, cardiovascular disease, cognitive impairment, falls, and frailty, perpetuating health decline. In heart failure, it doubles adverse event risks. It profoundly impairs physical, cognitive, and emotional wellbeing, often leading to dependency. Treatments like erythropoiesis-stimulating agents carry risks, including thromboembolism. With the global population aged ≥65 projected to reach 16% by 2050, addressing anemia is urgent. This review, distinguished by synthesizing multiple studies with multiple statistical methods, examines prevalence, causes, and consequences of anemia in the elderly population, providing insights to enhance diagnosis and management, addressing gaps in tailored diagnostics and origins of unexplained anemia.

Methods

Search strategy
A systematic literature search was conducted across four major databases, like PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, for studies published between January 2004 and April 2024. The search strategy was designed to capture a broad range of studies on anemia in the elderly, using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms. Key search terms included “anemia” AND “elderly” OR “older adults” OR “aged” AND “prevalence” OR “causes” OR “aetiology” OR “outcomes” OR “management,” refined with Boolean operators (e.g., ”anemia AND (elderly OR aged) NOT (children OR paediatric)”). Truncation (e.g., ”anaemi*” to capture “anemia” and “anaemic”) and synonyms (e.g., ”older adults,” “geriatric”) were used to ensure comprehensive coverage. Additional sources were identified through manual searches of reference lists of key articles and gray literature, including conference proceedings from the British Society of Haematology (BSH) and the American Society of Hematology (ASH).
The initial search yielded 1100 articles (PubMed: 600, Scopus: 250, Web of Science: 200, Google Scholar: 50). After removing 250 duplicates using EndNote X9, 950 unique articles remained. Title and abstract screening shortlisted 400 articles based on relevance to anemia in the elderly. Full-text review resulted in 47 eligible studies, with 353 excluded (150 for focusing on nonelderly populations, 100 for lack of full text, 103 for irrelevant outcomes). Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Studies were included if they focused on anemia in individuals aged 65 years and older, reporting data on prevalence, etiology, clinical outcomes, or management strategies. Eligible study designs included observational studies (cross-sectional, cohort), case-control studies, and systematic reviews. Studies were required to be published in English and have full text available to ensure comprehensive data extraction. Exclusion criteria encompassed studies focusing on populations younger than 65 years, those addressing non-anemia-related conditions (e.g. cancer without mention of anemia), or those lacking relevant outcomes (e.g., prevalence, mortality). Non-English studies, abstracts without full text, editorials, and opinion pieces were also excluded to maintain methodological rigor.

Data extraction
Data were extracted using a standardized Excel template designed to capture key variables, including study design, population characteristics (age, sex, sample size, geographic location), setting (community-dwelling, hospitalized, or mixed), prevalence of anemia, etiological classifications [e.g., iron deficiency anemia (IDA), anemia of chronic disease (ACD), and unexplained], clinical outcomes (e.g., mortality, hospital stay, QoL metrics), and management strategies [e.g., iron supplementation, erythropoiesis stimulating agents (ESAs)]. Additional variables included study duration, follow-up period, and diagnostic criteria for anemia (e.g., WHO thresholds). Two independent reviewers performed the extraction, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer. Inter-rater agreement was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.90).

Quality assessment
The quality of included studies was assessed using validated tools tailored to study design. Observational studies were evaluated with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assesses three domains: selection (e.g., representativeness of the cohort), comparability (e.g., adjustment for confounders), and outcome (e.g., adequacy of follow-up). Scores ≥7 (out of 9) indicated high quality, 4–6 moderate quality, and <4 low quality. Systematic reviews were assessed using the A MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews, version 2 (AMSTAR 2) checklist, which evaluates 16 items, including search comprehensiveness, risk of bias assessment, and data synthesis. Reviews were categorized as high (≥8/16), moderate (4–7), or low (<4) quality. Two reviewers independently conducted assessments, achieving high inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s κ = 0.87). Disagreements were resolved through consensus.

Statistical analysis
A narrative synthesis was employed to summarize findings, given the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcome measures. Quantitative data were pooled where feasible using random-effects models to account for anticipated heterogeneity, calculating pooled prevalence, hazard ratios (HRs), odds ratios (ORs), and mean differences, all with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with I² >50% indicating substantial heterogeneity, and the Cochran’s Q test (P < 0.10 indicating significant heterogeneity). Subgroup analyses were conducted by setting (community vs. hospital), age group (65–79 years vs. ≥80 years), sex, geographic region, and anemia type (IDA, ACD, unexplained) to explore sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses excluded low-quality studies (NOS <7, AMSTAR 2 <4) to assess the robustness of pooled estimates. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test, with P < 0.05 indicating significant bias. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) with the meta and metafor packages. All analyses adhered to a two-sided alpha level of 0.05.

Results

Study characteristics
This review synthesized data from 47 studies, comprising 20 observational studies, 12 cohort studies, 8 cross-sectional analyses, and 7 systematic reviews, published between 2004 and 2024. The studies collectively included 152,348 participants, with sample sizes ranging from 100 to 67,595. The mean age of participants was 78.4 ± 6.2 years, with 54% female. Settings varied, with 20 studies focusing on community-dwelling elderly, 15 on hospitalized patients, and 12 on mixed populations. Geographic distribution included North America (n = 18), Europe (n = 15), Asia (n = 10), Africa (n = 2), and Australia (n = 2). Follow-up durations ranged from 1 to 15 years (median 5.2 years). Quality assessment identified 38 studies as high quality (NOS ≥7, AMSTAR 2 ≥8) and 9 as moderate quality, with no low-quality studies included after sensitivity analysis.

Prevalence of anemia
Anemia prevalence varied widely across populations, settings, and age groups. The overall pooled prevalence across all studies was 27.8% (95% CI: 22.1–33.5, I² =92%, n = 47 studies, P < 0.001 for heterogeneity). In community-dwelling elderly, the pooled prevalence was 23.1% (95% CI: 18.2–28.0, I² =88%, n = 20 studies), and Safiri et al. reported a global age-standardised prevalence of 23,176.2 per 100,000 population. Hospitalized patients exhibited a significantly higher pooled prevalence of 37.4% (95% CI: 29.8–45.0, I² = 92%, n = 15 studies, P < 0.001), and De Amicis et al. reported a prevalence of 48% in elderly inpatients. Subgroup analysis by age revealed a prevalence of 18.5% (95% CI: 14.2–22.8, I² = 85%, n = 30 studies) in those aged 65–79 years, increasing to 42.3% (95% CI: 35.6–49.0, I² = 90%, n = 25 studies) in those aged ≥80 years. Nonagenarians (≥90 years) had a pooled prevalence of 38.2% (95% CI: 32.1–44.3, I² = 87%, n = 5 studies). Gender differences were notable in community settings, with men showing a prevalence of 27.2% (95% CI: 22.0–32.4, I² = 80%, n = 10 studies) compared to 20.5% (95% CI: 16.1–24.9, I² = 82%, n = 10 studies) in women. Geographic variation was evident, with higher prevalence in African studies (41.5%, 95% CI: 34.0–49.0, I² = 89%, n = 2 studies) compared to North American studies (25.3%, 95% CI: 20.1–30.5, I² = 86%, n = 18 studies). Egger’s test indicated potential publication bias (P = 0.03), suggesting underrepresentation of smaller studies reporting lower prevalence. Sensitivity analysis excluding moderate-quality studies yielded a slightly lower pooled prevalence of 26.5% (95% CI: 21.0–32.0, I² =90%). These prevalence data are summarized in Figure 2 and Table 1.

Etiology of anemia
The etiology of anemia in the elderly was heterogeneous, with IDA, ACD, and unexplained anemia being the most prevalent. IDA had a pooled prevalence of 27.5% (95% CI: 21.3–33.7, I² =84%, n = 25 studies), with gastrointestinal blood loss as the primary cause in 18.2% (95% CI: 13.5–22.9, I² =80%, n = 15 studies) and poor dietary intake in 8.1% (95% CI: 5.2–11.0, I² =76%, n = 12 studies). Gastrointestinal blood loss was often linked to peptic ulcers (7.5%, 95% CI: 4.8–10.2), colorectal cancer (5.2%, 95% CI: 3.0–7.4), and angiodysplasia (3.1%, 95% CI: 1.5–4.7). Poor dietary intake was more common in lower-income settings, with African studies reporting a higher contribution (12.3%, 95% CI: 8.0–16.6) compared to European studies (6.5%, 95% CI: 3.9–9.1).
ACD affected 20.1% of cases (95% CI: 15.6–24.6, I² =79%, n = 20 studies), driven by chronic inflammation from cancer (7.5%, 95% CI: 4.8–10.2, I² =74%), CKD (6.2%, 95% CI: 3.9–8.5, I² =70%), and heart failure (4.3%, 95% CI: 2.5–6.1, I² =68%). Elevated hepcidin levels, a hallmark of ACD, were reported in 60% of cases (95% CI: 52.0–68.0, I² =82%), impairing iron absorption and utilization. Unexplained anemia was the most common etiology, with a pooled prevalence of 34.2% (95% CI: 28.0–40.4, I² =88%, n = 22 studies), often attributed to age-related hematopoietic decline (20.5%, 95% CI: 15.6–25.4), low-grade inflammation (10.3%, 95% CI: 7.2–13.4), and suboptimal EPO response (8.1%, 95% CI: 5.0–11.2). Other causes included vitamin B12/folate deficiencies (12.4%, 95% CI: 8.9–15.9, I² =76%, n = 10 studies) and hematologic disorders such as myelodysplastic syndromes (4.1%, 95% CI: 2.3–5.9, I² =70%, n = 8 studies). Heterogeneity in etiological estimates (I² =70%–88%) likely reflects variability in diagnostic criteria, such as the use of ferritin levels for IDA or hepcidin assays for ACD. Clinical outcomes
Anemia in the elderly was associated with significant adverse clinical outcomes, affecting mortality, morbidity, and QoL. The pooled HR for all-cause mortality was 2.85 (95% CI: 2.10–3.87, I² =78%, n = 15 studies, P < 0.001), with frail patients at higher risk (HR: 4.03, 95% CI: 2.95–5.52, I² =75%). Subgroup analysis by setting showed a higher mortality risk in hospitalized patients (HR: 3.42, 95% CI: 2.56–4.58, I² = 80%) compared to community-dwelling elderly (HR: 2.31, 95% CI: 1.72–3.10, I² =76%). Cardiovascular mortality was significantly elevated, with a pooled HR of 3.12 (95% CI: 2.34–4.16, I² = 65%, n = 12 studies, P < 0.001), particularly in heart failure patients (HR: 3.15, 95% CI: 1.89–5.24, I² = 70%). The risk of major cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and stroke, was increased (OR: 2.78, 95% CI: 2.05–3.77, I² =68%, n = 8 studies).
Hospital length of stay (LOS) was prolonged by a mean of 4.2 days (95% CI: 3.1–5.3, I² =82%, n = 10 studies, P < 0.001), with severe anemia (Hb <8 g/dL) associated with an additional 6.8 days (95% CI: 5.5–8.1, I² =85%). This prolongation was more pronounced in surgical patients (mean + 5.8 days, 95% CI: 4.5–7.1) compared to medical patients (mean + 3.5 days, 95% CI: 2.3–4.7). Cognitive decline affected 25.3% of anemic elderly (95% CI: 20.1–30.5, I² =74%, n = 8 studies), with a higher incidence in those with Hb <10 g/dL (30.2%, 95% CI: 24.0–36.4) compared to mild anemia (Hb 10–12 g/dL, 20.1%, 95% CI: 15.0–25.2). Falls and fractures were increased by 18.7% (95% CI: 14.2–23.2, I² =70%, n = 7 studies), with a greater risk in women (22.5%, 95% CI: 17.0–28.0) than men (15.3%, 95% CI: 11.0–19.6). Frailty risk was elevated 2.5-fold (OR: 2.52, 95% CI: 1.89–3.36, I² =68%, n = 7 studies), with a stronger association in those aged ≥80 years (OR: 3.10, 95% CI: 2.25–4.27). In cancer patients, anemia was associated with poor overall survival (6.5% at 24 months, 95% CI: 4.0–9.0, I² = 80%, n = 5 studies), particularly in those with lung cancer (4.8%, 95% CI: 2.5–7.1).
QoL metrics, assessed using validated tools like the Short Form-36 (SF-36), showed significant declines in anemic elderly. Physical component scores were reduced by a mean of 12.4 points (95% CI: 9.8–15.0, I² =78%, n = 6 studies), while mental component scores decreased by 8.7 points (95% CI: 6.2–11.2, I² =74%). Fatigue, a common symptom, was reported in 45.3% of anemic elderly (95% CI: 38.0–52.6, I² =82%), with a higher prevalence in hospitalized patients (52.1%, 95% CI: 44.0–60.2) compared to community-dwelling individuals (38.5%, 95% CI: 31.0–46.0). The risk of depression was increased (OR: 1.92, 95% CI: 1.45–2.54, I² =70%, n = 5 studies), particularly in those with severe anemia (OR: 2.35, 95% CI: 1.78–3.10). Heterogeneity in outcome estimates (I² =65%–85%) was likely driven by differences in follow-up duration, outcome definitions, and patient comorbidities. Management and complications
Management strategies for anemia in the elderly varied by etiology and severity. For IDA, oral iron supplementation was used in 68.4% of cases (95% CI: 62.1–74.7, I² =79%, n = 12 studies), increasing Hb by a mean of 1.5 g/dL (95% CI: 1.2–1.8, I² =72%) within 6–8 weeks. Intravenous (IV) iron was used in 31.6% (95% CI: 25.3–37.9, I² =76%), with similar efficacy (Hb increase 1.5 g/dL, 95% CI: 1.2–1.8) but faster onset (4–6 weeks). IV iron was more commonly used in hospitalized patients (45.2%, 95% CI: 38.0–52.4) due to better tolerability. ACD management focused on treating underlying conditions, with anti-inflammatory therapies (e.g. corticosteroids, biologics) used in 42.3% of cases (95% CI: 35.6–49.0, I² =75%), though efficacy was variable (Hb increase 0.5–1.0 g/dL in 60% of cases). ESAs were employed in 17.8% of severe cases (95% CI: 13.2–22.4, I² =80%, n = 8 studies), raising Hb by 1.8 g/dL (95% CI: 1.4–2.2, I² =78%), but their use was limited by a 16.5% risk of thromboembolism (95% CI: 12.0–21.0, I² = 82%). Blood transfusions (Hb <8 g/dL) decreased from 14.8% to 5.2% over a decade (P < 0.001, n = 6 studies), reflecting more conservative practices. Transfusions provided immediate Hb correction (mean increase 2.5 g/dL, 95% CI: 2.0–3.0) but carried risks of volume overload (6.3%, 95% CI: 4.0–8.6) and transfusion reactions (4.1%, 95% CI: 2.5–5.7).
Nutritional interventions showed promise, particularly for preventing frailty. Increased dietary fiber intake (20–30 g/day) was associated with a 30% reduction in frailty risk (OR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.55–0.89, I² =70%, n = 4 studies). Vitamin B12 and folate supplementation corrected deficiencies in 85% of cases (95% CI: 78.0–92.0, I² =74%, n = 5 studies), raising Hb by 1.2 g/dL (95% CI: 0.9–1.5). However, adherence to oral supplementation was low (60.5%, 95% CI: 53.0–68.0), particularly in community settings, due to polypharmacy and gastrointestinal side effects.
Complications of treatment were notable. Oral iron caused gastrointestinal side effects (e.g. nausea, constipation) in 7.8% of patients (95% CI: 5.1–10.5, I² =68%), leading to discontinuation in 15% of cases. IV iron was associated with hypersensitivity reactions in 1.8% (95% CI: 0.9–2.7, I² =65%), with severe reactions (anaphylaxis) in 0.3% (95% CI: 0.1–0.5). ESAs increased thromboembolism risk by 16.5%, with a higher incidence in cancer patients (20.2%, 95% CI: 15.0–25.4) compared to noncancer patients (12.5%, 95% CI: 8.0–17.0). Transfusions led to volume overload in 6.3% of patients, with a higher risk in those with heart failure (10.5%, 95% CI: 7.0–14.0). Discussion
This systematic review of 47 studies provides a robust analysis of anemia in the elderly, revealing its substantial prevalence and variability across settings, age groups, genders, and regions. The higher burden in hospitalized patients compared to community-dwelling elderly highlights the role of acute illnesses, such as infections or surgical procedures, which exacerbate inflammation, blood loss, and nutritional deficits. The age-related rise in prevalence among those over 80 years reflects physiological declines, including diminished bone marrow reserve, impaired erythropoietin production from declining renal function, and increased oxidative stress, all hindering red blood cell production. Gender differences, with men showing higher rates, may stem from increased gastrointestinal bleeding due to conditions like peptic ulcers or colorectal cancer, though hormonal factors, such as lower estrogen in postmenopausal women reducing inflammation, could contribute. Geographic disparities, with African studies reporting elevated prevalence, likely arise from poorer nutritional status, limited healthcare access, and higher rates of infectious diseases like malaria. High heterogeneity in prevalence estimates suggests variability in diagnostic criteria (e.g. WHO hemoglobin thresholds vs. age-adjusted standards), population characteristics, and study settings, underscoring the need for standardized definitions in future research.
The etiological complexity of anemia in the elderly, with unexplained anemia as the most common form, followed by IDA and ACD, reveals significant diagnostic challenges. Prevalence of unexplained anemia points to a critical knowledge gap, likely driven by age-related hematopoietic decline, low-grade inflammation, and suboptimal erythropoietin response, involving mechanisms like chronic inflammation (e.g. elevated interleukin-6) and stem cell senescence. IDA, frequently linked to gastrointestinal blood loss from peptic ulcers, colorectal cancer, or angiodysplasia, necessitates routine endoscopic screening in symptomatic older adults, such as those with melena or weight loss. Poor dietary intake, a notable IDA contributor in resource-limited settings, reflects restricted access to iron-rich foods, highlighting the need for nutritional interventions. ACD, tied to chronic conditions like cancer, CKD, and heart failure, is marked by elevated hepcidin levels, which impair iron absorption and cause functional iron deficiency. Variability in diagnostic approaches, such as ferritin thresholds for IDA or hepcidin assays for ACD, and differences in population comorbidities contribute to high heterogeneity in etiological estimates, complicating clinical decision-making.
Clinical consequences of anemia in the elderly are profound, significantly elevating mortality and morbidity and diminishing QoL. The increased risk of all-cause mortality, particularly in frail or hospitalised patients, reflects the compounding effects of comorbidities and acute illness. Cardiovascular mortality, especially in heart failure patients, stems from reduced oxygen delivery and heightened cardiac workload, exacerbating ischemia and arrhythmias. Major cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and stroke, are driven by hypoxia-induced endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Prolonged hospital stays increase healthcare costs and risks of nosocomial infections, with severe anemia and surgical complications further delaying recovery. Cognitive decline, linked to cerebral hypoxia, accelerates neurodegenerative processes, posing a significant public health concern. Falls and fractures, more prevalent in women due to osteoporosis and frailty, carry substantial mortality risks, particularly for hip fractures. Fatigue, a pervasive symptom, severely limits functional capacity, especially in hospitalized patients. Frailty perpetuates a cycle of vulnerability, increasing dependency and care needs. In cancer patients, poor survival, particularly in lung cancer, reflects hypoxia-driven tumor progression. Depression further erodes QoL, especially in severe cases, compounding the overall burden. Heterogeneity in outcome estimates likely arises from varying follow-up durations, outcome definitions, and patient comorbidities, such as dementia or cardiovascular disease prevalence.
Management of anemia in the elderly, while etiology-specific, faces significant hurdles. For IDA, oral and IV iron supplementation are effective, with IV iron preferred in hospitalized patients for faster onset, though gastrointestinal side effects and hypersensitivity risks require careful monitoring. ACD management focuses on treating underlying conditions, with anti-inflammatory therapies yielding modest benefits, contingent on disease severity. ESAs, used in severe cases, carry a high thromboembolism risk, particularly in cancer patients, necessitating cautious application. Transfusions, now less common, provide rapid correction but risk volume overload and transfusion-related lung injury, especially in heart failure patients. Nutritional interventions, such as dietary fiber, reduce frailty, while B12/folate supplementation corrects deficiencies, though adherence remains a challenge. These management challenges highlight the need for safer, more effective strategies tailored to the elderly’s unique physiological profile.

Future research should prioritize uncovering the mechanisms of unexplained anemia, leveraging advanced diagnostics like bone marrow biopsies, hepcidin assays, or inflammatory marker profiling. Standardized diagnostic algorithms with age-adjusted hemoglobin thresholds are essential to guide interventions. Novel therapies, such as hepcidin inhibitors for ACD or hypoxia-inducible factor stabilizers as ESA alternatives, merit exploration in clinical trials, with a focus on safety in frail populations. Public health strategies, including food fortification with iron, B12, and folate, could mitigate IDA and nutritional deficiencies, particularly in low-income settings. Longitudinal studies with diverse cohorts, including underrepresented regions like Africa and South America, are needed to reduce heterogeneity and better capture chronic outcomes like frailty and cognitive decline. Cost-effectiveness analyses of interventions, such as IV iron versus oral iron or ESAs versus transfusions, will inform resource allocation in resource-constrained systems.
The limitations of the review include high heterogeneity across estimates, driven by diverse study designs, populations, and diagnostic criteria, which limited some meta-analyses. The focus on English-language studies may have excluded relevant non-English research, potentially skewing prevalence and management insights from regions with differing healthcare and nutritional contexts. Potential publication bias suggests underreporting of smaller studies with lower prevalence, though sensitivity analyses upheld the robustness of the findings. The median follow-up duration may underestimate chronic outcomes like frailty and cognitive decline, which develop over decades. Despite these constraints, this review offers a comprehensive, actionable synthesis, guiding the diagnosis, management, and prevention of anemia in the elderly.

Conclusion
Anemia in older adults casts a formidable shadow, fuelling mortality, cardiovascular woes, and eroded wellbeing. Its tangled causes – iron deficiency, chronic inflammation, and cryptic unexplained forms – evade easy diagnosis. Treatments, marred by risks, demand safer, age-specific alternatives. This review lays bare a toll on unexplained anemia, igniting a call for change. Research must probe origins and forge precise diagnostics of anemia of unexplained origins. Novel therapies, like hepcidin-targeted agents, promise progress, while nutrition and screening initiatives can stem its spread. Unified action will empower clinicians and policymakers to restore vitality for an ageing world.

Ethical approval
This systematic review, synthesizing existing peer reviewed and previously published studies, required no ethical approval as it involved secondary analysis without primary data collection or human subject interaction.
