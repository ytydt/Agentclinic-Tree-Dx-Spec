"A novel approach to understanding the role of TCF3 mutations in childhood B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia"
Highlights
Role of TCF3 gene in leukemia development: The TCF3 (E2A) gene encodes a crucial transcriptional factor essential for the differentiation of lymphoid progenitor cells. Its chromosomal rearrangements with various partner genes, such as PBX1, HLF, and ZNF384, lead to uncontrolled proliferation and the development of BCP-ALL.
WHO Classification: The 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumors distinguishes two types of B-cell lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma with TCF3 rearrangement: those with TCF3::PBX1 fusion and those with TCF3::HLF fusion.
Diagnostic Significance: TCF3 rearrangements are observed in 5-11% of pediatric BCP-ALL patients, making TCF3 an essential component of diagnostic panels for this leukemia.
Review Objective: This manuscript provides a comprehensive description of known TCF3 rearrangements and their associated gene partners, as well as addressing the impact of TCF3 fusion genes in pediatric ALL according to the latest data and modern treatments.
Latest Research: The review incorporates the most recent research results from the Children’s Oncology Group presented at the 66th American Society of Hematology (ASH) meeting, and explores a promising biomarker in B-ALL – circular RNA – in relation to TCF3 rearrangements.
The TCF3 (also known as E2A) gene is responsible for encoding a critical transcriptional factor that plays a pivotal role in the differentiation of lymphoid progenitor cells. The TCF3 has been implicated in chromosomal translocations involving various genes, including PBX1, HLF, and ZNF384, as evidenced by recent clinical case studies (rare occuring). These include TLX1, FLI1, and TEF. These rearrangements have been observed to result in uncontrollable proliferation and development of B-cell progenitor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (BCP-ALL). The 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumors distinguishes B-cell lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma with TCF3 rearrangement into two types: with TCF3::PBX1 fusion and TCF3::HLF fusion. TCF3-positive pediatric BCP-ALL accounts for 5-11% of patients, making TCF3 an essential component of diagnostic panels for this leukemia. The objective of this manuscript is to provide a comprehensive description of the known TCF3 rearrangements and their associated gene partners. The review will also address the impact of TCF3 fusion genes in pediatric ALL according to the latest data and modern treatments, including the latest research results of Children’s Oncology Group from the 66th American Society of Hematology (ASH) and promising biomarker in B-ALL - circular RNA with regard to TCF3 rearrangements. Additional genetic alterations, termed "secondary hits" are involved in lymphoid development and pathogenesis of ALL. The most frequently observed rearrangements in TCF3 are: PAX5, IKZF1, SETDB2, EBF1, as well as CDKN2A/B.

Introduction
Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is characterized by the clonal proliferation of lymphoid progenitor cells - lymphoblasts. B-cell progenitor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (BCP-ALL) is the most prevalent form of ALL in pediatric patients, accounting for approximately 85% of cases and occurring at a median age of 5 years. Over time, both the diagnosis and treatment of ALL have undergone significant developments. Over the past six decades, the event-free survival (EFS) rate has increased almost ninefold, reaching approximately 90%. Identification of risk stratification parameters and targeted therapy is facilitated by genetic diagnosis. Modern genome-wide analysis subdivides BCP-ALL into more than 30 distinct subtypes, each with varying implications. One such example is the TCF3 rearrangement, which is present in 5-11% of patients and is crucial for the diagnosis of leukemia. The TCF3 gene plays a pivotal role in the development of B lymphocytes, as it encodes a transcription factor. The rearrangement of TCF3 results in the generation of a novel transcript and a fusion protein that disrupts the normal lymphopoiesis process. As a consequence, lymphoblasts proliferate uncontrollably. The differentiation of TCF3 fusion genes is essential for risk stratification, as the effects of each partner gene on leukemia progression vary.

TCF3
The TCF3 gene, previously designated as E2A, is located on the short arm of chromosome 19 at position 19p13.3 and consists of 21 exons. This gene encodes two proteins belonging to the E family of helix-loop-helix (HLH) transcription factors, E12 and E47, which are the result of alternative splicing of exons coding the HLH domain. These proteins have the capacity to bind as homodimers or heterodimers to other members of the HLH family. E proteins possess two conserved transcriptional activation domains, AD1 and AD2, which are critical for their transcriptional activity. These domains enable E proteins to modulate gene expression by binding to specific DNA sequences through a basic region located at the amino-terminal of the HLH, and interacting with other transcription factors and co-regulators. E proteins are broadly expressed, though not ubiquitous. The restriction of certain E protein complexes to specific cell types has also been observed.
The importance of E proteins in T cell development is underscored by their role in initiating T lineage-specific gene expression, inhibiting cell cycle progression, and enforcing the pre-TCR checkpoint. These proteins regulate the rearrangement of T cell receptor genes and are involved in positive selection during thymocyte maturation. Additionally, E proteins interact with Id proteins to modulate their activity, influencing the commitment of progenitor cells to the T cell lineage. The absence of E proteins leads to abnormalities in the early stages of T-cell development.
In B cell development the key point is to drive common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) towards B-cell fate by induction of B-cell-associated transcription factor EBF1. The E proteins induce EBF1 expression in a macrophage cell line through their binding to the EBF1α promoter, which is active at the CLP stage. This suggests that E proteins may function as regulators of the initial expression of the EBF1 gene.

Moreover, TCF3 has been identified as a critical factor in the development of the central nervous system and is considered an integral component of the core regulatory circuitry of embryonic stem cells. Furthermore, the analysis identified multiple isoforms encoded by alternatively spliced transcripts were also identified in this gene, as well as a pseudogene of TCF3 located on the short arm of chromosome 9. This gene is involved in chromosomal translocations with different genes like PBX1, HLF, TLX1, FLI1, TEF or ZNF384 that are linked to lymphoid malignancies and are described in Table 1. The occurrence of TCF3 translocations results in the disruption of lymphopoiesis, and TCF3 plays an important role in this process, as the expression of fusion genes is under the control of TCF3. A comparable interaction between TCF3 and genetic patterns is postulated due to shared mechanisms that lead to ALL. The N-terminal region of TCF3 is separated from the C-terminal internal DNA-binding domain resulting in the formation of a chimeric protein. That binds to the partner gene's DNA-binding domain, thereby forming an oncogenic factor.

TCF3 translocations
The t(1;19)(q23;p13) TCF3::PBX1 (formerly E2A::PBX1) translocation is present in 5–6% of pediatric and 3.8% of adult B-cell precursor ALL and is associated with a pre-B in transition (cytoplasmic immunoglobulin heavy chain positive) immunophenotype. Previously considered high risk due to higher central nervous system involvement and relapse. The five-year event free survival rate in the absence of events is 85% and nowadays TCF3::PBX1 ALL is classified as favourable or intermediate risk with current treatment regimens. Approximately 80% of cytogenetic results identify t(1;19) as an unbalanced translocation der(19)t(1;19). Additionally, cases of balanced t(1;19) have been documented, as well as cases where both lines are present simultaneously. In the case of a balanced translocation, the chimeric protein TCF3::PBX1 is rarely transcribed.
At the 66th ASH Annual Meeting and Exposition presented outcomes for 6417 patients with B-ALL of Children’s Oncology Group. Among the patients 4.2% had t(1;19) diagnosed in karyotype, including twice more with unbalanced translocation. Pinchinat A. et al. deduced that translocation TCF3::PBX1 was associated with: female sex, age ≥10 years and white blood cells ≥50000/μL. One conclusion is no increased rates of central nervous system (CNS) relapse. Contemporary therapy makes it possible t(1;19) didn’t confer an inferior prognosis overall and doesn't need therapy intensification.
Children’s Oncology Group (COG) analyzed 16 115 patients with childhood ALL in years 1996-2014. Children with TCF3::PBX1 were very short time-to-relapse with a median 15.8 months. Among 414 patients, 13.8% relapsed and EFS 83.1 ± 1.9%. In the study cohort, patients with TCF3 gene rearrangement stood out despite their low number. The patients were the most frequent relapse in CNS.
Recently, the literature also described the first case of a pediatric patient with B-ALL with TCF3::PBX1 rearrangement, who presented orbital symptoms at the onset of the disease in the form of an isolated retro-orbital mass.
It is worth mentioning that orbital involvement in non-recurrent acute lymphoblastic neoplasms is extremely rare and is considered to be an indicator of a worse prognosis.
There have also been reports of B-LBL cases in which the aforementioned rearrangement was accompanied by masses in the femur.

The consequence of the TCF3::PBX1 fusion is the truncation of PBX1 and the subsequent overactivation of the gene. The expression of the resulting oncogenic transcription factor, TCF3::PBX1, contributes to the disruption of gene transcription regulated by the PBX/HOX/MEINOX complex. The TCF3 transcription factor exerts a strong inductive effect on gene transcription, while PBX1 binding to HOX proteins drives hematopoiesis, resulting in the accumulation of lymphoblasts. In most patients with this chromosomal translocation, lymphoblasts display characteristics associated with mature B lymphocytes (pre-B cells). Decreased levels of TCF3 protein are insufficient for normal B-cell differentiation.
Circular RNA (circRNA) is recently non-protein-coding RNA (ncRNA) promising biomarker in childhood B-ALL. In research Gutierrez-Camino et al. from among six genetic types of B-ALL highlighted subtype TCF3::PBX1-positive with the highest number of overexpressed and subtype-specific circRNAs. Additionally, in the group of patients indicated NUDT21 involved in biogenesis of this new type of ncRNA. The scientists suggest that NUDT21 regulated overexpression circRNA in patients with TCF3::PBX1 and could influence pathogenesis of B-ALL in children. The results confirm the role of the newly discovered RNA as a future biomarker, especially in leukemia with TCF3::PBX1.
The translocation t(17;19)(q22;p13.3) TCF3::HLF fusion is formed by the translocation of the homeobox-related TCF3 to the leucine zipper domain of HLF. The product of this rearrangement is the transcription factor Aurora A kinase, which has been demonstrated to increase the number of immature cells of the lymphoid lineage.
Two distinct types of this rearrangement have been identified, both involving exon 4 of HLF. Type 1 involves exon 16 of TCF3 and is clinically associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Type 2 involves exon 15 of TCF3 and results in hypercalcemia, while type 3 involves exon 14 of TCF3. The fusion of the TCF3 gene with HLF, is a rare occurrence, present in less than 1% of children. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the breakpoints of various TCF3::HLF fusion transcripts are delineated.
The TCF3::HLF is typically observed up to two years after diagnosis and is associated with patient relapse and death. The five-year survival rate of ALL with TCF3::HLF is approximately 25%, while TCF3::PBX1-positive ALL reaches up to 85% with intensive chemotherapy. The TCF3::HLF subtype occurs virtually exclusively in pediatric patients, particularly those undergoing puberty.
Lymphoblastic cells in TCF3::HLF-positive ALL exhibit a predominance of stem cell and myeloid lineage features, whereas TCF3::PBX1-positive ALL is characterized by leukemic cells with lymphoid characteristics.
According to the most recent 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours (2022), both translocations are classified as distinct subtypes of B-cell lymphoblastic leukemias/lymphomas.
In recent years, new gene partners of the TCF3 have been identified: FLI1 and TEF. One case of TCF3::TEF and two patients with TCF3::FLI1 have been reported. The number of reported ALL patients is insufficient to allow an evaluation of the fusions in terms of prognosis. The t(19;22)(p13.3;p13.3) translocation results in the fusion of exon 16 of TCF3 with exon 4 of TEF. The t(11;19)(q24.3;q13.2) translocation leads to the fusion of exon 16 of TCF3 with exon 17 of FLI1. The final fusion product is constituted of the first 16 exons of TCF3 and the last three exons of FLI1. While FLI1 and TEF may also serve as fusion partners, their identification remains elusive to the routine fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) method.A notable exception is the TCF3::TLX1 fusion, t(10;19)(q24;p13), which has been identified in a single center. It is noteworthy that such cases may only be detected through the employment of molecular methods, underscoring the importance of utilizing these techniques for more precise diagnosis of the TCF3 gene partners.
In 2022 the International Consensus Classification introduced a new entity, B-ALL with ZNF384 rearrangement, which is most prevalent in children and young adults. The ZNF384 rearrangement defines a distinct subtype of leukemia that can be diagnosed as BCP-ALL or B/myeloid mixed phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL). As a C-terminal partner of several genes, ZNF384 is rearranged, including the histone acetyltransferases and transcriptional regulators EP300 and CREBBP, SWI/SNF proteins SMARCA2 and ARID1B, and others (TAF15, EWSR1, TCF3, NIPBL, and CLTC). The most prevalent rearrangements involve the following: EP300::ZNF384 (particularly in BCP-ALL) and TCF3::ZNF384 (in both BCP-ALL and B/myeloid MPAL). The last rearrangement is the result of a translocation t(12;19)(p13.31;p13.3). Notably, this translocation remains undetectable by conventional karyotype analysis due to the gene locus being at the ends of the short chromosome arms. A number of TCF3::ZNF384 fusions have been identified, including those involving exon 11 of TCF3 and exon 3 of ZNF384, as well as exon 13 and exon 2, exon 13 and exon 3, exon 16 and exon 2, exon 17 and exon 7. A comparative analysis was conducted by Japanese researchers to examine the profiles of the most prevalent partner genes of TCF3, namely TCF3::ZNF384 and TCF3::PBX1. Patients in these two groups exhibited notable differences in B-cell developmental genes and transcriptional regulators. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that patients with TCF3::ZNF384 are predominantly eligible for intermediate risk (IR) and high risk (HR) - due to elevated WBC at diagnosis and respond poorly to conventional treatment, with a high predisposition to relapse - the most recurrent fusion gene to ZNF384. This fusion is most frequently diagnosed in children and adolescents and is characterized by recurrent rearrangement.
In contrast to ALL with TCF3::HLF fusion, intragenic deletions of PAX5 or VPREB1 and mutations in the RAS signaling pathway genes are rarely diagnosed in ALL positive for TCF3 rearrangement with PBX1 and ZNF384. A significant distinction between ALL TCF3::HLF-positive and TCF3::PBX1-positive cases is the involvement of the methylation process, whereby the DNA is subjected to differential methylation, resulting in the expression of altered genes, including those that have a role in leukemogenesis.

Additional genetic alterations
While additional genetic alterations are less prevalent in TCF3::PBX1 ALL than in other ALL subgroups, they play a significant role in the pathogenesis of disease progression. The most frequently observed alterations, termed “secondary hits”, in this ALL subgroup involve genes associated with B-cell differentiation and development, such as PAX5, IKZF1, EBF1, and genes involved in the p53/Rb signaling pathway (CDKN2A/2B, TP53, RB1). In a study by Li et al., significant involvement of point mutations in HIST family genes (HIST1H2AG and HIST1H2AI) was demonstrated. Furthermore, Ueno H. et al. recently reported positive correlations between ALL TCF3::PBX1 and mutations in the PHF6 (homeodomain finger protein 6) gene.
PAX5 (paired-box domain 5) is a critical transcription factor belonging to the family of paired-box domain transcription factors, which plays a pivotal role in regulating B cell development. Dysregulation of PAX5 impairs the ability to maintain B cell identity and inhibits the process of terminal differentiation, resulting in the stagnation of B cell maturation at early stages, such as pro-B or pre-B, and the accumulation of immature cells. PAX5 deletions are among the most prevalent alterations observed in B-ALL with TCF3::PBX1, although they occur less frequently than in other subtypes. The frequency of these alterations in analyses ranges from 6.9% to 37.7%, and they are considered a significant "second hit." Moreover, Barbosa et al. have described a novel case of a PAX5::SPECC1 translocation occurring concurrently with TCF3::PBX1. Both rearrangements were identified at the time of diagnosis and relapse, indicating that these alterations may collectively contribute to malignant transformation and a poor prognosis.
The deletion of PAX5 (locus 9p13.2) is frequently associated with the deletion of CDKN2A/2B (cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2a/b), mapping at 9p21.3. Deletions of CDKN2A or CDKN2B, commonly observed in studies of patients with ALL harbouring TCF3::PBX1, result in the loss of function of the p16INK4a and p15INK4b proteins. Consequently, this leads to the overactivation of CDK4/6 kinases, promoting an unregulated transition from the G1 to S phase. Additionally, it disrupts the function of the Rb protein through its excessive phosphorylation. Furthermore, the loss of p14ARF (encoded by CDKN2A) impairs the ability to stabilize p53, leading to its excessive degradation by MDM2. Some studies have observed that approximately 30.8% to 40% of ALL cases with a t(1;19) translocation exhibit a deletion of the CDKN2A gene. In contrast, Daisuke and colleagues reported a lower deletion rate of 18.9% in their Japanese study.
In their study, Lin et al. provided a comprehensive explanation of the oncogenic role of SETDB2, a lysine methyltransferase identified as a significant epigenetic regulator, which may play a key or cooperative role in the development of TCF3::PBX1 leukemia. Zhou et al. observed abnormalities in SETDB2 in approximately 6.9% of ALL cases with TCF3::PBX1. It was demonstrated that SETDB2 promotes the proliferation of leukemic cells through the epigenetic silencing of the CDKN2C cell cycle inhibitor through trimethylation of H3K9 histone. Consequently, an oncogenic pathway subordinated to TCF3::PBX1 is established. Furthermore, the analysis of normal HSPCs (hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells) revealed lower SETDB2 expression levels compared to leukemic cells, confirming its oncogenic function and establishing it as a potential therapeutic target. Notably, the CDKN2A gene is also silenced in leukemic cells with TCF3::PBX1, although through a separate mechanism dependent on BMI1, independent of SETDB2.
Loss of function in the IKZF1 gene, which encodes the transcription factor Ikaros, disrupts the epigenetic regulation of key genes responsible for cell cycle control and B-cell differentiation, including CRLF2 (cytokine receptor‐like factor 2). A reduction in IKZF1 function is associated with an increase in CRLF2 expression, which subsequently enhances signaling within the CRLF2/JAK-STAT pathway. This suggests the possibility of a therapeutic opportunity for kinase inhibitors, such as JAK2 inhibitors. Although IKZF1 alterations are less frequently observed in the BCP-ALL subtype with TCF3::PBX1 compared to other leukemia subgroups, their presence is significantly associated with an elevated risk of relapse. In a study conducted by Daisuke and colleagues, 25% of patients who experienced relapse exhibited deletion of IKZF1, whereas no such deletions were observed in patients who achieved sustained remission. These findings suggest that IKZF1 deletion may serve as an independent prognostic factor in pediatric BCP-ALL with the TCF3::PBX1 translocation.
Moreover, deletions in the EBF1 gene, a transcription factor responsible for the specification, development, and maintenance of the B-cell lymphoid lineage, are common in patients with BCP-ALL positive for the TCF3::PBX1. Furthermore, EBF1 has been demonstrated to reduce the expression of B-limp1 (B lymphocyte-induced maturation protein 1), a repressor of several transcription factor-encoding genes, including PAX5. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that ZNF521 expression is elevated, and Iglesias P. et al. observed increased ZNF423 expression in vitro in TCF3::PBX1-positive ALL cells. Both ZNF521 and ZNF423 act antagonistically to EBF1, inhibiting its ability to activate genes responsible for B-cell maturation, thereby promoting the accumulation of immature lymphoblasts and leukemia development.
The p53/RB signaling pathway is frequently disrupted in ALL with the presence of TCF3::PBX1. These abnormalities are more commonly attributed to disruptions in regulatory genes of this pathway, such as CDKN2A/CDKN2B, as previously mentioned. Such abnormalities may also involve the RB1 or TP53 tumor suppressor genes. In their study, Andersen and colleagues emphasized the frequency of del(13q), involving the RB1 locus, as a common secondary structural abnormality. Furthermore, studies have indicated that RB1 deletions are markedly more prevalent in patients with ALL and TCF3::PBX1, occurring in approximately 15.4% to 31.1% of cases in comparison to other cytogenetic subgroups. Barbosa et al. observed that the majority of RB1 deletions occurred in females (68.7%) and frequently co-occurred with 9p21 deletions. The significant correlation between RB1 and TCF3::PBX1 is also corroborated by studies from Ueno et al.
TP53 mutations are infrequently identified at the initial diagnosis of acute lymphoblastic leukemia with TCF3::PBX1 rearrangement. However, these mutations are more frequently observed in relapsed cases, suggesting that they may occur at later stages of tumor development and contribute to disease progression. These findings are consistent with observations in other types of ALL. Nevertheless, more recent studies have not corroborated a direct link between this mutation and relapse, potentially due to alterations in treatment protocols.
The impact of mutations occurring in conjunction with the TCF3::PBX1 rearrangement on the course of ALL remains unclear. Nevertheless, reports associating IKZF1 mutations with an elevated risk of relapse and the potential utilization of Janus kinase inhibitors, such as JAK2, in therapy are noteworthy. Previously proposed associations between relapses and TP53 mutations have not been corroborated by subsequent studies. Furthermore, a greater number of driver mutations have been identified in adolescents and adults, with a more diverse genetic profile. This includes a higher frequency of mutations in genes involved in cell cycle regulation, chromatin organization, NGF signaling, and transcriptional regulation, such as TP53, PAX7, and loss of BCR gene copy.To gain a more nuanced understanding of the impact of these co-occurring mutations on disease progression and treatment outcomes, further studies involving larger patient cohorts are necessary to provide a more accurate assessment of their clinical significance and potential therapeutic targets.
The identification of additional genetic alterations in leukaemias associated with the TCF3::HLF rearrangement has significant clinical implications, particularly in the context of poor prognosis and high treatment resistance. Studies in transgenic and knock-in mice indicate that the TCF3::HLF mutation alone is insufficient for leukemia development, suggesting the necessity of "secondary hits" similar to the TCF3::PBX1 rearrangement. It has been demonstrated that common alterations observed in other ALL subtypes, including the TCF3::PBX1 fusion, such as deletions of PAX5 and CDKN2A/B, have been identified. This emphasizes the role of the translocation itself in shaping the resistant phenotype. Moreover, the occurrence of frequent deletions of VPREB1 and BTG1 is also indicated. The deletion of VPREB1 is associated with a poorer prognosis in pediatric patients, resulting in the disruption of pre-B cell receptor function. Furthermore, the presence of activating mutations in the RAS pathway (NRAS, KRAS, PTPN11) is a distinguishing feature of this ALL subtype. These mutations are rare in TCF3::PBX1 and are associated with resistance to multiple drugs. This may contribute to the aggressive clinical course of ALL with t(17;19). Furthermore, the coexistence of a 13q deletion with RB1 loss and NOTCH1 duplication has been documented in a patient with TCF3::HLF-positive ALL. Additionally, biallelic NR3C1 loss, TP53 mutations, and CDKN2A/B deletions have been observed in a steroid-resistant relapse in an adult patient. It is crucial to highlight that NR3C1 mutations are linked to drug resistance. Although the TCF3::HLF fusion is uncommon in ALL, current research emphasizes the imperative for comprehensive genetic profiling of this subtype to enable more precise risk stratification and the development of more efficacious treatment regimens.

Additional genetic alterations
B-ALL is a heterogeneous disease entity, which can be observed at the genetic, clinical and demographic levels. Genetic variability refers to a sizable set of abnormalities, exemplified by translocations involving TCF3. Depending on the fusion partner of the gene, the effects exerted on the cell and the impact on the development of the cancer process are different. As a result, the course of treatment and patient prognosis is in some way dependent on the gene with which TCF3 is rearranged.

TCF3::PBX1 fusion
Over the years, there has been a steady shift in the treatment and patient prognosis with the TCF3::PBX1 fusion. Significant progress was made when patients with the aforementioned mutation were assigned to the high risk (HR) group (previously associated with low risk (SR) and thus undertreated).
The current regimen using intensive chemotherapy is a change that has transformed the reality of pediatric patients and ultimately made the unfavourable prognosis a thing of the past. It is worth mentioning that the effects of fusion therapy have been compared with favourable tumor outcomes such as hyperdiploidy and ETV6::RUNX1. However, in contrast to TCF3::PBX1, no CNS relapse has been reported.
An important group of drugs that has applications in the treatment of hematopoietic malignancies and many other tumors and autoimmune diseases, and in the case of ALL with a TCF3::PBX1 translocation has proved to be a great disappointment, are Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKis). Among them, particular hopes were pinned on ibrutinib, which initially showed promising results, while subsequent studies showed a lack of efficacy and a negative effect on cell sensitivity to vincristine used in chemotherapy.

It has been mentioned that the therapeutic effects after intensive chemotherapy are satisfactory, but it is well known that traditional chemotherapy is associated with many side effects, which can be avoided by reaching for targeted treatment with greater selectivity and efficacy. The mutation we describe leads to changes in the signaling pathways of pre-B ALL cells, which implies hyperactivation of the effector enzyme PLCγ2, important for oncogenesis, which enhances leukemic cell proliferation. Therefore, due to the functions performed by the enzyme, lowering its levels is a therapeutic target for selectively acting drugs. PLCγ2 phosphorylation is dependent on a group of kinases encoded by ZAP70, SYK and LCK, whose transcription is controlled by the TCF3::PBX1 fusion protein. Thus, the cycle of dependency closes: high levels of TCF3::PBX1 drive the expression of kinases, which activate PLCγ2, which is responsible for increased proliferation of cancer cells. Silencing ZAP70, SYK and LCK along with the subsequent depletion of kinase stores, results in lower levels of active PLCγ2 and thus leads to reduced cancer cell divisions. The use of kinase inhibitors has the same effect. Jesús Duque-Afonso et al. conducted a study that identified the inhibitors, distinguishing the tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) dasatinib, SYK inhibitor fostamatinib and LCK inhibitors. Dasatinib inhibits SRC and LCK kinases, and can be used to treat additional ALL subtypes, which includes TCF3::PBX1 mutation and BCR::ABL-like ALL. Fostamatinib is currently being used to treat autoimmune diseases, while the use of LCK inhibitors is only in the research stage.
The elucidated relationship between kinases and PLCγ2 in leukemias with TCF3::PBX1 fusion ensures the susceptibility of tumor cells to the mentioned inhibitors. Although research on their use is in its early stages, the results could prove extremely promising. A relatively fresh look toward the treatment of ALL with TCF3::PBX1 mutations takes into account the potential of epigenetics. Drugs that can affect chromatin regulators are new and are still in the preclinical and clinical research stages. Chiou-Hong Lin et al. described the role played by the lysine methyltransferase SETDB2 in leukemogenesis. They showed that the enzyme is crucial for the development of ALL, the oncogenic pathway involving it is as follows; TCF3::PBX1 regulates the expression of SETDB2, which in turn limits the expression of the cellular inhibitor CDKN2C. It should be mentioned that in non-tumor cells, SETDB2 expression is several times lower compared to ALL cells. This means two things, firstly, the methyltransferase is a potential therapeutic target, and secondly, the significant differences in SETDB2 levels between healthy and ALL-laden cells mean that inhibitors of the enzyme may show a safe therapeutic index.
Another of the targeted therapy proposals involves receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) and Wnt16B. TCF3::PBX1 cells exhibit consistently elevated levels of Wnt16b (ligand) and ROR1 (signalling pathway receptor), which indirectly contributes to increased tumor cell proliferation. Therefore, blocking ROR1 was taken as the target of therapy, which intensified the effect of other drugs, for example, venetoclax, navitoclax and Bcl-2 family inhibitors.

TCF3::HLF fusion
The TCF3::HLF rearrangement, which occurs in <1% of cases of pediatric ALL, has an extremely poor prognosis and high resistance to traditional treatment.
Patients with type B-ALL with the TCF3::HLF fusion show a poor response to induction therapy due to drug resistance. In addition, they usually experience early relapses within two years of diagnosis, which are resistant to stem cell transplantation and intensive chemotherapy. Their EFS rate is dramatically low at 25%. However, recently published case reports are promising and offer hope for successful remission in patients using blinatumomab combined with applied stem cell transplantation or the use of CD19 chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T).
Blinatumomab is a bispecific antibody that binds CD19-positive B cells and CD3- positive cytotoxic T cells and leads to T-cell-mediated B-cell lysis. It is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of children with relapsed or refractory Philadelphia chromosome negative BCP- ALL. Its efficacy is currently being tested in leukemia with the TCF3::HLF mutation. The potential benefit of combining blinatumomab with allogeneic stem cell transplantation (SCT) and using it specifically for minimal residual disease (MRD) is indicated.
Although CAR-T CD19 therapy shows greater increased efficacy compared to conventional chemotherapy, and Jao Jao et al. found no significant complications from its use in their study, the described strategy exposes its drawbacks in B-ALL TCF3::HLF-positive. It is believed that the biological features of ALL are not correlated with the efficacy of CAR-T therapy, but this is not the case with the mutation in question. The mechanism of this phenomenon can be explained as follows: the transcription factor TCF3::HLF increases the expression of DR4 and DR5 receptors, at the same time the expansion of abnormal cells is promoted by the tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL). It follows that despite the many advantages of CAR-T CD19 therapy, relapse can occur. Studies by Park JH et al. indicate that this is most likely related to premature depletion of CAR-T CD19 cells after transfusion, rather than evolution of the tumor cell clone. Therefore, more aggressive regimens, such as CAR-T bypass transplantation, should be used for recurrent TCF3::HLF-positive B-ALL.
The TCF3::HLF fusion cells have also been shown to be sensitive to the poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors veliparib and olaparib. PARP is involved in, among other things, regulation of the DNA damage response pathway, maintenance of genome stability, cell death and energy metabolism. Hypersensitivity to PARP inhibitors is associated with homologous recombination (HR) deficiency in cancer cells. The protein product TCF3::HLF controls the G2/M cell cycle checkpoint regulator, leading to decreased HR activity and increased expression of the anti-apoptotic factor BCL2, which further indirectly suppresses HR activity.
Targeted therapies focusing on selected components of the ALL-specific transcriptional dependency network with the mentioned mutation are also being explored. In their study, Lena Blümel et al. identify the following therapeutic targets: MDM2, DNA/RNA synthesis, mTOR and BCL2.
On the other hand, Ute Fischer et al. proposed using alisertinib to inhibit Aurora kinase (the expression of which in ALL with TCF3::HLF mutation is overexpressed), dasatinib, which inhibits SRC family kinases, and navitoclax or venetoclax (BCL2 inhibitors) to counteract the effects of BH3 family proteins. Treatment with venetoclax has its limitations, namely, its chronic use leads to the acquisition of drug resistance by increasing the expression of the anti- apoptotic factors MCL1 and BCL-XL.
A potential therapeutic target could also be the central integrator of epigenetic regulation in the described type of ALL, the cotranscription factor EP300. Its catalytic inhibitor is A-485, which induces anti-leukemic effects by interfering with TCF3::HLF-driven gene expression. The impact of EP300 and its future potential use in therapeutic regimens requires further study.

Conclusions
Genetic diagnosis of patients, particularly pediatric patients with suspected ALL, constitutes an essential element of the treatment process. The identification of rearrangements has implications for the treatment plan and monitoring of response. One of the genes that undergoes rearrangements is TCF3, which has multiple partner genes. The most prevalent of these have a significant impact on the course of therapy and treatment responses. It is therefore evident that an accurate diagnosis is significant to patients with leukemia. The role of new TCF3 fusions in the pathomechanism of ALL is under investigation. The review shows the role and significance of all TCF3 fusion genes.
