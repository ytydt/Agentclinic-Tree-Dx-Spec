A systematic review of methods used for COVID-19 telehealth systems evaluation: lessons from past experiences for future use
Background
During the COVID-19 emergency, telehealth systems were rapidly designed and integrated into healthcare delivery frameworks. The main question is whether these systems have been adequately evaluated and whether they are worthy of entering the health service cycle. Thus, this study’s objective was to critically analyze the literature on remote health systems developed in response to COVID-19, focusing specifically on the evaluation approaches used.
Methods
The present investigation was executed in 2024, focusing on literature published over five years following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, extending until January 2024. Comprehensive searches were conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases. A team of four researchers systematically evaluated and critically appraised relevant articles. Subsequently, the extracted data underwent rigorous analysis, comparison, and reporting to distill key findings.
Results
Overall, 26 articles met the inclusion criteria. The results indicated that the predominant objective of evaluations was to examine system performance (n = 8, 30.77%). Also, the methodology used in most evaluations was Qualitative and quantitative (non-integrated) (n = 18, 69.23%). In terms of evaluation period, most evaluations were summative (n = 12,46.15%), and the evaluator population in most systems was the healthcare team (n = 14, 53.85%).
Conclusions
Given the COVID-19 emergency conditions, evaluations of telehealth systems have not been conducted systematically and with adequate methods, tools, and populations. Therefore, appropriate systems should be designed and adequately evaluated for future epidemics. Ultimately, the proactive and comprehensive design and evaluation of telehealth systems will create infrastructures that can respond effectively and sustainably to future outbreaks.

Introduction
Coronavirus is a pathogenic agent that typically causes respiratory tract infections in humans. The disease swiftly spread to all countries within two months, prompting the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare a public health emergency in January 2020. During the public health emergency, the world witnessed new virus strains and different waves of infection and death statistics. Sudden increases in new cases and deaths following the release of new strains led to severe pressure on the healthcare system worldwide, necessitating the exploration of new healthcare delivery methods. Social quarantine was proposed as a significant preventive measure to reduce disease transmission in such a situation. However, quarantine and the need to observe social distancing as one of the essential solutions to control COVID-19 are considered obstacles to the movement of patients, especially patients with COVID-19, at the community level and receiving health care services. Therefore, there is a contradiction between the need to receive health services and quarantine and social distancing. Health information technology, especially telehealth, can be vital in such a situation. These systems could bridge the gap between the provider and recipient of care, offering hope for improved healthcare delivery.
Telehealth means providing and receiving health services in two or more places far apart. In the early definitions of telehealth, this distance meant very long and inaccessible distances, such as between a hospital and ocean liners, spaceships, remote islands, villages, etc. After passing through COVID-19, it can be stated that this distance can be short and limited (within the scope of the hospital rooms) to create social distances and reduce the transmission of pathogens. Telehealth systems were designed and used for two purposes during the COVID-19 era. First, to minimize disease transmission (by reducing traffic at the community level, properly implementing public quarantine, and reducing the accumulation of patients in health centers) and second, to reduce the pressure and workload of healthcare workers and provide 24-hour healthcare services. Various studies show that these systems are used for multiple purposes of prevention, diagnosis, treatment, disease management, monitoring, and providing consulting services using various information and communication technologies such as simple telephone, email, video conference, applications, Websites, etc., which are designed and used online and offline. The review studies conducted on telehealth systems during COVID-19 show that these systems have improved the existing situation in most cases and have met the initial goals to some extent during the period of use. However, the main question is whether the designed systems have been adequately evaluated.
Evaluation of health information technologies (such as telehealth systems, mobile health, clinical decision support systems, etc.) is one of the most critical steps that must be done before it applies to providing health care. This evaluation should be done using the appropriate methods and tools within a reasonable time and with the participation of its primary users, including healthcare professionals, patients, and information systems evaluation specialists at the evaluation laboratory or the main settings of system use. Their input is crucial in understanding telehealth systems’ practical implications and effectiveness. The purpose of telehealth systems evaluation can differ. Various types of evaluations, including system usability, user satisfaction, system impact, performance, effectiveness, and others, can be conducted per the specific requirements and expectations of developers, users, and stakeholders. These evaluations enable us to assess a system and identify weaknesses, strengths, and areas for improvement. In specific scenarios, a telehealth system’s sensitivity can be exceedingly high, making it unsuitable for deployment in the clinical domain and catering to patients’ needs. Before using the system in such instances, a comprehensive evaluation of the system is essential. This necessitates the involvement of many specialized evaluators and experts to conduct a thorough evaluation. Such a comprehensive evaluation process can identify areas for improvement and enhance the system’s effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes. This stress on the potential benefits of a comprehensive evaluation process will make the audience feel optimistic about the future of telehealth systems, motivating them to engage with the study’s findings and recommendations.
Amid the urgent need for telehealth systems during the COVID-19 pandemic, these systems were swiftly introduced into the healthcare industry, often without a comprehensive ethical framework for evaluation. This underscores the critical importance of assessing the dimensions and implications of these systems. While their rapid deployment may offer short-term benefits, it can also lead to many problems. For instance, if a system fails to address the primary needs of patients, such as the need for face-to-face communication for accurate diagnosis and treatment, it can result in an incorrect diagnosis, erroneous therapy, and severe complications for the patient. Moreover, it can impose significant liability on the care provider. Therefore, it is crucial to ensure that telehealth systems are evaluated and implemented correctly to avoid such adverse outcomes. Given the pivotal role of evaluation in telehealth systems developed and utilized for managing the COVID-19 pandemic, this study explores the evaluation methodologies adopted for such systems during the pandemic. The study aims to introduce and demonstrate various evaluation strategies for COVID-19 telehealth systems and their strengths and weaknesses as valuable insights that could be utilized as lessons learned from the COVID-19 era.

Methods
The study used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Supplementary File 2, outlining our adherence to each of the 27 items. While most items were addressed within the manuscript, a few items, such as protocol registration and risk of bias assessment, were not explicitly applicable to the nature of our review.

Search keywords, search strategies, and databases
Based on the research’s main question (“What are the evaluation methodologies for telehealth systems in COVID-19?”), Keywords were extracted along the three axes of “Telehealth,” “COVID-19,” and “Evaluation” using the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) thesaurus. The keywords used to formulate the search strategy are presented in Supplementary File 1 (Supplementary Table 1). We combined extracted keywords to formulate effective search strategies using logical operators (AND, OR). These strategies were then refined using the principles of search strategy formulation in three databases - PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. The entire process of extracting keywords, selecting databases, and formulating search strategies was conducted under the guidance of two experts in medical library and information science, who provided their expertise to ensure the thoroughness and accuracy of the search strategies. The formulation of search strategies is presented in Supplementary File 1 (Supplementary Table 2).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Journal articles.
Original articles with the full text in english.
Telehealth systems that were developed for COVID-19 during the pandemic.
Telehealth systems that have been evaluated and their evaluation results reported.
Telehealth systems with two-way or multi-way (client and server) communication.
Telehealth systems were developed to diagnose, treat, manage, and follow up on COVID-19.
Inclusion criteria
Other articles include review articles, short communications, letters, commentaries, and case reports.
Articles in languages other than english.
Articles that were not about using telehealth to provide healthcare services to COVID-19 patients during COVID-19, such as those about other types of diseases (except COVID-19) or other types of health information technologies (except any telehealth), as well as articles that were not published during the COVID-19 period.
Exclusion criteria
The search was conducted over four years, from December 2019 to December 2023. The study employed specific criteria for the inclusion or exclusion of articles, which were rigorously evaluated for their relevance and suitability. These criteria were designed to ensure the accuracy and validity of the study’s findings.

Study selection
PRISMA flow chart of included studies
Figure 1 illustrates the procedure for selecting studies. After searching the databases, all studies were systematically screened and chosen for the final review based on their titles, abstracts, and full texts.

Data extraction and analysis
Data were collected using a structured form developed by the research team, refined over three sessions to align with study objectives, and included 23 clearly titled entries capturing evaluation objectives, methodologies, period, duration, and population. Evaluation methodologies were classified as qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods, with mixed methods further categorized as “qualitative and quantitative (non-integrated)” when studies collected both data types without explicit synthesis (e.g., triangulation or complementary analysis), as per established guidelines (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2017). Studies lacking a methodological framework for integrating findings were deemed non-integrated. Three researchers independently screened articles, holding ten consultation meetings to resolve ambiguities in titles, abstracts, or full texts through consensus. If extracted data were insufficient, the team re-evaluated the article, and when necessary, a researcher contacted the corresponding author to obtain additional information. Data were analyzed quantitatively and qualitatively and presented in tables and figures.
Due to the methodological heterogeneity of the included studies, a formal risk of bias assessment was not feasible. However, a minimal risk assessment was conducted by classifying studies based on their design type according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) Levels of Evidence. Each study was assigned an evidence level and estimated risk of bias. The results are summarized in Supplementary File 1 (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion
Evaluating a product based on information and communication technology from different dimensions and verifying its performance from the user’s and evaluation expert’s perspectives can be considered an essential step in the life cycle of systems. Logically, every technological product should be subjected to rigorous and scientific evaluations before being used in practice. This issue is more critical for products related to human health. However, this can be different in conditions far from natural conditions. An important example of abnormal conditions was the world situation during the COVID-19 pandemic. In this era, even vaccines were not adequately evaluated. Most of the high-speed clinical trial studies, with limited populations, were conducted in the very early phases, and conclusions about the product supply were made without considering side effects. Despite the side effects that occurred, vaccination has controlled the disease around the world. However, with the gained experience, more careful action will be taken in future pandemics.
The rapid evolution of technological products, particularly Telehealth solutions, during the COVID-19 pandemic, underscores a significant trend in healthcare delivery. Although developed under stringent time constraints and lacking comprehensive evaluative processes to ascertain their accuracy, usability, and overall effectiveness, these systems managed to bridge the communication gap between patients and healthcare providers effectively and reduce the workload of the health system. However, the lessons learned from these experiences highlight the imperative for a more systematic approach to designing and evaluating such systems in the context of future pandemics. Future advancements should emphasize thorough validation and optimization to enhance performance and ensure reliability in critical healthcare interactions.
The study’s results indicate that evaluations of COVID-19 telehealth systems are predominantly focused on performance (n = 8, 30.77%) and hybrid evaluation (n = 8, 30.77%), emphasizing system performance and user satisfaction. In contrast, process (n = 1, 3.84%) and usability (n = 2, 7.69%) evaluations received less attention. This trend aligns with the urgency of the pandemic, where the priority was to manage high patient volumes without compromising service quality. Developers aimed to implement these systems rapidly, assess their performance, and ensure user satisfaction, often at the expense of comprehensive usability and impact evaluations. These findings are well mentioned in the study by Chuo et al. They have emphasized that evaluating system performance is an essential step in emergency software during the COVID-19 era. However, neglecting broader evaluation aspects, such as usability, impact, and safety assessments, may lead to significant challenges ranging from minor inefficiencies to severe operational issues.
The predominance of qualitative and quantitative non-integrated methodologies (69.23%, n = 18) in COVID-19 telehealth evaluations reflects the pandemic’s urgency, which prioritized rapid data collection over methodological synthesis, such as triangulation or complementary analysis. While quantitative methods effectively captured system performance metrics (e.g., response time, user volume), qualitative approaches better elucidated user experiences and satisfaction challenges. However, the lack of predefined mixed methods frameworks, clear methodological rationale, or specified evaluation tools and populations in these studies limited the depth of insights. This was likely due to time constraints and ad-hoc implementation, leading to non-integrated data collection without synthesis. Future telehealth evaluations should adopt standardized mixed methods approaches to integrate qualitative and quantitative findings, ensuring comprehensive assessments that enhance system reliability and user satisfaction.
The results of the present study show that summative evaluation (n = 12, 46.15%) has received more attention than formative and in-use evaluations. It appears that, in crises such as COVID-19, although conducting summative evaluation after the rapid implementation of systems can be efficient, it is evident that the system’s initial flaws and fundamental problems must be accurately identified and resolved before its use. Ekeland et al. point out in their study that to extract the issues of the systems and understand them, formative assessments should be conducted initially, and then in-use evaluations should be performed. Field also discussed the issue that, after resolving the initial and existing problems of the system at the period of its use, summative assessments could accurately reflect the final issues, which is not the case in COVID-19 telehealth systems. It is important to discuss how the pressure caused by the COVID-19 pandemic inevitably affected standard evaluation methods. In general, compressed timelines forced stakeholders to execute telehealth services without conducting comprehensive evaluations. Furthermore, in some cases, ethical considerations have taken a lower priority than the immediate delivery of services to patients, and resource constraints (both human and financial) have limited the scope of real-time monitoring and data collection.
The present study’s results show that most evaluations were conducted within 1 to 3 months or 6 to 9 months (n = 6, 23.08%). Although the evaluation duration depends on the evaluation method and approach, according to studies a complete evaluation requires sufficient time. Therefore, it can be acknowledged that, in terms of the length of the evaluation period, COVID-19 telehealth systems have not been evaluated within a specific and sufficient timeframe. In general, it can be stated that conducting evaluations within a very limited time frame may lead to the concealment of system issues that manifest only in the long term.
In terms of the population of system evaluators, the results of the present study showed that most evaluators of COVID-19 telehealth systems (n = 14, 53.85%) are members of the healthcare team. In the later stage, patients participated in some of the evaluations. However, evaluations by experts and specialists in medical informatics and health information technology were conducted in only two studies. Given that evaluation by experts can reflect system issues and problems from different dimensions and angles, the lack of use of evaluation experts and reliance on the treatment team is one of the problems with the evaluations conducted. Lee et al. emphasize the participation of a multidisciplinary team in evaluating information systems in their study.
In this regard, it can be stated that although the urgency resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic justified performance-based and summative evaluations, the neglect of formative and usability evaluations needs to be examined. One of the structural factors contributing to this issue could be the lack of evaluation experts within the development teams of telehealth systems during the COVID-19 era. These teams were often composed of healthcare providers whose primary focus was on delivering urgent and emergency care. From a logistical perspective, the necessity for rapid implementation and integration of these systems left insufficient time for iterative evaluations and user-centered designs, which are typically the foundation of formative and usability assessments. Organizationally, many healthcare centers lacked the protocols or sufficient resources to implement multidimensional evaluation methods during the crisis.
Overall, it can be stated that although the urgency caused by the COVID-19 pandemic justified performance-based and summative evaluations, the lack of attention to formative and usability evaluations needs to be addressed. One of the structural factors behind this issue may have been the shortage of evaluation specialists in the telehealth system development teams during the COVID-19 era. These teams were often composed of healthcare providers whose primary focus was on delivering urgent and emergency care. From a logistical point of view, the need for rapid implementation and integration of these systems left insufficient time for iterative evaluations and user-centered designs, which typically form the foundation of formative and usability evaluations. Organizationally, many healthcare centers lacked the protocols or sufficient resources to implement multidimensional evaluation methods during the crisis.
Before the COVID-19 pandemic, the evaluation of tele-health systems was conducted with careful planning and extensive attention to formative and user-centered methods. These approaches involved comprehensive assessments of usability, user satisfaction, clinical effectiveness, and system safety, which, despite being time-consuming, contributed to continuous improvement and high-quality systems. However, with the onset of COVID-19, the urgent need for telehealth solutions shifted the focus toward performance-based and summative evaluations aimed at assessing efficiency under emergency conditions and high demand. Due to time and resource constraints, aspects such as user experience and user involvement were deprioritized, leading to issues such as reduced quality, incompatibility with clinical workflows, and safety challenges. These differences indicate that there must be a balanced future approach—one that ensures both rapid deployment and the preservation of quality.
Ultimately, the lessons learned for future use indicate that investments should be made promptly in well-designed telehealth systems that have undergone formative and summative evaluations, have been utilized in at least one simulated crisis, and are prepared for integration into clinical processes. Secondly, regarding the population of evaluators, the systems employed to deliver services during a crisis should be assessed by a unified and multidisciplinary team of evaluation specialists, healthcare professionals, and patients to analyze and identify systemic issues from various viewpoints. Third, criteria must be created for the evaluation of telehealth systems; both current and prospective systems should be evaluated according to these criteria. This facilitates the accurate ranking of systems and enables reasonable comparisons among them. Finally, implementing proactive and thorough design and evaluation of telehealth systems will establish infrastructures capable of responding effectively and sustainably to future epidemics.
Despite the urgency of the COVID-19 pandemic, the widespread reliance on rapid, summative, and non-integrated evaluations raises critical concerns about their long-term implications. Limited usability testing, absence of longitudinal data, and lack of integration between user experience and performance metrics meant that decision-makers often had to act on fragmented evidence, potentially resulting in suboptimal or unsafe care delivery. For instance, several systems were implemented without validating their compatibility with existing clinical workflows or assessing accessibility for vulnerable populations, which may have compromised patient safety or exacerbated health disparities. These issues highlight a deeper structural gap: the absence of comprehensive frameworks such as MAST (Model for Assessment of Telemedicine) or RE-AIM, which provides multidimensional evaluation across clinical, organizational, economic, and ethical domains. Studies show that systems not assessed via such frameworks tend to perform poorly in scalability and sustained impact. Furthermore, insufficient evaluation can hinder adaptive system redesign and impede evidence-based policy formation. Thus, beyond acknowledging the limitations of COVID-era evaluations, there is an urgent need to institutionalize robust evaluation frameworks that not only assess short-term effectiveness but also inform system resilience, equity, and safety over time. Future health crises will demand rapid yet rigorous telehealth deployment—something only achievable through proactive integration of structured, interdisciplinary evaluation models from the outset.
A key methodological consideration in systematic reviews is the registration of a predefined protocol, such as PRISMA, which ensures transparency, reproducibility, and credibility. In this study, due to the urgent need for rapid dissemination of COVID-19 telehealth evaluation findings, a pre-registered protocol was not established. While this approach allowed timely access to critical data, it inherently limits some aspects of methodological rigor and transparency. To mitigate these effects, comprehensive methodological details have been provided throughout the study. Future research should balance the urgency of reporting with adherence to protocol registration to maintain both timeliness and scientific robustness.

Conclusion
The findings of this study showed that evaluations conducted on telehealth systems developed for COVID-19 often did not follow a pre-defined plan. These evaluation methods were conducted in an emergency manner and, in most cases, to assess system performance and measure user satisfaction in a limited manner and with a few healthcare team members. Therefore, it can be stated that these evaluations are not sufficient to identify the positive and negative points and strengths and weaknesses of COVID-19 telehealth systems. In general, it can be said that despite the methodological shortcomings, evaluator population, evaluation duration, and other issues in the assessment of telehealth systems during the COVID-19 period, the competence of none of these systems has been fully confirmed. Therefore, it appears that using these systems cannot fully guarantee clinical decision-making and patient safety, as they have not been evaluated using appropriate methods, with enough evaluators, and over an adequate time duration.
Despite the challenges faced, these experiences provide invaluable lessons for managing future public health emergencies. Key insights include the importance of flexibility in evaluation design, the adoption of iterative and agile assessment methods, and prioritizing active stakeholder engagement during crises. These lessons can significantly enhance the rapid and effective deployment of telehealth systems in emergencies. To better prepare for future crises, it is essential to develop predefined rapid evaluation frameworks that are adaptable and resilient to the dynamic nature of emergency contexts. Finally, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the evaluation methodologies for COVID-19 telehealth systems and offers practical recommendations for future telehealth evaluations and implementations, helping prepare for subsequent pandemics.

Research limitations
This study had several limitations:
During the COVID-19 era, various telehealth systems have been developed and used; however, scientific reports have yet to be published. Therefore, we were unable to include this group of systems in our study.
It was observed that some published articles lacked comprehensive information about their evaluation sections. We attempted to gather additional data by contacting the corresponding authors to compensate for this limitation. However, in some instances, we did not receive any response despite the relevance of the studies, leading to their exclusion from the research.
This systematic review did not have a pre-registered protocol, which may limit its transparency and reproducibility. However, every effort was made to report the study methodology clearly and in detail. Due to the necessity of rapid dissemination of results and the importance of timely access to findings for the scientific community, protocol registration was not feasible at the time of the study.
Due to the high heterogeneity in article designs, purposes, and evaluation approaches, a formal risk of bias or quality appraisal was not feasible. However, a minimal risk assessment was conducted based on study design types and corresponding levels of evidence to support interpretation. While this approach provides a general indication of evidence strength, it may still limit the depth of comparability across studies.
