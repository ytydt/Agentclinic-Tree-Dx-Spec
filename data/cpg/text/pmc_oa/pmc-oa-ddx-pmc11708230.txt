Update on the management of ductal carcinoma in situ of the breast: current approach and future perspectives
Abstract
The standard treatment for ductal carcinoma in situ became well established through the results of several valuable clinical trials, and its therapeutic benefits have now come to be taken for granted. Ductal carcinoma in situ has an extremely good prognosis with the current treatment approach, with a 10-year breast cancer-specific survival rate of 97–98%. According to one retrospective cohort study, the breast cancer-specific survival rate of patients with low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ does not differ significantly between patients undergoing and not undergoing surgery. Some patients with ductal carcinoma in situ are not at a risk of progression to invasive cancer, but the predictors of such progression have not yet been clearly identified. Therefore, the same therapeutic strategies have been used to treat ductal carcinoma in situ and under the assumption that they have risks of invasive breast cancer, and a well-balanced risk/benefit ratio in respect of treatment has not yet been achieved. Based on the results of several recent clinical trials aimed at ensuring provision of a well-balanced treatment for patients with ductal carcinoma in situ which carries a good prognosis, de-escalation of postoperative adjuvant therapy has now begun. Currently, not only is the optimization of postoperative adjuvant therapy accelerating, but also clinical trials to de-escalate basic surgical treatments are under way. There is a possibility of achieving individualized treatment for patients with ductal carcinoma in situ of the breast with reduced treatment intervention. In this review, we present an overview of the current treatment approaches and potential future management strategies for ductal carcinoma in situ of the breast.

Introduction
Approximately one in nine Japanese women will develop breast cancer during their lifetimes. According to a projection of the breast cancer incidence in 2023, the number of new primary breast cancer cases was estimated to be 97 300, with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) estimated to account for ~14 000 cases. In the USA, one in eight women will develop cancer in their lifetimes. Invasive breast cancer (IBC) was expected to be diagnosed in an estimated 297 790 women in 2023, with an additional 55 720 cases of DCIS.
The prognosis of DCIS is extremely good, and the 10-year cumulative breast cancer death rate has been reported to be 1.4–2.8% [National Surgical Adjuvant Breast and Bowel Project (NSABP) B-17, B-24]. In cases of DCIS without micrometastasis, significantly high local control rates have been obtained with surgery and postoperative adjuvant therapy, however it has been reported that the selection of the surgical procedure (mastectomy vs. breast-conserving therapy) and adjuvant therapy does not influence the rate of death from breast cancer. Although the prognosis after surgery is excellent in patients with DCIS, treatment often results in poor cosmetic outcomes, because the surgery is similar to that for IBC. Issues of overtreatment of DCIS have drawn attention around the world.
In this review, we present the current treatments for DCIS and the potential for future risk-based treatment strategies.

Standard treatment for DCIS
a) Surgery for DCIS
The standard local treatment for DCIS is breast-conserving surgery (BCS) or total mastectomy, the same as the treatment options for invasive carcinoma. Although no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have established the validity of BCS for DCIS, a number of meta-analyses and reviews have indicated it as a valid treatment option for DCIS. If negative margins can be ensured, the choice of BCS or mastectomy is a decision based on the patient’s wishes and requires discussions with the individual for shared decision-making.
Axillary lymph node metastasis is extremely rare in DCIS, and axillary lymph node dissection is generally not performed in these patients; the reported frequency of metastatic lymph nodes in patients with pure DCIS is 0–2.0%. On the other hand, the positivity rate of sentinel lymph node metastasis (excluding isolated tumor cells) in cases of DCIS associated with invasive carcinoma ≤1 mm in size, defined as DCIS with microinvasion (DCISM), was 2.9–7.4%. However, as reagents used for sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) cannot be administered if invasive cancer is pathologically confirmed after mastectomy, therefore, concurrent SLNB may be considered in patients undergoing mastectomy. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Clinical Practice Guidelines recommend that patients with DCIS should undergo ‘BCS without lymph node surgery’ or ‘total mastectomy with SLNB’. The Japanese Breast Cancer Society Clinical Practice Guidelines also suggest that SLNB is not necessary in patients with preoperatively diagnosed non-invasive ductal carcinoma of the breast with no suspicion of invasive carcinoma in the overall clinical diagnosis, in whom breast-conserving therapy is planned. However, SLNB is acceptable when it is difficult to perform a two-stage biopsy at the same time as a resection of the primary tumor.
It has been reported that the diagnosis is underestimated in 10–38% of cases diagnosed as DCIS by needle biopsy. Mammographic evidence of a mass, a wide tumor area, presence of a palpable mass, nuclear grade (NG) 3, histological grade (HG) 3 and comedo necrosis have been suggested as being risk factors for mixed invasive cancer. While selecting the surgical method, it is important to recognize that needle biopsy may underestimate the presence of invasive cancer.
Achieving negative histological margins is crucial for patients undergoing BCS, as positive surgical margins are one of the important risk factors for local recurrence (LR). In a meta-analysis conducted to determine the association between the surgical margin status and the risk of LR in women with DCIS of the breast undergoing breast-conserving therapy, the odds ratio (OR) for LR was associated with the surgical margin status (logistic regression analysis: OR 0.53 for negative vs. positive/close surgical margin [P < 0.001]. However, there was weak evidence of lower odds at 2 mm compared with >0 or 1 mm [relative OR = 0.72, 95% credible interval (CrI) 0.47–1.08], and no evidence of a difference between 2 and 10 mm (relative OR = 0.99, 95% CrI 0.61–1.64). The Society of Surgical Oncology (SSO)—American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO)—American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Consensus Guideline recommends a negative surgical margin of at least 2 mm based on this meta-analysis showing a reduced risk of ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence (IBTR). However, the risk of recurrence for DCIS patients with positive margin differs depending on the expected amount of residual tumor and tumor grade, and data available until date are insufficient to determine whether surgical resection or radiation therapy (with or without boost radiation) is better for the risk reductions of LR. Treatment for a positive surgical margin requires careful discussions, including with the patient. Furthermore, a comprehensive analysis will only be possible after clarification of the definition of a positive margin and the availability of data to determine the long-term prognosis.
b) Radiotherapy for DCIS
Results of four RCTs of radiotherapy for DCIS after BCS have been reported: the NSABP B-17 trial, European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) 10 853 trial, the UK, Australia and New Zealand (UK/ANZ) DCIS trial and the Swedish DCIS (SweDCIS) trial. All of these trials demonstrated a reduced incidence of IBTR in the radiotherapy arm. A meta-analysis conducted by the Early Breast Cancer Trialists’ Collaborative Group (EBCTCG) reported that radiotherapy reduced the absolute 10-year risk of any IBTR (either recurrent DCIS or invasive cancer) by 15.2% (SE 1.6%; 12.9 vs. 28.1%; 2P < 0.00001). Similarly, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews confirmed a statistically significant benefit from the addition of radiotherapy in terms of the incidence of all ipsilateral breast events [hazard ratio (HR), 0.49; 95% CI, 0.41 to 0.58; P < 0.00001]. However, there was no significant effect of radiotherapy on the breast cancer mortality, mortality from causes other than breast cancer, or all-cause mortality after 10 years of follow-up.
In 2011, the ASTRO published an evidence-based guideline on hypofractionated—whole breast irradiation (HF-WBI) for Stage pT1–2 pN0 breast cancer (satisfying the following criteria: age 50 years or older, chemotherapy-naive and radiation dose homogeneity within ±7% in the central axis plane). Subsequently, the 2018 ASTRO guidelines were updated to remove restrictions such as age and prior systemic chemotherapy, and hypofractionated irradiation was recommended for all cases that did not require regional lymph node irradiation outside the tangential irradiation field. HF-WBI is also the standard treatment for patients who have undergone BCS.
More recently, the radiation doses and fractionation schedules for non-low-risk DCIS were evaluated in the Breast International Group 3-07/Trans-Tasman Radiation Oncology Group 07.01 study (the BIG 3-07/TROG 07.01 study). Eligible patients were women aged 18 years or older with unilateral, histologically proven, non-low-risk DCIS treated by BCS with a clear radial resection margin of at least 1 mm. Patients were randomly assigned to ‘whole breast irradiation-conventional fractionation (WBI-CF) only’, ‘WBI-hypofractionation (WBI-HF) only’, ‘WBI-CF with tumor bed boost (WBI-CF +TBB)’ or ‘WBI-HF + TBB’. At a median follow-up of 6.6 years, the 5-year LR-free rates were 92.7% (95% CI, 90.6–94.4%) in the no-TBB arm and 97.1% (95% CI, 95.6–98.1%) in the TBB arm (HR 0.47; 95% CI, 0.31–0.72; P < 0.001). However, the TBB group had higher rates of grade 2 or higher breast pain [10% (8–12%) vs. 14% (12–17%); P = 0.003] and induration [6% (5–8%) vs. 14% (11–16%); P < 0.001]. On the other hand, there was no statistically significant difference in the 5-year LR-free rate between the HF and CF arms. The BIG 3-07/TROG 07.01 study reported results that lent support to the adoption of moderately HF-WBI to improve the balance between local control, toxicity and socio-the economic burden of treatment.
The NCCN Guidelines recommend ‘Whole breast radiation therapy (WBRT) [category 1] with or without boost to tumor bed’ or ‘Accelerated partial breast irradiation (APBI) / partial breast radiation (PBI) [category 2A]’ or ‘No radiation therapy (RT) [category 2B]’. ‘PRINCIPLES OF RADIATION THERAPY’ states the following: APBI/PBI offers comparable local control to WBRT in selected patients with low-risk early-stage breast cancer. However, the optimal external beam-APBI/PBI technique/fractionation for minimizing the long-term cosmesis effects has not yet been determined. Patients are encouraged to participate in clinical trials.
c) Endocrine therapy for DCIS
Both the NSABP B-24 and UK/ANZ DCIS trials demonstrated the effectiveness of tamoxifen after BCS for DCIS of the breast.
In the NSABP B-24 trial, which was a double-blind RCT, 1804 patients with DCIS after BCS were assigned to a group that received treatment with oral tamoxifen (20 mg/day) for 5 years or a group that received placebo, concurrently with RT. The median follow-up period was 163 months. The 15-year cumulative incidence of invasive-IBTR was 8.5% in the tamoxifen group and 10.0% in the placebo group (HR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.49–0.95; P = 0.025). In addition, the 15-year cumulative incidence of contralateral breast cancer was 7.3% in the tamoxifen group and 10.8% in the placebo group (HR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.48–0.95; P = 0.023). There was no significant difference in the 15-year cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality or breast cancer mortality between the two groups. Patients with estrogen receptor (ER)-positive DCIS treated with tamoxifen (vs. placebo) showed a significant decrease in the incidence of subsequent breast cancer (HR, 0.64, P = 0.003). In addition, the effect of tamoxifen was more pronounced in patients with a positive resection margin.
The UK/ANZ DCIS trial was a randomized 2 × 2 factorial trial of radiotherapy or tamoxifen at the dose of 20 mg once daily for 5 years, or both, and 1701 postoperative patients with DCIS were enrolled. This trial involved two 2-way randomizations post-lumpectomy: first, surgeon/patient choice about radiotherapy (yes or no), then randomization to receive tamoxifen or no tamoxifen, and second, surgeon/patient choice about tamoxifen (yes or no), then randomization to receive radiation therapy or not receive radiation therapy. The median follow-up period was 12.7 years. Both the incidences of IBTR (13.2 vs. 17.0%; HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.59–0.98; P = 0.04) and contralateral breast cancer incidence (0.9 vs. 3.1%; HR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.12–0.59; P = 0.001) were lower in the partial dissection of the breast + tamoxifen group than in the partial dissection of the breast alone group. There were no significant differences in the death rate across the treatment groups.
A Cochrane review was conducted on postoperative endocrine therapy for DCIS based on the results of a meta-analysis covering two trials (the NSABP B-24 trial and the UK/ANZ DCIS trial) was performed. This review concluded that overall, tamoxifen did reduce the number of future cancers or DCIS in either breast, but it did not reduce the risk of overall mortality (Table 1). It is possible that the effect of tamoxifen was ‘diluted’ by the effect of radiotherapy.

Adverse effects of tamoxifen were reported in the NSABP B-24 trial, including hot flashes (placebo group vs. tamoxifen group, 59.0 vs. 69.6%), vaginal discharge (20.0 vs. 32.4%), deep venous thrombosis (0.2 vs. 1.0%) and endometrial carcinoma (0.45/1000 vs. 1.53/1000 person-years), all of which were noted more frequently in the tamoxifen group than in the placebo group. However, there was no case of fatal pulmonary embolism or death from endometrial cancer. Studies have reported improvement of the local control with the addition of endocrine therapy after partial dissection of the breast, but its effect is larger in breast irradiation. For patients who are scheduled to receive radiotherapy, it is necessary to consider whether addition of endocrine therapy might be appropriate, based on the risk of recurrence and side effects.
Two double-blind RCTs of anastrozole versus tamoxifen for postmenopausal women with ER-positive DCIS after BCS have been reported.
In the IBIS-II DCIS trial, the median follow-up period was 7.2 years, and there was no statistically significant difference in the overall recurrence rate between the anastrozole and tamoxifen arms (67 recurrences in the anastrozole arm vs. 77 recurrences in the tamoxifen arm; HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.64–1.23). While non-inferiority of anastrozole was established (upper 95% CI, < 1.25), its superiority to tamoxifen was not established (P = 0.49). The number of women reporting any adverse events was similar between the anastrozole arm (1323 women, 91%) and tamoxifen arm (1379 women, 93%); however, the side-effect profile differed between the two arms, with a higher frequency of fractures, musculoskeletal events, hypercholesterolemia, and stroke in the anastrozole arm and a higher frequency of muscle spasms, gynecological cancers and symptoms, vasomotor symptoms, and deep vein thrombosis in the tamoxifen arm.
In the NSABP B-35 trial, the median follow-up period was 9.0 years. The primary endpoint was breast cancer-free interval (BCFI), the time from randomization to any breast cancer event including local, regional, or distant recurrence or contralateral disease, invasive or DCIS. Both the frequencies of BCFI events (90 vs.122; HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.56–0.96; P = 0·0234) and contralateral breast cancer events (39 vs. 60; HR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.43–0.96; P = 0.0322) were lower in the anastrozole group than in the tamoxifen group. However, there was no significant difference in the IBTR rate or overall survival (OS) across the treatment groups. Only when the analysis was limited to postmenopausal women under 60 years of age, the disease-free survival events were statistically significantly lower in the anastrozole group (74 vs.104; HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.51–0.93; P = 0.0151). The incidence of adverse events did not differ between the groups, except for that of thrombosis or embolism (overall incidence 2.7% in the tamoxifen group vs. 0.8% in the anastrozole group, with 17 grade 4 or worse events in the tamoxifen group vs. 4 in the anastrozole group).
Based on these studies, both tamoxifen and anastrozole could be considered as being useful therapeutic options after BCS for postmenopausal women with hormone receptor-positive DCIS. However, since treatment with these agents do not affect the life prognosis, treatment must be selected taking into consideration the patient’s age, the adverse effect profile of the drugs, the cost, the patient’s wishes, etc.

De-escalation of DCIS treatment
In a survey of autopsy cases of women who died of other diseases (40–70 years of age), DCIS was noted in 8.9% (0–14.7%) of cases. According to a review of the follow-up data of DCIS patients who had not received any treatment owing to misdiagnosis as a benign lesion in the initial biopsy (around the 1950s–1970s), 14–53% of DCIS cases showed progression to invasive cancer, in other words about 50% of DCIS cases actually do not progress to invasive cancer. Additionally, it was reported that there was no difference in the breast cancer-specific survival rate between low-grade DCIS patients undergoing or not undergoing surgery. This retrospective longitudinal cohort study was performed using the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results database. The weighted 10-year BCSS of low-grade DCIS was similar between patients that were treated and not treated by surgery (98.8 vs. 98.6%; P = 0.93).
Retrospective data suggest that some DCIS patients are not at a risk of progression to invasive cancer, but the predictors of such progression have not yet been clarified. Therefore, all patients with DCIS are treated the same way as patients with IBC.

As compared with the older studies in unresected cohorts, the incidence of invasive cancer appears to have reduced. ‘Low-grade DCIS’ and ‘low-risk subgroups’ appear to show limited progression to invasive cancer.

Based on the results of clinical trials aimed at providing treatment with a good risk/benefit balance for patients with DCIS, which carries a good prognosis, de-escalation of treatment for DCIS has now begun. The standard de-escalated treatment options proposed are presented below.
a) Omission of radiotherapy
The Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) 9804 trial is the only RCT conducted so far to verify the efficacy of radiotherapy after partial resection of the breast in patients with low-risk DCIS. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) 5194 trial is a single-arm trial. The eligibility criteria in the two trials were almost the same (NG 1, NG 2, tumor diameter <2.5 cm and negative resection margin). In the RTOG 9804 trial, after a median follow-up period of 13.9 years, the 15-year cumulative incidence of IBTR was 7.1% in the RT arm vs. 15.1% in the observation arm (HR, 0.36; 95% CI, 0.20–0.66; P = 0.0007) and that of invasive IBTR was 5.4% with RT versus 9.5% in the observation arm (HR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.21–0.91; P = 0.027). However, there was no significant difference in the incidence of distant metastasis, OS or diease-free survival (DFS) between the two arms. The ECOG 5094 trial conducted more recently, in February 2024, reported the results after a median follow-up duration of 19.2 years. Patients were enrolled in cohort 1 or cohort 2 [cohort 1: low- or intermediate-grade DCIS, size ≤2.5 cm (n = 561); cohort 2: high-grade DCIS, size ≤1 cm (n = 104)]. There were 104 (15.5%) ipsilateral breast events (IBEs), of which 54 (52%) were invasive. The IBE and invasive IBE rates increased in both cohorts up to 15 years, and then plateaued. The 20-year IBE rate was 17.8% for cohort 1 and 28.7% for cohort 2 (P = 0.005). Invasive IBE occurred in 9.8 and 15.1% (P = 0.09) of cases, respectively.
No difference in the 20-year OS rate (P = 0.60) was observed between the two cohorts. These results confirmed that while radiotherapy statistically significantly improved the local control rate in patients with low-risk DCIS, it also did not affect the survival prognosis. Therefore, the possibility of omission of radiation therapy may be suggested to reduce overtreatment of DCIS. It is important to share information (risk/benefit) from these studies and discuss treatment decisions with individual patients.
The NCCN guidelines recommend ‘omission of radiation’ after partial mastectomy for patients with low-risk DCIS, classified as ‘Category 2B’.
b) Low-dose tamoxifen
Standard-dose tamoxifen has the adverse effect of increasing the incidence of menopausal symptoms and also requires the treating physicians to take into account the risks of venous thromboembolism and endometrial cancer, which are extremely rare events.
The TAM-01 trial was a phase III RCT conducted to verify the efficacy and safety of low-dose tamoxifen (5 mg/day for 3 years) after partial resection of the breast in patients with hormone-sensitive high-risk breast lesions. A total of 500 postoperative patients with intraepithelial neoplasia were enrolled (including atypical ductal hyperplasia in 20%, lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) in 11% and DCIS in 69%). After a median follow-up period of 9.7 years, breast cancer was diagnosed in 66 cases, including 25 cases in the tamoxifen group and 41 cases in the placebo group (HR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.35–0.95; P = 0.03). There was a significant reduction in the rate of recurrence by 50% in the DCIS cohort treated with tamoxifen (HR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.28–0.91; P = 0.02). No inter-group difference in the incidence of serious adverse events was reported during the prolonged follow-up period. Patient-reported outcomes were not significantly different between the treatment arms, except for a slightly increased frequency of daily hot flashes in the tamoxifen arm (P = 0.02). However, the TAM-01 trial was a placebo-controlled trial, and it is important to note that this was not a direct comparison with standard-dose tamoxifen.
The NCCN guidelines state that low-dose tamoxifen (5 mg/day or 10 mg/every other day for 3 years) is a reasonable treatment option for patients who are symptomatic or unwilling to take the standard 20-mg dose.

Future perspectives
The basis of breast cancer treatment is local treatment by surgery and radiotherapy, and systemic treatment with drugs to eradicate any micrometastases. However, in cases of pure DCIS, theoretically with no micrometastases, the main role of adjuvant therapy is local control. Currently, not only is the optimization of postoperative adjuvant therapy accelerating, but also clinical trials to de-escalate basic surgical treatments are under way.
a) Radiotherapy versus low-dose tamoxifen
The Taiwan Breast Cancer Consortium-Japan Academic Research Organization DCIS trial (TBCC-ARO DCIS trial) is a prospective, international, open-label, randomized, non-inferiority trial (NCT04046159). The aim of this trial is to determine whether administration of low-dose tamoxifen (5 mg/day for 5 years) is non-inferior to RT (50 Gy in 25 fractions or 40.05 Gy in 15 fractions) for preventing IBTR in patients with low-risk characteristics (> 40 years old, low BRCA1/2 risk: Manchester Score < 10, mammographically detected unicentric and non-mass lesions, low- or intermediate-grade without comedo or necrosis, measuring <2.5 cm in diameter with a tumor-free margin of ≥3 mm, and ER-positive status) of DCIS. This trial was started in May 2019 and the estimated enrollment target is 810 patients. The primary endpoints are breast tumor recurrence, including ipsilateral, regional, contralateral, and distant recurrence of breast DCIS or invasive cancer. The secondary endpoints are overall survival and adverse effects of RT and tamoxifen. Patients are currently being recruited into the trial. If this study confirms the non-inferiority of low-dose tamoxifen therapy, it will be possible to provide this treatment as an important option for low-risk breast DCIS patients who do not wish to receive adjuvant RT due to side effects or other reasons.
b) Delayed SLND
SLNB reagents cannot be administered if invasive cancer is pathologically confirmed after mastectomy. Therefore, the NCCN guidelines recommend concurrent SLNB in patients with DCIS undergoing mastectomy. The SentiNot technique offers the alternative of marking the SLN in a simple manner in patients who would otherwise be considered for SLNB and remove it afterwards, if needed. Currently, the multicenter, ‘SentiNot 2.0’, randomized, controlled trial (NCT04722692) is ongoing. This trial was started in March 2020 and the estimated enrollment target is 500 patients. The aim of this trial is to investigate the use of superparamagnetic iron oxide (SPIO) nanoparticles as a tracer for delayed sentinel lymph node dissection (d-SLND) in patients in whom upfront axillary surgery (SLND) is oncologically deemed unnecessary and should be avoided. SPIO is injected during the primary operation, and operation in the axilla (d-SLND) is performed only if the final histopathology specimen shows IBC. If the primary endpoint of this study is positive, it will be possible to avoid unnecessary SLNB and thereby avoid the various complications and sequelae caused by axillary manipulation in patients with DCIS.
c) Active surveillance (AS)/omission of surgery
Four AS clinical trials for low-risk DCIS commenced enrollment between 2014 and 2017 in the UK (LORIS), Netherlands/Europe (LORD), USA (COMET) and Japan (LORETTA). The aim of these trials is to examine the effectiveness and safety of AS as compared with that of surgery-based treatment approaches for patients with low-risk DCIS. A review of the four AS trials was presented in the 2019 ASCO. An updated summary of these low-risk DCIS trials is shown in Table 3. The LORIS, COMET and LORETTA trials are in registration closed/completed status and in the follow-up period, respectively. The LORD trial is currently recruiting. If the efficacy and safety of AS for patients with low-risk DCIS is confirmed, the adverse events associated with current standard treatments and the psychological damage caused by breast deformity can be avoided in many patients. Furthermore, they do not need to be hospitalized and can avoid the hospital visits for surgery and radiation therapy, and it may be possible to minimize the impact of their disease on their work life, child care and nursing care needed. Also, adding endocrine therapy to AS can minimize the disadvantages resulting from underestimating and missing the diagnosis of mixed invasive cancer, suppress the occurrence of contralateral breast cancer, and also relieve the patients’ concerns about non-resectional treatment.

Personalized treatment for DCIS is beneficial to all patients, from the physical, psychological, social, as well as economic aspects.

Conclusion
The standard treatment for DCIS has been established through several valuable clinical trials conducted in the past, and we now take the efficacy of the established treatments for granted. On the other hand, without compromising the good prognosis obtained with the current treatment approaches, attempts need to be made to develop personalized treatments for patients with DCIS based on elucidation of the pathogenesis of this disease and new risk stratification in order to resolve clinical questions and diverse patient requests. It is also important to share with patients the latest knowledge that will be revealed one after another in the future and encourage their participation/support in the making treatment decisions.
