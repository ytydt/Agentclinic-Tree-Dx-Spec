[18F]FDG PET/CT as a biomarker for response evaluation in neoadjuvant treatment of early breast cancer: could it become a game-changer in the scenario of the emerging (de)-escalation strategies?
Over the past decades, neoadjuvant systemic treatment (NAT) has been increasingly adopted in early-stage breast cancer (BC), highlighting the need for a more accurate assessment of treatment response. Imaging tools such as [18F]2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose ([18F]FDG) positron emission tomography combined with computed tomography (PET/CT) may enhance diagnostic accuracy in this context. By comprehensively reviewing the available literature, [18F]FDG PET/CT generally shows good sensibility but lower specificity for predicting and evaluating pathological complete response (pCR), respectively, during NAT and preoperatively, in both the breast and lymph nodes. Thereby its use may support timely escalation of systemic treatment or surgery in patients with poor metabolic response. However, definitive conclusions are limited by small, heterogeneous studies with variable patient selection, timing, and response definitions. Consequently, while international guidelines remain inconsistent, further evidence is needed to define its role in response assessment, establish the optimal use in clinical practice, and clarify its integration into (de)-escalation strategies.

Introduction
Neoadjuvant systemic treatment (NAT) is increasingly used in the management of early-stage breast cancer (BC), both to facilitate less extensive and invasive surgery and to provide valuable insights into tumor responsiveness and individual outcomes. Indeed, achieving pathological complete response (pCR) is associated with favorable survival outcomes, especially for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+) and triple-negative (TNBC) BC subtypes. Thus, as pCR has emerged as a strong prognostic factor and surrogate endpoint, predicting and presurgically assessing the extent of residual disease has become crucial for guiding systemic and surgical treatment decisions. However, both the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines acknowledge the challenges in optimally evaluating response both during and after NAT. [18F]2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose ([18F]FDG) positron emission tomography combined with computed tomography (PET/CT) plays a key role in lymph node and distant metastases assessment in early and locally advanced BC. In this context, [18F]FDG PET/CT could intercept early changes in tumoral metabolic activity before any visible tumor shrinkage or size reduction. Additionally, it may correctly distinguish between active cancer persistence and fibrotic scar tissue. However, currently, neither the ESMO nor the NCCN guidelines recommend [18F]FDG PET/CT for treatment response evaluation during or after NAT. Instead, they only suggest its implementation for baseline staging in high-risk patients and in cases of suspected distant metastases, where it could provide superior diagnostic accuracy and more effective treatment planning compared with conventional imaging. Conversely, the recently published European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM) and Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (SNMMI) joint guidelines state that it may be used to assess early metabolic response in patients with early BC, particularly those with TNBC and HER2+ , undergoing NAT. Additionally, it may be employed to exclude metabolically active regional lymph nodes or distant metastases after NAT.
In this evolving and still uncertain scenario, our narrative review aims to summarize the major evidence on [18F]FDG PET/CT performance in predicting and assessing pCR in patients with early BC undergoing NAT. By delving into the literature, we aim to provide comprehensive insights into its diagnostic accuracy and limitations in this context, and subsequently clarify its potential role in both daily clinical practice and emerging trials of innovative (de)-escalation strategies.

Methods
A comprehensive literature search was conducted using two bibliographic databases (PubMed and Scopus) to identify articles published from January 01, 2010 up to September 30, 2025 focusing on the role of [18F]FDG PET/CT for response assessment to NAT in early BC. This time frame was selected to ensure a comprehensive and thorough review and moreover as it best reflects the latest advances in both technology and the management of early BC, including the increasingly routine use of nuclear imaging tools and the broader implementation of neoadjuvant therapy. Only complete articles published in English and reporting clinical data (observational studies, clinical trials) were considered. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, and full-text review was performed for all selected studies. References from included articles were also examined to identify additional eligible reports. Prospective studies with at least 50 enrolled patients were included to ensure methodological robustness and minimize biases inherent to small and retrospective analyses. We excluded those evaluating novel radiotracers or techniques (such as [18F]FDG PET/CT–breast magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] combined), as these were outside the scope of our review. The search keywords included: ((((early breast cancer) AND (neoadjuvant chemotherapy)) AND (positron emission tomography))) AND (pathological complete response)))). For each of the selected studies, the data extracted was general study information (authors, study design), patients’ features (patient number, histological and molecular features), imaging methods (imaging timing and metabolic response parameters), histopathological response classification, and [18F]FDG PET/CT performance for pCR prediction or assessment (sensibility, specificity, negative and positive predictive value, accuracy, and receiver operating characteristic [ROC] curve and area under the curve [AUC]). Semiquantitative parameters, such as the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) and its percentage change (%ΔSUVmax), are usually considered for metabolic response estimation, together with visual–qualitative assessment.

[18F]FDG PET/CT as a predictor of pCR during NAT
As current evidence indicates that metabolic changes may precede morphological ones, [18F]FDG PET/CT performed early after the first cycles of NAT could play a crucial role in guiding timely de-escalation or escalation decisions during treatment.

Table 1 summarizes the key findings of the major studies examining the role of interim-NAT [18F]FDG PET/CT for pCR prediction in patients with early BC. Across the considered evidence, early assessment during NAT with [18F]FDG PET/CT demonstrates overall a good sensibility (ranging from 60 to 85%) and high negative predictive value (NPV) in predicting subsequent pCR, particularly in relation to the primary breast lesion. In the same context, despite lower specificity, [18F]FDG PET/CT may have an higher sensibility even compared with breast MRI. This indicates that, among poor metabolic responders, the modality can correctly identify most patients who will not subsequently achieve pCR, thereby enabling clinicians to switch promptly to alternative strategies such as salvage surgery or different systemic treatments. Conversely, [18F]FDG PET/CT shows overall lower specificity and positive predictive value (PPV), which limits its ability to correctly identify patients who will actually achieve pCR after initial favorable metabolic response.

[18F]FDG PET/CT in post-NAT assessment of residual disease
After completion of NAT, accurately assessing the extent of residual disease is pivotal for guiding subsequent surgical decisions. Breast MRI, combined with baseline imaging, is considered as the most accurate method for this purpose. However, while providing only morphological information, its utility is tempered by a significant rate of false-positive findings, often overestimating the persistence of residual disease. On the other hand, significant differences in pCR rates between metabolic responders at presurgical [18F]FDG PET/CT imaging (both qualitatively or quantitatively) and nonresponders have been clearly reported in literature Notably, although SUVmax changes occur early during NAT and tend to level off toward the end of treatment, the reduction persists throughout the course of NAT. Thus, metabolic assessment after NAT completion could indeed provide valuable insights into the overall efficacy of the treatment and, consequently, more precisely forecast the pathological response.

A detailed review of the literature allowed us to identify all major studies evaluating the role of post-NAT [18F]FDG PET/CT assessment, as summarized in Table 2. Despite conflicting results and some inconsistencies, most studies indicate that presurgical [18F]FDG PET/CT, as observed for the interim-NAT evaluation, shows higher sensibility and NPV but lower specificity and PPV for evaluating pCR in both the breast and lymph nodes. The limited performance in detecting minimal residual disease in case of metabolic response may be attributed to several factors. First of all, the small size of residual lesions may fall below the scanner spatial resolution. Second, chemotherapy-induced metabolic alterations, including glucose metabolism changes and “metabolic stunning” of residual viable tumor.

Impact of methodological variabilities on study results
Although older and small retrospective studies were excluded to minimize bias, significant methodological differences and heterogeneity resulted in wide variability in [18F]FDG PET/CT performance across studies, hampering the possibility of definitive conclusions.
For instance, some studies focused on specific histological/molecular BC subtypes, while others included unselected or poorly characterized patients, leading to significant variability in evaluated populations and affecting substantially [18F]FDG PET/CT performance. Firstly, basal cancer cell metabolic activity and tracer avidity are closely associated with key biological factors including the histological type, molecular profile (estrogen receptor [ER], progesterone receptor [PR], HER2), tumor grade, and proliferation index (Ki-67). Secondly, each histological and molecular BC subtype is associated with different disease extension at baseline, type of NAT regimens, chemosensitivity, and likelihood of pCR achievement, all of which further influence imaging diagnostic accuracy. Notably, even just the order of drug sequence could affect differently SUVmax changes during NAT. On the same trajectory, for example, [18F]FDG PET/CT may be suboptimal for assessing Luminal A BC subtype, typically exhibiting low [18F]FDG uptake and achieving less frequently pCR, in sharp contrast with TNBC and HER2+ . Similarly, [18F]FDG PET/CT is less sensitive for lobular histology compared to non-special type (NST), due to lower expression of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), lower proliferation rates, and diffuse infiltrative tumor growth patterns.
Another crucial methodological issue stems from the difference in metabolic parameters to distinguish responders from nonresponders, with most studies relying on semi-quantitative measures, such as SUVmax and ΔSUVmax (rarely others as metabolic tumor volume [MTV], total lesion glycolysis [TLG]), while others employing only qualitative criteria. Moreover, variations in [18F]FDG PET/CT technology and nuclear medicine expertise across different centers may result in under- or overestimation of parameters, further compromising the reproducibility and consistency of results. Notably, the timing of interim-NAT assessment varied as well, with most performing [18F]FDG PET/CT after the first or second cycle. Lastly, only a few studies addressed lymph node status and response, and different systems were used to assess histopathologic response, some including ypTis (residual carcinoma in situ) in the definition of pCR, while others restrict to ypT0 (no residual tumor).

Discussion and future perspectives
Overall, despite study heterogeneity across studies and controversial results, the main prospective available evidence indicates that [18F]FDG PET/CT, when used as a standalone tool, shows limited specificity and PPV value both during and after NAT. Thus, in case of metabolic response, surgical specimen is still required for pCR confirmation in both the breast and lymph nodes. On the other hand, emerging evidence supports the safety, non-inferior survival, and improved quality of life of “surgery-sparing” strategy in selected patients with clinical complete breast and/or axilla response after NAT. However, while ongoing trials aim to validate their feasibility, minimally invasive confirmatory procedures, such as sentinel lymph node biopsy and image-guided vacuum-assisted breast biopsy (VABB), remain necessary to verify pCR. Future analyses should determine whether combining different clinical techniques, including imaging modalities and others such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), can provide sufficient specificity to eliminate the need for even minimally invasive procedures.
On the other hand, [18F]FDG PET/CT displays valuable sensibility and NVP in both predicting pCR at interim evaluation and assessing residual disease in presurgical setting. Therefore, it may play a crucial role in preventing inappropriate surgical de-escalation in patients with metabolically active disease, particularly in the axillary nodes. Conversely, it may serve as a valuable tool for triggering timely escalation strategies in cases of poor response, as strongly supported by the recent positive results of the phase II randomized “PHERGain” trial. Specifically, the latter randomized patients with HER2+ early BC to receive neoadjuvant standard of care or a “chemo-free” regimen with only anti-HER2 agents (trastuzumab and pertuzumab). Metabolic responders, defined as those with a ΔSUVmax reduction (> 40%) at [18F]FDG PET/CT after two cycles, continued the same treatment, whereas nonresponders were escalated adding chemotherapy. Despite correctly identifying among metabolic responders only 37% (86/227) of patients who actually achieved pCR, [18F]FDG PET/CT displayed a considerable sensitivity in early detecting who did not subsequently achieve pCR (74%, 43/58) among nonresponders. Remarkably, thanks to this [18F]FDG PET/CT-based “response-adapted” design, patients in the experimental arm achieved an excellent three-year invasive disease-free survival while safely avoiding unnecessary chemo-related toxicity. Given these encouraging results, additional trials with a similar design are currently underway across different BC subtypes, including the “NeoADAPT” trial in TNBC. Moreover as observed metabolic assessment may support improved risk stratification and more tailored management, irrespective of early prediction or final achievement of pCR. Indeed, although further evidence is needed, metabolic response on both interim and posttreatment [18F]FDG PET/CT scans may stand as an independent prognostic marker, predicting both disease-free and overall survival.
Looking ahead, artificial intelligence models trained on baseline [18F]FDG PET/CT radiomic features show promise in predicting pCR and, together with novel advanced digital systems or technique such as the combined [18F]FDG PET/CT-breast MRI, may further enhance accuracy. Moreover, beyond [18F]FDG, novel target-specific radiopharmaceuticals are currently under investigation and could potentially be employed. For instance, [18F]-fluoroestradiol (FES), targeting ER, may provide more precise insights into hormone receptor-positive BC, while HER2-targeted radiotracers could enhance the assessment of HER2+ subtype. Similarly, radiolabeled fibroblast activation protein inhibitors (FAPI) show promise in visualizing tumor stroma by targeting cancer-associated fibroblasts, potentially improving imaging in luminal-like and lobular BC, although the role of NAT in these subgroups remains limited and debated.
Due to the marked heterogeneity and resulting inconsistencies across studies, no definitive recommendations can yet be made regarding the optimal timing, parameters, or cutoffs for [18F]FDG PET/CT to be applied in clinical practice or future trials. Thus, future studies are warranted in this context, ideally focusing on more homogeneous populations within specific histological or molecular subtypes and accounting for evolving NAT drugs and regimens. By tailoring evaluation criteria to the study aim, whether prioritizing sensitivity for escalation or specificity for de-escalation, such investigations could ultimately clarify these undefined aspects and establish [18F]FDG PET/CT role and recommended use. These advances hold the potential to refine the management of early BC, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes and quality of life.
