A Novel Approach to Neuropathic Pain Treatment: Lactylation Targeting Microglia
Neuropathic pain (NP) is a high-prevalence syndrome of chronic pains around the world. Currently, the efficacy of existing medications is suboptimal, and their use is associated with potential adverse effects, posing a major challenge for current clinical practice. Recent research sufficiently suggests that central regulations of pathogenesis and progression of NP are caused and regulated by abnormal activation of microglia. The new paradigm of this field is the use of the emerging mechanism of the epigenetic modification, namely the lactylation. The paper under review focuses on microglial lactylation, which systematically elucidates the molecular processes underlying lactylation and its critical role in NP pathogenesis. It has been shown that in the conditions of NP-induced abnormal lactate accumulation in microglia stimulates pro-inflammatory gene expression by lactylation of histone H4 lysine 12 (H4K12) and creates a vicious cycle of metabolism-epigenetics-neuroinflammation. This review further explores the possibilities of therapeutic value of intervening in the lactylation pathway. Targeted intervention strategies for this pathway hold promise in breaking the pathological vicious cycle of NP, advancing treatment paradigms from mere symptom control toward disease-modifying therapies. This will provide an essential theoretical basis and translational study pathways to create highly effective and precisely targeted new treatment medicine for NP.

Introduction
Neuropathic pain (NP) arises from damage or disease affecting the somatic sensory nervous system. As a chronic form of pain, it affects approximately 6.9%–10% of the general population, constituting a significant public health challenge. Clinically, patients often present with spontaneous burning pain, electric shock-like pain, or hyperalgesia. These symptoms not only severely impair their quality of life but also co-occur frequently with anxiety and depression, imposing a significant economic burden on society. Currently, first-line pharmacological treatment for NP primarily relies on anticonvulsants (such as gabapentin and pregabalin) and antidepressants (such as duloxetine). However, the efficacy of these medications is suboptimal, and they are often accompanied by significant side effects including dizziness, drowsiness, and dry mouth, leading to poor patient compliance. Additionally, opioid medications are restricted from long-term use due to their addictive potential and risk of tolerance. This treatment landscape, characterized by limited efficacy and pronounced side effects, underscores the need for further exploration into the mechanisms of NP. It also highlights the urgent clinical demand for innovative therapies based on novel mechanisms and targeting new pathways.
In recent years, research perspectives have undergone a revolutionary shift—expanding from a focus on neurons themselves to encompass the entire “neuro-immune” microenvironment. Among these, the innate immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS)—microglia—have emerged as the most prominent central players in NP research. Following nerve injury, microglia are rapidly activated and release multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β). These mediators directly sensitize neurons and disrupt inhibitory synaptic transmission, thereby driving and sustaining the abnormal amplification of pain signals. Targeting the abnormal activation of microglia and intervening in their pathogenic interactions with neurons has emerged as one of the most promising new strategies to break the treatment impasse in NP. NP is not merely an electrophysiological disorder; altered cellular metabolism and epigenetic states—particularly in microglia—play a significant role. In particular, the recently discovered lactylation converts glycolytic product lactate into a histone modification precursor, driving pro-inflammatory gene expression. Research indicates that lactate accumulation in microglia within the nucleus pulposus may “lock in” their pro-inflammatory phenotype through lactylation at the H4K12 site, forming a vicious cycle of “metabolism-inflammation.” This discovery provides a novel therapeutic target for NP treatment. Against this backdrop, this review aims to systematically explore the immense potential of a novel strategy—lactylation targeting microglia—in NP treatment. We will delve into the molecular basis of lactylation, elucidate its pivotal role in activating microglia during NP, and envision novel opportunities for overcoming current therapeutic challenges through pharmacological interventions targeting this pathway.

The Biological Basis of Lactylation

The Paradigm Shift of Lactate
The traditional concept of glycolysis was that lactate is the final metabolite and its build up is commonly linked to hypoxic tissue damage and metabolic stress. Recent research has however indicated that lactate plays an active and central role in the process of inflammation. Even in hyperoxic conditions, under various chronic inflammatory conditions including neuroinflammation, metabolic reprogramming of activated immune cells (including macrophages and microglia) takes place, focusing aerobic glycolysis (the so-called Warburg effect) to generate high levels of lactate. The process is not merely a passive by-product of energy metabolism; rather, it is an important bioenergetic aspect of inflammatory cell activation. In the neuroinflammatory microenvironment, lactate has become a versatile inflammatory signaling hub. It may be used as an energy substrate relayed between cells, supporting the bioenergetic needs of continuously activated microglia. It is also a precursor directly involved in protein modification. More significantly, it can have an impact on cellular fate determination and performance under normal physiological conditions, through regulating the activity of paramount proteins, achieving a shift from a “metabolic endpoint” to a “regulatory starting point”.

The Development Process of Lactylation
Among these, the discovery of lactylation provides the most compelling mechanistic explanation for lactate’s function as a signaling molecule. Lactylation, as a novel reversible post-translational modification (PTM) of proteins, is characterized by the covalent attachment of a lactate moiety to lysine residues on histones or non-histones using intracellularly produced lactate as a precursor. This process alters the structure, function, or stability of the target protein. Compared to traditional acylation modifications, lactylation links cellular metabolic states—particularly glycolysis levels—to transcriptional regulation, providing a crucial molecular bridge for understanding metabolic-epigenetic dialogues.
Zhang et al. initially identified and reported this modification in a systematized fashion in the year 2019. It is a significant innovation in the area of epigenetic regulation, and such innovative methods as acetylation and methylation were the classical ones, and nowadays a new chapter in functional studies of lactate is entailed by the use of epigenetic regulation. This finding quickly drew curiosity in the sphere. Between 2020 and 2021, the extensive existence of lactylation in systems like macrophages and tumor cells was successively confirmed by several research groups, which also provided an initial demonstration of its relationship to inflammatory responses and metabolic reprogramming. The development of detection technologies has experienced a stage of immersion into research on understanding mechanisms underlying detection since 2022. Spatial site maps have been increasingly improved so that they can be explicitly localized to key functional sites such as histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) and histone H4 lysine 12 lactylation (H4K12la); methods, such as Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), have been used to specify the distribution patterns of the various lactylations throughout the genome which are increasingly enriched in promoter regions of inflammatory genes. It has turned the current research in the area of systematic analysis of pathophysiological functions. Different studies have established the important regulatory role of lactylation in neuroinflammation, tumor immunity, and metabolic diseases and have initiated research into their potential as disease biomarkers.

Dynamic Regulation of Lactylation
This modification directly modifies the charge, conformation and interaction with DNA or other proteins by covalently linking lactate to lysine residues on histones (e.g., H3K18, H4K12) and non-histones, thus changing the target protein itself. This is achieved dynamically through a regulatory system composed of three classes of functional proteins: “writers”, “erasers”, and “readers”. There are two categories of writers. The former are metabolic enzymes, including pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), that control lactate production. Alterations in PKM2 activity and conformation have a direct effect on glycolytic flux and lactate yield, which gives the substrate base of lactylation. The other group consists of transferases that directly catalyze the transfer of lactyl groups. It has been confirmed that the histone acetyltransferases p300 and CBP also have a strong lactate transferase activity and are the main writers of histone lactylation. The removal of lactylation is primarily mediated by erasers, which maintain it in a dynamic equilibrium. Presently, the following histone deacetylases have been known to have de-lactylation activity: histone deacetylases 1–3 (HDAC1-3) and sirtuins (SIRT) family of type III deacetylases (SIRT1 and SIRT2). These enzymes specifically hydrolyze the lactyl group from lysine residues. Readers are proteins that are able to specifically recognize and bind lactylation tags, to cause downstream biological effects. The notion of lactylation "readers" is still in its early stages of exploration. According to available literature, this role may be performed by certain proteins containing a YEATS domain, which recognize lactylation marks and subsequently recruit transcription complexes or chromatin remodeling factors. This also enhances signal output, allowing tight spatiotemporal control of lactylation dynamics involved in epigenetic regulation and cell fate determination.

Key Molecular Features of Lactylation
Lactylation exhibits several key molecular characteristics that collectively establish its unique identity as a central hub in the “metabolic-epigenetic” network. First, it is characterized by its most distinctive feature, which is metabolic substrate dependency, i.e. the use of lactate, which is a core product of glycolysis, as the direct precursor. This enables changes in its level to serve as a real-time sensor of intracellular glucose utilization and glycolytic flux. Lactylation is more sensitive and specific to changes in the local metabolic microenvironment compared to other modification mechanisms that rely on universal cofactors (e.g., acetyl-CoA to effect acetylation). Secondly, this modification has site specificity and functional specificity. The core sites (e.g. histone H3K18 and H4K12) are not randomly located; specifically, they are clustered in the regulatory regions of particular functional genes. They regulate individual genes with high accuracy by recruiting particular reader proteins, but do not cause a global response of chromatin structural reorganization. Further, lactylation is not a separate process but contributes to an elaborate epithelial modification code, together with classical modifications such as acetylation and methylation at nearby sites. Through competitive or synergistic cross-talk, these changes collectively regulate the functional output of specific genomic loci, thereby integrating into the global cellular epigenetic control system. Lastly, the alteration also has remarkable specificity in cellular and pathological states, being quite low in resting cells, and very high in metabolically active immune cells and tumor cells. This state dependency and inducibility not only makes it a candidate biomarker to report functional states of cells and disease progression, but also, in effect, explains why lactylation is a driving force in disease progression, such as in neuroinflammation.

Lactylation plays a primary regulatory role in immune cell polarization, tumor microenvironment remodeling, and neuroinflammation maintenance by converting glycolytic metabolic states into epigenetic codes (Table 1). Its ability to rapidly adapt to metabolic stresses through dynamic modification—translating short-term signals into functional outputs—underpins its capacity to remodel cellular structures. Thus, lactylation is not only a dynamic regulatory switch for cellular adaptation to metabolic stress but also a critical driver in the pathogenesis of different diseases, such as neuroinflammation, infection, and tumor. This hub integrates core epigenetic processes, linking cellular metabolic states to functional outcomes by specifically coupling inflammatory gene expression programs with metabolic reprogramming. This has not only provided a trinity of coupled factors—metabolic flux, epigenetic regulation, and functional output at the molecular level—but has also offered a new theoretical framework for uncovering the chronicity mechanisms of chronic diseases such as NP.

The Specificity of Lactylation Regulation in NP
In NP, as resident immune cells of the central nervous system, microglia exhibit unique characteristics in the regulation of lactylation that are distinct from those in other inflammatory contexts (e.g., tumor-associated macrophages, peripheral tissue macrophages). First, the microglia microenvironment is specific to the CNS. The CNS spatiotemporally controls lactate level through the astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle (ANLS), compared with the persistent high levels of lactate in the peripheral tumor microenvironment. Second, the functional output of microglia is pathologically specific. During NP, microglia activation is not aimed at tumor expansion or pathogen elimination, but instead leads to central sensitization, the release of inflammatory factors, and synaptic remodeling. Third, the activation state of microglia is time-specific. As pain becomes chronic, microglia transition from a resting state to an activated state and eventually to a sensitized state. It is hypothesized that lactylation may act as a molecular switch in this dynamic process. Thus, elucidating the regulatory pattern of lactylation in microglia in the framework of NP would be a vital step toward understanding the key mechanisms of pain.

Metabolic Reprogramming and Lactate Accumulation in Microglia in NP

The Warburg Effect in Microglia
Under the pathological conditions of NP, spinal dorsal horn microglia undergo a complete switch in their energy metabolism program, exhibiting a pronounced Warburg effect. This means they shift to using aerobic glycolysis, rather than oxidative phosphorylation, as their primary pathway for ATP production, despite the presence of ample oxygen. This metabolic reprogramming is not a passive change in energy supply but an active regulatory process closely linked to the functioning of microglia. It has been found that its molecular activation pathway involves damage-associated molecular patterns, such as ATP and chemokines, released upon neural injury. It stimulates downstream PI3K/Akt/mTORC1 signaling cascades by activating receptors such as P2X4R, TLR4, and so forth, leading to systemic expression of glucose transporter as well as to a reorganization of glycolytic enzyme activity, which transforms metabolic phenotyping.
Using metabolomics analysis and 13C-glucose isotope tracing, this has been demonstrated in multiple animal models of NP, including chronic sciatic nerve compression injury and spinal nerve ligation. At the same time, the activity and expression of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) is increased leading to a drastic rise in the rate and concentration of lactate production in the cell. This metabolic change has serious functional consequences: on the one hand, active glycolysis on the spot provides a rapid ATP production and biosynthetic precursors that meet energy and mass needs in the process of cell activation; on the other hand, the lactate produced acts not only as a simple metabolic product, but also as a strong signaling molecule and precursor for epigenetic changes, playing a direct role in the formation and maintenance of later phenotypes of inflammation. In this way, the Warburg effect is more than an adaptation to metabolism demands; it is the cornerstone of metabolic support provided to microglia in order for them to achieve immunoregulatory and neuromodulatory functions.

The Formation of the Lactate Microenvironment and Its Effects on Neurons
Activated microglia generate excess lactate, which is released into the extracellular environment through monocarboxylate transporters and forms a complex microenvironment rich in lactate at the neuron-glia interface of interaction. There are two pathophysiological effects of this microenvironment. First, localized accumulation of lactate decreases the pH of the extracellular environment. This acidic environment immediately inhibits acid-sensitive ion channels (including the ASIC family) on nociceptive neurons, thereby increasing neuronal excitability and inducing and maintaining a hyperalgesic condition. Second, and more importantly, lactate, as a multipurpose signaling molecule and cross-metabolic substrate, can be absorbed by adjacent neurons or astrocytes via monocarboxylate transporters, thereby participating in their intracellular metabolic and signaling regulatory systems.

In physiological homeostasis, neurons have the full capability of metabolizing lactate. These cells are able to take up extracellular lactate via monocarboxylate transporter 2 (MCT2), convert it into pyruvate through lactate dehydrogenase, and then efficiently utilize oxidative phosphorylation via the tricarboxylic acid cycle. This mechanism allows lactate to serve as a viable alternative energy substrate to glucose for sustaining neural activity. The physiological importance of microglial metabolic coupling can be seen in this process. However, under the persistent pathological state of NP, abnormal activation of microglia leads to sustained excessive accumulation of lactate locally. Excessive exposure to these high-lactate microenvironments of neuron activation initiates a cascade of metabolic dysregulations. Sustained high levels of lactate loading can exceed the normal metabolic rate of neurons, leading to reduced efficiency of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and electron transport chain (ETC) function, as well as increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby disrupting intracellular redox homeostasis (Fig. 1). This mitochondrial dysfunction not only reduces the capacity of neurons to supply energy, but also causes additional damages to other vital physiological processes—such as synaptic plasticity, neurotransmitter release, and signal integration—through mitochondrion-mediated apoptosis pathway activation and inflammasome activation, among others. As a result, it increases the pathophysiology of abnormal sensitization and chronicity of pain pathways at metabolic and functional levels. Consequently, the microenvironment around lactate created by activated microglia is a dynamic signaling hub. By acidifying the extracellular milieu and activating acid-sensing ion channels on neuronal membranes, lactate directly enhances the excitability of nociceptive neurons, thereby participating in the induction and maintenance of hyperalgesia at the rapid signaling level.

Molecular Landscape of Lactylation in NP Microglia

Spatiotemporal Specificity
Lactylation of dorsal horn microglia is found to have specific spatiotemporal dynamics in NP development. Through research in animal models over time, it has been found that the increase and decrease in the level of lactylation is also closely related to the stage of microglial pathological activation. As an example, after chronic constriction injury (CCI) of the sciatic nerve, there is a significant increase in the level of H4K12la in the dorsal horn microglia which peaks at a rate between days 7 and 14. This is highly consistent with the peak period of microglial proliferation after injury. This suggests that lactylation may function as a form of “epigenetic memory,” participating in the conversion of transient neural injury signals into persistent cellular inflammatory phenotypes. Furthermore, lactylation exhibits cell-type and regional specificity in its spatial distribution. Single-cell epigenomic studies also reveal that under pain conditions, activated microglia display high levels of lactylation alongside concurrent upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes, identifying a distinct pain-related subpopulation within the microglial pool. The modified signals were identified to be expressed with a single distribution across the CNS, not uniformly distributed but rather highly concentrated in the superficial layers (laminae I–II) of the dorsal horn of the spinal cord where peripheral nociceptive information enters. This region serves as the core anatomical site where primary afferent nerve fibers form synaptic connections with secondary neurons in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. It is also a crucial interface and functional hotspot for neuroimmune cellular and molecular interactions. This very pattern of change that occurs under definite time intervals and in certain areas of the anatomy points strongly to the fact that lactylation is a particular, removable phenomenon in NP microglia functional reprogramming, and not a metabolic waste product. The spatiotemporal course of it gives invaluable hints concerning the epigenetic nature of pain chronicity and creates a possibility of a unique therapeutic window within which to interfere in that process.

Genomic Localization Characteristics
The lactylation of microglia will not only be spatio-temporally specific in the pathological development of NP but also its localization of functional capabilities to target genomic areas may further demonstrate epigenetic processes controlling neuroinflammatory gene response circuits. H4K12la is one of the most extensively studied modifications, and its localization is not random or ubiquitous. Recent results of research suggest that under pathological conditions, H4K12la presents a unique pattern of enrichment of the promoter. This site specificity has been preliminarily shown in microglia: for example, after spinal cord injury, the level of H4K12la specifically increases in microglia and is highly enriched at the promoters of key genes such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), as shown by ChIP-seq analysis, thereby activating their transcription. More critically, a study revealed by motif enrichment analysis that regions enriched for lactylation highly overlap with binding motifs of the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) transcription factor family. Taking into account that STAT3 is a major transcription factor involved in neuroinflammation, and the fact that lactylation is tightly linked to inflammatory genes expression means that in NP microglia, lactylation can establish an effective transcription activation complex via genomic co-localization with STAT3, therefore, accurately regulating the expression of pro-inflammatory genes. The specific pattern of genomic localization serves as the functional basis for lactylation to precisely and efficiently regulate pain-related neuroinflammatory gene networks. To date, there is no established genome-wide spatiotemporal distribution map of lactylation in NP. The dynamic distribution of this modification across different developmental stages of NP, different spinal cord regions, and different microglial subpopulations remains to be thoroughly elucidated. In particular, microglia themselves are highly heterogeneous; cells in distinct activation states may exhibit drastically different lactylation modification profiles, which in turn shape their response patterns to pain signals.

Functional Validation Study
The invention and application of gene editing technologies provide potent methodologies for directly testing the biological roles of lactate at both the molecular and cellular levels. This allows us to go beyond observational correlations and establish causal connections between lactate alterations and pain phenotypes. At the whole-animal level, researchers have begun to explore the functions of lactylation using cell-type-specific gene-editing experiments. Most existing studies observe pain-relieving effects by inhibiting or knocking down LDHA, which conversely indicates the critical role of lactate production in pain maintenance. Such loss-of-function experiments demonstrate that reducing lactate production can alleviate pain behaviors, providing preliminary functional evidence for the pathogenic role of lactylation in NP. To further establish its direct regulatory function with precise targeting and exclusion of confounding factors, future studies may draw on the CRISPRi/a platform established by Dräger et al. in induced pluripotent stem cell-derived microglia, but upgrade this technology to site-specific epigenome editing tools (e.g., fusing dCas9 with the catalytic domain of p300, or employing nanobodies targeting H4K12la, etc.). This would enable the precise introduction of H4K12la modification at specific gene loci. In summary, multi-perspective evidence from loss-of-function experiments using LDHA inhibition/knockdown and the prospect of site-specific editing technologies has preliminarily established an evidence chain pointing to the function of lactylation, ranging from whole-animal behaviors to single-gene locus functions. Such observations indicate that lactylation may play an important role in pain-related microglial activation. This provides critical clues for elevating lactylation from a concomitant pathology-related phenomenon to one of the potential core driving mechanisms underlying the initiation and progression of Neuropathic pain, and lays a theoretical foundation for the subsequent development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Core Mechanism of Lactylation in Driving Neuroinflammation

Histone Lactylation and the Activation of the Pain-Promoting Gene Network
Histone lactylation as an emergent regulatory process of epigenetics is a key transition point between metabolic reprogramming and gene deregulation during the onset and progression of Neuroinflammation-related chronic pain. More importantly, histone lactylation is not a random process but it is highly specific to important regulatory elements like promoters and enhancers of pro-pain-related genes. This coordinately stimulates a multi-layered pro-pain gene expression program. This network also works through the temporal and spatial organization of the amplification and persistence of neuroinflammation and pain signaling. The main elements of such a network are: first, a dramatic increase in histone lactylation levels at the promoter regions directly induces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine genes. This rearranges the local chromatin accessibility condition, attracting RNA polymerase II and universal transcription factors, and robustly modifies the levels of expression of these genes. The resulting surge in products (e.g., IL-6, IL-1β) not only amplifies local neuroinflammatory signaling but also further recruits peripheral immune cells to infiltrate neural tissues. This produces a vicious cycle that continues to impair the neural functioning and reduce the pain threshold.
Second, the expression of genes involved in pain signal transduction is also finely regulated by lactylation. Following peripheral nerve injury, lactate accumulates within dorsal root ganglion neurons (DRG). This causes H3K9la/H3K18la alterations catalyzed by p300 that augment the chromatin accessibility involved in the promoter areas of the pain-identifying ion-channel genes (such as TRPV1 and P2X7), and further enhances their expression. In vivo silencing of LDHA or p300 reverses this phenomenon and suppresses thermal hyperalgesia, which implies that lactate levels and channel gene expression have a positive relationship. The increased expression of these molecules on primary sensory neurons and dorsal-horn neurons of the spinal cord directly enhances neuronal sensitivity to noxious stimuli, a phenomenon known as peripheral sensitization. At the same time, it enhances the transmission of pain signals and changes in synaptic plasticity at the spinal cord level, which converts into activation and sustenance of central sensitization.
Also, this network can provide an amplification loop of epigenetic cascade through the regulation of the expression of some epigenetic modulator genes. Although activation of the pro-pain genes is initiated, lactylation can also change the worldwide chromatin condition by varying the quantity or efficacy of other epigenetic modifiers. The combined impacts of this result in the long-term consolidation of the epigenetic state of pain as the overall gene network is transcriptionally activated. To summarize, the histone lactylation changes a multidimensional pro-pain gene network that includes inflammatory mediators, signaling molecules, cellular phenotype and epigenetic regulators. Under specific activation of this network by targeting it accurately and in a systematic way, it converts transient alterations in metabolism to stable and strong transcriptional responses and eventually has lasting and extensive effects on pathological pain conditions in the scenario of neuroinflammation.

Potential Role of Non-Histone Lactylation
A major metabolic-epigenetic regulatory process, lactylation has expanded its regulatory network from histones to a wide range of functional non-histones to create a more multidimensional system of signal amplification of inflammation. However, in the field of NP, direct evidence for non-histone lactylation in microglia remains elusive. Our current understanding of this mechanism is largely extrapolated from advances in cancer biology; nevertheless, these findings provide important mechanistic insights and entry points for exploring microglia-mediated neuroinflammation. The evidence in the available cancer models has offered a valuable point of analogy on the mechanism of the lactylation in the microglia. Lactylation drives pathological progression by regulating the activity of key proteins in the tumor microenvironment. For example, in lung cancer, lactylation of vacuolar protein sorting 34 (Vps34) at residues K356 and K781 serves as a key regulator in the expression of autophagy-related proteins such as Beclin1 and autophagy-related protein 14 (Atg14). Autophagy is precisely the core mechanism by which microglia clear abnormal protein aggregates and modulate inflammasome activity. This would indicate that Vps34 lactylation could also serve as a major switch involved in autophagy regulation in microglia, further affecting the change in phenotype to pro-inflammatory. Moreover, metabolic reprogramming (i.e., the Warburg effect) is not exclusive to cancer cells but also represents a hallmark feature of M1 pro-inflammatory microglia. In cervical cancer, lactylation of the discoidin, cub and lccl domain containing 1 (DCBLD1) at residue K172 enhances glycolytic flux by targeting glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD). In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), HK1-associated lactylation (H4K8la) is also involved in metabolic reprogramming. Given that glycolysis is the main energy source of the activated microglia, these results highly indicate that similar lactylation alterations of glycolytic enzymes or regulatory proteins can also be found in microglia. These changes can also strengthen the glycolysis in a positive feedback loop, which gives the energy sustenance needed in persisted neuroinflammation. In the meantime, lactylation as a part of transcriptional regulation also should be mentioned. A pancreatic cancer study has indicated that cell proliferation and survival were positively impacted when transcription factor eb (TFEB) was lactylated at residue K91 which controls the ww-domain-containing e3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2 (WWP2). TFEB is one of the transcription factors that regulate lysosomal biogenesis and scavengerism of autophagy. TFEB lactylation under metabolic stress in reaction to neuroinflammation might be a determinant of whether microglia attain homeostasis or pathological activation. It is noteworthy that this metabolic change also controls the components of the core inflammatory responses. As an example, in the conditions of ischemic/reperfusion injury, the nod-like receptor family pyrin-domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome-mediated lactylation of the site of K245 upregulates LDHA in human lung bronchial epithelial cells and macrophages, which are associated with metabolic adaptation to lung inflammatory activation. These results demonstrate that non-histone lactylation is a diverse and critical regulator in pathophysiology in various diseases and cells.

Lactylation as a Signal Integration Hub
The lactylation is an important signaling integration hub in the pathological process of neuroinflammation. It is more than simple regulation of expression of genes, effectively providing a fundamental regulatory base that integrates upstream metabolic reprogramming and immune-inflammatory signaling with downstream epigenetic remodeling and cellular reprogramming of functions. This catalytic role is initially mediated through the direct conversion of dynamic changing metabolic information into robust epigenetic tags. The ability of lactate, a product of glycolysis, to regulate fluctuate its concentration levels among cells represents the most direct response mechanism linking metabolic sensing to genetic reprogramming through covalent modification of substrates such as histones. This enables cells to rapidly adapt to the metabolic demands of the inflammatory microenvironment.
Its integrative role begins with mTORC1 signaling axis activation by upstream metabolic signaling. In response to stress conditions such as nerve injury, metabolic reprogramming is induced by PKM2 upregulation, which is mediated by the PI3K/Akt pathway, ultimately leading to a surge in lactate synthesis. It not only induces pro-inflammatory genes but also further alters the glycolytic phenotype by regulating metabolic sensors such as hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), thereby establishing a self-reinforcing metabolism-epigenetics-metabolism feedback loop. Of greater significance, the regulation of lactate oxidation can integrate multi-source information, such as oxidative stress (through NAD⁺/NADH balance) and cytokine signaling (through STAT3 stimulation), and convert them into coordinated transcriptional outcomes in a unified manner. In NP, it transforms the detrimental microenvironmental signals, including metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, into epigenetic messages that maintain the microglia activation thus having an essential central role in the progressive worsening stage of the disease. Interfering with this dynamic equilibrium, particularly by targeting lactate transferase or de-lactating enzymes, may offer a novel therapeutic approach to disrupt the vicious cycle of neuroinflammation. This multi-level integration system not only reveals the massively coupled regulatory networks among metabolic, signaling, epigenetic and genetic levels in the pathogenesis of neuroinflammation, but also suggests new therapeutic strategies that can promote disrupting this integrative center to break the vicious circle of neuroinflammation. It provides the essential conceptual foundation for creating effective combination therapies that modulate disease.

The Vicious Cycle of Chronic Pain

Establishing a Self-Sustaining Inflammatory Epigenetic Memory
A self-perpetuating cycle of pro-inflammatory gene expression driven by lactylation. Lactylation maintains a persistently accessible chromatin state, enabling pro-inflammatory genes to retain basal transcription even after the initial inflammatory stimulus has subsided. The sustained production of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α further exacerbates nerve injury and reactivates intracellular signaling pathways, particularly NF-κB. Activation of NF-κB in turn stimulates lactate production, promoting the deposition of more lactylation and forming a self-amplifying positive feedback loop. We have elaborated above how lactylation drives pro-inflammatory gene expression in microglia. However, its broader pathological significance lies in the fact that this modification can maintain an open chromatin conformation even when the initial inflammatory signal diminishes, thereby sustaining constitutive basal transcription of these pain-promoting genes. These epigenetic marks cause an open chromatin structure to persist even following the withdrawal of the original inflammatory signal and keep these pro-pain genes at a basal level of transcription. More importantly, these continuously secreted inflammatory mediators could in turn stimulate intracellular signaling pathways, such as the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), to induce lactylation and glycolysis-derived lactate production (Fig. 2). This creates a positive feedback process that is self-sustaining regardless of the injury that caused it. This, in turn, leads to the inability of the inflammatory response to naturally resolve, confines cells to a pro-inflammatory phenotype, and drives the transition of acute pain into chronic pain.

Interactions with Neurons: From Neuroimmune Dialogue to Central Sensitization
Microglia “enhanced” by lactylation release more pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1β and IL-6) and neuroactive molecules (e.g. glutamate). These inflammatory mediators act directly on adjacent neurons: IL-1β potentiates the activity of neuronal N-Methyl-D-Aspartate (NMDA)-receptors and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) suppresses the expression of the inhibitor transporter potassium-chloride cotransporter 2 (KCC2), interfering with neuron chloride ion homeostasis. The result will be a pathological elevation of neuronal excitability and change in synaptic plasticity, which is referred to as central sensitization. The cells of a central-sensitization state are highly responsive to normal stimuli and produce pain impulses. More importantly, these stimulated neurons release signaling molecules such as ATP, which in turn activate adjacent microglia, thereby creating a neuron-glia positive-feedback loop that culminates in a sustained chronic pain neuropathic network.

Therapeutic Insights: Novel Targets to Break the Cycle
Since the role of lactylation in the vicious cycle of NP is central, new avenues in the clinical management of NP are highly promising through intervention strategies that modulate lactate. Targeted lactylation can be designed and implemented across multiple levels, including “writing”, “erasing”, and “metabolic regulation,” encompassing key strategies such as inhibitor development, enzyme activity regulation, and metabolic intervention. First, in inhibitor development, the focus is on directly inhibiting the activity of the “writing enzyme” responsible for lactylation. It has been shown that the histone acetyltransferase activity of p300/CBP not only performs classical acetylation functions, but also utilizes lactyl-CoA as a substrate to catalyze lactylation, thereby facilitating the abnormal transcriptional activation of pain-related genes. Hence, the establishment and utilization of small-molecule inhibitors with the selective action to inhibit or hinder the p300/CBP-mediated lactylation activity has become a straightforward and operative intervention method. This method silences pro-pain gene sets, consequently preventing pain behavior to a great extent.
Second, another essential pathway involves the regulation of eraser enzyme activity, which controls lactylation. Studies have shown that SIRT2, a class III histone deacetylase, possesses not only deacetylation activity but also the ability to remove lactylation. The intervention of NP through the activation of SIRT2 to clear pathological lactylation is a novel approach. The viability of this hypothesis is preliminarily justified: in depression models, S-ketamine can relieve neuroinflammation by targeting SIRT2; ononin has also been shown to suppress microglial inflammation regulating SIRT2 activity. Moreover, SIRT2 has been demonstrated to suppress pain behavior in NP models by inhibiting ferroptosis and modulating the Nrf2-mediated oxidative stress response. Nonetheless, direct confirmation that pharmacological stimulation of SIRT2 reduces NP through pathological lactylation removal is yet to be satisfied, and therefore, it is a scenario worth scientific interest. Moreover, metabolic intervention is an indirect but extremely important regulatory dimension, which is expected to regulate the availability of the lactate substrate at its origin. These strategies are: preventing the activity of important lactate-producing enzymes, e.g. LDHA, to decrease the amount of lactate molecules available to be modified; or affecting the intracellular lactate concentration indirectly by altering metabolic pathways, such as glycolytic flux.
However, the above intervention strategies still face multiple challenges on the path toward clinical translation. First, current regulatory tools (such as the above-mentioned inhibitors) lack sufficient selectivity and specificity to distinguish lactylation and other acylation modifications. Besides, the effect of microglia heterogeneity on lactylation patterns is still unclear; and, finally, species differences between animal models and human diseases raise the need in favor of improved translational research efforts. Whether intervention strategies validated to be effective in animal models can achieve comparable therapeutic efficacy in human patients remains to be verified through systematic and rigorous preclinical safety and efficacy evaluations. Moreover, since there is interaction among epigenetic modification networks, joint regulation with other modifications which demonstrate crosstalk with lactylation (including methylation and acetylation) can induce synergistic intervention effects and promote the overall efficacy and sustainability of treatment. The aim of all the above intervention strategies is to destabilize the self-perpetuate cycle of the metabolic reprogramming-epigenetic remodeling-neuroinflammation-pain signal amplification. They not only promise to diminish the symptoms of pain but also allow a shift from simple analgesia to disease-modifying therapy. It paves the way to an essential research line of creating new chronic pain treatment methods with targeted action and inextinguishable effects.

Conclusions and Future Perspectives
The discovery of lactylation has opened up a novel dimension for understanding the chronicization mechanisms of NP. This review systematically elaborates how nerve injury drives metabolic reprogramming characterized by the Warburg effect in spinal microglia, leading to local lactate accumulation and the formation of a unique lactate microenvironment. This microenvironment is not only directly able to influence neuronal excitability through acidification but what is more important is that lactate acts as a substrate for epigenetic modifications. It converts transient metabolic signals into persistent inflammatory epigenetic memory by driving histone lactylation in microglia. Microglia “enhanced” by lactylation release abundant pro-inflammatory mediators, forming a malignant positive feedback loop with neurons, which ultimately results in central sensitization and pain chronicization. Therefore, lactylation serves as a molecular bridge linking metabolic disorders, neuroimmune activation, and neuronal dysfunction, which offers a unified theoretical framework for dissecting the complex pathological network of NP.
Targeting this “metabolic-epigenetic hub” is emerging as a highly promising therapeutic strategy. Various methods of intervention are currently under active development, including controlling the metabolism of lactate and the modulation of “writing” and “erasing” lactylation processes. Although this field is still in its infancy and faces numerous challenges in translating basic discoveries into clinical applications, lactylation undoubtedly provides a rich pool of targets and novel research strategies for developing next-generation pharmacotherapies for NP. In the future, with advances in spatial single-cell multi-omics technologies and highly selective intervention tools, we can reasonably anticipate breakthroughs in solving the intractable clinical problem of pain chronicization.
