Targeting cell death: a promising approach for colorectal cancer therapy
Colorectal cancer is one of the most common digestive malignancies worldwide. Cell death plays a crucial role in maintaining normal biological functions and homeostasis, with dysregulation of cell death leading to tumorigenesis. In addition to apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis, it is PANoptosis, cuproptosis and disulfidptosis that are involved in the development of CRC. Radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy are the main treatments for advanced CRC. These therapies induce CRC cell death through various pathways, including increased intracellular ROS, induced DNA damage, inhibiting the specific signaling pathways and activating the immune system. Cell death can regulate CRC cells’ sensitivity to treatment through complex mechanisms, suggesting that targeting cell death could not only inhibit rapid CRC cell proliferation but also overcome treatment resistance. This review summarizes the current understanding of various CRC mechanisms and the primary mechanisms by which existing CRC treatments induce cell death. Additionally, we highlight recent research advancements in targeting cell death for CRC treatment and describe numerous challenges encountered in clinical practice. Aiming to provide effective strategies and new perspectives for CRC treatment and to lay a solid theoretical foundation for the development of drugs targeting CRC cell death.

Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common diagnosed digestive cancers worldwide. In America, colon cancer has the fourth highest incidence behind breast, lung and prostate cancers, and is the second leading cause of death after lung cancer. Currently, it is estimated that there are more than 1.5 million patients with CRC in America, and 152,810 new cases will be expected in 2024. It is worth noted that the median age at diagnosis for CRC has rapidly shifted younger since the early 2000s because of incidence rising in adults younger than 50 years while declining in older age groups. In China, CRC is one of the top five diagnosed cancers and causes of cancer-related deaths. Aging, unhealthy dietary habits, obesity, smoking and the disturbance of intestinal microorganisms and their metabolites are high-risk factors for CRC. Due to the improvement including both earlier diagnosis through screening and advances in surgical techniques and novel systemic therapies, the overall 5-year relative survival rate for CRC patients is greatly improved. Current treatment strategies for CRC predominantly consist of endoscopic therapy, surgery, radiotherapy, local therapy, and systemic therapy using chemical drugs. Endoscopic therapy and surgery are used for CRC patients in early stage, while a series of chemotherapeutic and targeted drugs are used for advanced CRC patients to suppress the progression of CRC. Importantly, the emergence of immunosuppressants such as pembrolizumab et al. have provided a novel treatment strategy for advanced CRC, especially patients with deficient mismatch repair protein (dMMR) mCRC. Owing to the high metastatic potential and extensive infiltration of CRC, above treatment strategies showed unsatisfactory effects in the clinic, and the therapy of CRC remains a great challenge.
According to its morphology, biochemistry, and function, cell death can be divided into accidental cell death and regulated cell death (RCD). Accidental cell death is a biologically uncontrolled process of cell death in response to accidental injury stimuli. Regulated cell death (RCD) plays an indispensable role in various pathological and physiological processes in organisms, including cellular homeostasis, tissue remodeling, and tumor development. However, RCD is characterized by controlled signaling pathways that play key roles in organismal development or tissue renewal. The identification of CED9 (also known as BCL2 in mammalian cells) and CED4 (also known as apoptotic peptidase-activating factor 1[APAF1] in mammalian cells) from the studies of Caenorhabditis elegans development in the 1990s marks the beginning of an era of molecular apoptosis research that triggered the rapid expansion of RCD research. In decades, in addition to apoptosis, it is uncovered that necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, cuproptosis and disulfidptosis have a closely relationship with the development and progression of CRC. Oncogenic mutations can disrupt RCD, allowing CRC to acquire the ability of anti-cancer and sustain unlimited proliferation. In fact, majority CRC treatment drugs effectively induce cell death through multiple mechanisms, thereby inhibiting their progression. However, CRC cells also can adapt to drug-induced cell death through multiple mechanisms, ultimately leading to cell survival. Understanding the mechanism of cell death and discovering new therapeutic targets to induce cell death or combination therapy will provide perspective strategies for CRC therapy. While the mechanisms of cell death are still unknow, and further research is needed to elucidate its molecular mechanisms and develop targeted therapies. This review not only comprehensively described the mechanisms of cell death, but also summarized the mechanisms by which current CRC therapy via regulating RCD. In addition, the latest progress in targeting cell death to inhibit CRC progression is summarized, aiming to provide a solid theoretical basis and potential strategies for improving the treatment of advanced CRC.

The mechanism of cell death in CRC
Since the 1990s, ongoing research has unveiled a series of RCD processes. RCD plays a crucial role in maintaining organismal homeostasis and facilitating cell self-renewal. However, tumor cells often evade cell death through complex mechanisms. Understanding the mechanisms underlying RCD not only offers potential strategies for targeted drug development but also provides new insights into treating tumors. In the following section, we comprehensively reviewed the mechanisms of various types of RCD.

Apoptosis
The concept of “cellular apoptosis” was first proposed by John Kerr et al.. in 1972, while in 1992, Horvitz and Hengartner et al.. identified the first apoptosis related gene ced-9 of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans acts to protect cells from programmed cell death. In the following decades, research on apoptosis surged and made significant breakthroughs, successively unraveling the functions of various apoptosis-related genes. Recently, the apoptotic mainly includes intrinsic apoptosis and extrinsic apoptosis pathways. The extrinsic apoptosis pathways are mediated by the activation of death receptors on the plasma membrane, while intrinsic apoptosis pathways are initiated without the involvement of death receptors (Fig. 1).

Intrinsic apoptosis
Intrinsic apoptosis can be activated following cellular alterations such as DNA damage, withdrawal of growth factors and mitochondrial damage. Mitochondria serve as the regulatory center of intrinsic cell apoptosis, which can be classified into caspase-dependent and non-caspase-dependent apoptotic signaling pathways based on the mode of signal transduction. Alterations in mitochondrial outer membrane permeability (MOMP) leading to the release of cytochrome C are the initiating event of caspase-dependent intrinsic apoptosis. Subsequently, the activation of caspase-9 mediated by APAF1 is facilitated. Activated caspase-9 sequentially cleaves downstream caspases, including caspase-3 and caspase-7, to mediate the occurrence of the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. Moreover, activated caspase-3 and caspase-7 can also activate upstream caspases in a feedback loop, thereby amplifying the caspase cascade. It is noteworthy that the activation of caspase 3 is negatively regulated by IAP proteins such as XIAP, IAP1, and IAP2. Some proteins released from mitochondria along with cytochrome C (such as diablo IAP-binding mitochondrial protein [diablo, also known as Smac] and HtrA serine peptidase 2 [HTRA2]) can block XIAP to promote the release and activation of caspases, inducing the occurrence of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. The non-caspase-dependent cell apoptotic signaling pathway primarily involves the release of AIF and EnDOG, both of which can induce DNA fragmentation and cell apoptosis.
The BCL2 protein family (including pro-apoptotic proteins such as BCL2 associated X (BAX), BCL2 antagonist/killer 1 (BAK1), and anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL2 and BCL2 like 1 (BCL2L1)) are crucial regulatory molecules upstream of intrinsic apoptosis, strictly controlling cell apoptosis by regulating MOMP. The BH domains in the BCL-2 family mediate the interaction between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic family members, thereby inhibiting or activating cell apoptosis. It is worth mentioning that the pro-apoptotic BH3-only BCL-2 proteins utilize their BH3 domains to both actively inhibit anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins, such as BCL-2, BCL-xL, MCL-1 and BCL-W, and directly activate pro-apoptotic BAX and BAK to drive the formation of oligomeric pores in the mitochondrial outer membrane, resulting in the release of cytochrome C. The balance between anti-apoptotic proteins and pro-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins is regarded as a vital regulator controlling cell apoptosis.

Extrinsic apoptosis
The extrinsic apoptotic pathway is initiated by death receptors located on the cytoplasmic membrane, including pro-apoptotic receptors Fas, tumor necrosis factor receptors (TNFR1, TNFR2), and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) receptors (DR4, DR5).
Among them, the interaction of Fas-L and Fas recruit adaptor FADD to initiate cell apoptosis. FADD binding with Caspase-8 can result in Caspase-8 dimerization and activation. Activated Caspase-8 then activates Caspase-3/9, inducing cell apoptosis. Activated caspase 8 not only initiate extrinsic cell apoptosis but also induce the release of cytochrome C from mitochondria by cleaving the bcl-2 inhibitory proteins BID, thereby activating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. It is noteworthy that decoy receptors compete with death receptors for binding death ligands, thereby reducing the activation of pro-apoptotic death receptors. For instance, decoy receptor DcR3 for FasL is overexpressed in primary lung, colon, and breast tumors.
Accumulating evidence indicates that CRC cells commonly exhibit defects in apoptotic signal transduction accompanied by aberrant activation of anti-apoptotic pathways. Overexpression of the anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family member Bcl-XL has been recognized as an important predictor of poor prognosis in patients with colorectal adenocarcinoma. In addition, elevated levels of several apoptosis-regulatory factors with anti-apoptotic functions—such as Caspase-8, c-FLIP, XIAP, and cIAP2—are closely associated with disease progression and reduced overall survival in colon cancer patients.Mechanistic studies further demonstrate that suppression of apoptotic programs in CRC not only promotes tumor cell survival and facilitates tumor progression, but also confers resistance to cytotoxic anticancer therapies. In vivo evidence supports this notion: Fas-deficient mice display a markedly increased incidence of colon tumorigenesis following exposure to pro-apoptotic stimuli, underscoring the critical role of death-receptor pathway inactivation in colorectal carcinogenesis. Collectively, colorectal cancer is characterized by a typical apoptosis-evasion phenotype driven by reinforcement of anti-apoptotic signaling networks.

Necroptosis
In 2005, Alexei Degterev et al. identifies a previously undescribed basic cell-death pathway “necroptosis”, also known as programmed necrosis (Fig. 1). In contrast to apoptosis, the regulatory process of necroptosis operates independently of Caspase activity. It is can be triggered by multiple stimuli, including the activation of death receptors, toll-like receptors, nucleic acid sensors, retinoic acid receptor responder 3, transmem-brane protein 173, and adhesion receptors. Among them, TNFα is the most important upstream signaling molecule of necroptosis, transmitting signals by binding to tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1). Subsequently, a series of proteins are recruited to form complex I, which consists of receptor-interacting serine-threonine kinase 1 (RIPK1), TNFRSF1A-associated death domain (TRADD), TNF receptor-associated factor 2/5 (TRAF2/5), and cellular inhibitor of apoptosis protein 1 (cIAP1) and cIAP2. Within this complex, RIPK1 can be ubiquitinated by cIAP1/2 to promote cell survival, thereby initiating a cascade reaction activating NF-κB and promoting cell survival. Importantly, the deubiquitinating enzyme cylindromatosis (CYLD) can remove the ubiquitin chains of RIPK1, promoting the dissociation of TRADD and RIPK1 from complex I. Subsequently, FAS-associated death domain protein (FADD) and caspase-8 are recruited to TRADD and RIPK1. Importantly, caspase-8 plays a crucial role in necroptosis. Activation of Caspase-8 inhibits the phosphorylation of RIPK1 and RIPK3, deactivating the necrosome complex and inducing apoptosis. However, under conditions of cellular stress, the activity of caspase-8 is inhibited, leading to the phosphorylation of RIPK1 and subsequent activation of RIPK3, resulting in the recruitment and subsequent phosphorylation of MLKL, translocation of MLKL to the plasma membrane, and formation of pores disrupting membrane integrity. This leads to the release of cellular contents and the appearance of an inflammatory phenotype, as well as the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as IL-1α, IL-β, and IL-33, triggering an immune response, ultimately resulting in cell death.
Necroptosis, a programmed form of necrotic cell death, exhibits a similarly complex bidirectional regulatory role in CRC. Evidence indicates that depletion of NR4A1 markedly increases the susceptibility of CRC cells to TSZ-induced necroptotic cell death, suggesting that NR4A1 may serve as a potential therapeutic target in CRC. Furthermore, RIPK3-mediated inflammatory signaling has been shown to facilitate colorectal tumor initiation and progression, implying that RIPK3-associated pathways not only participate in the execution of necroptosis but also modulate tumor development through remodeling of the inflammatory microenvironment.

Pyroptosis
As early as 1992, Zychlinsky et al. observed the phenomenon of pyroptosis through experiments, but the concept of pyroptosis was not clearly defined until 2001 by Cookson et al. (Fig. 1). Through in-depth studies on pyroptosis, it has been discovered that its main characteristic is the formation of pores on the cell membrane, dependent on cysteine proteases (caspases) (caspase-1/4/5/11), leading to cell swelling and rupture, and the release of multiple of cytoplasmic contents and various pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β and IL-18) into the extracellular space, resulting in lytic cell death. Of note, an increasing number of studies revealing pyroptosis plays an important role in CRC, indicating the close association between pyroptosis and the occurrence and progression. Therefore, uncovering the molecular mechanisms of pyroptosis is of great significance for the clinical treatment and diagnosis of CRC. Pyroptosis can be divided into classical and non-classical pathways. Of note, under certain circumstances cell apoptosis can switch to pyroptosis. For example, chemotherapy drugs induce could induce the switch of apoptosis to pyroptosis through caspase-3 cleavage of Gasdermin E (GSDME). Similarly, PD-L1-mediated Gasdermin C (GSDMC) expression switches TNFα-induced apoptosis into pyroptosis.

Classical pyroptosis pathways
Inflammatory bodies mediate the classical pathway of cell pyroptosis, with main types including nod-like receptors (NLRs) and absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), whose basic structure consists of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and the precursor of caspase-1 (pro-caspase-1, CASP1). Among them, PRRs recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) on the surface of pathogens and interact with them to trigger innate immune responses. Inflammatory bodies can sense various stimuli and induce pyroptosis. NLRP1, NLRP3, NLRC4, and AIM2 recruit CASP1 and activate it into Caspase-1. Activated Caspase-1 can cleave Gasdermin D (GSDMD) protein to generate N-terminal and C-terminal domains. The N-terminal domain, also known as the “pore-forming domain,” forms GSDM pores in the cell membrane; the C-terminal domain, also known as the “inhibitory domain,” can inhibit the formation of pores by the N-terminal domain. Furthermore, precursors of IL-1β and IL-18 are cleaved by Caspase-1 into mature forms, which are released extracellularly during pore formation or membrane rupture mediated by GSDM, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response.

Non-classical pyroptosis pathways
In the non-classical pyroptosis pathway, after stimulation by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), Caspase-4, Caspase-5, and Caspase-11 can directly bind to LPS and initiate the pyroptosis process. On one hand, activated Caspase-4/5/11 can cleave GSDMD protein, where the N-terminus of GSDMD not only mediates cell membrane dissolution and pyroptosis but also activates the NLRP3 inflammasome to activate Caspase-1, ultimately leading to the production and extracellular release of IL-1B; on the other hand, activated Caspase-4/5/11 activates the Pannexin-1 channel, releasing ATP to open the P2 × 7 membrane channel, inducing cell pyroptosis by forming pores in the cell membrane. Additionally, activated Pannexin-1 further activates NLRP3 inflammasome through releasing the K+, ultimately leading to the production and extracellular release of IL-1B.
Members of the Gasdermin family, which are central executors of pyroptosis, display differential regulatory patterns in CRC. Studies have shown that reduced expression of GSDMD is significantly associated with poor prognosis in CRC patients, whereas its activation exerts tumor-suppressive effects on colorectal tumorigenesis. Further analyses suggest that GSDME may function as a positive regulator of tumor invasion and metastasis by modulating cell migration and angiogenesis. In contrast, GSDMA, GSDMB, and GSDMD appear to negatively regulate migratory capacity during tumor cell dissemination. Collectively, these findings indicate that the pyroptotic pathway not only mediates inflammatory cell death but also shapes CRC biological behavior by influencing the tumor microenvironment and metastatic potential. Key molecules such as GSDMD therefore represent promising prognostic biomarkers and potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

PANoptosis
PANoptosis, was named by Malireddi in 2019 as a novel modality of cell death that integrates features of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis(Fig. 2). This inflammatory PCD pathway is triggered by specific activators leading to the formation of the PANoptosome complex, encapsulating the key characteristics of pyroptosis, apoptosis and necroptosis, yet it cannot be fully represented by any single one of these cell death mechanisms alone. These PCD pathways exhibit extensive interconnections and can occur simultaneously. Upon activation of PANoptosis, inhibiting just one of these pathways proves ineffective in preventing cell demise or disease induced by cytokine storms, primarily due to the role of the PANoptosome. Therefore, it is imperative to simultaneously target al.l three PCD pathways to adequately block PANoptosis when necessary. As an inflammatory PCD, panoptosis acts as a double-edged sword in host defense against pathogenic infections. On one hand, it triggers substantial secretion and release of cytokines and inflammatory factors, potentially leading to cytokine storms and subsequently causing inflammatory diseases. Three upstream molecules of PANoptosis have been characterized to date including ZBP1, RIPK1 and AIM2. These molecules detect specific stimuli and trigger the assembly of PANoptosomes, forming ZBP1-PANoptosomes, AIM2-PANoptosomes and RIPK1-PANoptosomes. Additionally, the activation of PANoptosomes is inhibited by TAK1, PSTPIP2, SHARPIN, HOIP, HOIL-1 and A20.
Cellular Regulatory Pathways of PANoptosis. The regulatory pathways of PANoptosis mediated by different PANoptosomes, including ZBP1-PANoptosomes, RIPK1-PANoptosomes, AIM2-PANoptosomes and NLRP12- PANoptosomes

ZBP1-PANoptosomes
Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1) is an innate immune receptor identified as a sensor for influenza A virus (IAV) and a key regulator of cell death during IAV infection. Upon IAV stimulation, ZBP1 is activated as a specific sensor, triggering the assembly of PANoptosome. Once ZBP1-PANoptosis is formed, it can initiate PANoptosis, a form of cell death comprising pyroptosis, apoptosis and necroptosis components, ultimately leading to lytic inflammatory cell death characterized by the activation of Caspase-1, Caspase-3, Caspase-8 and the phosphorylation of MLKL.

RIPK1-PANoptosomes
The RIPK1- PANoptosome is primarily composed of RIPK1, ASC, Caspase-1, Caspase-8, and FADD. TAK1 serves as the master switch regulating the RIPK1-PANoptosome. The deletion or functional inactivation of the TAK1 gene can trigger the assembly of the RIPK1-PANoptosome. Subsequent research found that the absence of TAK1 can also induce the activation of the RIPK1-independent PANoptosis pathway, which is primarily mediated by RIPK3-MLKL.

AIM2-PANoptosomes
The components of AIM2-PANoptosomes include AIM2, ZBP1, pyrin, ASC, Caspase-1, Caspase-8, RIPK1, RIPK3 and FADD. AIM2 regulates the innate immune sensors pyrin and ZBP1 to drive inflammatory signaling and inflammatory cell pyroptosis. It plays a significant role in human normal development, infectious diseases, inflammation, and tumors.

NLRP12-PANoptosomes
The NLRP12-PANoptosome primarily consists of NLRP12, Caspase-8, ASC, and RIPK3. It drives inflammatory cell death via Caspase-8/RIPK3. NLRP12 mediates inflammation and PANoptosome activation in response to heme plus PAMPs or TNF, leading to cell death and inflammatory responses.
As an integrated cell death paradigm that converges apoptotic, necroptotic, and pyroptotic signaling, PANoptosis has recently gained increasing attention in CRC. Evidence suggests that four PANoptosis-related genes-TIMP1, CDKN2A, CAMK2B, and TLR3-serve as independent prognostic indicators for patients with CRC. Another study identified five key PANoptosis-related genes (PRGs), including BCL10, CDKN2A, DAPK1, PYGM, and TIMP1, which participate in CRC initiation and progression through modulation of the immune microenvironment, immune cell infiltration, chemosensitivity, and tumor advancement. Moreover, differential expression profiling of PANoptosis-associated genes revealed significant downregulation of UNC5, while UNC5D expression showed a positive correlation with overall survival in CRC patients. Elevated NFS1 expression has been linked to poorer survival outcomes and reduced chemotherapy responsiveness; conversely, NFS1 deficiency promotes PANoptosis by increasing intracellular ROS levels and synergizing with oxaliplatin treatment. Mechanistically, ADAR1 suppresses PANoptosis activation by interacting with ZBP1 and preventing formation of the ZBP1–RIPK3 complex. Collectively, these findings highlight the critical biological significance of PANoptosis in CRC with respect to prognostic stratification, immune infiltration characteristics, and therapeutic responsiveness.

Ferroptosis
In 2012, Brent Stockwell et al. first proposed that the oncogenic RAS-selective lethal small molecule Erastin activates a novel cell death pathway, termed “ferroptosis” (Fig. 3). Over the past decade, ferroptosis has rapidly advanced in cancers. Iron metabolism, glutathione synthesis, and lipid metabolism collectively regulate the initiation and execution of ferroptosis. Since the proposal of the GPX4-centered mechanism of ferroptosis in 2014, increasing studies have aimed to explore new regulatory mechanisms. Pathways independent of GPX4 have also been identified. Sebastian Doll et al. proposed AIFM2 as an independent inhibitor of ferroptosis, distinct from GPX4, synergistically inhibiting lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis with glutathione. These studies provide a robust theoretical framework for understanding ferroptosis regulation. Based on current research, ferroptosis regulation is categorized into three main pathways: lipid metabolism, iron metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. Autophagy-dependent pathways of ferroptosis have been reported in recent years, but their specific mechanisms require further elucidation.
Cellular Regulatory Pathways of Ferroptosis, Cuproptosis, and Disulfidptosis (A) Ferroptosis Regulatory Pathways: The accumulation of intracellular lipid peroxides and the imbalance of redox systems, including GPX4 and SLC7A11, lead to cell membrane damage and cell death, triggering ferroptosis. (B) Cuproptosis Regulatory Pathways: Copper ions entering the cell bind to sulfurylated tricarboxylic acid cycle proteins, leading to instability of Fe-S cluster proteins and promoting toxic stress within mitochondria, ultimately resulting in cuproptosis. (C) Disulfidptosis Regulatory Pathways: Under conditions of high SLC7A11 expression and glucose starvation, there is excessive consumption of NADPH, leading to the abnormal accumulation of disulfides such as cystine, inducing disulfide stress and rapid cell death
A growing body of evidence demonstrates that ferroptosis plays a pivotal tumor-suppressive role in limiting CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Multiple ferroptosis-associated genes—including AURKA, LPCAT3, TP53, CDKN2A, GPX4, PRNP, SLC7A11, and TFRC—have been shown to correlate significantly with overall survival in CRC patients, highlighting their potential value as prognostic biomarkers. Functional investigations further revealed that targeting SLC7A11 suppresses colorectal cancer stem cell progression through ferroptosis induction. In parallel, inhibition of the splicing factor SRSF9 enhances CRC cell susceptibility to Erastin-induced ferroptosis by downregulating GPX4 expression. From a therapeutic perspective, vitamin C in combination with cetuximab has been reported to trigger ferroptosis by disrupting intracellular iron homeostasis and promoting ROS accumulation, thereby providing a mechanistic rationale for combinational treatment strategies. Collectively, ferroptotic sensitivity in CRC appears to depend on coordinated reprogramming of antioxidant defenses, iron metabolism, and lipid peroxidation networks.

Cuproptosis
Serving as a cofactor for mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, Cu participates to provide necessary energy for development (Fig. 3). Copper ion carriers represent a class of lipophilic molecules with the capacity to bind copper ions reversibly, facilitating their transport across cellular or mitochondrial membranes. Extensive research has been conducted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying cell death induced by various copper ion carriers, such as disulfiram (DSF) and elesclomol (ES). Notably, the cell death pathway induced by Cu ionophores was not prevented by treatment with inhibitors of other already known cell death pathways, such as pan-caspase inhibitors (antiapoptotic compounds), ferrostatin-1 (an antiferroptotic compound), necrostatin-1 (an antinecroptotic compound), or N-acetyl cysteine (a suppressor of oxidative stress), suggesting that thecuproptosis mechanism differs from that of previously identified cell death pathways.
In a seminal contribution in 2022, Tsvetkov et al. introduced the term “cuproptosis” to describe the novel cell death triggered by copper ions and intricately linked to mitochondrial respiration. Studies underscore a markedly heightened sensitivity of mitochondria-dependent cells to ES, surpassing that of glycolytic cells by nearly 1000-fold. Remarkably, interventions targeting the electron transport chain complexes or mitochondrial succinate uptake alleviate cell demise, whereas mitochondrial uncoupling agents exhibit negligible impact. Moreover, temporal profiling reveals a selective disruption in TCA cycle-related metabolites following ES treatment. Through comprehensive whole-genome CRISPR/Cas9 functional knockout screens, Tsvetkov et al. identified seven crucial cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs). These CRGs are conceptually organized into three functional clusters: FDX1, genes associated with the lipoic acid (LA) pathway (LIAS and LIPT1), and those encoding components of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (DLAT, DLD, PDHA1, and PDHB), collectively implicated in LA metabolism. Mechanistically, FDX1 is posited as an upstream regulator of the LA pathway, wherein LIAS, under FDX1 modulation, orchestrates sulfur moiety conjugation to DLAT, critically modulating mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) function. Cu(I) engagement with the sulfur moiety of sulfurized DLAT via disulfide bonding precipitates DLAT oligomerization, precipitating iron-sulfur cluster loss-induced protein toxicity stress and eventual cellular demise.
In recent years, multiple studies have revealed that copper ions not only involving in critical components of various metalloproteins, including matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), vascular adhesion protein-1, and lysyl oxidase (LOX), but also acting as a cofactor within pro-angiogenic molecules, such as fibroblast growth factor (FGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and angiopoietins. Furthermore, copper is involved in the regulation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). Copper-induced cell death has emerged as a novel form of regulated cell death capable of killing cancer cells. Consequently, drugs targeting copper ions exert their cytotoxic effects by disrupting intracellular copper homeostasis, offering promising prospects in cancer therapy.
Cuproptosis-related genes also exhibit characteristic dysregulation in CRC. Expression levels of FDX1, SDHB, DLAT, and DLST are reduced in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal counterparts and show positive correlations with patient survival outcomes. Additional studies have confirmed that cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs), such as CDKN2A and DLD, are significantly associated with overall survival, with their expression levels closely linked to immune cell infiltration patterns. Moreover, CRGs display genomic alterations and differential expression profiles in CRC. These findings suggest that cuproptosis may contribute to CRC progression through modulation of mitochondrial metabolism and the immune microenvironment, supporting its utility in prognostic modeling. Targeting cuproptosis is therefore considered a promising strategy to overcome therapeutic resistance.

Disulfidptosis
In 2020, X. Liu et al. discovered that under glucose starvation conditions, cells overexpressing SLC7A11 deplete intracellular NADPH, leading to the abnormal accumulation of disulfides such as cysteine, triggering disulfide stress and rapid cell death (Fig. 3). In 2023, Gan et al. elucidated the mechanism by which disulfide stress induces cell death and coined this novel form of cell death “Disulfidptosis”. Under normal conditions, NADPH provides reducing equivalents to counteract disulfide stress and maintain cellular homeostasis. However, under specific conditions, excessive consumption of NADPH leads to the accumulation of disulfide-bonded molecules. This triggers the formation of disulfide bonds between cytoskeletal proteins, particularly actin filaments (F-actin), resulting in the collapse of the cytoskeletal network and ultimately culminating in cell death caused by disulfide accumulation.
Recent investigations have further identified systemic alterations in disulfidptosis-associated genes in CRC. Clinical sample analyses revealed downregulation of NDUFS1, NDUFS2, NDUFC1, and EPAS1, alongside upregulation of SLC7A11, OXSM, LRPPRC, NDUFA11, NUBPL, and COXNT1. Filamin A (FLNA) has been characterized as a key mediator of disulfidptosis in CRC, participating in tumor cell migration and invasion through regulation of cytoskeletal remodeling. Collectively, emerging evidence indicates that disulfidptosis holds regulatory significance in CRC with respect to prognostic evaluation, immune infiltration status, and drug responsiveness.

Current CRC treatment strategies and the mechanisms underlying their induction of RCD
Current treatment strategies for CRC predominantly consist of endoscopic therapy, surgery, radiotherapy, local therapy, and systemic therapy using chemical drugs. Over the past half-century, an increasing number of carcinogenic pathways have been systematically revealed, providing a solid theoretical basis for comprehensive understanding the pathogenesis of tumors. The mutation of signaling pathways and related transduction genes in CRC has always been a research hotspot, and through the development of novel targeted therapeutic drugs for precise tumor treatment, the five-year survival rate of CRC has been significantly improved. Importantly, the development of immune checkpoint inhibitors has provided effective treatment strategies for CRC, especially MSH-I. Despite continual adjustments, the therapeutic strategies of CRC remain challenges such as tumor recurrence, drug resistance, and drug toxicity. Currently, the mechanisms by which clinical therapeutic drugs induce CRC cell death mainly include disrupting the redox system balance, inducing DNA damage, inhibiting the activation of specific signaling pathways, and activating the immune system (Fig. 4). Understanding the mechanisms through which different drugs cause CRC cell death can provide potential strategies to further improve the therapeutic efficiency. In this section, we comprehensively review the main clinical agents of CRC and further elaborate the mechanism of RCD induced by them.

Oxidative stress
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are highly reactive oxygen-containing molecules with short lifespans, including superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals. The increase in ROS has been detected in various cancers and has been shown to play multiple roles. For example, ROS can activate pro-tumor signaling, enhance cell survival and proliferation, and drive DNA damage and genetic instability. These highly reactive molecules attack various biomolecules within cells, including DNA, proteins, and lipids, triggering cell death processes such as apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis through complex molecular signaling pathways involving mitochondrial apoptosis and endoplasmic reticulum stress. Specifically, ROS can directly react with DNA, causing base oxidation, deoxyribose breaks, and DNA strand breaks. This oxidative damage can lead to base mismatches, DNA strand breaks, and mutations, hindering normal DNA replication and transcription. Additionally, sustained oxidative stress depletes cellular antioxidant resources (such as glutathione), weakening the cell’s DNA repair capacity, making DNA damage more difficult to repair, and leading to the accumulation of damage that triggers cell death.
Of note, tumor cells adapt to oxidative stress and maintain homeostasis by increasing NADPH production through various pathways, including AMPK activation, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and reductive glutamine and folate metabolism, which helps sustain carcinogenic signaling and resistance to cell death. Treatments such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy can increase the level of ROS in CRC cells, disrupting the redox system’s homeostasis and consequently triggering multiple cell death. Additionally, previous studies indicated the development of chemotherapy resistance in CRC cells may be associated with the reduction of ROS. In the future, enhancing the fragility of redox system to improve the ROS levels within CRC cells may be a potential strategy to promote cell death.

DNA damage and repair
Radiotherapy and chemotherapy induce DNA damage in CRC cells, thereby activating the cellular DNA damage response (DDR), which includes key proteins such as ATM (Ataxia Telangiectasia Mutated), ATR (ATM and Rad3-related), p53, and other apoptosis-related proteins. These proteins initiate downstream signaling pathways that promote DNA repair, cell cycle arrest, and, when damage is irreparable, activate the mitochondrial and death receptor pathways, ultimately leading to apoptosis. However, in tumor cells, DNA repair mechanisms are often dysregulated, leading to genomic instability and increased mutation rates, contributing to cancer development and progression. The DNA repair system plays a crucial role in maintaining genomic stability and preventing the accumulation of mutations, primarily including homologous recombination repair (HRR), non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), base excision repair (BER), and mismatch repair (MMR). Single-strand breaks (SSBs) are repaired through direct and indirect BER, while double-strand breaks (DSBs) are repaired through HR and NHEJ. MMR primarily addresses errors arising during DNA replication, prevent the accumulation of mutations. DSBs are the most severe form of DNA damage and often lead to cell death. Notably, the high mutation burden and neoantigen load resulting from MMR deficiency make tumor cells more easily recognizable and attackable by the immune system. This renders MMR-deficient tumors more sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. With drugs inhibiting the DDR pathways, CRC cells will be difficult to repair the damaged DNA, thus leading to cell death.
Radiotherapy exerts its effects by emitting high-energy radiation that directly impacts DNA molecules, causing DSBs and SSBs, triggering DDR. Chemotherapeutic agents induce DNA damage and activate DDR, leading to CRC cell death. Platinum-based drugs are a cornerstone in the treatment of advanced CRC, functioning primarily by crosslinking DNA strands and initiating a cascade of DNA damage responses. Fluoropyrimidine drugs, such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), primarily inhibit thymidylate synthase (TS) activity. For instance, 5-FU is metabolized to 5-fluoro-2’-deoxyuridine monophosphate (FdUMP), which forms a stable complex with TS, inhibiting its activity and subsequently blocking dTMP synthesis. This inhibition results in impaired DNA synthesis and triggers DNA damage, inducing apoptosis in CRC. Irinotecan is primarily metabolized to SN-38, which binds to topoisomerase I, causing extensive double-strand DNA breaks and severe DNA damage, ultimately leading to cell death. Additionally, radiotherapy, oxaliplatin, 5-FU, and irinotecan can indirectly promote DNA damage by increasing intracellular ROS in tumor cells, further inducing CRC cell death. It is noteworthy that cancer cells sometimes utilize residual repair pathways to resist treatment, developing resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Therefore, targeting DNA repair pathways, particularly by inhibiting key repair proteins like PARP, can enhance therapeutic efficacy and induce CRC cell death. This strategy has shown promising results in the treatment of various cancers, indicating broad clinical application potential.

Targeted inhibition of specific signaling pathways
Various signaling pathways mediate the proliferation, survival, and migration of CRC, such as Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, Hedgehog, and TGF-β/SMAD, as well as pathways that activate signal cascades like PI3K/AKT or RAS/RAF. Targeted therapy, as an emerging alternative therapy, inhibits tumor cell signal transduction by acting on specific binding sites within these pathways, effectively suppressing tumor cell proliferation and CRC cells survival. Currently, in the treatment of CRC, targeted therapies primarily focus on inhibiting the EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) signaling pathway. Although the Wnt/β-catenin pathway plays a significant role in the development of CRC, there are currently no approved drugs targeting this pathway. This may be due to the diversity and complexity of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. The following section will primarily introduce existing CRC targeted therapies that decrease cell survival by specifically inhibiting certain signaling pathways.

MAPK signaling pathways
The MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase) signaling pathway is a crucial downstream pathway of EGFR and is often aberrantly activated in CRC. The primary reasons for this include EGFR gene amplification or mutations, as well as mutations in downstream genes such as Ras and Raf, leading to sustained activation of EGFR signaling. EGFR is a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase that, upon binding with its ligand (e.g., EGF), dimerizes and undergoes autophosphorylation, thereby activating the downstream MAPK signaling pathway.
In CRC, approximately 25% to 82% of patients exhibit EGFR gene overexpression. This overexpression promotes cancer cell proliferation, survival, and migration by activating downstream PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways. Specifically, EGFR-mediated ERK phosphorylation activates a series of transcription factors, increasing Cyclin D1 expression, which drives cell cycle progression from the G1 phase to the S phase, thus promoting cell proliferation. ERK signaling can also upregulate anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 and inhibit pro-apoptotic proteins, enhancing cell survival. Additionally, ERK signaling can upregulate MMPs, leading to the degradation of the extracellular matrix and promoting cancer cell migration and invasion. ERK signaling further increases VEGF expression, promoting tumor angiogenesis, which provides the necessary nutrients and oxygen for tumor growth and reduces hypoxia-induced oxidative stress. Targeting the EGFR pathway conventionally involves anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) that target intracellular kinases. Cetuximab and panitumumab, FDA-endorsed anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies, constitute frontline treatments for CRC. In 2004, cetuximab, the inaugural targeted therapy for CRC, garnered FDA approval, heralding substantial promise in ameliorating progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in CRC patients. However, the application of anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies in CRC patients is limited due to mutations in Ras and Raf in some patients. Understanding the molecular mechanisms and clinical applications of targeted therapies can aid in the development of new therapeutic strategies. Importantly, targeting multiple cell death could provide potential strategies for CRC patients with EGFR downstream gene mutation.

PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathways
In addition to activating the MAPK pathway, EGFR also activates the PI3K/Akt pathway to regulate the proliferation, survival, and metabolism of CRC cells. Akt is a key pro-survival kinase that inhibits apoptosis and promotes cell growth and metabolism. Mechanistically, EGFR activates the PI3K/Akt pathway, with Akt phosphorylating and inhibiting TSC2, thereby promoting mTORC1 activity. Activation of mTORC1 increases protein and fatty acid synthesis, counteracting the energy-conserving effects of AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase). AMPK is a crucial regulator of cellular energy metabolism, promoting energy production and inhibiting energy consumption. Under conditions of energy deficiency, AMPK is activated by LKB1, which phosphorylates and activates TSC2, inhibiting mTORC1 activity and reducing cell growth and proliferation signals.
Currently, mTOR inhibitors Everolimus and Temsirolimus have been approved for the treatment of various tumors; however, their efficacy in treating CRC is limited when used alone. Nonetheless, when combined with other therapeutic approaches (such as chemotherapy or other targeted drugs), these inhibitors can significantly suppress the growth and survival of CRC cells. The application of mTOR inhibitors in CRC treatment remains under investigation, particularly in combination therapy regimens, where they have shown potential clinical value.

VEGFR signaling pathways
The VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor receptor) signaling pathway exerts pivotal effects in tumor progression, metastasis, and angiogenesis. Elevated levels of VEGF and heightened VEGFR activity in CRC patients correlate with adverse prognostic outcomes. VEGFR primarily binds with VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor), activating downstream signaling pathways, including the MAPK cascade, PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, and PLCγ/PKC pathway, promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and new blood vessel formation. This process is vital for tumor growth and metastasis as new blood vessels provide the necessary nutrients and oxygen to the tumor. Conversely, angiogenesis inhibition leads to hypoxia, which induces cell death through various pathways. Bevacizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting VEGF, blocks its binding to VEGFR, thereby inhibiting the VEGFR signaling pathway and preventing tumor angiogenesis. Bevacizumab, combined with FOLFOX/FOXFIRI regimens, has shown significant efficacy, resulting in marked improvements in OS and PFS, and has been FDA-approved for the treatment of mCRC. Notably, continuing bevacizumab in patients with disease progression after first-line chemotherapy has been shown to improve PFS. Additionally, two other anti-VEGFR drugs, aflibercept and ramucirumab, are recommended as second-line treatments for CRC. Fruquintinib and regorafenib, multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitors of VEGFR, are potential important treatment options for CRC patients with disease progression following standard therapies. While, further evaluation of fruquintinib’s efficacy outside of China is required.

Activation of the immune system
In the past decade, immunotherapy has made significant breakthroughs, with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICB) being widely applied to treat various tumors and showing remarkable efficacy. In 2015, le et al. firstly reveal that anti-PD-1/PD-L1 can significantly improve the prognosis of patients with deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) or high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) molecular phenotypes in mCRC. Subsequently, pembrolizumab and nivolumab have been approved for use in patients with advanced MSI or dMMR CRC patients. PD-1 is an inhibitory receptor on T cells, while PD-L1 is its ligand, typically expressed on tumor cells and other immunosuppressive cells. Tumor cells overexpress PD-L1, which binds to PD-1 on T cells, inhibiting T cell activation and proliferation, thereby reducing T cell-mediated attack on tumor cells. This interaction leads to immune evasion, allowing tumors to grow and spread within the host. Immune checkpoint inhibitors block the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, reactivating T cells and enhancing anti-tumor immune responses. However, according to the result of NCT01876511, ICB are mainly effective in MMR-deficient CRC patients, but not in other types of CRC patients. This is because MMR deficiency leads to the insertion or deletion of short repeat sequences (microsatellites) that cannot be repaired, resulting in genomic instability. Previous studies have shown that the high mutation burden and neoantigen load resulting from MMR deficiency make tumor cells more easily recognizable and attackable by the immune system. This renders MMR-deficient tumors more sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, based on the association between MMR deficiency and high immunogenicity.
In addition to enhancing T cell recognition and killing of tumor cells, ICB can also induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), leading to the release of tumor antigens and increasing tumor immunogenicity. This process promotes immune cell recognition and killing of tumor cells. These signals include damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as heat shock proteins (HSPs), high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and calreticulin. HSPs are highly expressed under cellular stress conditions and are released during ICD, promoting the maturation and antigen-presenting functions of antigen-presenting cells (APCs). HMGB1, once released, can bind to Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), enhancing the maturation and antigen-presenting capabilities of dendritic cells (DCs). During the process of ICD, calreticulin is exposed on the cell surface, facilitating the phagocytosis and presentation of tumor antigens by macrophages and DCs. RCD often releases numbers of viral antigens and DAMPs, which can activate the innate immune system and promote adaptive immune responses. Previous studies have shown that various physical therapies and certain chemotherapeutic drugs (such as anthracyclines and platinum-based drugs) can also enhance tumor immunogenicity by inducing CRC cell death, thereby eliciting a strong anti-tumor immune response. This may be a potential strategy to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors and warrants further research.

Targeting cell death provides potential strategies for CRC therapy
Since the mid-20th century, scientists have continuously discovered various modes of RCD activated by a series of signaling pathways. Concurrently, with the advancement of technology, the mechanisms and targets of different tumor cell death modes have gradually been elucidated, providing a reliable basis for the screening and development of CRC therapeutic drugs. Notably, numerous studies have indicated that targeting RCD could be a potential strategy to overcome the current clinical treatment resistance in CRC. Synergistically targeting RCD can not only enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy but also represents a potential strategy to overcome treatment resistance. However, the toxicity and efficacy of these drugs require further evaluation in humans. In this section, we will elaborate the latest progression in CRC treatment through intervening different RCD pathways.

The relationship between RCD and radiotherapy
Radiotherapy directly damages the DNA of CRC cells, initiating the apoptosis process. Several genes play crucial roles in apoptosis, including the Bcl-2 family, caspase family, and p53. Specific genes such as PREX2, hCINAP, and TRAF4 can influence radiotherapy-induced apoptosis and determine the efficacy of CRC treatment. Additionally, previous studies indicated ncRNAs play critical regulatory roles in radiation-induced apoptosis Assessing the status of apoptosis-related genes in patients can predict their responsiveness to radiotherapy, allowing for personalized treatment strategies. Combining radiotherapy with other treatment modalities may synergistically enhance radiation-induced apoptosis. Radiotherapy promotes ferroptosis by altering the expression of GPX4 and ACSL4, facilitating the synthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acids and lipid peroxidation. Furthermore, radiation-induced activation of p53 inhibits the expression of SLC7A11, suppressing glutathione synthesis and promoting lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. However, cancer cells with TP53 mutations enhance their resistance to radiotherapy by inhibiting ferroptosis. Studies have also shown that lncRNAs can enhance the sensitivity of CRC to radiotherapy by inhibiting GPX4-mediated ferroptosis. Nevertheless, the specific role of ferroptosis in CRC radiotherapy requires further research. Radiotherapy induces Caspase-3-GSDME-dependent pyroptosis, which further recruits and activates NK cells, enhancing anti-tumor immunity. The relationship between RCD and chemotherapy
Chemotherapy promotes CRC apoptosis through multiple pathways involving in DNA damage, mitochondrial activation and cell cycle arrest. The emergence of chemotherapy resistance indicates treatment failure and involves complex biological processes, including the activation of multiple genes and signaling pathways and abnormal drug metabolism. The Bcl-2 family proteins, which maintaining MOM stability, can block apoptotic signaling pathways and inhibit cell apoptosis. Dehydroxyhispolon methyl ether (DHME) and HNPMI are potential chemotherapeutic agents for treating CRC by targeting Bcl-2. However, their safety and adverse effects require further evaluation. Previous studies have shown that ML264 and cycloparamine can reverse chemotherapy resistance by targeting Bcl-2. Additionally, targeting other apoptosis-related proteins to enhance chemotherapy sensitivity has also been of great interest. Targeting the IAP family member survivin with tanshinone IIA significantly overcomes 5-Fu resistance in CRC patients. Chemotherapy-induced cell death can also trigger anti-apoptotic programs in neighboring surviving tumor cells, thereby affecting treatment outcomes.
Necroptosis can inhibit tumor progression by substituting apoptosis or promote tumor metastasis and immune suppression through inflammatory responses. Strategies targeting IAPs, such as Tolinapant (ASTX660) and birinapant combined with caspase inhibitors, can induce necroptosis in CRC, particularly effective in RIPK3-positive cases. For CRC patients lacking caspase-8, SMAC mimetic therapy may be a significant personalized treatment strategy, though its safety in humans requires further evaluation. Additionally, multiple experimental results have indicated that targeting necroptosis through small-molecule drugs may be a promising strategy to inhibit CRC progression and overcome apoptosis resistance. GDC-0326, a novel PI3Kα inhibitor, induces 5-FU induced necroptosis in CRC cells by upregulating RIPK1 and RIPK3. Research by Z. Zhang et al. demonstrated that the combination of GDC-0326 and 5-FU exhibits potent antitumor efficacy and favorable safety in CRC. ABIN-1 deficiency enhances sensitivity to TNF-induced necroptosis in CRC, highlighting a potential therapeutic target. Increasing resistance to apoptosis often leads to CRC treatment failure. Inducing necroptosis in apoptosis-resistant CRC cells may be a critical strategy to overcome therapy resistance, particularly in the absence of caspases.
Pyroptosis has emerged as a pivotal mechanism in the anti-tumor response, demonstrating dual roles of inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and enhancing chemotherapy efficacy. X. Guan et al. discovered that the combination of Santacruzamate A with 5-fluorouracil or regorafenib suppresses HDAC2, thereby promoting NLRP3/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis. MACC1 impedes GSDME cleavage-induced pyroptosis, contributing to CRC resistance against irinotecan. Y. Huang et al. revealed that oxaliplatin induces pyroptosis through upregulation of GSDME-N expression and the release of LDH, IL-1β, and TNF-α, while downregulation of Nrf2 exacerbates GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. GW4064 enhances oxaliplatin sensitivity in CRC by inducing BAX/caspase-3/GSDME mediated pyroptosis. Furthermore, the gut microbiota also plays a crucial role in tumor development via affecting the process of pyroptosis. For instance, Fusobacterium nucleatum modulates the Hippo pathway to increase BCL2 expression, thereby inhibiting chemotherapy-induced caspase-3/GSDME pyroptosis pathway and promoting chemoresistance in CRC. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that compounds such as GA and T22-PE24-H6 induce GSDME-dependent pyroptosis, significantly enhancing anti-cancer immune responses, particularly by augmenting immune cell infiltration in both the tumor microenvironment and spleen.
Chemotherapeutic drugs induce intracellular ROS elevation, leading to cell death. However, cancer cells can modulate antioxidant systems to tolerate ROS and develop resistance to chemotherapy. Ferroptosis, a programmed cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, depletes glutathione and prevents cancer cells from coping with ROS accumulation, thereby inducing cell death. Numerous studies have suggested that ferroptosis plays an critical role in CRC development and therapeutic resistance. Overexpression of FAM98A in CRC activates translation of xCT, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis and promoting cell proliferation. Metformin can reverse FAM98A-mediated 5-FU resistance. Lipocalin 2 inhibits ferroptosis by reducing intracellular iron levels and stimulating the expression of GPX4 and xCT, contributing to 5-FU resistance. Cisplatin has been shown to induce ferroptosis by depleting glutathione, and its combination with Erastin demonstrates significant synergistic effects in killing tumors. Additionally, the ferroptosis inducer Erastin has been found to exert cytotoxic effects on CSCs and reduce treatment tolerance. Numerous studies report that activation of Nrf2 promotes chemotherapy resistance in tumors, with high GPX4 expression in CRC tissues indicating poor prognosis. Besides, Fusobacterium nucleatum promotes oxaliplatin resistance by overexpressing GPX4, thus inhibiting ferroptosis. This suggests that the gut microbiota may play an important role in the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy drugs. Additionally, several studies indicate ferroptosis affects the sensitivity of CRC cells to oxaliplatin. The relationship between RCD and target therapy
Targeted therapy plays an important role in inducing apoptosis in CRC cells. Although CRC cells typically resist apoptosis by overexpressing anti-apoptotic molecules such as Bcl-2 and IAPs and by activating the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways, drugs targeting these molecules can restore sensitivity to apoptosis. For example, the combination of cetuximab and 3-bromopyruvate (3-BP) significantly induces apoptosis in resistant cells, and inhibiting SLC1A5 to promote EGFR degradation shows potential to improve cetuximab response. Ferroptosis is a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of targeted therapies and overcome resistance, especially in KRAS-mutant CRC cells, which are resistant to drugs like cetuximab. Studies have revealed that KRAS mutations in CRC cells can inhibit ferroptosis; however, promoting this process can enhance cetuximab’s cytotoxicity, thus overcoming resistance. Natural compounds such as β-elemene, 3-BP, osthole, and vitamin C have been found to induce ferroptosis and synergize with cetuximab in treating KRAS-mutant CRC. Gefitinib, an EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor, shows limited efficacy in KRAS-mutant CRC, while apatinib appears promising by promoting ferroptosis. Ferroptosis emerges as a novel therapeutic strategy, offering renewed hope for CRC patients resistant to or with limited response to current targeted therapies, especially those with KRAS mutations. As research progresses and the understanding of ferroptosis mechanisms deepens, more refined ferroptosis inducers and drugs targeting other cell death pathways are expected to provide more effective treatment options for CRC patients.
Copper ions are believed to primarily influence tumor growth and resistance through pathways involving mitochondrial function, ROS generation, and membrane permeability. Activation of the MAPK pathway is crucial for tumor proliferation. Studies have shown that copper enhances Mek1 phosphorylation of Erk in a dose-dependent manner. Another study highlights the essential role of copper ions in sustaining the growth of BRAF(V600E) tumor cells. Using copper ion chelators significantly reduces BRAF(V600E)-driven signal transduction and tumor occurrence. However, the efficacy of these treatments in BRAF-mutant CRC patients remains unclear and requires further clinical evaluation. Additionally, recent research indicates that manipulating copper ion accumulation or utilizing copper ion-mediated cytotoxic effects could pave the way for new cancer treatment strategies. While research on drug discovery for inducing cuproptosis and disulfidptosis in cancer therapy is limited, certain studies have shown potential therapeutic effects in cellular and animal models. As the underlying mechanisms of these processes are gradually elucidated, compounds targeting key proteins involved in cuproptosis and disulfidptosis are expected to be developed, potentially advancing cancer treatment in the future (Table 3).

The relationship between RCD and immune therapy
Immunotherapy resistance in CRC is associated with Fas dependent apoptosis impairment. Upregulation of PIR inhibits Fas transcriptional activation, promoting tumor survival and inhibiting CD8+ T cell-induced cell death. Inhibiting specific histone methyltransferases (e.g., with Verticillin A) can restore Fas expression and induce apoptosis in CRC cells. Additionally, the circ0007422/miR-1256/PD-L1 pathway promotes immune evasion by reducing apoptosis. Combining PD-L1 antibodies with FXR agonists has demonstrated significant antitumor effects, potentially enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy. D. Seneviratne et al. found that all MSI CRC contain a truncated regulatory deoxyadenosine tract element (DATE) in the hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) gene promoter. This truncation activates HGF expression, leading to autocrine signaling through MET, promoting cell proliferation and resistance to necroptosis. Additionally, therapeutic protein nanoparticles targeting CXCR4+ cancer stem cells (T22-PE24-H6) effectively inhibit CRC metastasis. Photodynamic therapy (IR700DX-6T-PDT) induces GSDME-dependent pyroptosis, increasing the sensitivity of MSS-CRC cells to PD-1 inhibitors and promoting host anti-tumor immune responses. Furthermore, previous studies have indicated that ferroptosis plays a crucial role in reshaping the TME and enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy. Altering apoptosis-related genes can enhance anti-tumor immune responses. For instance, magnetic mesoporous silica nanoparticles loaded with 6-gingerol induce lipid peroxide-mediated ferroptosis and bolster immune responses against tumors. Higher APOL3 expression boosts CD8+ T cell sensitivity to ferroptosis, enhancing their anti-tumor activity through increased IFN-γ production and lowered lactate levels, potentially improving immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Additionally, inhibiting molecules like CYP1B1, involved in arachidonic acid metabolism, may sensitize tumor cells to ICB therapy. In summary, the treatment of CRC not only directly affects tumor cell death pathways but also has the potential to reshape the immune landscape of the TME, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of immunotherapy. Conclusions and future perspectives
Collectively, accumulating evidence indicates that diverse forms of RCD in CRC undergo highly specialized molecular reprogramming. Through coordinated alterations in gene expression, signaling network remodeling, and dynamic interactions with the tumor microenvironment, these death programs exert pivotal regulatory effects on tumor progression and therapeutic responsiveness, thereby providing an essential mechanistic foundation for the development of precision oncology strategies. Nevertheless, the clinical translation of RCD-targeted interventions continues to face multiple challenges.
First, therapeutic resistance remains one of the most critical barriers. Chemotherapy, still the most widely applied treatment modality for CRC, exerts antitumor efficacy partly by inducing various RCD pathways, ultimately leading to tumor cell death and suppression of proliferation, invasion, and migration. However, sustained therapeutic pressure frequently drives adaptive reprogramming of death signaling in tumor cells. When specific chemotherapeutic agents target defined death pathways, cancer cells may acquire resistance through activation of compensatory survival mechanisms. For instance, in mucinous colorectal carcinoma, downregulation of key mitochondrial apoptotic proteins—including Bax, Bak, and Bcl-xL—reduces cellular sensitivity to apoptosis induction and consequently diminishes cytotoxic efficacy. Similarly, although TRAIL preferentially triggers apoptosis via the TRAIL-R2/DR5 receptor axis without inducing necrosis, heterogeneity in receptor expression and downstream signaling defects can limit its therapeutic effectiveness.
Second, intrinsic tumor heterogeneity substantially increases variability in RCD dependency. Genetic background, molecular subtype, and metabolic state collectively shape CRC cell susceptibility to different death modalities, such as apoptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis, or pyroptosis. Notably, while inactivation of the necroptotic executor MLKL has traditionally been considered cytoprotective, MLKL knockout paradoxically enhances cell death in certain CRC models, suggesting the existence of exploitable therapeutic vulnerabilities within specific molecular contexts. This context dependency further underscores the complexity of translating RCD-targeted strategies into clinical practice.
Third, limitations in biomarker development hinder patient stratification and response prediction. Although numerous RCD-associated molecules have been proposed as prognostic or predictive indicators, only a small fraction have achieved clinical validation. The dynamic regulation of RCD pathways, extensive crosstalk among distinct death programs, and modulatory influences from the tumor microenvironment collectively weaken the specificity of single biomarkers, thereby restricting their utility in therapeutic decision-making.
To address these challenges, several future directions merit emphasis. One strategy involves co-targeting multiple RCD pathways to circumvent compensatory resistance mechanisms—for example, simultaneously inducing apoptosis and ferroptosis or pyroptosis to amplify antitumor efficacy. Another approach is the implementation of RCD-based molecular subtyping using genomic and transcriptomic signatures to guide individualized treatment. Additionally, therapeutic exploitation of necroptotic rewiring under MLKL-deficient conditions or ferroptosis addiction driven by redox homeostasis imbalance represents promising avenues. Finally, integration with immunotherapy holds substantial potential, as inflammatory forms of cell death—such as pyroptosis and PANoptosis—can enhance tumor immunogenicity and improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.
In summary, dysregulation of RCD in colorectal cancer does not reflect impairment of a single death pathway but rather arises from a dynamically interconnected network integrating multiple death modalities. The intricate interplay among apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and emerging death programs not only drives tumor evolution but also profoundly shapes therapeutic responsiveness. Comprehensive delineation of this integrated cell death regulatory landscape will be critical for overcoming treatment resistance, deciphering tumor heterogeneity, and developing reliable biomarkers, ultimately advancing mechanism-guided precision therapy for CRC.
