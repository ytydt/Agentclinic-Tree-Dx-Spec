A systematic review of the Wide Awake Local Anesthesia no Tourniquet (WALANT) approach in pediatric hand surgery
Background and purpose
Wide Awake Local Anesthesia No Tourniquet (WALANT) for hand surgery in children and adolescents remains limited due to concerns regarding patient cooperation. This systematic review aims to synthesize the existing evidence on WALANT in the pediatric population to guide future directives.
Methods
This systematic review was performed in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. In June 2025, a comprehensive search of MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and CENTRAL was conducted. All studies reporting on WALANT in patients <18 years old were retrieved without date restrictions. For those meeting inclusion criteria, study and patient characteristics, interventions, and outcomes were extracted and analyzed. In addition, study quality was assessed based on the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence.
Results
Eleven studies were identified, including 441 patients and 507 procedures. No major complications were documented, and conversion to sedation or general anesthesia was required in only 0.6% of patients. Overall, the procedures were successful, as shown by consistent improvements in hand function. Patient feedback suggested high levels of satisfaction and minimal perioperative pain. Additionally, certain studies observed marked reductions in operative time, room time, recovery time, hospital stay, and costs.
Conclusions
Although the literature is limited, our findings demonstrate that WALANT is a safe, feasible and effective alternative to systemic anesthesia in children and adolescents. Key considerations include appropriate patient selection, child-centered communication strategies, distraction techniques, and supportive environments. This supports the broader integration of WALANT into pediatric practice. However, further research is warranted to optimize patient experience and assess institutional benefits.

Introduction
Wide Awake Local Anesthesia No Tourniquet (WALANT) is a surgical technique that allows hand procedures to be performed without the need for systemic anesthesia or a tourniquet. It provides effective analgesia and hemostasis using a combination of local anesthetic and vasoconstrictive drugs, enabling patients to maintain full consciousness during surgery. This approach eliminates the risks associated with sedation or general anesthesia, permits intraoperative assessment of function, shortens recovery times, and reduces resource utilization.
Despite these advantages, WALANT remains limited in the pediatric population. A common perception is that young patients may experience fear or anxiety when awake during surgery, resulting in poor cooperation and potential psychological distress. Consequently, many surgeons resort to systemic anesthesia for pediatric patients. Another potential barrier may relate to institutional or administrative policies that have not yet evolved to support the implementation of WALANT in hospital settings. However, we hypothesize that WALANT may be a feasible, safe, and effective alternative in select pediatric cases, particularly when appropriate patient selection, child-centered communication strategies, distraction techniques, and supportive environments are taken into consideration.
Given the potential benefits, a systematic review of the literature is required to better comprehend WALANT’s role in pediatric hand surgery. Patient-related factors (e.g., tolerability and satisfaction), clinical factors (e.g., complications and functional outcomes), and institutional factors (e.g., efficiency and cost-effectiveness) are among the main areas of interest. Thus, this review aims to clarify the advantages and limitations of WALANT in the pediatric population by analyzing the existing evidence. Additionally, we aim to provide guidance for future clinical practice and research in pediatric hand surgery.

Materials and methods

Protocol registration
A protocol for this systematic review was registered on PROSPERO on June 24, 2025 (ID: CRD420251067657).

Search strategy
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. In June 2025, four electronic databases were searched: MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and CENTRAL. The search terms included permutations of “WALANT,” “pediatrics” and “hand surgery.” Studies published from inception to 2025 were included to ensure the inclusion of all relevant literature. The search strategy was reviewed and validated by an experienced health sciences librarian. A detailed search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) full text available (i.e., open and restricted access); (2) randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, case series, case reports; (3) subjects underwent hand surgery using the WALANT technique for traumatic or acquired pathologies; (4) subjects were <18 years of age at the time of surgery. There were no limitations on study language. Studies were excluded if they were reviews or meta-analyses. Additionally, studies were excluded if they focused solely on the surgical repair of congenital differences such as polydactyly, as these are typically performed in infants (first weeks or months after birth) with distinct perioperative considerations.

Study selection
Two reviewers independently screened and selected eligible studies on Covidence. Following the removal of duplicates and a title/abstract review, full-text manuscripts of potentially relevant studies were retrieved and assessed based on inclusion/exclusion criteria. Both reviewers manually searched the bibliographic references of all included articles to ensure all those eligible were included.

Data extraction
Two reviewers independently performed data extraction in duplicate, employing a standardized data collection form designed a priori on Microsoft Excel. In selected studies, study characteristics, patient demographics, interventions and outcomes were extracted and summarized.

Quality assessment
Two reviewers independently appraised the included articles using the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence.

Overall, 441 patients underwent 507 hand surgeries using the WALANT technique. The mean age (± standard deviation) of patients was 9.1 ± 6.0 years. A total of 341 (67.3%) tendon-based procedures were performed, including trigger finger release, tenorrhaphy, tenolysis, tendon transfer, and pulley reconstruction. Ninety-four (18.5%) excision-based procedures were performed, including cysts, tumors and foreign bodies. Thirty-two (6.3%) bone-based procedures were performed, including open reduction/internal fixation (ORIF), hardware removal, and closed reduction/percutaneous pinning (CRPP). Forty (7.9%) other procedures were performed, including scar revision, cubital tunnel release, digital nerve/artery repair, and irrigation/debridement. Interventions are provided in Table 3.

B. Complications and conversion rates
Across all included studies, the WALANT technique demonstrated a strong safety profile. No major intraoperative or postoperative complications were reported. The only complication identified was a single case of superficial wound dehiscence following ganglion cyst excision, which healed with conservative management. In a retrospective cohort study comparing cases using WALANT to cases under general anesthesia, there was no statistically significant difference in complication rates between groups (adjusted OR = 0.94 [95% CI: 0.28–3.11], p = 0.92). Conversion to sedation or general anesthesia was rare, occurring in only three cases due to intraoperative intolerance, representing a conversion rate of 0.6% (3/507 procedures).
C. Functional assessment
When reported, postoperative functional outcomes were generally favorable. In studies performing tendon repairs and transfers, patients demonstrated satisfactory restoration of function at final follow-up, with marked improvements in ROM and strength. One study employed the Total Active Motion (TAM) score and found no significant difference between the WALANT and general anesthesia groups (adjusted OR = 1.96 [95% CI: 0.54–7.71], p = 0.32). Similarly, studies on percutaneous trigger thumb release reported high success rates (88.5%−96.8%) with minimal recurrence. Notably, no study reported inferior functional outcomes in the WALANT group compared to those receiving systemic anesthesia.

Patients expressed high levels of satisfaction with the WALANT technique. In the only study that implemented a custom feedback questionnaire, most respondents reported little to no pain during both the local anesthetic injection and the procedure itself. The experience was described as less painful than a dental visit, easier than anticipated, and preferable for future hand surgeries. Two studies used a visual analogue scale (VAS) to assess pain, both demonstrating lower postoperative pain scores in WALANT patients compared to those who received general anesthesia (0.5 vs. 1.2, p < 0.001; 0.07 vs. 1.5). Additional studies included qualitative feedback, which reinforced these findings, highlighting minimal pain and overall positive experiences. The specific intraoperative management and distraction strategies used across studies are summarized in Table 5, providing additional context on the approaches implemented to optimize the WALANT experience.
E. Time-based parameters and healthcare costs
The WALANT technique was associated with significant reductions in operating room time, recovery duration, and total hospital stay. In three studies comparing the use of WALANT to general anesthesia, WALANT cases demonstrated shorter operating room times by approximately 5–11 min, recovery times by 43–47 min, and overall hospital stay by 120 min. Although formal cost analyses were limited, findings suggest considerable financial advantages. In fact, one study reported a substantial reduction in post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) related costs, with mean savings of approximately $1236.90 USD per case compared to general anesthesia. Few studies specified the location where procedures were performed. However, in those that did, all surgeries took place in outpatient minor procedure rooms.

Discussion

Key findings
Our findings suggest that the WALANT technique is a feasible, safe and effective approach for pediatric hand procedures. High procedural success, low complication rates, and minimal need of conversion to sedation or general anesthesia were reported across a wide variety of surgical indications. Other reported benefits include substantial time and cost savings.
These preliminary results highlight WALANT’s potential to become a viable alternative to systemic anesthesia in the pediatric population, especially with appropriate patient selection, child-centered communication techniques, distraction strategies, and supportive procedural environments. However, the current literature is limited by the lack of studies, small sample sizes, inconsistent outcome reporting, and retrospective designs. Therefore, additional high-quality research is required to validate these findings, quantify institutional benefits across various settings, and explore ways to enhance patient experience.

Optimal patient selection
Selecting suitable patients is essential for the WALANT technique to succeed in the pediatric population. The average age of patients in our review was 9.1 years. This reflects a predominance of school-aged children who typically have the capacity to understand instructions and cooperate during wide-awake procedures. However, some children as young as 3 to 4 years may also be able to remain still and comprehend the process when appropriately supported. At this age, many children reach developmental milestones such as improved language skills and ability to follow simple instructions, both of which can contribute to their potential eligibility.
Given the variability among patients, selection should be based on an evaluation of psychological resilience, procedural complexity, parental support, and availability of supportive measures rather than age alone. For example, some authors have emphasized that candidates should be able to follow simple directions, remain still, and engage in a brief discussion about the planned procedure during the initial consultation. Others state that the final decision should be ultimately made by the child in collaboration with their parents, after a clear explanation of all available options, including sedation and general anesthesia, using age-appropriate language. Together, these factors are critical in ensuring intraoperative cooperation and comfort, while reducing the anxiety and fear associated with future medical procedures.

Integrating patient experience into practice
Understanding the patient experience is also essential for evaluating the WALANT technique in the pediatric population. This assessment should encompass pain levels during local anesthesia injection and surgery, preoperative anxiety, postoperative impressions (e.g., feelings of pride), and factors contributing to patient comfort. For pain assessment, validated subjective tools such as the VAS, or the Faces Pain Scale are typically used. Additionally, objective measures like the Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) Behavioral Pain Assessment Scale or the Children’s Hospital of Eastern Ontario Pain Scale (CHEOPS) can be used to support patient-reported outcomes and assess pain in children who are incapable of reasoning verbally.
Only one study included in our review implemented a feedback questionnaire, although several of the above elements were absent. No comprehensive assessment tool currently exists that integrates all these components for this specific population. The development of such a tool would be valuable for future research and clinical practice. Parental perceptions should also be incorporated into evaluations, as they play a key role in shaping the child’s overall experience and acceptance of the WALANT technique.
Factors contributing to patient comfort may include the presence of a parent or family member, access to distraction techniques (e.g., tablets, television, virtual reality), engaging and reassuring communication during surgery, and comfort-enhancing positioning (e.g., hand holding, hand massaging). Additional measures, such as using small-gauge needles, buffering the local anesthetic with sodium bicarbonate, applying vapocoolant spray before injection, and providing concurrent sensory distraction (e.g., gentle skin pressure or pinching), can further reduce injection discomfort. These methods have been described across the included studies but have not been systematically compared or quantitatively assessed. Future research should focus on identifying and standardizing the most effective protocols to optimize patient experience.

Future directions in cost and sustainability
Other important but underexplored outcomes include cost and environmental impact. WALANT procedures are typically performed in a minor procedure room, or in an ambulatory setting. Resource utilization is significantly reduced by eliminating the need for a traditional operating room, surgical day center, and PACU unit. Notably, several studies reported significant reductions in operating room duration, recovery duration and hospital stay. These findings suggest that the WALANT technique may reduce both direct and indirect healthcare costs and improve system efficiency. In our review, only one study reported PACU cost differences, while none reported overall cost savings. Future studies should incorporate formal cost-effectiveness analyses to better quantify these potential benefits, particularly in pediatric settings where additional resources are often required.
Similarly, the environmental advantages of the WALANT technique also require further investigation. The shift toward performing procedures in an outpatient or clinic-based setting significantly reduces energy consumption and waste generation, Additionally, by avoiding anesthetic gases, WALANT offers a more environmentally responsible approach. Given the increasing emphasis on sustainable healthcare practices, future research should evaluate the environmental implications of WALANT using validated sustainability metrics and life cycle assessments. Although these benefits have been documented in adult populations, their generalizability to pediatric cases have yet to be evaluated.

Strengths and limitations
This systematic review has several strengths. A comprehensive methodological approach was used following recognized guidelines. To allow for an inclusive capture of the relevant literature, there were no restrictions on study language or publication year. The included studies described a wide variety of surgical indications and procedures, offering valuable insight into the current applications of WALANT in pediatric hand surgery.
However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The number of available studies remains small, reflecting the emerging nature of WALANT in the pediatric population. Additionally, children with congenital hand differences were excluded from this review due to the unique considerations inherent to this subgroup, as previously reported. Most of the included studies were retrospective or observational, with limited sample sizes and inconsistent outcome reporting. Patient and parental experiences, in addition to system-level benefits, were particularly underreported and represent an important area for future investigation.
