Critical Evaluation of Methods for the Identification of Aneugens
ABSTRACT
The genotoxic potential of chemicals must be evaluated in regulatory safety assessment settings, including but not limited to, the development of new pharmaceuticals, industrial chemicals, food and cosmetic ingredients, and agrochemicals. Initial assessment of the chromosome‐damaging potential of chemicals is often conducted in mammalian cells using the micronucleus (MN) assay, a method capable of detecting both aneugenicity and clastogenicity. When differentiation between these modes of action (MOAs) is necessary, microscopy‐based analyses using fluorescent In Situ Hybridization (FISH) or CREST staining have traditionally been employed. More recently, semi‐automated in vitro new approach methods (NAMs), which leverage technologies like flow cytometry and high‐content imaging, have increasingly been used across sectors due to their higher throughput and faster turnaround times. A SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats) analysis was conducted to systematically evaluate the merits and limitations of widely used NAMs in industry, with a focus on the pharmaceutical sector. Data from cultured mammalian cells exposed to reference aneugens (colchicine, taxol, and AMG900) and DNA‐reactive clastogens (mitomycin C and methyl methanesulfonate) across methodologies are presented to illustrate the process of distinguishing aneugens from clastogens for the different techniques described herein. Collectively, these analyses highlight the capabilities of NAMs to distinguish aneugens from clastogens. The newer, high information content, semi‐automated approaches were considered preferable to traditional microscopy‐based FISH and CREST techniques as they provide insight into molecular mechanisms of aneugenicity and help optimize the design of future in vivo genotoxicity studies to facilitate deriving points of departure which may contribute to margin of exposure estimates.

Introduction
Aneuploidy is characterized by an abnormal number of chromosomes that deviate from the standard set. Specifically, aneuploid cells carry additional or fewer chromosomes relative to the typical 2N complement, or, in the case of germ cells, relative to 1N. In cell culture, aneugenicity can result from non‐disjunction, where sister chromatids fail to separate during mitosis, and a whole chromosome is lost from one daughter cell. This condition differs from polyploidy, which involves multiple complete sets of chromosomes. As a form of chromosomal damage involving numerical changes, aneugenicity is distinct from clastogenicity, which involves structural chromosomal damage, arising from DNA single or double strand breaks. Aneuploidy may result from exposure to chemicals, which are described as aneugens, and are potent inducers of micronuclei. At cellular and molecular levels, chemical‐induced aneugenicity is frequently linked to disruption of the mitotic spindle apparatus or interference with specific components of the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC), both of which are critical for accurate chromosome segregation during cell division.
The biological significance of aneuploidy is complex and has been extensively reviewed by the International Workgroups on Genotoxicity Testing (Lynch et al. ; Pacchierotti et al. ; Tweats et al. ). Aneuploidy is frequently observed in solid tumors, especially in metastases, and to a lesser extent in hematopoietic malignancies. However, whether aneuploidy serves as a cause or a consequence of carcinogenesis remains a subject of ongoing debate, and currently aneugens are not thought to be initiators of cancer (Ben‐David and Amon ; Tweats et al. ). For instance, many tubulin‐disrupting aneugens have demonstrated negative results in rodent carcinogenicity studies, and well‐characterized aneugenic agents like colchicine and albendazole have not been associated with increased cancer risk in humans or carcinogenic effects in rodents (Hollingshead et al. ; Longstaff et al. ; Slobodnick et al. ).
Aneugens disrupt cell division, which has been exploited as a therapeutic target in the treatment of malignancies. For example, many hallmarks of cancer involve unregulated proliferation (Hanahan and Weinberg ) and tubulin‐binding drugs halt this by preventing or delaying mitosis, which results in senescence, polyploidy, cytotoxicity, or apoptosis. Beyond cancer, aneuploidy has also been implicated in various birth defects, miscarriages, and genetic disorders, including autosomal trisomies (e.g., Down syndrome, Patau syndrome, Edwards syndrome) and sex chromosome abnormalities (e.g., Klinefelter syndrome, Turner syndrome) (Hassold and Hunt ). While most trisomies are fatal, other syndromes caused by aneuploidy are not embryonically lethal but significantly affect both life expectancy and quality. Aneuploidy has also been associated with other disorders such as congenital heart disease, neurodegenerative diseases, and other age‐related conditions (Arendt et al. ; Potter et al. ; Wimalasundera and Gardiner ).
Many regulatory safety assessment frameworks require the evaluation of xenobiotics for genotoxic potential, and this inherently includes an assessment of aneugenic properties as the micronucleus (MN) assay is typically requested (Figure 1). Micronuclei can be formed by both aneugenic and clastogenic modes of action (MOAs), and conventional microscopy‐based execution of the assay (without specialized staining) does not distinguish between the two. In the pharmaceutical sector, identifying the specific MOA can be crucial, as aneugens—causing numerical aberrations without direct DNA interactions—and clastogens—inducing structural aberrations likely with DNA interactions—can elicit distinct toxic effects.

This paper explores the development and utilization of traditional microscopy‐based fluorescent In Situ Hybridization (FISH) and CREST methods, along with the growing application and rapid advancement of alternate in vitro new approach methods (NAMs) to detect aneugenicity. The work within was conducted under the auspices of the In Vitro Working Group of HESI's Genetic Toxicology Testing Committee (GTTC) as part of a larger effort to classify and educate investigators on non‐DNA reactive genotoxic MOAs.

Mode of Action—Biological Considerations
Aneugens exhibit distinct mechanisms characterized by their ability to disrupt chromosome segregation through interference with the mitotic processes (Figure 1). The primary mechanisms currently linked to aneugenicity include the action of tubulin stabilizers, tubulin destabilizers, and mitotic kinase inhibitors (e.g., Aurora B kinase inhibitors) (Lynch et al. ). Each mechanism targets critical steps in cell division and produces unique patterns of aneugenic fingerprints, detectable by experimental methods.
Tubulin binders exert their effect either by stabilizing or destabilizing tubulin polymerization, which directly impacts the function of the mitotic spindle apparatus. This disruption activates the SAC, a critical cell cycle checkpoint that halts mitosis to prevent abnormal distribution of chromosomes in daughter cells (Matson and Stukenberg ). SAC activation can be detected through DNA cell cycle analysis, revealing a prominent G2/M block shortly after exposure. This is often correlated with a global increase in histone H3 phosphorylation‐positive (pH3POS) nuclei, indicating prolonged chromosome condensation (i.e., mitotic block). While the SAC permits additional time for the cell to establish amphitelic attachment, if kinetochores are eventually attached to microtubules—even in a merotelic state—the SAC is overridden, and cells proceed to anaphase (McAinsh and Kops ). In this merotelic state, during anaphase, laggard chromatids appear due to the failure of sister chromatid separation caused by unequal tension across the centromere. This induces non‐disjunction, generating one hypodiploid and one hyperdiploid daughter cell and is indicative of aneugenic activity.
Mitotic kinases play a critical role in the accurate alignment and distribution of chromosomes and sister chromatids in mitosis (Lynch et al. ). Kinase inhibitors have been pursued for the treatment of a number of different diseases, but in addition to the target kinase, they often inhibit other kinases in the kinome (Olaharski et al. ). Aurora kinases play a fundamental role during mitosis and are among the most promiscuous off‐target kinases (Anastassiadis et al. ). For example, Aurora kinase B is critical for chromosome condensation, correcting unequal kinetochore‐microtubule attachment, regulating the spindle assembly checkpoint, and lastly, completing the final stages of cytokinesis (Vader et al. ). Aurora B inhibition leads to a lack of H3‐phosphorylation, chromosome misalignment, and increases in binucleated cells, consequently leading to aneuploidy (Lynch et al. ). This occurs as Cyclin B, which maintains the mitotic state, is slowly degraded over time, and once it reaches a critical threshold, the cell exits mitosis despite unresolved SAC activation. This “mitotic slippage” ultimately results in polyploid cells (Huang et al. ; Shenk and Ganem ). Other kinases, like cyclin‐dependent kinases (Lynch et al. ) and polo‐like kinases, do not show the same promiscuity, and therefore, most kinase inhibitors induce aneuploidy that is phenotypically similar to the signature created by aurora B inhibitors (Lynch et al. ).

Mode of Action—Practical Considerations
Understanding whether an MN‐inducing agent acts via an aneugenic or clastogenic MOA has several practical applications in the pharmaceutical sector. First, chemists can use this knowledge during early product development to better understand the moiety driving the genotoxic response. By incorporating data into internal structure–activity relationship (SAR) databases, knowledge is gained and the synthesis of future compounds can have reduced (or eliminated) genotoxic potential. Second, having MOA knowledge is critical as aneugens generally have steep dose–response curves and exhibit genotoxicity within a narrow concentration range before excessive cytotoxicity (or lethality) occurs. This understanding can be used to design a more appropriate in vivo MN study, such as including additional dose groups and more tightly spacing the doses. Third, a detailed understanding of aneugenicity and the molecular targets involved is critical for human health risk assessments. This third point is important and related to the fact that, unlike many clastogens, aneugens do not directly interact with DNA, resulting in thresholded dose responses below which no chromosomal damage is expected (EFSA Scientific Committee (SC) et al. ; Elhajouji et al. ).
During drug development and regulatory evaluation, different strategies are required to manage the risks associated with clastogens and aneugens. Substances with clastogenic or mutagenic activity in vitro, which are considered DNA reactive, typically present higher risks due to their potential to induce heritable mutations. To proceed to clinical evaluation, at least two independent tissues must then be evaluated in vivo (e.g., comet assay in liver and the MN test in bone marrow) to demonstrate the absence of genotoxic activity and lack of risk. In contrast, substances with aneugenic activity in vitro are considered non‐DNA reactive and generally require only a single in vivo genotoxicity test, usually a MN assay, to establish health‐based guidance values or safety margins.

Method Comparison
Herein, we describe the current state‐of‐the‐art techniques for classifying aneugenicity in cultured mammalian cell models (N.B., while some of the methods described herein are applicable to in vitro‐ and in vivo‐derived specimens, in practice, it is generally the former that is used to elucidate genotoxic modes and mechanisms of action). These methods are each evaluated using the SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats) framework (Mercieca et al. ).
First, the traditional approaches for experimentally identifying aneugenic MOA, CREST, and FISH probing are described (Eastmond and Pinkel ; Eastmond and Tucker ; Miller et al. ). These methods are contrasted with the NAMs, including high‐content imaging and flow cytometric approaches, which enhance the ability to investigate the molecular underpinnings of aneugenicity. The evaluation focuses on the response profiles of these methods following exposure to reference chemicals representing three mechanisms: aneugenic tubulin perturbation (colchicine or taxol), aneugenic kinase inhibition (AMG900), and DNA‐reactive clastogens (mitomycin C [MMC] or methyl methanesulfonate [MMS]). These reference chemicals have been extensively studied for their genotoxic potential, making them ideal for a comparative analysis of the methodologies.
Through this SWOT assessment, stakeholders are provided with guidance for selecting various in vitro methods that are available for elucidating aneugenic mechanisms. This effort aims to inform regulatory genotoxicity testing paradigms as all methods have been used for internal decision making for many years with thousands of compounds evaluated.

Fish
FISH is a well‐established cytogenetic technique that is widely used to identify the MOA of MN formation in daughter cells using fluorescent microscopy. FISH employs species‐specific DNA probes targeting centromeric (satellite) regions, which are structural hallmarks of intact chromosomes. The absence of centromeric signal suggests MN formed via an acentric fragment, due to potential DNA reactivity and subsequent strand breakage. Hence, to discriminate between aneugenic and clastogenic MOAs, MN can be evaluated for the presence or absence of centromeric probe hybridization. This analysis is, by design, integrated into a MN assay, generally with the intent of studying MOA. Modifications to experimental design may include additional concentrations or tighter dose spacing, as aneugens often exhibit a steep cytotoxicity curve.
The FISH technique has been thoroughly described in previous literature (Elhajouji et al. ; Touil et al. ). Briefly, fixed slides undergo standard cytogenetic preparation, where DNA probes and samples are denatured to unwind and linearize DNA strands. Fluorochrome‐conjugated DNA probes are then annealed to the complementary DNA region prior to counterstaining with a nuclear stain. At least 100 MN are evaluated for the presence or absence of centromeric probe signal to discriminate between chromosome loss/gain (aneugenic) and structural damage (clastogenic). FISH is often considered the gold standard for identifying aneugenicity due to its reference in OECD test guideline 487 (OECD, 2023). Studies using robust reference genotoxins have demonstrated that even limited sample sizes are sufficient to differentiate between aneugens and clastogens (Elhajouji et al. ; Touil et al. ). However, the method is not without limitations. Increasing the number of scored MN and incorporating automated scoring systems could enhance assay precision and throughput. While centromeric probes effectively identify whole chromosome laggards, incorporating telomeric probes has been proposed to provide a more comprehensive characterization of MN origins (Miller et al. ). Another area requiring attention is the lack of statistical analyses to determine optimal sampling sizes or harmonized interlaboratory criteria for positive responses in FISH assays. Such efforts could improve consistency and standardization across studies, increasing the method's applicability and reliability.
FISH (and CREST, discussed below) has the advantage of being amenable to evaluating the predominate MOA of MN formation in vivo. This is valuable when assessing unique genotoxic metabolites that require MOA determination. Whether applied in cell culture or animal bone marrow, it is advantageous for the induced levels of MN to exceed a 5‐fold increase relative to the vehicle control. This enhances the speed of scoring and ensures results are informative as predominantly induced MN, instead of basally occurring MN, will be scored. Crest
CREST labeling is a cytogenetic technique that employs antibodies derived from the sera of patients with CREST syndrome, an autoimmune disorder named for its hallmark clinical manifestations: calcinosis, Raynaud's phenomenon, esophageal dysmotility, sclerodactyly, and telangiectasia. The antibodies produced in these patients recognize centromere‐associated proteins, particularly kinetochores (Kallenberg ), allowing for the identification of micronuclei formed by whole chromosome laggards. Unlike the species‐specific DNA probes required for FISH analysis, CREST antibodies cross‐react with centromere‐associated proteins in various mammalian species, making this technique versatile for in vitro and in vivo studies where species‐specific probes may be unavailable. The success of CREST labeling relies on careful experimental design. As with FISH, a well‐constructed experimental design with multiple dose levels and appropriate concentration spacing will mitigate unnecessary follow‐up experiments due to overt cytotoxicity. One main difference for CREST lies in the preparation of slides, which must preserve centromeric proteins. Standard cytogenetic fixatives containing acetic acid are thus unsuitable as they degrade these proteins as well as other epitopes. Instead, alcohol‐based fixation or other types of dehydrative fixing methods are employed. Following fixation, specimens on slides are blocked to enhance the specific binding of the primary anti‐centromeric antibodies. Detection is achieved using secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescent dyes, and slides are evaluated by fluorescent microscopy after counterstaining the DNA.
Scoring criteria for CREST labeling mirror those of FISH; 100 MN are evaluated for the presence (aneugenic) or absence (clastogenic) of kinetochore signals. While this threshold has not been statistically validated, it remains a widely accepted industry standard. Automating the scoring process with advanced microscopy and image analysis systems is possible and offers an opportunity to improve robustness and efficiency (Figure 3).

Despite its advantages, CREST labeling is not without challenges. The availability of anti‐centromere antibodies is limited, as these are derived from patient sera, and significant variability in antibody composition between different batches may affect staining consistency and signal quality (Kallenberg ). While commercially available antibodies are quality‐controlled, batch‐to‐batch differences may still arise, particularly with high sample densities. Additionally, the compatibility of CREST labeling with previously conducted MN studies depends on the fixative used and how slides were stored. Improper fixatives or prolonged storage may compromise epitope stability, leading to inconclusive results.

High Content Imaging
The advent of advanced imaging technologies has facilitated the development of multiplexed, high‐content genotoxicity screening platforms. These tools provide high‐throughput capabilities with low compound requirements while offering regulatory endpoints and biomarker analyses that aid in MOA assessments. Adherent cell lines are generally preferred in image‐based platforms because apoptotic bodies, which are not attached to the plate, can be eliminated during washing steps, minimizing confounding effects on MN identification. Biomarker analyses are often performed at different time points post‐treatment to capture the most sensitive and compound‐related cellular responses (Figure 3A,B). A customized, automated image analysis algorithm is applied to score many cells, outputting MN percentages and other biomarker responses. Due to the high content nature of these screens, computational models or decision trees are often employed to streamline decision‐making processes (Sun et al. ).
High‐content imaging platforms utilize various fluorescent probes combined with antibodies to achieve parallel assessment of multiple biomarkers within the same sample. DNA staining is used to identify nuclei and MN, and antibodies targeting CREST or centromere proteins distinguish whole chromosomes from chromosome fragments inside MN (Figure 4, left column). γH2AX routinely serves as a marker for double‐stranded DNA breakage (Sun et al. ; Takeiri et al. ; Wilson et al. ) (Figure 4, right column), and mitotic biomarkers such as Mpm2 and pH 3 help identify mitotic populations. These mitotic biomarkers further differentiate between aneugens with tubulin‐binding or Aurora kinase‐inhibiting phenotypes (Sun et al. ). For instance, tubulin‐binding aneugens increase Mpm2 and pH 3 levels, whereas Aurora kinase inhibitors induce mitotic arrest accompanied by pH 3 dephosphorylation and increased Mpm2‐positive populations. Additional cell cycle information can be derived by analyzing DNA content or through follow‐up imaging assays targeting other cell cycle markers, such as cyclin proteins (Wilson et al. ) (Figure 4, middle column). High‐content imaging platforms offer numerous advantages, including their adaptability to various adherent cell lines, provision of regulatory endpoints alongside MOA insights, and minimization of apoptosis‐related confounding in MN analysis (Sun et al. ). However, high‐content imaging has areas that require further refinement. Cell segmentation remains a significant challenge, particularly with polyploid cells, due to inconsistencies in nuclear size, shape, and spacing. While cytoplasmic dyes or cell adhesion markers have been used for segmentation with healthy cells, these methods often struggle with polyploid populations. Improved algorithms and staining techniques are needed to enhance segmentation accuracy and enable precise cell cycle analyses.
Additionally, high‐content imaging platforms differ across manufacturers, with each offering unique analysis pipelines that can be customized by users. The analysis sequences of each imaging platform vary in image resolution, the number of endpoints included in the assay, and the object selection methodologies employed. While the customizable algorithms are user‐friendly and adaptable to different cell lines and fluorescent probes, the lack of standardization complicates direct comparisons between platforms. The current evaluation of platform performance focuses on testing model compounds across a wide range of concentrations and using benchmark dose analysis to assess sensitivity and specificity relative to established slide‐based or flow cytometry‐based methods.

Flow Cytometry
Flow cytometry is a widely accessible and versatile platform for genetic toxicity investigations. This analytical instrument uses hydrodynamic focusing to align specimens, generally suspended cells or nuclei, into a single file enabling the interrogation of each particle with lasers tuned to provide one or more different excitation wavelengths. The light scatter properties and emitted fluorescence, from stains or bound conjugated antibodies, of individual events can be quantitatively measured. Currently, flow cytometry is a widely accessible and powerful tool for assessing cellular responses to genotoxic agents. This platform can be applied to various mammalian test systems to identify SAC activation and the induction of polyploid daughter cells, both indicative of aneugenic MOAs (Table 4).

One of the key advantages of flow cytometry is its ability to simultaneously assess micronuclei and hypodiploidy, which provides a robust indication of aneugenicity, particularly when using hamster‐derived test systems, such as Chinese dwarf hamster ( Cricetulus griseus ) cell lines (Bryce et al. ; Nicolette et al. ). In this system, sub‐2N nuclei can be resolved using standard flow cells and optics, which is believed to be a consequence of hamsters' small karyotype (22 modal chromosomes). Although mammalian species share similar total DNA content, the distribution of this DNA across fewer chromosomes, as opposed to human‐derived cell lines with 46, results in a more pronounced fluorescence shift in hypodiploid nuclei, making it easier to enumerate with standard optics.
Flow cytometry is widely regarded as a benchmark method for analyzing cell cycle kinetics, having been extensively used in prognostic studies of soft tissue tumors (Tribukait ). The availability of a wide range of nucleic acid stains allows for the adaptation of flow cytometry to whole, intact cell cultures. However, when integrating intracellular epitopes, such as pH 3 or p53, standard fixation and/or permeabilization protocols should be employed (Bryce et al. ; Muehlbauer and Schuler ). Multiplexing capabilities enable the simultaneous assessment of pH 3, p53, and DNA staining to differentiate aneugenic mechanisms (Figure 5). For example, kinase inhibitors induce high levels of polyploidy after 24 h of exposure, whereas tubulin binders arrest cells in mitosis. When combined with γH2AX labeling, this panel can effectively discriminate aneugens from clastogens (Figure 6) (Bryce et al. ).

During early screening, integrating kinetic cell cycle analysis also can identify aneugenic mechanisms (Brandsma et al. ). A 4‐h timepoint is particularly useful for detecting SAC activation in asynchronous cell cultures, as cells in or entering S‐phase during exposure become arrested in M‐phase (Figure 7). In contrast, clastogens activate the G2 checkpoint, resulting in a G2/M block only after 24 h of continuous exposure, as a mechanism to prevent the inheritance of damaged chromosomes (Stracker et al. ). By combining SAC activation data at 4 h with polyploidy assessments at 24 h, flow cytometric cell cycle analysis can clearly distinguish aneugenic MOAs from those involving DNA‐reactive molecular initiating events.

Conclusion
Assessing chromosome damage is essential for pharmaceutical safety, requiring robust and reliable methods. The in vitro MN assay, recommended in the ICHS2 (R1) guideline, is a key tool used to assess genotoxic hazards in many industries, but differentiating between clastogenicity/mutagenicity and aneugenicity is particularly important for shaping the developmental strategies of novel pharmaceutical programs. This follows from the appreciation that aneugenicity, a non‐DNA reactive process, requires different risk management strategies compared to clastogens or mutagens. Herein, the SWOT analysis focused on 5 common tool molecules with data availability covering specific mechanisms of action. It's worth noting that the aneugenic and clastogenic/mutagenic data patterns from each of the NAMs would apply broadly to their respective MOA, regardless of their specific mechanism (e.g., centrosomal amplification as an aneugenic mechanism still induces polyploid cells). While other sectors could benefit from the efficiency NAMs offer in identifying aneugenicity, managing risk is reliant upon controlling human exposure, which is nearly impossible for a food additive or cosmetic ingredient. Hence our focus on the pharmaceutical sector, since patient exposure to medicine is controlled, identifying non‐DNA reactive mechanisms is a natural part of the drug developmental process when faced with a positive MN test result.
Classical assays, FISH and CREST, examine the presence of centromeric regions to differentiate full chromosomes from chromosome fragments within micronuclei, which is sufficient to distinguish between an aneugenic and a clastogenic MOA. While FISH and CREST are technologically less demanding and historically considered gold standard techniques, they lack harmonized assay protocols and will not contextualize why a xenobiotic is aneugenic. In modern and high‐performing safety assessment laboratories, genotoxicity assessments seek more mechanistic‐based information, including the distinction of different aneugenic mechanisms and derivation of points of departure. Unfortunately, as typically practiced, FISH and CREST cannot provide this information. Multiplexed NAMs provide higher throughput and more comprehensive strategies for detecting aneugens while offering deeper insights into the molecular events leading to MN formation.
Flow cytometric or high‐content imaging‐based NAM approaches allow evaluating full dose–response curves with low compound requirements. These methods also provide additional biological activity details, aid in SAR development, and paint a comprehensive picture of upstream biological effects. Benefiting from the high throughput nature of NAMs, large compound sets have been evaluated in various inter‐lab settings, which have demonstrated the robustness and high transferability of these technologies (Bernacki et al. ; Bryce et al. ; Kuo et al. ; Sun et al. ; Wilson et al. ). Nevertheless, as highlighted in the SWOT analyses, standardization of assay conduct, analytical pipelines, and data interpretation across NAMs remains an area for improvement.
In summary, both classical techniques and NAMs are capable of providing important information on genotoxic MOAs. When identifying aneugenicity, one should evaluate the purpose of each study, lab capability, technology accessibility, and resource availability prior to identifying the best fit‐for‐purpose method. When the infrastructure and resources for conducting NAMs are in place, and laboratory proficiency has been demonstrated, it is clear that multiplexed NAMs are preferable to FISH and CREST due to their higher throughput, lower costs, and faster turn‐around times.
