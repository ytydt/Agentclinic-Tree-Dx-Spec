Evaluating the effects of Climate Smart Agricultural (CSA) practices on productivity, adaptation, and mitigation indicators in Ethiopia: A meta-analysis approach
Climate-smart agricultural (CSA) practices have been adopted in various agroecological zones in Ethiopia to enhance productivity, improve resilience to climate change, and reduce greenhouse gas emissions through carbon sequestration. However, the overall impact of different CSA practices on productivity, adaptation, and mitigation metrics has not been exhaustively evaluated. The study employed a meta-analysis approach based on data from 220 peer-reviewed articles to assess the effects of commonly used CSA practices on these indicators across Ethiopia's diverse agroecological regions. The analysis identified over 20 CSA practices, with most—except soil bunds, level Fanya juu, and deficit irrigation—showing positive effects on productivity, peaking with drip irrigation (effect size of 2.15). Almost all practices also effectively reduced runoff and soil erosion, particularly crop residue mulching, which had a remarkable effect size of 2.95. Additionally, the findings indicated that various CSA practices enhanced soil organic matter and carbon stocks. Water management practices, especially drip and deficit irrigation, demonstrated significantly higher water productivity than traditional flood irrigation, with an effect size of up to 2.6. This water use efficiency suggests that these methods could free up substantial water resources for irrigating additional land, thus boosting crop production in water-scarce areas. However, the analysis revealed a negative effect size of up to −0.74 for income derived from drip irrigation, primarily due to the high costs of the necessary equipment. This highlights the need for reforms in duty and tax exemptions to improve farmers' profitability from drip irrigation systems. Overall, this meta-analysis assesses the impact of various CSA practices on key performance indicators productivity, adaptation, and mitigation—providing insights that can guide the packaging and implementation of the most effective CSA strategies across Ethiopia's agroecological zones.

Introduction
Agriculture, in the form of crop and livestock production, is the main driver of the Ethiopian economy which contributes 36 % and 66 % of the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and national employment, respectively. Nevertheless, the sector is influenced by climate change and/or variability which significantly undermines the GDP and national employment. Increases in average temperatures, changes in precipitation trends, increasing frequency of extreme weather events including flooding and droughts, and changes in planting time have been observed in various agro-ecological zones of the country. This shows that climate change in Ethiopia is already happening, and the impacts are being felt in various places. Studies showed that all four dimensions of food security (availability of food, access to food, utilization, and stability) are potentially affected by climate change through their effects on agricultural production and the incomes of rural households, food prices, and markets.
To reduce the impacts of climate change, Climate-smart agriculture (CSA) practices, approaches, and/or technologies have been advocated. The concepts of CSA have emerged from the various empirical literature and international development organizations. For example, according to Ref., CSA is not a single specific agricultural technology or practice that can be universally applied, rather it is "an approach that transforms and reorients agriculture under the new realities of climate change". CSA is defined as agricultural practices that increase productivity, contribute to enhancing resilience (adaptation), and minimize greenhouse-GHGs (mitigation) where applicable, and also augment the attainment of food security and development goals (18). Regardless of the definitions used, the CSA concept has three key pillars including i) sustainably increasing food security through increases in productivity and incomes, ii) building resilience and adapting to climate change (adaptation), and iii) reducing greenhouse gas emissions (mitigation) compared to a business as usual or baseline scenario.
The use of best-bet CSA (Climate-Smart Agriculture) practices helps various stakeholders increase productivity and income, improve adaptation to climate change, and reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions or sequester carbon. Numerous site-specific CSA practices, options, and interventions have been tested across different agroecological zones in Ethiopia. Several studies have been conducted to assess the impact of these CSA practices on productivity, adaptation, and mitigation in Ethiopia. Despite the growing body of evidence on the effects of Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) practices in Ethiopia, several key knowledge gaps remain that limit the comprehensive understanding and effective scaling of these practices across diverse agroecological zones and farming systems. While studies highlight the overall benefits of CSA practices (e.g., soil bunds, stone bunds, minimum tillage), the effectiveness of these practices varies significantly across regions due to differences in soil type, topography, rainfall patterns, and socio-economic conditions. Nevertheless, studies have shown that the contribution of CSA practices on productivity (P), adaptation (A) and mitigation (M) were inconsistent; results were site-specific and affected by experimental characteristics. For example, studies showed that physical soil and water conservation practices (e.g. soil bunds, stone bunds) improved farmers’ adaptation (A) to soil erosion (via reducing soil loss, runoff and nutrient loss) while decreased crop productivity (P) in the first three years and increased P after three years.
On the contrary, stone and soil bunds improved adaptation to moisture stress (drought) and increased productivity in the drier part of the country (36; 42). Hence, there is a lack of comprehensive assessment to quantify the impacts of CSA practices under varying biophysical and socio-economic conditions. The interplay and potential trade-offs between productivity, adaptation, and mitigation goals are poorly understood. For example, some practices may enhance productivity but have limited mitigation benefits or vice versa. Meta-analysis is a powerful tool to combine and summarize independent studies from multiple sources to increase the accuracy of individual CSA findings. Therefore, this study used a review of the literature and meta-analysis to assess the effect of widely used CSA practices/interventions on productivity, adaptation and mitigation indicators in various agroecological zones of the country.

Methodology

Data collection
We collected various CSA tested and evaluated in Ethiopia from 1985 to 2022. The first step in the study was to collect data regarding the effect of CSA practices on key pillars of CSA practices. Both electronic and hard-copy literature sources were used to collect data on the effect of CSA practices on productivity, adaptation and mitigation. Several Keywords were employed to search Scopus and Web of Science literature. Some examples of keywords include water use efficiency, animal feed efficiency, land management, climate resilience, adaptation, mitigation, soil conservation, water conservation, soil bunds, stone bunds, fanya juu, agroforestry, home garden, productivity, income, crop yield, animal productivity, irrigation, integrated soil fertility management, compost, animal manure, low emission, carbon sequestration, water harvesting, moisture conservation and drought tolerant. Additionally, publications in hard copy were obtained from libraries of different institutions such as MoA, Water and Land Resource Center and the Ethiopian Institute of Agricultural Research. Based on the keywords used, 1865 publication records were found in Scopus and Web of Science. Most of the publications had no relevant data that could be used for Meta-analysis. This resulted in a total of 220 publications that can be qualified for Meta-analysis.
Once appropriate publications were found, the database was created using a data collection template in Microsoft Excel. The most important variables included in the template include publication ID, the title of the publication with authors, name of CSA practices, year of publication, agroecology, location, CSA pillar, CSA indicators, effects of CSA practices (treatment and control), unit of indicators, etc.

Data analysis
Once the database was created in Microsoft Excel, various descriptive analysis and graphs were done to understand the characteristics of studies including the proportion of publications over the years, the proportion of publications across major agro-ecological zones, the proportion of each CSA practices studies, the distribution of CSA pillars recorded across the publications. The main analysis in this study was conducting a meta-analysis to identify the effects of CSA practices on productivity, adaptation and mitigation. The key characteristic of meta-analysis is calculating the effect size across the studies. The parameters needed for effect size determination include the number of observations in the experimental group, the sample mean of the experimental group, the standard deviation in the experimental group, the number of observations of the control group, the sample mean of the control group and the standard deviation in the control group. An author suggested the standardized mean difference (SMD) or effect size as the measure of the magnitude of the treatment effect. The larger the effect size the stronger the relationship between the two variables. We also employed the random effect method in the meta-analysis as the average effect size in the studies varies randomly from one study to another study. That means studies in a meta-analysis come from studies that have different average effect sizes and the effect sizes are heterogeneous because they come from studies with varying average effect sizes. As a rule of thumb, values of ≥0.20, ≥0.50, and ≥0.80 are considered to be small, medium and large effects, respectively (see Fig. 1).

Effects of CSA practices on the productivity, adaptation and mitigation
The key findings of the meta-analysis on CSA practices centered around three main pillars. These pillars are: i) productivity (sustainably maintaining and increasing productivity), ii) adaptation to enhance the resilience of farmers and agricultural systems in response to climate change, while also aiding in mitigation by lowering greenhouse gas emissions and sequestering carbon dioxide. Various indicators for CSA practices have been established within these pillars.

Effect of CSA practices on productivity
Productivity-yield
Given the availability of publications for meta-analysis, the impact of CSA practices on yield and income was utilized to assess the productivity pillar of these practices. The subsequent discussion focuses on the effects of CSA practices on yield and income.

Fig. 7
Forest plot in Fig. 7 shows the effect size of productivity (e.g. yield) for various CSA practices studied in Ethiopia. The y-axis shows the different CSA practices, and the x-axis shows the effect size of cop yield for CSA practices. The bars represent the average effect size of yield from CSA practices, with the error bars indicating the 95 % confidence interval. The larger effect size implies the more effective the CSA practices are. The results indicate that yield had a larger effect size for most CSA practices, suggesting that these practices are effective in improving productivity (e.g., crop yield) compared to conventional agricultural practices used as control treatments. For example, CSA practices such as animal feed management (ES = 1.014), compost (ES = 1.34), crop residue mulching (1.85), drip irrigation (ES = 2.14), drought tolerant crops (ES = 3.67), weed management (ES = 1.45) and stone bund (ES = 1.40) had large effect size, meaning that they are associated with the greatest productivity. Other practices had a range of positive effect sizes. However, stone bunds and deficit irrigation had negative effect sizes (Fig. 7). Previous studies in Ethiopia have reported inconsistent results concerning the effects of various CSA practices on crop yield. For instance, practices such as the application of farmyard manure, compost, tied ridging, and crop residue mulching have been associated with improved crop yields. In contrast, other interventions, including graded Fanya juu, graded soil bunds, level Fanya juu, level soil bunds, and grass strips, exhibited a negative mean difference in crop yield, suggesting that the implementation of these practices may lead to a reduction in yields. This variability highlights the need for context-specific assessments of CSA practices to better understand their effects on agricultural productivity.
Productivity-income
The use of compost has also been extensively studied as a method to improve soil fertility and boost crop yields. Studies showed that compost application had a positive impact on crop productivity, particularly in Ethiopia's highlands. Another effective practice is crop residue mulching, which has been shown to improve soil moisture retention and reduce erosion, leading to higher crop yields. Studies reported significant productivity improvements from mulching, especially in drought-prone areas. These findings align with the high effect size observed in our study. Drip irrigation has been recognized as a highly efficient method for improving water use and increasing crop yields, particularly in semi-arid regions like Tigray and Oromia. Research showed that drip irrigation significantly boosted crop productivity, particularly for high-value crops. Our finding of a high effect size (ES = 2.14) supports the potential of drip irrigation to enhance yields. The adoption of drought-tolerant crops has proven highly effective in increasing productivity in water-scarce regions. Studies confirmed that drought-tolerant varieties of crops, such as maize and teff, substantially enhance yields under drought conditions (). The high effect size (ES = 3.67) in our study further validates the effectiveness of these crops. Effective weed management practices, including crop rotation, herbicide application, and manual weeding, have also shown positive impacts on crop yields in Ethiopia. Studies documented the benefits of integrated weed management, which aligns with our finding of an effect size of ES = 1.45. Although stone bunds are commonly employed in Ethiopia to reduce soil erosion and improve water retention, research demonstrated that stone bunds, combined with other conservation measures, improved yields in areas facing erosion and water scarcity. Our study's positive effect size of ES = 1.40 supports these findings, although the effectiveness of stone bunds can vary depending on local conditions. Interestingly, our study found a negative effect size for stone bunds and deficit irrigation, which contrasts with many previous studies. For instance, research results in Ethiopia reported positive impacts of stone bunds on productivity, particularly when integrated with other soil conservation techniques. Similarly, previous research acknowledged the challenges of deficit irrigation and its potential to enhance water use efficiency and crop yields, particularly in water-scarce regions.
Effect size of income (productivity pillar) for five climate-smart agricultural (CSA) practices studied in Ethiopia (1986–2022). Error bars represent 95 % confidence interval.
Fig. 8
Fig. 8 presents the effect size of five CSA practices on the income of farmers. The figure shows that most of these CSA practices had a positive effect size on the income of households. Accordingly, effect sizes of income of 0.56, 0.79, 0.82 and 0.36 were recorded from application of compost, farm yard manure, and intercropping and integrated fertilizer application, respectively. However, drip irrigation had a negative effect size (ES = −0.74), indicating that it is less profitable compared to furrow and flood irrigation. This negative effect is attributed to the high cost of drip irrigation equipment. The findings on the effect sizes (ES) of various agricultural practices on income in Ethiopia provide valuable insights into their relative profitability. A review by Ref. identified practices such as bench terraces, stone bunds, level and graded Fanya Juu, graded and level soil bunds, and grass strips as economically viable. Studies in Ethiopia consistently emphasize the benefits of compost in enhancing soil fertility and crop yields. For instance, research in the central highlands of Ethiopia demonstrated significant income improvements, largely due to reduced reliance on chemical fertilizers and the long-term benefits of improved soil health. Similarly, findings from southern Ethiopia highlighted the positive effects of farmyard manure on crop yields, particularly maize and teff. The high effect size of farmyard manure is attributed to its dual role in enhancing soil structure and providing essential nutrients. Intercropping has also shown substantial income benefits. Studies revealed that intercropping systems combining legumes with cereals improve land-use efficiency, diversify income sources, and reduce risks, making them highly profitable. Studies which combine organic and inorganic fertilizers, promotes long-term soil productivity. However, the relatively high initial costs may limit its short-term income benefits. On the other hand, drip irrigation shows a negative effect size in similar studies in Ethiopia. The high upfront costs and maintenance requirements of drip irrigation systems pose significant challenges, particularly for smallholder farmers. In contrast, flood and furrow irrigation, despite being less efficient, are more affordable and accessible, making them more economically viable for many farmers. In general, these findings underscore the importance of promoting low-cost, sustainable agricultural practices while addressing the financial barriers to adopting advanced technologies like drip irrigation.

Effect of CSA practices on adaptation
Adaptation-runoff
Enhancing the adaptation/resilience of farmers and agricultural systems to climate change is the second pillar of CSA practices. In this pillar, based on the availability of indicators in the meta-analysis, four indicators were selected to represent the adaptation pillar of CSA practices. These indicators include runoff, soil loss, irrigation water and water productivity. Hence, we discussed these indicators under the adaptation pillar.

For example, graded soil bund, level soil bunds, stone bunds, tied-ridge, grass strips, farm yard manure and crop residue mulching reduced runoff. Exclosure practices, particularly in Ethiopia's highland and semi-arid regions, have proven effective in reducing runoff and promoting vegetation recovery. Studies in Ethiopia confirm that exclosure significantly improves water retention by restoring vegetation cover and reducing soil erosion [51, 52). ] Thus, our result, with an effect size (ES) of −0.89, aligns with these findings, highlighting the potential of exclosure to mitigate runoff. Integrating exclosure with stone bunds has also been recognized as an effective strategy for conservation in Ethiopia. Research indicates that combining exclosure with physical soil conservation structures like stone bunds enhances water retention and further reduces runoff. Our result of an ES of −0.83 supports these conclusions, suggesting that this integrated approach is highly effective in reducing runoff. The use of stone bunds with trenches is a well-established conservation practice in regions like Tigray and Amhara, where soil erosion and runoff are major challenges. Studies have shown that this practice is highly effective in controlling runoff and soil erosion. Our result of an ES of −1.74 suggests that this practice is among the most effective at reducing runoff, consistent with the findings of these studies. Similarly, level soil bunds are commonly used in Ethiopia's highland regions, where they help control runoff and reduce soil erosion. Research has demonstrated that level soil bunds are effective in retaining water and controlling soil erosion. Our result of ES = −1.07 supports these studies, indicating that level soil bunds are effective at reducing runoff as well. Overall, these findings underscore the effectiveness of integrated soil and water conservation practices, such as exclosure, stone bunds, and level soil bunds, in reducing runoff and promoting sustainable land management in Ethiopia.

Effect of CSA practices on mitigation of climate change
Mitigation-soil organic matter
The mitigation component of the CSA pillar reflects each practice's contribution to reducing greenhouse gas emissions or sequestering carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. We used soil organic matter and soil carbon stock as proxy indicators for the mitigation aspect of CSA practices, as discussed below.

Fig. 12 shows the effect size of soil organic matter (SOM) for various CSA practices. A positive effect size indicates that the CSA practice is associated with increased soil organic matter content. As shown in the figure, the effect sizes of SOM varied across the CSA practices. For example, the highest effect size of SOM was recorded in most of the CSA practices such as animal feed management (ES = 1.37), exclosure (ES = 0.83), farm yard manure (ES = 1.38), use of integrated fertilizer (ES = 1.74). Mitigation-soil carbon stock
Practices like animal feed management (ES = 1.37), farmyard manure (ES = 1.38), and integrated fertilizer use (ES = 1.74) show significant positive impacts on SOM. A study in Tigray, Ethiopia, found that applying farmyard manure increased SOM content by over 40 % in degraded soils, highlighting its role in soil restoration. Integrated nutrient management (combining organic and chemical fertilizers) improved SOM levels in maize fields in Oromia, indicating its potential for sustainable soil health improvement. In the Ethiopian highlands, soil bunds and Fanya juu terraces increased SOM levels by trapping organic material and moisture, but their effects were more pronounced when combined with organic amendments. Grass strips and vegetative barriers contributed to SOM improvement in sloped lands but required complementary practices like manure application to maximize benefits. Research in the Amhara region observed that mulching had a limited direct impact on SOM but played a critical role in moisture conservation and reducing evaporation, indirectly supporting SOM preservation over the long term. Similar to our reference to minimum tillage improving SOM, studies in Ethiopia confirm that reduced tillage prevents SOM degradation by reducing soil disturbance. Combining structural practices (e.g., terraces) with biological measures (e.g., grass strips) and organic inputs amplifies SOM improvement. For example, integrated practices in the Central Rift Valley increased SOM levels by over 50 % compared to standalone methods.

Fig. 13 presents the effect sizes of soil carbon stock (SCS) of various CSA practices studied in Ethiopia. As shown in the figure, the effect sizes of SCS differ among the CSA practices studied. The figure shows that most CSA practices had a positive effect on the soil carbon stock except graded Fanya juu. The negative effect size of SCs from graded fanya juu implies that soil treated with graded fanya juu had no positive effect in the accumulation of soil organic carbon. The highest effect size (ES) was recorded from exclosure (ES = 1.56), stone bund (ES = 2.26) and minimum tillage treatments (ES = 2.26). Previous studies elsewhere in Ethiopia showed that soil bunds, stone bunds and grass strips improved soil carbon stock.
The positive effect of stone bunds on soil carbon is consistent with findings in other studies. For instance, Jiru and Wegari 2022 observed that soil bunds, including stone bunds, improve soil carbon stock by reducing erosion and increasing water retention, which can lead to better organic carbon accumulation in the soil. The finding that exclosures have a positive effect on soil carbon aligns with broader literature in Ethiopia, where exclosures have been shown to restore degraded lands and significantly improve soil fertility, including carbon stocks. The exclusion of grazing allows natural vegetation to recover, enhancing soil structure and carbon storage capacity. The negative effect observed here contrasts with the general expectation that Fanya juu improves soil organic carbon due to its role in water conservation and erosion control. However, this result might be context-dependent, with variations in effectiveness based on the local environment, soil type, and how the practice is implemented. Some studies suggest that while Fanya juu works well in controlling erosion, it may not always result in significant increases in soil carbon stocks if the practices are not adequately managed or if there is insufficient organic matter to begin with. The positive effect of minimum tillage on soil carbon is well-documented in Ethiopian agriculture. By minimizing soil disturbance, this practice helps preserve organic matter, leading to better carbon storage in the soil. This finding aligns with broader agricultural practices that emphasize the importance of reducing tillage for sustainable soil management.

Fig. 14 presents the relative effect of CSA practices related to land and soil management (Fig. 14a) as well as water management (Fig. 14b) practices. Fig. 14 (a), shows the effect size of various indicators of CSA pillars across the various CSA practices.
All CSA practices, except for soil bunds, showed a positive effect on crop yield. This suggests that CSA practices typically lead to higher agricultural productivity. The increase in yield could be due to better soil fertility, improved water retention, and effective erosion control, which all contribute to more favorable growing conditions for crops. The finding that soil bunds did not improve crop yield or reduce soil loss as effectively as other CSA practices is interesting. While soil bunds are generally designed to control erosion and water runoff, their effectiveness can vary depending on factors like bund design, slope, and soil type. Studies elsewhere in Ethiopia suggest that when properly maintained, soil bunds can significantly reduce erosion and runoff. However, in some cases, if bunds are not properly constructed or maintained, they may not have the expected positive effects on soil retention or water management.
Most CSA practices resulted in a negative effect on soil loss, meaning they effectively reduced erosion. Soil conservation practices, such as stone bunds, minimum tillage, and grass strips, are likely contributing to this outcome by reducing surface runoff and stabilizing the soil. However, soil bunds did not show the same level of effectiveness in reducing soil loss, which may be due to variations in design, maintenance, or local environmental conditions. Many CSA practices focusing on water management (e.g., water harvesting, contour bunding, and diversion ditches) have been shown to reduce runoff and improve soil moisture retention, which can enhance crop yield and reduce soil erosion. These findings align with the general understanding that integrated water management practices are critical for improving productivity and adapting to climate change in Ethiopia, where drought and irregular rainfall patterns are common.
CSA practices also had a generally positive impact on reducing runoff (negative effect size). Techniques such as stone bunds, grass strips, and water harvesting methods are designed to manage water flow and prevent excessive runoff, which can carry away fertile topsoil and nutrients. The decrease in runoff is a critical adaptation strategy, as it helps improve water availability for crops, especially in areas prone to drought.
A positive effect on soil organic matter accumulation was observed across most CSA practices, except for soil bunds. This suggests that CSA practices are contributing to increased soil carbon sequestration, which is essential for long-term soil fertility and climate change mitigation. Practices like minimum tillage, exclosures, and stone bunds may help build up organic matter in the soil, improving both soil health and carbon storage. The positive impact of CSA practices on soil organic matter accumulation is consistent with findings from various studies in Ethiopia. Practices like minimum tillage, agroforestry, and exclosure have been shown to increase soil organic carbon content by reducing soil disturbance and enhancing plant cover, both of which help sequester carbon in the soil.

This indicates that drip irrigation increased crop yield, improved water productivity, and reduced the amount of irrigation water needed compared to conventional systems like furrow and flood irrigation. However, the net income from using drip irrigation is less favorable for smallholder farmers due to the high costs associated with drip irrigation infrastructure, such as drip lines, emitters, filters, and timers. This demonstrates a trade-off between higher water savings and increased costs, impacting overall income. This means, the use of drip irrigation increased crop yield, water productivity and saved irrigation water as compared to conventional irrigation systems such as furrow and flood irrigation methods. Conclusion
This study uses a meta-analysis approach based on 220 publications to examine the effects of various climate-smart agricultural (CSA) practices on productivity, adaptation, and mitigation indicators. The objective was to assess the impact of widely used CSA practices on these indicators across different agro-ecological zones in Ethiopia. The analysis revealed varying effects across productivity, adaptation, and mitigation indicators. Most CSA practices, such as animal feed management, compost, crop residue mulching, and drought-tolerant crops, significantly improved crop yield, while practices like stone bunds and deficit irrigation had negative effects on productivity. In terms of adaptation, CSA practices effectively reduced runoff and soil erosion, with crop residue mulching, stone bunds, and exclosures showing the largest reductions. Water-saving techniques, including deficit and drip irrigation, demonstrated high water-use efficiency but faced productivity trade-offs due to reduced water application. Mitigation benefits included enhanced soil organic matter and carbon stock, particularly with practices like farmyard manure, minimum tillage, and exclosures, although graded Fanya juu showed minimal impact on soil carbon. These results highlight the context-dependent nature of CSA effectiveness and the need for tailored strategies to optimize their benefits across diverse Ethiopian agroecosystems. This indicates that adopting various CSA practices is crucial for enhancing productivity in Ethiopia's major agro-ecological zones. Similarly, almost all of the CSA practices studied, reduced runoff and soil loss (the highest effect being from crop residue mulching with an effect size of 2.95), implying that they are effective in adaptation strategies to soil erosion. Furthermore, the meta-analysis demonstrated that various CSA practices improved soil organic matter and soil carbon stock. Enhancing access to drip irrigation to smallholder farmers in Ethiopia can have a high potential to save irrigation water, increase yield and improved water productivity as compared to conventional irrigation methods such as flood irrigation. However, the income from drip irrigation was not attractive to farmers due to costs related to drip lines, emitters, filters, and timer. Hence, incentive mechanisms such as access to tax-free irrigation equipment can enhance farmers' benefit from irrigation. Moreover, introducing water pricing can incentivize smallholder farmers to adopt drip irrigation. Similarly, deficit irrigation improved water productivity but reduced crop yield as the expense of low irrigation water was applied. This implies that there is a trade-off between water saving and yield reduction of deficit irrigation.
