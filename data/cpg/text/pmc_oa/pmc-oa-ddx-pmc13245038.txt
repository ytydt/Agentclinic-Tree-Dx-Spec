Integrated use of natriuretic peptides and DASI for cardiac reserve evaluation in high-risk surgical patients: a narrative review
Background
The perioperative management of high-risk patients undergoing major noncardiac surgery remains a significant challenge in modern healthcare. Accurate preoperative risk stratification is crucial for optimizing patient outcomes and resource allocation. This narrative review explores the emerging approach of combining natriuretic peptide measurements with the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) to evaluate cardiac reserve in high-risk surgical patients.
Main body
We examine the individual roles of natriuretic peptides (primarily B-type natriuretic peptide and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide) and the DASI in preoperative risk assessment, highlighting their strengths and limitations. The rationale for integrating these two measures is discussed, emphasizing their complementary nature in providing both objective biomarker data and patient-reported functional status. The review synthesizes current evidence supporting this combined approach, including recent studies demonstrating improved risk discrimination compared to traditional methods. We outline a methodology for implementing this integrated assessment, addressing the timing of measurements, interpretation of results, and integration with existing risk stratification tools. Furthermore, we provide guidance on the management of identified high-risk patients, including prehabilitation and optimization strategies. The natriuretic peptide/DASI approach is also positioned within the broader context of perioperative assessment to guide appropriate clinical implementation.
Conclusion
The integration of natriuretic peptides and DASI represents a promising strategy for enhancing preoperative cardiac risk assessment in high-risk surgical patients. While further research is needed to refine and validate this approach, it offers a practical and potentially powerful tool for improving perioperative care and patient outcomes.

Introduction
The perioperative management of high-risk patients undergoing major surgery remains a significant challenge in modern healthcare. As surgical techniques and anesthetic management have advanced, an increasing number of patients with complex comorbidities are being considered for invasive procedures. This trend has heightened the importance of accurate preoperative risk stratification, particularly for cardiovascular complications, which remain a leading cause of perioperative morbidity and mortality. Cardiac reserve, defined as the heart’s ability to respond to increased physiological demands, is a critical factor in determining a patient’s capacity to withstand the stress of surgery. Traditional methods of assessing cardiac reserve, such as clinical risk indices and functional capacity estimation, have limitations in their predictive accuracy, especially in high-risk populations. This has led to a growing interest in more objective and comprehensive approaches to evaluate cardiac function and risk in the perioperative setting. In recent years, the combination of biomarkers and functional assessments has emerged as a promising strategy for enhancing preoperative risk stratification. Natriuretic peptides, particularly B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), have gained significant attention as powerful predictors of adverse cardiac events in both cardiac and noncardiac surgery. These biomarkers provide valuable information about cardiac stress and dysfunction, offering insights beyond traditional clinical assessment. Complementing the objective data provided by natriuretic peptides, the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) has proven to be a valuable tool for assessing functional capacity. This self-administered questionnaire correlates well with peak oxygen uptake during exercise testing and has been shown to predict postoperative outcomes in various surgical populations. The DASI offers a practical and cost-effective method for estimating functional capacity, particularly in settings where formal exercise testing may not be feasible or readily available. The integration of natriuretic peptide measurements with DASI scores represents a novel approach to evaluating cardiac reserve in high-risk surgical patients. This combination has the potential to provide a more comprehensive assessment of cardiovascular risk by incorporating both objective biomarker data and patient-reported functional status. Early studies suggest that this combined approach may offer superior predictive value compared to either method alone, particularly in identifying patients at highest risk for perioperative cardiac complications. This narrative review aims to explore the current evidence supporting the use of natriuretic peptides in conjunction with the DASI questionnaire for evaluating cardiac reserve in high-risk patients undergoing major surgery. We will examine the physiological basis for these assessments, their individual and combined predictive value, and their potential impact on perioperative management strategies. Additionally, we will discuss the practical considerations for implementing this approach in clinical practice, its limitations, and future directions for research in this rapidly evolving field.

Methods
This narrative review was conducted following a structured approach to literature identification and synthesis, though it did not employ a formal systematic review methodology. We performed a comprehensive literature search using PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases from inception to April 2025. Key search terms included combinations of “natriuretic peptides,” “BNP,” “NT-proBNP,” “Duke Activity Status Index,” “DASI,” “functional capacity,” “cardiac reserve,” “perioperative,” “noncardiac surgery,” and “risk assessment.” Additional relevant articles were identified through manual review of reference lists from key publications and review articles. We prioritized randomized controlled trials, prospective observational studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and practice guidelines published in English. Study selection focused on articles examining the predictive value of natriuretic peptides and/or DASI for perioperative outcomes, with particular emphasis on investigations combining both assessment methods. The narrative synthesis was organized thematically to address the physiological basis, evidence base, implementation considerations, and future directions of the integrated approach. Due to the heterogeneity of study designs and outcomes, a qualitative rather than quantitative synthesis was performed. The evidence quality and recommendations were evaluated according to the strength of supporting data, though formal grading was not applied.

High-risk patients and high-risk surgery
The concept of “high-risk” in perioperative medicine encompasses both patient-related factors and the nature of the surgical procedure itself. Understanding and accurately identifying high-risk patients undergoing high-risk surgeries is crucial for optimizing perioperative care and improving outcomes.

High-risk patients
Defining high-risk patients in the perioperative context has been a subject of ongoing research and debate. Generally, high-risk patients are those with a significantly elevated probability of adverse postoperative outcomes, including major morbidity and mortality. Various factors contribute to this increased risk, including advanced age, multiple comorbidities, poor functional status, and the presence of specific cardiac conditions.

Revised cardiac risk index
One widely used tool for identifying high-risk patients is the Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI), which considers six independent predictors of major cardiac complications: high-risk surgery, history of ischemic heart disease, history of congestive heart failure, history of cerebrovascular disease, preoperative treatment with insulin, and preoperative serum creatinine > 2.0 mg/dL. Patients with two or more of these factors are typically considered high risk.

American college of surgeons national surgical quality improvement program
However, the definition of high-risk extends beyond cardiac considerations. The American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) has developed risk calculators that incorporate a broader range of factors, including pulmonary and renal function, to predict the risk of various postoperative complications. These tools provide a more comprehensive assessment of perioperative risk but may be more complex to implement in routine clinical practice.

Frailty
Age is a significant factor in defining high-risk patients. With the aging population, an increasing number of elderly patients are undergoing surgery. Advanced age is associated with physiological changes that can impair the ability to cope with surgical stress, including reduced cardiovascular reserve, decreased renal function, and altered drug metabolism. However, it is important to note that chronological age alone is an imperfect predictor of risk, and functional status often provides more valuable information. Frailty has emerged as an important concept in identifying high-risk surgical patients. Defined as a state of increased vulnerability to stressors, frailty is associated with adverse postoperative outcomes independent of age and comorbidities. Various frailty assessment tools have been developed, such as the Clinical Frailty Scale and the Fried frailty phenotype, which can help identify patients at increased risk of complications.

High-risk surgery
Turning to the surgical aspect, high-risk procedures are generally defined as those associated with a high probability of adverse outcomes or significant physiological stress. The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the European Society of Anesthesiology (ESA) have classified surgical procedures into low-risk, intermediate-risk, and high-risk categories based on the 30-day risk of cardiac events. High-risk surgeries typically include major vascular procedures (such as open aortic surgery and major vascular surgery), major thoracic surgery, major abdominal surgery, and some orthopedic procedures like hip fracture repair. These surgeries are characterized by factors such as prolonged operative time, significant fluid shifts, potential for major blood loss, and marked changes in cardiovascular dynamics. The physiological impact of high-risk surgery extends beyond the immediate operative period. The surgical stress response, characterized by neuroendocrine, metabolic, and inflammatory changes, can persist for days to weeks postoperatively. This prolonged stress state can unmask subclinical organ dysfunction and contribute to postoperative complications, particularly in high-risk patients with limited physiological reserve. The combination of high-risk patients and high-risk surgeries presents unique challenges in perioperative management. These patients often have complex medical histories and are at risk for a wide range of complications, including cardiac events, pulmonary complications, acute kidney injury, and delirium.

Patient management
The interplay between patient factors and surgical stress necessitates a comprehensive approach to risk assessment and management. Preoperative optimization becomes particularly crucial in this population. Strategies may include medication adjustment, prehabilitation programs to improve functional capacity, and targeted interventions for specific comorbidities. However, the optimal timing and extent of preoperative interventions remain areas of active research and debate. Intraoperative management of high-risk patients undergoing high-risk surgery requires careful attention to hemodynamics, fluid management, and organ protection strategies. Advanced monitoring techniques, such as goal-directed hemodynamic therapy, have shown promise in improving outcomes in this population. However, the heterogeneity of both patients and procedures makes standardized approaches challenging. Postoperative care for high-risk patients after high-risk surgery often necessitates a higher level of monitoring and intervention. Many of these patients require intensive care unit admission, at least for the immediate postoperative period. Early recognition and management of complications are crucial, as high-risk patients may have limited reserves to cope with additional physiological stressors.

Natriuretic peptides
Natriuretic peptides have emerged as valuable biomarkers in the assessment of cardiac function and risk stratification across various clinical settings, including the perioperative period. This family of peptide hormones plays a crucial role in cardiovascular homeostasis, with BNP and NT-proBNP being the most clinically relevant in perioperative risk assessment. BNP is primarily synthesized and secreted by ventricular cardiomyocytes in response to increased wall stress, volume overload, or ischemia. Upon release, BNP is cleaved into the biologically active BNP and the inactive NT-proBNP. Both forms can be measured in the bloodstream and serve as indicators of cardiac stress and dysfunction. The physiological actions of BNP include natriuresis, vasodilation, and inhibition of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. These effects collectively act to reduce cardiac preload and afterload, thereby alleviating myocardial wall stress. The plasma half-life of BNP is approximately 20 min, while NT-proBNP has a longer half-life of 60–120 min, making the latter potentially more stable for clinical measurements. In the context of perioperative risk assessment, natriuretic peptides have demonstrated significant prognostic value. Numerous studies have shown that elevated preoperative levels of BNP or NT-proBNP are associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiac events and mortality following noncardiac surgery. A landmark meta-analysis by Rodseth et al. involving individual patient data from 2179 patients undergoing noncardiac surgery found that preoperative BNP and NT-proBNP measurements significantly improved risk prediction beyond clinical risk factors alone. The predictive power of natriuretic peptides extends across various surgical populations and types of procedures. For instance, in vascular surgery patients, who are generally at higher cardiac risk, preoperative BNP levels have been shown to independently predict postoperative cardiac events and long-term mortality. Similarly, in patients undergoing major orthopedic procedures, elevated NT-proBNP levels were associated with increased risk of perioperative myocardial injury and 30-day mortality. The optimal timing for natriuretic peptide measurement in the perioperative setting has been a subject of investigation. While most studies have focused on preoperative values, some research suggests that postoperative measurements or the change in levels from pre- to post-surgery may provide additional prognostic information. A study by Cuthbertson et al. demonstrated that both pre- and postoperative NT-proBNP levels were independent predictors of cardiac events and mortality at 6 months following major noncardiac surgery. Despite their proven utility, the interpretation of natriuretic peptide levels requires consideration of various factors that can influence their concentrations. Age, renal function, body mass index, and the presence of comorbidities such as heart failure or atrial fibrillation can all affect baseline levels. Moreover, the optimal cut-off values for risk stratification may vary depending on the specific surgical population and the chosen outcome measure. Several professional societies have recognized the value of natriuretic peptides in perioperative risk assessment. The Canadian Cardiovascular Society guidelines recommend measuring NT-proBNP or BNP before noncardiac surgery to enhance perioperative cardiac risk estimation. Similarly, the European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care (ESAIC) guidelines suggest that these biomarkers may be considered for perioperative risk stratification in high-risk patients. The integration of natriuretic peptide measurements into clinical practice offers several potential benefits. These biomarkers can help identify patients who may benefit from further cardiac evaluation or optimization before surgery. Additionally, they may guide decisions regarding the level of postoperative monitoring or the need for specific management strategies to mitigate cardiac risk.

Duke Activity Status Index (DASI)
The DASI has emerged as a valuable tool in the preoperative assessment of patients undergoing noncardiac surgery. Developed by Hlatky et al. in 1989, the DASI is a self-administered questionnaire designed to evaluate functional capacity based on the patient’s ability to perform various daily activities. Since its inception, the DASI has gained recognition for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and correlation with more sophisticated measures of cardiopulmonary fitness. The DASI consists of 12 questions that assess a patient’s ability to perform activities of increasing metabolic demand, ranging from personal care to strenuous sports. Each item is weighted based on its known metabolic cost, and the sum of weights for positive responses provides a DASI score. This score can be used to estimate peak oxygen uptake (VO2 peak), a key indicator of cardiorespiratory fitness, using a validated formula. The relationship between DASI scores and objective measures of functional capacity has been extensively studied. In their original work, Hlatky et al. demonstrated a strong correlation (r = 0.80) between DASI scores and peak oxygen uptake as measured by cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET). Subsequent studies have confirmed this association across various patient populations, including those with cardiovascular disease and candidates for major surgery. In the context of preoperative risk assessment, the DASI offers several advantages over traditional methods of evaluating functional capacity. Unlike clinician-estimated metabolic equivalents (METs), which have been shown to have poor accuracy and reliability, the DASI provides a standardized, patient-reported measure of functional status. Moreover, the DASI can be easily administered in outpatient settings without the need for specialized equipment or personnel, making it particularly suitable for widespread use in preoperative clinics. The prognostic value of the DASI in predicting postoperative outcomes has been demonstrated in multiple studies. Reilly et al. found that lower DASI scores were associated with increased risk of cardiac complications following major noncardiac surgery. In a large prospective cohort study, Wijeysundera et al. showed that the DASI was an independent predictor of myocardial injury, myocardial infarction, and death within 30 days of noncardiac surgery. Furthermore, the DASI has shown promise in identifying patients who may benefit from additional preoperative testing or optimization. A study by Goswami et al. demonstrated that patients with low DASI scores were more likely to have abnormal findings on dobutamine stress echocardiography, suggesting that the DASI could help guide selective use of advanced cardiac testing. The integration of the DASI into existing risk stratification models has been an area of active research. Wijeysundera et al. found that incorporating the DASI into the Revised Cardiac Risk Index significantly improved its predictive performance for major cardiac complications. This finding underscores the potential of the DASI to enhance current approaches to preoperative risk assessment. Despite its many advantages, the DASI is not without limitations. As a subjective measure, it is susceptible to reporting biases and may be influenced by factors such as depression, cognitive impairment, or cultural differences in activity patterns. Additionally, the DASI may have ceiling effects in highly functional patients, potentially limiting its discriminative ability in this population. The optimal timing for DASI administration in the preoperative period has not been definitively established. While most studies have used DASI scores obtained during preoperative evaluation visits, some researchers have explored the value of serial DASI assessments. Shulman et al. found that changes in DASI scores over time were associated with postoperative outcomes, suggesting that tracking functional capacity in the lead-up to surgery may provide additional prognostic information. In recent years, there has been growing interest in combining the DASI with other preoperative assessment tools to improve risk stratification. The METS study, a large international prospective cohort study, examined the integration of DASI scores with biomarkers and other clinical variables for predicting postoperative outcomes. This approach holds promise for developing more comprehensive and accurate risk prediction models.

The integration of biomarkers and functional capacity assessments represents a promising approach to enhancing perioperative risk stratification, particularly for high-risk patients undergoing major surgery. The primary rationale for this integrated approach is that natriuretic peptides and DASI provide complementary rather than redundant information. While natriuretic peptides objectively quantify cardiac stress and subclinical dysfunction, they cannot assess a patient’s functional capacity and physiological reserve. Conversely, the DASI evaluates functional status but may miss underlying cardiac pathology. By combining these measures, clinicians can identify critical discordance patterns—patients with elevated natriuretic peptides but preserved functional capacity or those with poor functional status despite normal biomarker levels—that significantly influence risk stratification and management decisions. Evidence supporting this combined approach has emerged from several key studies. In the METS study, Wijeysundera et al. demonstrated that preoperative NT-proBNP measurements and DASI scores were independent predictors of myocardial injury and death within 30 days of noncardiac surgery, with their combination providing superior risk discrimination compared to either measure alone. Similarly, in patients undergoing vascular surgery, integrating BNP measurements with functional capacity assessment significantly improved risk stratification compared to established risk indices. Methodology for cardiac reserve evaluation
The assessment of cardiac reserve in high-risk patients undergoing major surgery requires a systematic and evidence-based approach.
Natriuretic peptide thresholds: For BNP, use 92 pg/mL as the cut-off value. For NT-proBNP, apply age-specific thresholds: ≥ 300 pg/mL for patients < 75 years and ≥ 450 pg/mL for those ≥ 75 years. These values have been validated in multiple perioperative studies.
DASI interpretation: A DASI score < 34 (corresponding to estimated VO₂ peak < 14 mL/kg/min) identifies patients with limited functional capacity at increased risk for complications. A score > 34 generally indicates adequate functional reserve.
For optimal implementation, a specific timing protocol based on current evidence is essential. The DASI questionnaire should be administered at the initial preoperative evaluation clinic visit, typically 2–4 weeks before surgery. This allows sufficient time for additional testing or optimization if indicated by the results. Natriuretic peptide measurement should be obtained within 7 days of the scheduled procedure, as this timing provides the strongest association with postoperative cardiovascular complications. For high-risk procedures or patients with significant comorbidities, consider serial DASI assessments, as changes in functional capacity over time may provide additional prognostic information. This two-stage approach balances early risk identification with accurate pre-surgical assessment. For standardized implementation, we propose the following specific interpretation thresholds based on current evidence:
Low risk: Normal natriuretic peptides AND DASI score > 34
Intermediate risk (discordant): Either elevated natriuretic peptides OR DASI score < 34
High risk: Elevated natriuretic peptides AND DASI score < 34

Systematic management to optimize patients
Once high-risk patients have been identified using the integrated natriuretic peptide/DASI approach, a systematic management strategy should be implemented to optimize outcomes.
The identification of elevated cardiac risk through this combined assessment provides an opportunity for targeted interventions prior to surgery. Patients with abnormalities in both biomarkers and functional capacity (high-risk category) generally require comprehensive optimization, while those with discordant results may benefit from interventions targeting their specific deficiency. This risk-stratified approach allows for more efficient resource allocation and personalized perioperative care, potentially reducing postoperative complications and mortality in vulnerable population.

Prehabilitation
Prehabilitation represents a cornerstone intervention for high-risk patients, particularly those with impaired functional capacity identified by low DASI scores. Current evidence supports multimodal prehabilitation programs initiated 2–4 weeks before surgery to improve physiological reserve and functional capacity. These programs typically include supervised aerobic exercise (3–5 sessions weekly at moderate intensity), resistance training targeting major muscle groups, respiratory muscle training, and nutritional optimization. For patients with elevated natriuretic peptides but preserved functional capacity, prehabilitation may include lower-intensity exercise combined with careful monitoring. A systematic review by Minnella and Carli demonstrated that structured prehabilitation programs can improve functional capacity, reduce postoperative complications by 30–40%, and shorten hospital length of stay in high-risk surgical populations. The implementation of prehabilitation requires coordination between surgical teams, anesthesiologists, physical therapists, and nutritionists, with protocols tailored to individual patient capabilities and surgical timelines.

Medication adjustments
Additional optimization strategies should address modifiable risk factors identified through the comprehensive assessment. Medical optimization often includes medication adjustments such as initiation or titration of beta-blockers in patients with elevated heart rates and known coronary artery disease, optimization of antihypertensive regimens, and management of volume status in patients with heart failure. Statin therapy should be considered for patients with atherosclerotic disease or multiple risk factors.

Multidisciplinary evaluation
For patients with significantly elevated natriuretic peptides, cardiology consultation may provide valuable guidance regarding the need for further cardiac testing or interventions. Geriatric consultation can address frailty and polypharmacy in elderly high-risk patients. In some cases, the risk assessment may prompt reconsideration of the surgical approach, with less invasive techniques or staged procedures being preferred for patients at highest risk. Optimization of chronic conditions such as diabetes, anemia, and renal dysfunction should also be prioritized based on individual patient profiles. This multidisciplinary approach to optimizing high-risk patients should be tailored to institutional resources and patient characteristics. The integration of preoperative assessment and optimization pathways into existing perioperative medicine services can facilitate systematic implementation of these strategies.
Ultimately, the goal is to improve the patient’s physiological reserve before surgery, potentially converting a high-risk patient to an intermediate or even lower-risk category through targeted interventions guided by the specific abnormalities identified through the natriuretic peptide/DASI assessment.

Future directions
The field of perioperative risk assessment is rapidly evolving, with ongoing research focusing on refining existing methodologies and exploring novel approaches to improve risk stratification and patient outcomes.
One of the most promising areas of research is the development and validation of integrated risk prediction models that incorporate multiple biomarkers, functional assessments, and clinical variables. The VISION study, a large international prospective cohort study, is investigating the combined use of high-sensitivity troponin measurements, natriuretic peptides, and functional capacity assessments in noncardiac surgery patients. This study aims to develop a comprehensive risk prediction model that could potentially improve upon existing tools and guide personalized perioperative management strategies.
Another area of active investigation is the exploration of novel biomarkers that could complement or enhance the prognostic value of natriuretic peptides. For example, emerging evidence suggests that growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15) may provide additional information about cardiovascular risk in various clinical settings, including the perioperative period. Future studies may examine whether the addition of GDF-15 or other novel biomarkers to the natriuretic peptide/DASI combination could further improve risk stratification.
The potential role of genomics and other “-omics” technologies in perioperative risk assessment is an exciting frontier. Advances in genomic medicine have led to the identification of genetic variants associated with increased cardiovascular risk and altered drug responses. Integration of genetic information with traditional risk factors, biomarkers, and functional assessments could potentially lead to more precise and personalized risk prediction models. However, significant challenges remain in translating genomic discoveries into clinically useful tools for perioperative care.
Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning techniques are increasingly being applied to perioperative risk prediction. These approaches have the potential to identify complex patterns and interactions among multiple risk factors that may not be apparent through traditional statistical methods. Future research may explore the use of AI algorithms to optimize the integration of natriuretic peptide measurements, DASI scores, and other clinical variables for risk stratification.
Wearable technology and remote monitoring devices offer new possibilities for assessing functional capacity and cardiac function in the preoperative period. These technologies could potentially provide more continuous and real-world assessments of physical activity and cardiovascular parameters, complementing traditional measures like the DASI. Research into the integration of data from wearable devices with biomarker measurements and clinical risk factors may lead to more dynamic and comprehensive risk assessment tools.
As the population ages and medical advances allow for more complex surgeries in higher-risk patients, there is a growing need for risk assessment tools specifically tailored to geriatric populations. Future studies may focus on validating and refining the natriuretic peptide/DASI approach in older adults, taking into account age-related changes in physiology and the prevalence of multimorbidity. The concept of shared decision-making in perioperative care is gaining prominence, emphasizing the importance of patient preferences and values in treatment decisions. Future research may explore how best to communicate the results of natriuretic peptide measurements and DASI assessments to patients and incorporate this information into shared decision-making processes regarding surgery and perioperative management. Standardization and implementation of best practices in perioperative risk assessment remain ongoing challenges. Future initiatives may focus on developing consensus guidelines for the optimal use of natriuretic peptides and functional capacity assessments in different surgical populations and healthcare settings. This could include recommendations for the timing of assessments, cut-off values, and integration with other risk stratification tools. Long-term outcomes beyond the immediate perioperative period are increasingly recognized as important endpoints in surgical research. Future studies may examine whether the combination of natriuretic peptides and DASI can predict longer-term outcomes such as functional status, quality of life, and survival at 6–12 months post-surgery. Finally, as healthcare systems worldwide grapple with resource constraints, future research will need to address the cost-effectiveness of various approaches to perioperative risk assessment. Economic analyses comparing the natriuretic peptide/DASI combination with other assessment strategies will be crucial in informing policy decisions and clinical practice guidelines.

Conclusions
The combination of natriuretic peptides and the DASI represents a promising approach to evaluating cardiac reserve in high-risk patients undergoing major surgery, offering a balance between comprehensive assessment and practical implementation. This integrated method provides valuable insights into both cardiac function and overall functional capacity, potentially enhancing risk stratification and guiding perioperative management decisions. While further research is needed to refine cut-off values, optimize timing of assessments, and validate this approach in diverse surgical populations, the current evidence suggests that this combination can significantly improve upon traditional risk assessment tools. As perioperative medicine continues to evolve towards more personalized and precise risk prediction, the integration of biomarkers and functional assessments is likely to play an increasingly important role in clinical practice. Ultimately, the goal of this approach is to improve patient outcomes by identifying those at highest risk for complications, guiding targeted interventions, and informing shared decision-making between clinicians and patients regarding the risks and benefits of surgery.
