Monitoring and evaluation instruments of individual health in community settings: a scoping review
Background
Individual health monitoring and evaluation instruments help reflect specific health status, enhance people’s acceptance and adherence to treatments, and reduce disease burdens. However, measurement instruments for individual health have not been adequately explored in community settings. This study is to scope the existing evidence with two aims: (1) to identify the individual health measurement instruments for the general population, (2) to synthesize thematic categories that are used to construct the instruments and the way that these instruments are combined in actual usage.
Methods
This scoping review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute methodological framework and adhered to the PRISMA-ScR reporting guideline. Seven electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, CNKI, and WanFang) were searched from their inception to 15 November 2025. Studies were included if they involved adults from the general population, applied individual health monitoring or evaluation instruments in community or home-based settings, and were peer-reviewed primary or secondary research published in English or Chinese. Studies targeting disease-specific populations or clinical therapeutic interventions were excluded. A double screening process of titles, abstracts, and full text was applied.
Results
The search initially identified 51,269 records, of which 257 studies met the eligibility criteria, yielding 310 individual health measurement instruments. Among these instruments, 53 were related to Health / Quality of Life, 158 focused on Physical Health, 47 on Mental Health, 21 on Health Behaviors, 16 on Healthcare Environment, and 15 on Social Health. The instruments were further categorized as Generic instruments, Mixed instruments, Self-developed instruments, and Specific Indicators, according to how they were used in the studies to generate the final health outcomes.
Conclusions
This scoping review maps individual-level health measurement instruments used in community settings, synthesizing six health domains and multiple instrument types. These findings highlight priorities for developing more balanced and policy-relevant systems for individual health monitoring. A shift from generic to mixed instruments is found, with physical indicators dominating while mental, behavioral, environmental, and social dimensions remain underrepresented. Emerging digital data offer opportunities for more continuous assessment, but challenges in interoperability, privacy, and equity need to be addressed before further progress can be made.

Background
There were 18 health-related indicators proposed in the Millennium Development Goals for maintaining sustainable development and eliminating poverty. Consequently, to promote the accessibility and standardize the measurement of these indicators, the World Health Organization published a global reference list in 2018. The list then served as a guide for countries to monitor health according to their health priorities and capacity, and to report the outcomes in a harmonized way. However, the indicators in the list are all for public health metrics that investigate health problems and hazards at the population level, such as life expectancy, mortality rate by different causes, birth rate, and morbidity of common diseases. Therefore, those indicators are unsuitable for assessing the individual health status. With the emergence of precision medicine, healthcare practitioners increasingly break through traditional healthcare models and utilize artificial intelligence technology to investigate health status in a more precise manner, reducing healthcare costs without compromising quality or outcomes. To such an extent, the identification of accurate, objective, and convenient personalized health indicators, along with real-time health assessments, has become a new demand.
The definition and operationalization of health have become increasingly important, as they are the foundation of reliable individual indicators, healthcare services, and research. Health is a complex concept that consists of what is happening within the body and the impacts of biological dysfunction on the individual’s social ability, including a wide range of dimensions such as risk factors, disease, impairment, and disability. The World Health Organization constitution states: “Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity”. However, complete health in this definition is utopian, which makes it unachievable for most people and hard to be measured by practitioners. As a result, many health and medicine debates were conducted and alternative concepts of health were formulated. For example, the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health, focusing on both performance and capacities, took a broader set of aspects of health into account: body functions, activity and participation, environmental and personal factors, and body structures. Huber et al. (2011) introduced positive health, which reframes health as “the ability to adapt and self-manage”, highlighting six domains: bodily functions, mental functions and perception, spiritual/existential well-being, quality of life, social and societal participation, and daily functioning. More recently, the “Whole Person Health” model further advocates a holistic approach that integrates physical, emotional, social, functional, and environmental dimensions. Together, these evolving concepts demonstrate a shift toward multidimensional interpretations of health and have begun to shape national health policies. For instance, the Netherlands has incorporated Positive Health into community and primary care practices, and Iceland has adopted a national well-being framework that conceptualizes health as a holistic construct, encompassing subjective well-being, objective conditions, and the capability to function well as individuals and communities.
As the operational definition of health needs further clarification, the terms “health”, “health-related quality of life (HRQoL)” and “quality of life” are often used interchangeably, and many researchers use patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) that capture individuals’ perceptions of their functioning and well-being as proxies for health status in community studies. There are several widely acknowledged PROMs to measure individual health. Self-rated health (SRH), typically assessed with a single-item question such as “In general, would you say your health is …”, is widely used as a reliable indicator of individual health. Apart from this, the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36), the EuroQOL Five-Dimension Questionnaire (EQ-5D), the Quality of Life Scale (QOLS), and the Health Utilities Index (HUI) system, as well as their various versions, are also widely used and validated in different populations. However, the subjective indicators and the objective ones may reflect inconsistent images of the health situations as each individual may have different interpretations of health. Meanwhile, many countries have established standardized reporting metrics and methods in the electronic health records and electronic medical records to utilize patient data effectively, which are usually objective.
There have been some attempts to integrate objective metrics into an individual health index. Sokoya et al. (2022) identified 29 indicators that could represent individual health through a literature review and conducted a pilot study with them. Hanson’s team (2021) evaluated health and well-being in accordance with the structure of medical databases and selected four dimensions: safety, health, health behaviors, and well-being. Yap et al. (2020) defined health from five different domains: physical, mental, social, risk, and healthcare utilization, and developed a scalable and extendable multi-dimensional health index based on individual data. Some researchers also attempted to utilize medical big data to perform real-time, objective evaluation of the individual health status.
A well-designed health measurement and evaluation (M&E) instrument should provide a comprehensive vision of health, as well as matching the aim of the research, so as to ensure the feasibility of the research and the validity of the results. Although the use of individual health M&E instruments has expanded rapidly in community settings, the existing evidence base remains highly fragmented. Instruments vary widely in their conceptual foundations, domains covered, and practical feasibility, making it difficult for researchers and practitioners to select appropriate tools for community health monitoring. A clearer understanding of what has been used, how health has been operationalized, and where gaps remain is essential for improving the consistency, comparability, and usefulness of community-level health assessments. Therefore, this scoping review aims to (1) identify and describe existing M&E instruments of individual health of the general population, including separate indicators and multidimensional indices, and (2) analyze how these instruments are conceptually organized and applied, with particular attention to the domains they measure and the ways in which multiple instruments are combined to produce overall health assessments.

Methods

Overview
The methodology of this scoping review was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute Reviewer Manual and reporting of the findings follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist. It was performed according to a preregistered protocol published in the Open Science Framework on 4 October 2023 (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/4PS5C).

Conceptual definition
A M&E instrument on health or quality of life is defined as any structured tool, such as a questionnaire, index, scale, or composite scoring system, that quantitatively reports on specific health-related variables or constructs, including but not limited to symptoms, functional status, and overall quality of life, based on self-report or other measurement modalities. Instruments that produce a composite score must clearly define their constituent domains or variables in the original study.

Eligibility criteria
According to the PPC (Population, Phenomenon of interest, Context) framework, we defined the inclusion and exclusion criteria for this review.
Our inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Population: general population; (2) Phenomenon of interest: All forms of M&E instruments on health or quality of life; (3) Context: Health monitoring and evaluation in the community or home-based settings. (4) No restriction is placed on publication year or study design.
Our exclusion criteria are: (1) Population: all individuals are adolescents or subject to specific conditions such as chronic diseases or disabilities; (2) Phenomenon: clinical therapeutic interventions; (3) Contexts: restricted to hospital, specialist clinical, or other treatment-focused environments; (4) Publications not written in Chinese or English. (5) Non-peer-reviewed publications.

Search strategy
The research team, including a bioinformatics expert, discussed together to generate a preliminary search strategy, and a research librarian with expertise in systematic and scoping reviews was consulted during the whole process of developing the search strategy. First, a preliminary search was conducted in PubMed, based on the terminology of health and quality of life screening. Second, we improved the search strategy by extracting the key terms from the highly related articles, and conducted a hypernym search to ensure a comprehensive inclusion of articles. Third, we specified the search strategy for each database (supplementary material 1). Totally, seven databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, CNKI, and WanFang. Articles published between the establishment of the databases and 18 September 2023 were collected, and an updated search was done on 15 November 2025. Finally, we conducted snowballing, including both backward and forward citation searching, to identify additional potentially relevant studies.
To ensure the rigor and completeness of the search strategy, we applied the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guideline during the development of the search strings. A member of the research team who was not involved in drafting the initial strategy conducted an independent PRESS-based review, focusing on the appropriateness of keywords, syntax, subject headings, and database-specific adaptations.

Study selection
All the retrieved records were placed into EndNote 21 and the duplicate records were removed. Initially, four researchers screened the titles and abstracts of the articles. Before the formal screening, four researchers worked in pairs and pre-screened eight articles to pilot the data charting form and assess the clarity of the variables, the consistency of interpretation, and the completeness of the items included, as recommended by the Covidence guidance. The research team then met to discuss discrepancies, refine the charting items, and standardize operational definitions. Next, two researchers screened by reading the full text. If there was any dispute, there would be a group discussion or the third researcher would decide. All four researchers participating in this procedure were trained in advance, and the screening process was dual-independent. Our research team will monitor the consistency and accuracy of the literature screening results.

Data extraction
A customized Excel form for data extraction was created by the first researcher and reviewed by the other authors. A unique identification code was assigned to each article for double-checking by a second researcher. The first researcher independently charted data from all eligible articles and the second researcher checked the accuracy.
The data chart contained information about the key study characteristics such as authors, year of publication, type of study, country, setting, population age, and the individualized health M&E instruments. During data charting, we also examined the structural and functional characteristics of the included instruments and grouped them inductively based on shared features such as indicator composition, validation status, and integration level. The included instruments were therefore categorized by their characteristics and the ways or scenarios that they were applied (referred to as “Combinations” hereafter). The publication trends of different combinations were then analyzed.

Results synthesis
In order to better understand, compare, and synthesize M&E instruments in individual health, we referred to the dimensionalization of health. This concept was mainly based on the World Health Organization’s definition of health that includes physical, psychological, and social aspects, and adjusted according to the latest articles about the concept of health. Building on this framework, two reviewers independently examined each instrument’s primary construct and application context, then assigned categorical labels to each instrument. The labels were compared and discrepancies were resolved through consensus, with a third reviewer available for adjudication when needed. After group discussions, the combinations and classification of instruments in each study were examined, compared, and synthesized according to the pyramid principle. Moreover, we visualized our results for better readability using R statistical software (version 4.2.3) with the ggplot2, networkD3, and rnaturalearth packages.

Results

Search results
A total of 51,258 records were retrieved through the research strategies in supplementary material 1, with additional 11 records identified from websites and citation searching. Among them, 1,734 duplicate studies were removed, and 48,594 records were removed by screening the titles and abstracts, leaving 931 records for further analysis. After full-text screening, 12 records were not available, and 673 records, including one from citation searching, were excluded for reasons outlined in Fig. 1. Finally, 257 articles were included for the analysis.

Characteristics of included studies
There are four forms of combinations that the measurement instruments are utilized: Generic instruments, Mixed instruments, Self-developed instruments, and Specific Indicators. As shown in Fig. 2, the first observational study using individual instruments to monitor health appeared in 1989. Before 2002, only a few studies examined residents’ health status and the influencing factors through scales or combined instruments. Later, however, such studies considerably increased. The majority of included studies are observational (n = 123, 48%) or secondary data analysis (n = 118, 46%). The rest of the studies are theoretical research (n = 5, 2%), cohort studies (n = 4, 2%), mixed-method research (n = 4, 2%), and reviews (n = 3, 1%). Figure 3 shows that the nations where the studies were conducted include China (n = 90, 30%), the United States (n = 42, 14%), the United Kingdom (n = 23, 8%), Canada (n = 20, 7%), and other countries (n = 121, 41%). The number of health M&E instruments in a single study ranged from 1 to 46. The list of the included studies and their details is in supplementary material 2.

Characteristics of individual health measurement instruments
As shown in Fig. 4, all of the 310 identified measurement instruments were assigned to six different health thematic categories: Health / Quality of Life and five Health Categories, based on whether they can measure the whole connotation of health. Those five categories were summarized as follows: Physical Health, Mental Health, Health Behavior, Healthcare Environment, and Social Health. Additionally, 26 sub-categories were derived according to the characteristics and the clinical significance of those measures. We further analyzed the combinations of instruments in different sub-categories in Fig. 5. A complete list of the identified measurement instruments, theme-specific categories, and combinations can be found in supplementary material 3.

Category 1: health / quality of life
There were 53 health M&E instruments in 200 studies that belong to Comprehensive Health / Quality of Life, with a total of 239 occurrences. These included SRH (n = 73, 31%), SF-36 (n = 36, 15%), EQ-5D (n = 17, 7%), SF-12 (n = 16, 7%), HUI3 (n = 13, 5%), EQ-5D-5 L (n = 11, 5%) and other comprehensive measures (n = 73, 31%). Many studies combined SRH and quality of life scales to monitor an individual’s health, yet more attempted to combine other indicators, such as medical history, BMI, depression, and smoking. Previous studies proved the popularity of the SF-36 for assessing quality of life, and it is adopted by almost one out of seven (36/257) articles included in this review. The SF-36 contained both physical and mental measures of health with eight scales, including the SRH, so many studies only used the SF-36 to monitor individual health. The EQ-5D, another popular measure of quality of life, measures health in terms of physical, emotional, and social functioning with five scales. To minimize ceiling effects, about a third (5/17) of the studies used the EQ-5D in combination with other indicators. Some researchers also tried other scales, developed their own instruments, or combined self-developed instruments with other scales or health indicators to improve the reliability of data.

Category 2: physical health
There were 158 physical health measures identified in the 98 studies, categorized by their clinical significance as Clinical Assessments (84 measures, 53%), Physical Medical History (45 measures, 28%), Physical Activity (26 measures, 16%), and Overall Physical Status (3 measures, 2%). Physical health is the most frequently mentioned category, with a total of 381 occurrences in the included studies. About 55% (n = 208) of physical health measures are taken as specific indicators, and 44% (n = 166) are used in combination with other tools, leaving only a few (n = 7, 2%) as generic tools. Clinical assessment (n = 198, 52%) and physical medical history (n = 138, 36%) composed the main part of application in this category. Among the clinical assessments, indicators such as BMI (n = 25, 13%), blood pressure (n = 16, 8%), and blood glucose (n = 8, 4%) were repeatedly reported due to their ease of measurement and their relevance for identifying risks of chronic conditions.

Category 3: mental health
A total of 47 mental health instruments from 57 studies were found and categorized as Overall Mental Status (18 measures, 38%), Anxiety (7 measures, 15%), Mental Medical History (6 measures, 13%), Depression (5 measures, 11%), and Other Mental Health Measure (11 measures, 23%). Among the total 95 times of usage, self-reported Emotional Status was the most popular indicator, as it was applied 23 (24%) times, and was often used in combination with other physical health indicators. In terms of established questionnaires, the General Health Questionnaire-12 (GHQ-12) was most popular, with 9 (9%) times of usage. Anxiety (n = 10, 11%) and depression (n = 6, 6%) were the two most commonly measured mental disorders. Many researchers used either or both to represent an individual’s current mental health, and evaluate overall health status when combined with quality of life scales or other physical indicators. Moreover, a history of mental illnesses could directly reflect an individual’s mental health level. Research interests on other factors that directly affect the level of health, such as personality traits, cognitive functions, and stress, have increased in recent years. Moreover, among all the 57 studies that assessed mental health, 42 of them simultaneously applied other comprehensive instruments, indicating that researchers tended to capture mental health as an independent domain.

Category 4: health behavior
Regarding the Health Behavior domain, 21 instruments were applied across 38 studies, with a cumulative usage frequency of 85 times, focusing on Risky Behavior (9 measures, 43%), Diet (5 measures, 24%), Sleep Status (4 measures, 19%), and DailyActivity (3 measures, 14%). Research on Risky Behavior often focused on smoking (n = 22, 26%), while fewer studies mentioned alcohol consumption (n = 15, 18%). Some claimed that diet, exercise, and sleep were the three pillars of a healthy life, which were used 10 (12%), 16 (19%), and 9 (11%) times, respectively, according to our research. When investigating people’s diet habits and sleep quality, researchers often use only one subjective question. In terms of Daily Activity, researchers paid much attention to the ability to exercise and work in people of working age, and the Activity of Daily Living (ADL) scale is designed for people with limited self-care ability. More and more studies showed a shift of interests towards health behaviors.

Category 5: healthcare environment
For the Healthcare Environment domain, we identified 16 instruments, which appeared 29 times across 16 studies, covering Living Environment (9 measures, 56%), Healthcare Service (6 measures, 38%), and Health Literacy (1 measure, 6%). Health resources are essential for health equity, which includes the coverage of healthcare services such as medication, vaccination, chronic disease screening, and healthcare accessibility. Living Environment is an issue of global attention due to its nuanced effects on health and was mentioned 11 (38%) times, including 7 (24%) applied in combination with other scales, 3 (10%) served as specific indicators, and 1 (3%) used to develop a new M&E instrument. Although Health Literacy is mentioned only by one study, it was included in the annual reports by the health departments of multiple countries.

Category 6: social health
There were 15 instruments in 12 studies and 23 applications, covering Sociodemographic Characteristics (7 measures, 47%), Social Support (5 measures, 33%), and Social Adaptance (3 measures, 20%). Sociodemographic Characteristics, such as race, education, and employment, are common information collected by many studies, but only 6 took them as influential factors to examine their relation with health. Social Support, an important protective factor for health, is often measured with general questions about family relationships or social activities, while Social Adaptance, the evidence that one can adapt to the external environment, is measured with existing scales. Nonetheless, instruments in both categories are usually combined with other indicators to represent one’s health.

Discussion
This scoping review advances public health knowledge by systematically mapping a broad range of M&E instruments of individual health in community settings. Unlike previous work that often focused on a single health dimension or population-based instruments, our review provides a cross-domain synthesis encompassing six overarching categories and 26 subcategories, offering a structured overview of how individual health has been conceptualized and assessed. Beyond descriptive mapping, the study outlines a conceptual framework that clarifies how existing instruments are structured and used, providing insights that may guide future research on instrument selection, adaptation, or development in community-based settings.
The large number of studies included demonstrates that the use of individual-level health measurement instruments has expanded substantially since the early 21st century, particularly with the growing adoption of generic and mixed instruments. Existing research consistently emphasized the feasibility and accessibility of outcomes, favoring validated scales and anthropometric indicators such as BMI, while rarely employing laboratory or imaging tests that are costly or impractical in community settings. Most instruments captured both physical and mental health, and many included quality-of-life scales as complementary indicators. Although further research is needed to refine selection strategies, combination approaches, and weighting methods, our synthesis provides a foundation for optimizing the validity and comparability of health measurement instruments, thereby strengthening their contribution to community health monitoring and policy evaluation.
The perception of health varies among people. Many researchers agree that health can be deconstructed into multiple dimensions. Though some understandings of health received wide acknowledgment, there has been no “one-and-only health”. SRH is typically measured with a single item and serves as a widely used simple, valid measure. However, its limited value for guiding interventions has led researchers to adopt quality-of-life instruments to better capture individuals’ own evaluations. While subjective assessments provide essential insights, they must be interpreted alongside objective measures to minimize bias. As our research focused on evaluating the individual health of general populations, some tools applied merely in clinical settings with possibly better reliability and validity in the patient population were not included. Moreover, in the era of information technology, our research prefers to use objective data that can be obtained from electrical systems like patient medical records for measurement.
Physical health is the most frequently measured dimension, which can be reflected by the presence of medical history or clinical and laboratory findings, as well as subjective discomfort. People may show abnormal indicators after physical assessments or auxiliary tests which are not enough for diagnosis but pose potential risks on health. The occurrence of chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes, was one of the foremost concerns, while medically unexplained symptoms are attracting increasing attention. Among the numerous indicators of physical health, we have identified some commonly employed items suitable for large-scale measurement. The most popular clinical assessments are BMI, blood pressure, and blood glucose, which are highly valued for both their predictive capability for chronic disease risk and their practicality for large-scale measurement. Additionally, the inclusion of functional ability, consistent with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health, further broadens the scope beyond disease-centered outcomes. However, the predominance of physical health measures risks overshadowing other important dimensions, reinforcing the need for more balanced approaches.
Despite long-standing debates about mind-body dualism, mental well-being has been widely accepted as an essential component of health. Several researchers of humanities and social sciences have transcended the limitations of traditional psychiatry and proposed the concept of Spirituality and Health, aiming to enrich the connotation of mental health. Mainstream measures remain focused on anxiety and depression, while emerging research on stress and personality traits illustrates an expansion of scope. Yet, many researchers preferred adding additional mental health measures to assess individual health, rather than relying on the mental dimension of comprehensive scales. While mental health is widely recognized as an important component of health, instruments of this category were used far less frequently than physical or comprehensive health measures in the included studies. This may indicate limited integration of mental health constructs into routine community-based monitoring, possibly due to the historical dominance of the biomedical model, the relative complexity of measuring psychological constructs, and stigma associated with mental health reporting in some populations. This gap in practice makes mental health risky of insufficient monitoring in population health systems, limiting the capacity of policymakers to address psychological well-being at scale.
Health behavior, healthcare environment, and social health have been acknowledged globally as fundamental factors of health. It is generally believed that the environment and society influence individual health through health behaviors. Smoking and drinking are commonly concerned health behaviors, but the number of studies on smoking is much larger than on drinking. This might result from the more obvious health hazards caused by smoking than alcohol consumption and some arguments in the possible beneficial effects of alcohol consumption. The availability of healthcare facilities and services is also a key factor of health. Some studies suggested that the healthcare environment be valued as a factor to evaluate individual health, but such practice is scarcely found. Similarly, social health instruments were limited, suggesting that the relational and structural determinants of health remain underexplored. This imbalance reflects public health priorities, as well as indicating that social participation and environmental exposures remain less systematically integrated into monitoring frameworks. Given the increasing emphasis on social determinants in health policy, developing and validating tools in these areas should be a research priority.
The growing availability of electronic health records and big data offers promising avenues for individualized health measurement and integration into health information systems. Such data can improve objectivity, reduce reliance on self-report, and allow for continuous monitoring. However, practical challenges must be addressed before these potentials can be realized. Interoperability remains limited, as fragmented systems hinder integration across providers and sectors. Privacy and data governance require strong safeguards to maintain public trust. Equity is also a concern, since digital health data may exclude populations with limited access or literacy and risk reinforcing existing disparities. By acknowledging these challenges, researchers and policymakers can work toward responsible and inclusive implementation of big-data solutions that truly support community health monitoring and local policy decision-making.
By providing a consolidated map of how individual health has been assessed in community settings, this scoping review offered a clearer understanding of the conceptual breadth, practical feasibility, and current gaps in existing instruments. More specifically, it highlights which types of indicators remain underrepresented in community monitoring, underscoring the need for more balanced, multidimensional assessment strategies. Moreover, by illustrating the growing but uneven use of validated generic and mixed instruments, the review can assist practitioners in making more informed choices about selecting feasible, scalable measures suited to community settings. In addition, the identified challenges related to data integration, interoperability, and equity provide a basis for future system-level improvements to enhance community health surveillance.

Strengths and limitations
This scoping review includes a substantial number of articles and provides a structured overview of existing individual health M&E instruments. All the instruments are synthesized into six categories and 23 subcategories on the basis of widely acknowledged concepts of health, forming a comprehensive multi-domain framework that may inform future work on individual health monitoring and offer preliminary conceptual insights for researchers and policymakers considering the selection or adaptation of health M&E tools.
This review also has several limitations. First, the breadth of the research question required restrictive inclusion criteria, and some relevant studies may have been missed. Second, the heterogeneity in granularity and dimensionality of the instruments limited the ability to make direct comparisons across categories. Third, as our review focused on community-based and general population instruments, tools used exclusively in clinical contexts were not included. These limitations mean that our findings should be interpreted as providing a structured overview and trend analysis, rather than a prescriptive list of instruments.

Conclusions
This scoping review provides a comprehensive mapping to date of individual-level health M&E instruments used in community settings. By synthesizing six major domains and diverse compositional types of instruments, the review clarifies how individual health has been operationalized across studies and highlights substantial variation in measurement practices, revealing a shift from predominantly using generic instruments to increasing adoption of mixed approaches that aim to capture the complexity of individual health. Physical health indicators remain dominant, whereas mental, behavioral, environmental, and social dimensions are incorporated far less frequently, limiting the opportunities to address broader determinants of well-being. Advances in digital health and growing access to electronic data offer promising pathways for more objective and continuous assessment, yet the absence of widely accepted database-integrated instruments and persistent challenges in interoperability, privacy, and equity continue to hinder their full realization. Together, these findings provide a foundation for subsequent analyses that can further advance the development of more balanced, inclusive, and policy-relevant approaches for monitoring and improving individual health.
