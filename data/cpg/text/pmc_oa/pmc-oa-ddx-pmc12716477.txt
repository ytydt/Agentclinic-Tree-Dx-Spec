Mpox animal models: a comprehensive evaluation of susceptible species for pathogenesis studies and drug discovery
ABSTRACT
Mpox, formerly known as monkeypox, has shifted from a regional concern to a global health priority. Progress toward targeted antivirals and vaccines, however, still hinges on a complex array of animal models developed over the last sixty years. This review distills all literature regarding Mpox animal models – from rodent to non-human primate species – into a clear, comparative narrative. We outline how host species, viral clade, and inoculation route shape clinical course and immune responses, and we identify which models best address questions of pathogenesis, transmission, and medical countermeasure efficacy. The resulting roadmap is designed to help researchers, funders, and regulators select the most informative tools and streamline mpox drug discovery pipelines.

Introduction
Monkeypox virus (MPV), a member of the Orthopoxvirus genus in the Poxviridae family, was first isolated in 1958 from cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) after a pox-like illness was observed in a laboratory colony. Although initially characterized in non-human primates (NHPs), MPV is now recognized as a zoonotic pathogen with a broad host range. It is capable of infecting various wildlife reservoirs and spilling over to humans, with the first documented human MPV infection occurring in 1970 in Zaire (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo; DRC). Cross-species transmission is initiated following skin or mucosal contact between humans and an infected animal. Human-to-human transmission is mainly mediated by close contact, although airborne transmission has been reported in some cases. In humans, mpox often presents with an initial prodrome of fever, headache, lack of energy, myalgia, lymphadenopathy, and sore throat following a 5- to 21-day incubation period (average 7–8 days). The characteristic rash that evolves through macular, papular, vesicular, and pustular stages usually occurs within a few days after the onset of fever and lymphadenopathy and usually resolves within 2–4 weeks. The number of lesions varies and can range from a few to several thousand. More than 150,000 confirmed cases have been reported globally by the World Health Organization (WHO) since the beginning of 2022 with a case-fatality ratio (CFR) of 0.25%. Prior to 2022, the CFR for mpox has been reported to range between 1% and 10%, depending on the clade and availability of supportive care given the lack of mpox-specific treatments.
Subsequent surveillance efforts, combined with the emergence of the first U.S. outbreak in 2003, have underscored the virus’s capacity to spread globally. Early genomic investigations, conducted in 2005, revealed two distinct MPV clades, categorized by their Central African (Congo Basin) or West African origins. However, the global mpox outbreak during 2022–2023 prompted concerns over stigma and led to a formal reclassification of MPV clades following a WHO–led meeting in August 2022. As a result, the traditional Congo Basin/Central African clade was redesignated as Clade I (encompassing subclades Ia and Ib), whereas the West African clade became Clade II (encompassing subclades IIa and IIb). Initially, the 2018–2022 outbreak was attributed to a West African–related virus (termed Clade 3), but by October 2022, it was more precisely defined as subclade Clade IIb. In contrast, the historical 2003 U.S. outbreak – associated with imported rodents from Ghana – has now been placed within Clade IIa, which sparked infections in prairie dogs housed at a vendor facility in Illinois and led to human cases in six states, primarily through animal-to-person contact. Notably, Clade IIa has demonstrated limited person-to-person transmission compared to Clade IIb, which has exhibited robust capacity for human-to-human spread. This enhanced transmission has been associated mainly with sexual practices and as such, the clinical presentation associated with Clade IIb infection differs from the typical disease described above. Instead, clinical disease is often milder, with lesions limited to mucosal, perigenital, or perianal areas. Similarly, Clade Ia represents the more established Central African MPV lineage, historically known to cause higher virulence and transmissibility, whereas Clade Ib is a recently characterized lineage circulating in the DRC. These genomic and epidemiological distinctions are critical for understanding mpox pathogenesis, identifying transmission patterns, and informing public health interventions. Critically, tecovirimat (also known as TPOXX) is a drug approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of smallpox (caused by the variola virus) and hopes that it was efficacious for the treatment of mpox were high. Unfortunately, while shown in clinical trials to be safe, the drug has also proved ineffective at reducing symptoms intensity and duration against both Clade I and Clade II mpox. Now, alternative therapies such as monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are being explored, and have been reviewed elsewhere.
Given the substantial global burden of mpox and its potential for widespread outbreaks, research efforts to develop effective therapeutics and vaccines have intensified. The creation and refinement of animal models that accurately replicate human disease remain pivotal for evaluating potential countermeasures. In this review, we synthesize the most recent information on MPV cladistic distinctions and discuss the currently available animal models. We place particular emphasis on how these models are used to study pathogenesis, explore transmission dynamics, and test emerging therapeutics and vaccine candidates, thereby providing a comprehensive resource for investigators seeking to address mpox at the bench and bedside.

Non-human primate models (NHPs)

Cynomolgus macaques (CMs)
MPV was originally identified in 1958, when laboratory workers observed diseased CMs in a research colony that developed a petechial rash. This rash rapidly evolved into maculopapular eruptions that frequently became umbilicated on the face, limbs, tail, and trunk of animals, with a predominance on the palms of their hands and the soles of their feet. No other clinical signs were apparent at necropsy or before the development of pox-like lesions. Following isolation of the virus, intradermal inoculation into a naïve animal resulted in a single pustule with surrounding edema 7 days post-infection (dpi) at the site of injection and a slight fever at 5–9 dpi. In 1960, Prier et al. reported that intradermal (i.d.), intravenous (i.v.), and subcutaneous (s.c.) injection of MPV (Strain 1744, unknown clade) resulted in a more pronounced clinical disease with cutaneous lesions, laboured breathing, facial swelling, and lymphadenopathy in challenged NHPs. In contrast with the limited clinical disease described above, an aerosol challenge of CMs with 6 × 105–9 × 105 egg infectious units of MPV resulted in coughing, cutaneous lesions, inappetence, and reduced activity, culminating in 50% (2/4) mortality. Upon necropsy, cytoplasmic inclusions in the respiratory epithelium were noted, as well as lymphoid depletion and necrosis in the spleen. Given their historical relevance, CMs have been extensively characterized as a disease model. A thorough histopathological study by Wenner et al. showed that inoculation of MPV into the skeletal muscle resulted in swelling and induration of affected tissues, often resulting in inflammation and cellular destruction of the connective tissue. Spleens of infected animals were moderately enlarged, with diffuse and follicular hyperplasia, cell necrosis in germinal centres, and phagocytic cells within vascular sinuses. Gross examination of the kidneys did not reveal any abnormalities, but basophilic cells were found in the cortical zone of the medulla and the medullary ray. Interestingly, a head-to-head comparison of challenged CMs and rhesus macaques (RMs, which are detailed further in the following section) highlighted a more pronounced disease course in CMs, with the onset of lesions occurring 18–48 h earlier and more frequently and apparent than in RMs.
Aerosol exposure, too, was established as a reliable challenge route for NHPs. Zaucha et al. reported that aerosol challenge of CMs with 1 × 104–1.41 × 105 plaque-forming units (PFU) of MPXV-V79-I-005 (Clade Ia) resulted in uniform death of animals 9–17 dpi. Initial clinical signs were noted 6–10 dpi and included cough, enanthema, exanthema, fever, inappetence, lesions, and nasal discharge. The main gross pathological findings included bronchopneumonia, colitis, dermatitis, glossitis, and stomatitis. Lungs were congested, failed to collapse, and necrosis was present throughout all lobes. Of note, the uniform lethality of an aerosol challenge model using MPXV-V79-I-005 (Clade Ia) has not been reported consistently, with other studies reporting a lethality ranging between 33–83% for doses of 2.51 × 104–3.53 × 107 PFU. Nonetheless, the main clinical features and average time to death across studies was consistent, ranging between 8–16 dpi. This challenge route was subsequently used to highlight virulence differences between different isolates (see Refs)
Despite its usefulness for rapidly causing fulminant disease, the i.v.-challenge model has been criticized for the high inoculum required. Recent work by Mucker et al. attempted to reduce the dose of virus required for this challenge route by enriching the challenge virus in enveloped virions which have an extra lipid membrane and harbour different surface proteins, in opposition to mature virions which are released through a lysis process. However, they reported no differences in clinical disease or lethality and were unsuccessful in reducing the challenge dose. An additional critique of the i.v.-challenge model is its lack of resemblance to human disease. As an alternative, intrabronchial (i.b.) challenge was evaluated in a dose-escalating challenge against the classical i.v. route. Ultimately, classical pox-like disease was observed for both routes when CMs were challenged with 5 × 106 PFU (i.b.) or 5 × 107 PFU (i.v.), although key clinical events, i.e. fever and lesion onset, peak viremia, viral shedding in nasal and oral swabs, and ultimately death, were delayed in i.b.-challenged NHPs. In response to the mpox-associated public health emergency of international concern declared from 2022–2023, an additional refinement of the current CM model was proposed using a then-circulating strain, WIBP-MPXV-001 (Clade IIb). A challenge using 1.2 × 108 PFU delivered by the combined intraocular, intraoral, intrarectal, and s.c. routes resulted in a disease profile much closer to that observed in humans. NHPs exhibited inappetence and lesions by 3 dpi, with clinical signs worsening up to 7–11 dpi before convalescence. Some NHP studies have also shown the feasibility and value of using advanced imaging to study MPV pathogenesis.

As a key historical model for MPV, CMs have been extensively used for the evaluation of MCMs. Using the stringent i.v.-challenge model described above, Earl et al. showed the efficacy of both the Dryvax and Modified Vaccinia Ankara (MVA)-based vaccines in protecting CMs from the development of lesions, overall disease, and death. A homologous prime-boost using the MVA vaccine or heterologous vaccination using MVA then Dryvax, resulted in equivalent or higher binding and neutralizing antibodies, as well as T-cell responses. Interestingly, only vaccine regimens containing Dryvax provided complete protection from lesion development, a key marker of disease severity in this model. Following this pivotal study, CMs have been extensively used for the evaluation of numerous vaccines (Refs) and therapeutics (see Table 1 and Refs).

Rhesus macaques (RMs)
Magnus et al. first identified the monkeypox virus in CMs, but an attempt to challenge RMs (Macaca mulatta) by the i.d. route in the palm of their hands with tissue culture material isolated from a pustule of naturally infected CMs (Copenhagen strain, Clade IIa) failed to result in any clinical signs. Subsequently, Prier et al. reported that i.v. administration and skin scarification of RMs with MPV (Strain 1744, unknown clade) resulted in disseminated or local lesions, respectively. Others reported that an intramuscular (i.m.) challenge results in fever around 3 dpi and the appearance of cutaneous lesions at 7–11 dpi. Of note, lesions were usually less pronounced in RMs than in CMs. Subsequent work to define a lethal model of infection for vaccine studies led to the i.v. challenge of NHPs with 5 × 106 PFU of MPOV-Z79 (Clade Ia). Although animals developed a fever and cutaneous lesions on 2 and 6 dpi, respectively, no animals (0/2) succumbed to infection and lesions had resolved by 21 dpi. In contrast, the use of 5 × 108 PFU caused hemorrhagic manifestations, and animals succumbed (2/2) before the development of the typical exanthema at 6 dpi. This disease course is unlike that observed in naturally-occurring mpox, resulting in the selection of 2 × 107 PFU as an intermediate dose for i.v.-challenge studies. This dose resulted in milder disease, while still resulting in 100% (3/3) mortality within 7–14 dpi and significant morbidity characterized by coagulopathy, fever, cutaneous lesions, and lymphadenopathy. Other studies have demonstrated the value of non-invasive methods for disease characterization, specifically sampling of the lung fluid following i.b. challenge using 2 × 105 PFU of MPXV-V79-I-005 (Clade Ia). This approach revealed a significant decrease of structural and metabolic proteins coinciding with an increase in inflammatory proteins, highlighting that inflammation may not be the sole contributor to disease progression and that maintaining pulmonary structural integrity is key for survival in this disease model.
NHPs were also successfully used to shed light on cladistic differences. The monkeypox inhibitor of complement enzyme (MOPICE) is a protein expressed by Clade I, but not Clade II viruses, a factor believed to be responsible for the increased virulence of viruses from the former clade. NHPs infected using a MOPICE-knockout virus displayed increased viral loads, similar lesion kinetics with delayed scabbing, but a diminished immune response suggesting that this protein is likely not the sole virulence factor of Clade I viruses. The availability of immunoreagents for NHP models was also used to study immune responses in relation to disease progression, highlighting the value of NHPs when compared to smaller animal models that lack such reagents. For example, Song et al. demonstrated that following an i.v. challenge of RMs with 1.5–2.5 × 106 PFU of MPXV-Zaire 79 (Clade Ia), natural killer cells would increase by 23- and 46.1-fold in the blood and lymph nodes, respectively, but that functions associated with cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion were mostly compromised, clarifying MPV pathogenesis.
Similar to CMs, RMs have shown value for evaluating MCMs against MPV (see Refs). Following the 2003 USA outbreak, Hooper et al. demonstrated the ability of a single dose of Dryvax in protecting NHPs (2/2) against a 2 × 107 PFU i.v. challenge with MPOV-Z79 (Clade Ia), including protection from the development of lesions. Similarly, five doses of a DNA vaccine encoding for two intracellular mature virion proteins (i.e. L1 and A27) and two extracellular virion proteins (i.e. A33 and B5), resulted in survival of all animals (3/3), although mild-to-moderate clinical signs, including lesions, were apparent. Further work using this DNA-based vaccine highlighted that a 2-dose regimen was sufficient to protect RMs (5/5) from a lethal i.v. challenge of 2 × 107 PFU of MPXV-Zaire 79 (Clade Ia), and that the resulting clinical disease was lessened compared to animals vaccinated using ACAM3000. Recently, thorough comparisons of the ACAM2000 vaccine, the MVA-BN vaccine, and an Adenovirus Type 35-based vaccine expressing either L1/B5 or L1/B35/A27/A33 proteins was conducted in RMs challenged with 105 median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) of MPXV-USA-MA001-2022 (Clade IIb B.1) by the i.v. route. Although all vaccines provided some level of protection, only ACAM2000 offered near complete protection, with no animal developing clinical signs, including cutaneous lesions or viremia, except for one animal that developed a single lesion. Additionally, this study reported that protection of RMs correlated with neutralizing antibody (NAb) titers, as well as L1/M1- and B5/B6-specific binding responses (Table 1).

Marmosets
The establishment of the common marmoset (Callithrix jacchus) as an animal model for MPV infection is a result of the high inoculum and short incubation time often associated with NHP models of mpox disease. In an initial experiment, Mucker et al. infected adult male marmosets with decreasing doses (2.4 × 107, 9.5 × 105, 7.8 × 104, 5.0 × 103, 510, or 48 PFU) of V79-I-005 (Clade Ia) by the i.v. route. While the time to death followed a dose-dependent effect, all animals (18/18) succumbed to infection between 6–15 dpi. The appearance of clinical signs, e.g. rash, lymphadenopathy, and lethargy, also correlated with viremia and followed a dose-dependent pattern, unlike gross pathology and histopathologic lesions.
In a follow-up study, the same group demonstrated the feasibility of a more natural route of exposure compared to the classic i.v. injection by administering 1000 PFU of V79-I-005 (Clade Ia) by the intranasal (i.n.) route in both male and female, adult marmosets. Overall, the manifestation of clinical signs occurred for the first time at 13 dpi and included laboured breathing, reduced activity, edema around the eyes, skin lesions, and nasal discharge. Animals were euthanized 15–17 dpi. While no vaccine studies have been conducted in this animal model, its value in evaluating therapeutics was successfully demonstrated by treating 3 animals using s.c. delivery of a 1:1 mixture of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs; c7D11 and c8A) at 20 mg/kg. Although all treated animals survived the critical period of disease without exhibiting any clinical signs, one animal succumbed to challenge at 25 dpi, exhibiting bloody stools and a single lesion on the chin (Table 1).

Published guinea pigs (Cavia porcellus) models are currently of limited relevance for studying MPV pathogenesis or its associated medical countermeasures (MCMs). An early report from 1959 established that neither an intraperitoneal (i.p.), i.v., or s.c. challenge of guinea pigs with either egg- or tissue culture-passaged MPV resulted in clinical disease. Unfortunately, the specifics of this challenge model, such as dose and viral strain, were not described. Complementary studies from 1976 have reported comparable results, where oral, i.n., intracardiac (i.ca.), and footpad (f.p.) delivery of an unknown dose of MPV (Copenhagen strain, Clade IIa) failed to generate clinical signs, except for f.p.-challenged animals, which exhibited foot edema. Despite the lack of clinical manifestations, virus was detectable in the lungs of animals inoculated through the i.ca. route 7 dpi, which was undetectable by 21 dpi. However, guinea pigs infected by the i.n., i.ca., and f.p. routes seroconverted to some extent, as reported by the presence of hemagglutination-inhibiting (HAI) antibodies at titers between 16 and 64 (Table 2).

Cricetidae (Cotton rat, deer mouse, and hamster)

Cotton rat
In a single study, the cotton rat (Sigmodon hispidus) was found to be susceptible to MPV infection (unknown strain) when inoculated with 1 × 105 PFU, both by the i.v. and i.n. route, resulting in 100% (10/10) and 50% (6/12) mortality, respectively, within 4–5 days. Clinical signs were mainly respiratory (laboured breathing, coughing, rhinitis, and sneezing), as well as weight loss and purulent conjunctivitis (Table 2).

Deer mouse
Unlike cotton rats, deer mice (Peromyscus maniculatus rufinus) challenged i.n. with MPXV-V79-1-005 (Clade Ia), MPXV-USA-2003 (Clade IIa), or MPXV-SP2833 (Clade IIb B.1.4) were not susceptible to MPV infection, as neither clinical signs nor lethality were observed. Nonetheless, some low levels of replication were observed in the lungs and nasal turbinates at 4 dpi, with titers dropping by 10 dpi, suggesting an abortive, short-term infection in these rodents (Table 2).

Hamster
Golden hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus) inoculated with doses ranging from 1.5–5.9 × 107 pock-forming units (PoFU) on scarified skin, or by the i.n., i.ca., or oral route were refractory to MPV (Copenhagen strain, Clade IIa) infection. While no clinical signs were reported in any of the animals, MPV could be detected in the liver, lungs, and spleen of animals during the first week, and in the kidneys during the first three weeks following inoculation. The authors described the presence of pathological alterations in the viscera of animals challenged through the i.ca. route, which they described in a follow-up report: focal lesions appeared in the liver, kidneys, and brain, including perivascular lymphocytic infiltrates and damaged endothelial blood vessels (Table 2).

Gliridae (Dormouse)
African dormice (Graphiurus kelleni) gained prominence as an MPV model after PCR-positive animals were linked to the 2003 U.S. outbreak, during which infected dormice were suspected to have transmitted the virus to prairie dogs that subsequently spread mpox to humans. In an initial experiment using 2- to 3-month-old animals, f.p.-delivery of MPXV-ZAI-79 (Clade Ia) at a dose of 1.4 × 104 PFU resulted in 92% lethality within 7–10 dpi. This animal model was found to be highly susceptible to i.n. infection, as doses of 2000, 200, 20, and 2 PFU were lethal in 100%, 100%, 63%, and 38% of infected dormice, respectively, with an established median lethal dose (LD50) of 12 PFU. Of note, i.n. injection of MPXV-COP-58 (Clade IIa) resulted in similar mortality and time to death in this animal model. Schultz et al. also have demonstrated the viability of this model in the context of vaccine studies (Table 2).
A study by Americo et al. showed similar results, where 4- to 8-month-old African dormice, divided in groups of 3–5 animals were challenged i.n. with 500 or 5000 PFU of MPXV-Z79-CB2 (Clade Ia), resulting in 100% lethality by 10 dpi. Interestingly, the calculated LD50 from this independent study was 12 PFU, the same value as reported by Schultz et al. Similarly, the LD50 for MPXV-USA-2003-044 (Clade IIa) was found to be 5 PFU. In a follow-up study, Earl et al. reported that bioluminescence imaging of live animals infected by the i.n. route presented with a variable dissemination pattern of MPV within dormice. Viral replication was often first detected in the head, with subsequent spread to the abdomen, although the opposite order was observed too. These results contrast with a parallel experimental group of CAST/EiJ mice, where replication was consistently observed first in the head. Recent work also demonstrated the susceptibility of forest dormice (Dryomys nitedula) to MPV infection (hMPXV/China/GZ8H-01/2023; Clade IIb C.1.1), with doses of 104.5–105.5 PFU resulted in not only clinical signs (e.g. diminished feeding behaviour, reduced activity, weight loss), but also 80% mortality within 5–9 dpi (Table 2).

Muridae (Mouse, natal multimammate mouse, rat)

Mouse
Following the isolation of MPV, Magnus et al. reported that intracerebral inoculation of the Copenhagen strain (Clade IIa) in adult mice (unspecified species and strain) resulted in encephalitis and 100% mortality. Similarly, i.n. challenge of 2-day-old mice also resulted in uniform lethality, with a reported time to death between 5–14 dpi. These results are in accordance with subsequent work from Marennikova et al., which showed the susceptibility of 8-, 12-, and 15-day-old white mice to MPV challenge. In contrast, i.d. delivery resulted in infiltrates with 50% (5/10) mortality, while oral inoculation of 8- and 12-day-old mice resulted in 40% (4/10) and 14% (7/29) lethality, respectively. Nonetheless, following oral inoculation, live virus could be isolated from the blood and from major organs (kidneys, liver, lungs, and spleen) one- and three-week post-inoculation. Surviving animals also exhibited reciprocal HAI titers over 256 at 42 dpi. Newborn Swiss mice (Webster strain) infected intracerebrally also were susceptible to MPV infection (unspecified dose). Subsequently, others have sought to provide a more detailed picture of MPV infection in mouse models. Numerous protocols studied the impact of MPV exposure to different mice strains, immunocompetency status, time of exposure, dose and other parameters. Although adult BALB/c and C57BL/6 display minimal clinical manifestations following MPV infection, other strains such as adult CAST/EiJ mice are highly susceptible to infection (see Refs).

Mice have also proved valuable in the evaluation of vaccines (see Refs) and therapeutics. C57BL/6 stat1-/- treated with CMX001 (10 mg/kg on day 0 + 2.5 mg/kg every other day for 14 days) or tecovirimat (100 mg/kg daily for 10 days) and challenged i.n. with 5 × 103 PFU of MPXV-ZAI-79 (Clade Ia) resulted in 100% protection. Similarly, ICR mice administered 60 µg/g of NIOCH-14, NIOCH-32, or tecovirimat resulted in 100%, 0%, and 83% protection from viral replication in the lungs following an i.n. challenge with MPXV-V79-I-005 (Clade Ia). Daily administration of tecovirimat for 5 days in CAST/EiJ mice at a dose of 100 mg/kg initiated −1 or 0 dpi and challenged by the i.n. route with 106 PFU of MPXV-SP2833 (Clade IIb B.1.4) resulted in a significant decrease in viral titers in the lungs of animals at 7 dpi. Recently, Tamir et al. showed that CAST/EiJ mice challenged by the i.n. route with 3.5 × 106 PFU of MPXV-Israel-2018 (Clade IIb A.1) could successfully be treated using mAbs capable of targeting the mature and enveloped forms of MPV up to 3 dpi. This result is encouraging given the prospect of mAbs for the treatment of infectious diseases and the recent failure of tecovirimat in accelerating recovery of individuals infected by MPV in the DRC (Table 3).

Natal multimammate mouse
While limited information has been published on this animal model, an early study reported that a laboratory strain of the natal multimammate mouse (Mastomys natalensis) was highly susceptible to inoculation of MPV by both the i.n. and i.p. routes. More recently, Port et al. evaluated the pathogenicity of hMPXV/USA/MA001/2022 (Clade IIb B.1) following a transdermal, rectal, vaginal, aerosol, or i.p. challenge. Overall, no mortality was reported, and clinical signs were limited. While no challenge routes resulted in weight loss, localized skin lesions developed 7–8 dpi at the site of transdermal inoculation and resolved 1–2 days later, while swelling of the rectum and genital organs were observed in rectally- and vaginally-challenged animals, respectively. This work highlighted that mucosal challenge routes, i.e. aerosol, rectal, and vaginal, led to increased viral shedding, with rectal inoculation resulting in the highest transmission rates under these conditions and suggesting that this model may be used to mimic human-to-human transmission (Table 2).

Rat
Adult white rats (unspecified species) were found to be refractory to infection by MPV (Copenhagen strain, Clade IIa) through the i.n., i.v., or scarification routes. However, a mortality rate of 100% (24/24) could be achieved in 1- to 3-day-old rats infected by the i.n. route, with animals exhibiting reduced activity throughout disease. Death occurred at 5 or 6 dpi, and live virus could be isolated from the liver and lungs of animals at titers of 102–103 PoFU (Table 2).

Nesomyidae (African pouched rat)

African pouched rat
African (Gambian) pouched rats (Cricetomys gambianus) were evaluated as a potential animal model for the study of MPV due to the potential implication of other members of this species in the 2003 outbreak linked to prairie dogs in the United States. Preliminary work revealed limited morbidity and mortality following infection by scarification using 4 × 104 PFU of either MPXV-2003-044 (Clade IIa) or MPXV-2003-358 (Clade Ia), where only the former virus resulted in mortality (25%; 1/4) at 13 dpi. Nonetheless, all animals experienced some form of disease with clinical signs ranging from skin hypopigmentation, lesions, reduced activity, and weight loss. Lesions developed as early as 3 dpi, and all lesions had resolved by 35 dpi in survivors. Infection of animals with 1 × 106 PFU of ROC-2003-358 (Clade Ia) expressing luciferase by the i.d. and i.n. route resulted in similar results, where only i.d. injection resulted in death (33%; 1/3). Bioluminescent imaging highlighted viral replication at the site of inoculation for either route before spreading to the eyes, mouth, and eventually to distant sites of the skin, peaking at 8–14 dpi. Ultimately, all animals seroconverted, as detected by ELISA (Table 2).

Sciuridae (Marmot, prairie dog, and squirrel)

Marmot
The bobak marmot (Marmota bobak) is one of the more recently developed medium-sized animal models used to study MPV. When inoculated by the s.c. route with 2.6, 4.1, 5.6, or 7.1 log10 PFU of MPXV-V79-1-005 (Clade Ia), animals exhibited a range of clinical signs starting 7–9 dpi with hyperthermia occurring concomitantly with submandibular lymphadenitis. The characteristic pox-like rash (macular to papular to vesicular to pustular) was visible on parts of the body and mucous membranes 1–2 days later. Finally, the animals exhibited aggressive behaviour, incoordination and tremor of the extremities, and a purulent rhinitis before succumbing to disease 12–18 dpi. Clinical signs in survivors resolved 12–18 days following their appearance. In animals that succumbed to infection, infectious MPV was recovered from multiple organs at various titers as early as 1–2 dpi: in the axillary and inguinal lymph nodes, kidneys, lungs, skin containing lesions, trachea, and testicles or ovaries (≥5.7 log10 PFU/mL); in the brain, submandibular and mesenteric lymph nodes, and pancreas (4.0–5.7 log10 PFU/mL); and in the heart, liver, and spleen (<4.0 log10 PFU/mL). In the context of an i.n. challenge with doses ranging from −1.8 to 7.8 log10 PFU, the severity and timing of clinical signs were no different than from the s.c. challenge. However, the disease was not uniformly lethal, as only 42% (5/12) of animals succumbed to infection 13–22 dpi, and marmots exhibited a reduced density of lesions on both the skin and mucous membranes. A follow-up i.n. challenge with 3.7 log10 PFU also revealed a diminished viral burden within organs of infected marmots, where the lungs and trachea were the first organs to test positive for MPV at 5 dpi. Scientists have also used marmots successfully to evaluate therapeutic countermeasures, specifically NIOCH-14 and tecovirimat. In an i.n. challenge model using 3.7 log10 PFU of MPXV-V79-1-005 (Clade Ia), oral administration of these compounds daily at a dose of 40 mg/kg one day before infection and for the following 6 days resulted in 100% protection for both drugs. Alternatively, the mortality in untreated animals was reported to be 50% (Table 2).

During the United States outbreak of 2003, most patients affected by mpox reported close contact with sick pet prairie dogs (Cynomys ludovicianus), making it a natural contender as an animal model. Necropsies of animals responsible for this outbreak revealed an infection of the cells within the bronchi, conjunctivae, and tongue. Replicating virus was also isolated from the lungs, which was hypothesized at the time to be an amplification factor for transmission. In an initial study, Xiao et al. reported infecting adult wild-caught prairie dogs by either the i.p. or i.n. route. Animals receiving 105.1 PFU of MPX 2003 (Clade IIa) by the i.p. route exhibited inappetence, reduced activity, and 100% mortality (3/3) with a time to death ranging between 8–11 days. Regarding viral kinetics, live MPV was readily detected in the blood and throat swabs of prairie dogs at 5–6 dpi. At necropsy, elevated titers were detected in the liver and spleen of animals and in the kidneys and lungs, to a lesser extent. Gross necropsy revealed centrilobular necrosis of the liver, moderate-to-severe necrosis of the spleen, and mild-to-moderate thickening of the lungs interstitium. A parallel experiment assessed the susceptibility of prairie dogs to an i.n. infection of 105.1 PFU of MPX 2003 (Clade IIa) per nostril. While this route achieved only 60% lethality (3/5), the clinical signs associated with infection were similar to those challenged by the i.p. route. Interestingly, the surviving animals displayed nasal congestion and mucopurulent discharge, as well as characteristic mpox lesions on the lips and tongue, in which infectious MPV was successfully isolated. These surviving animals appeared healthy when euthanized 25 dpi. In contrast with i.p.-infected animals, live virus was isolated consistently initially from the throat in i.n.-infected prairie dogs (3–6 dpi) before being detected in the blood (8–9 dpi). More work was performed to better understand MPV biology in prairie dogs, which is summarized in Table 4 (See Refs). Of importance to those interested in using the prairie dog as an animal model, a study reported that the use of meloxicam (a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug), but not buprenorphine (an opioid) was associated with increased morbidity and mortality in prairie dogs challenged with 4 × 103 PFU of MPXV-USA-2003-044 (Clade IIa).
Prairie dogs are also a useful animal model to evaluate the effect of recombinant viruses such as those expressing luciferase to study biodistribution, or to answer scientific questions specific to genomic differences between clades. The complement control protein (CCP), expressed by MPV, prevents the initiation of the classical and alternative pathways of the complement. Clade I viruses express CCP while Clade II viruses do not. In a recombinant MPXV-ROC-2003-358 (Clade Ia) virus lacking CCP, disease progression was slower, viral loads were lower, and mortality was reduced compared to animals infected with the parental virus. Alternatively, a recombinant MPXV-2003-USA-044 (Clade IIa) virus engineered to express CCP did not result in a significantly faster disease progression or increased mortality.
Prairie dogs have also demonstrated their usefulness in evaluating MCMs. Regarding therapeutics, animals infected by the i.n. route with 3.8 × 105 PFU of MPXV-ROC-2003-358 (Clade Ia) and treated with tecovirimat either on day 0, 3 or following rash onset all showed 100% survival. In parallel, control animals showed only a 25% survival rate. In a similar study, the efficacy of brincidofovir was evaluated for efficacy against MPXV, as the drug was originally approved to treat smallpox by the Food and Drug Administration. As such, animals were inoculated with 8.8 × 105 PFU of MPXV-USA-2003-044 (Clade IIa) and treated either on −1, 0, or 1 dpi, resulting in 57%, 43%, and 29% survival, respectively. In contrast, untreated animals exhibited a survival rate of 14%. In the context of prophylactics, Townsend et al. showed that the antigenic profile of immune responses of prairie dogs to Dryvax, ACAM2000®, and IMVAMUNE® was fairly similar to those of humans, highlighting the value of this animal model for vaccine studies. In a similar study, prairie dogs vaccinated with Dryvax (2 × 105 PFU), Acambis2000 (now ACAM2000®; 2 × 105 PFU), or IMVAMUNE® (1 × 108 TCID50) 60 days prior to an i.n. challenge with 1 × 105 or 1 × 106 PFU of MPXV-ROC-2003-358 (Clade Ia) showed a 100% protection from death and weight loss. Post-exposure prophylaxis was also attempted with the Dryvax, ACAM2000®, and IMVAMUNE® vaccines. Ultimately, none of the prairie dogs challenged with 4.31 × 106 PFU of MPXV-ROC-2003-358 (Clade Ia) and then vaccinated 1 dpi survived infection, while only one animal from the Dryvax (25% survival; 1/4) and one from the IMVAMUNE® (20% survival; 1/5) groups survived when the vaccine was administered 3 dpi.
Finally, prairie dogs challenged i.n. with MPXV-USA-2003-044 (Clade IIa) at a dose of 9 × 103 PFU served as a valuable model also in studying MPXV transmission, wherein bedding/fomites, co-housing, and respiratory secretions were all shown to be viable means of transmission. However, it should be noted that in the context of respiratory secretions, the authors could not guarantee that animals did not touch noses throughout the study, which could instead suggest transmission through nasal mucous secretions. Further studies observed that only a high dose (5 × 103 PFU) of MPXV-RCG-2003-358 (Clade Ia) could result in transmission by the respiratory route, although not efficiently (Table 4).

Ground squirrel
The thirteen-lined ground squirrel (Spermophilus tridecemlineatus) was established by Tesh et al. as an animal model for mpox. The authors challenged the animals by the i.n. or i.p. route with 105.1 PFU of MPX 2003 (Clade IIa). Animals developed inappetence and reduced activity 4–5 dpi but in none of the groups did animals develop clinical signs comparable to those observed in prairie dogs, i.e. lesions, respiratory issues, or rhinitis. Animals challenged by the i.n. route succumbed to disease 8–9 dpi, with viral loads first detected 2–4 dpi in the oropharynx of animals. In contrast, i.p.-infected animals succumbed to infection 6–7 dpi, and MPV was first detected in the blood of ground squirrels at 3 dpi. However, regardless of challenge route, high titers of infectious MPV could be isolated in the liver, lungs, and spleen, with moderate levels also found in the brain, heart, and kidneys. In accordance with viral load data, centrilobular hepatocytic degeneration or necrosis could be observed in the liver, along with splenic necrosis and focal consolidations within the lungs. The LD50 associated with MPXZ79 (Clade Ia) and MPX US03 (Clade IIa) were established in ground squirrels at 0.35 and 0.46 PFU, respectively. Thirteen-lined ground squirrels have shown their relevance also as an animal model to evaluate therapeutics against MPV (see ref and Table 2).

Tree squirrel
Adult Eurasian red squirrels (Sciurus vulgaris) were the first tree squirrels to be experimentally challenged with MPV. An i.n. or oral challenge of animals with 106 PFU of MPXV Z-249 (Likely Clade Ia) resulted in an accelerated disease progression compared to a scarification route, with clinical signs comparable to those observed in prairie dogs, such as cough and laboured breathing, fever, inappetence, reduced activity, and rhinitis resulting in death of all animals 7–8 dpi. However, unlike prairie dogs, the characteristic rash did not develop on these animals. Unpublished work by Shelukhina et al. also reported a MPV challenge of six squirrel species belonging to the Funisciurus sp. (Rope squirrel), Heliosciurus sp. (Sun squirrel), and Protoxerus sp. (African giant squirrel) genera. Inoculation of animals with 105–106 PFU of a Congo Basin strain by the i.n., s.c., and oral route resulted in an overall acute disease presentation and 100% lethality. In contrast to common squirrels, some animals within this study that were given a non-lethal dose of virus developed a rash in areas of their body lacking fur or at the borders of mucous membranes and skin such as around the nose and lips. The authors also reported airborne and direct contact transmission of MPV to naïve animals.
Rope squirrels (Funisciurus anerythrus) were further investigated for their susceptibility to infection by a recombinant MPV expressing firefly luciferase (Parental strain: MPXV-2003-Congo-358, Clade Ia). Animals were challenged by the i.n. or i.d. route with 1 × 106 PFU, resulting in mortality rates of 75% (3/4) and 50% (2/4), respectively. Animals challenged by the i.n. route exhibited respiratory distress as the main symptom and cause of death (11–13 dpi), with oral lesions appearing 8 dpi and the sole surviving animal exhibiting ocular lesions at 6–25 dpi. It should be noted that heavy shedding support the usefulness of this model for transmission studies, as a naïve animal housed in its own cage, but in the same room as the other animals developed clinical disease at 13 dpi. This contact animal displayed typical signs of MPV infection such as nasal discharge, oral lesions, and respiratory distress, requiring euthanasia at 18 dpi of the challenged animals. The authors attributed the transmission event to aerosols (Table 2).

Leporidae (Rabbit)
Following the initial isolation of MPV in 1958 (Copenhagen strain, Clade IIa), rabbits were the first animal model to be evaluated for susceptibility. In adult rabbits (Unspecified species), i.d. injection of 8 × 107 PoFU resulted in localized necrosis of about 10 mm at the inoculation site, while an unspecified dose inoculated by scarification resulted in a single pustule. The authors reported a generalized infection 5–6 dpi by both routes, as well as through i.v. challenge, in some animals but an absence of lesions on any organs during autopsy. In 2-day-old rabbits, inoculation by scarification or the intracutaneous route was 100% fatal by 5–8 dpi, with the latter route resulting in an earlier time to death. Nevertheless, both routes resulted in grey-white spots in liver tissues, as well as hemorrhagic foci in the kidneys of animals.

The i.n. challenge resulted in rapid progression of the disease with death occurring 4–5 dpi, albeit in absence of the characteristic rash. Of note, when uninfected young rabbits of the same litter were kept with orally- and i.n.-infected animals, transmission occurred 6–8 dpi in naïve animals, resulting in four sick animals, three of which succumbed to infection. Gispen et al. confirmed findings relating to transmission from i.d.-infected rabbits to naïve animals, resulting in hemorrhagic necrotic lesions. Of note, the authors also demonstrated neurovirulence when MPV was administered intracerebrally, with fatal meningoencephalitis leading to death at 6–9 dpi. In line with the studies above, recent work by Shang et al. demonstrated the susceptibility of adult rabbits to hMPXV/China/GZ8H-01/2023 (Clade IIb C.1.1) following an i.v. or i.d. infection. In i.v.-challenged animals, the authors reported the development of fever and skin lesions, while i.d.-challenged animals experienced localized red lesions at the site of injection, with hemorrhage and dense infiltrates of necrosis at its centre. Similarly to the prior work, they also demonstrated the susceptibility of 10-day old rabbits to i.n. infection, with doses of 103.5–105.5 PFU resulting in lethality in 3–15 dpi. Zhu et al. also demonstrated the value of this animal model for the evaluation of MCMs, showing that New Zealand rabbits vaccinated with gene-deleted live vaccinia virus (strain Tian Tan) exhibited reduced morbidity compared to unprotected animals (Table 5).

Suidae (Pig)
Despite the variety of animal models available to study MPV infection, few domesticated species sustain replication or can transmit the virus. The domestic pig (Sus crofa) is a common livestock animal for numerous zoonoses of clinical relevance to human health, including influenza and Nipah viruses. Additionally, pigs are natural hosts for another pox virus, swinepox virus, and pig embryonic kidney cells have been used successfully to grow MPV in vitro following the identification of the first human case in 1970, highlighting their potential susceptibility to MPV infection. To assess this, Mantlo et al. challenged piglets by the combined i.d., i.n., and i.v. route with a total dose of 9 × 107 TCID50 of MPXV-USA-MA001-2022 (Clade IIb B.1) and performed serial euthanasia to characterize disease progression. Although the disease presentation remained mostly subclinical, sparse skin lesions were observed on animals, mainly presenting as red macules around the nose and lips of pigs 12–18 dpi. Despite limited disease, inoculated animals were capable of transmitting MPV to contact animals. Indeed, viral shedding in inoculated animals could be detected as early as 1 dpi from nasal and rectal swab, with live virus or viral DNA being detected in the upper respiratory tract of all pigs included in the study (Table 5).

Naturally susceptible or understudied species
In 1964, chimpanzees (Pan satyrus, old nomenclature) from the Rotterdam zoo presented with pox lesions during a local outbreak. In 1968, McConnell et al. reported the i.v. challenge of chimpanzees with MPV (U65-32 strain, likely Clade IIa), resulting in widespread lesions and lymphadenopathy. More recently, others have shown that in wild chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes verus, new nomenclature) from the Taï National Park, mpox could manifest itself through the typical lesions, but also through a respiratory disease in absence of a rash, manifesting through coughing, breathing with an open mouth, as well as nasal and ocular discharge. Heberling et al. reported the serial passage of MPV (Copenhagen strain, Clade IIa) in baboons (Papio cynocephalus) through skin scarification. Initial passages resulted in secondary lesions to the trunk, extremities, and face, along with typical clinical signs present in other NHP models, including fever and lymphadenopathy. Lethality was only observed in one of two experiments during passages 4–8, where animals succumbed to infection 9–17 dpi, exhibiting increasingly hemorrhagic scarified sites. Ultimately, MPV was isolated from rectal and throat swabs in most animals regardless of survival. In diseased animals, virus could also be identified from various organs, albeit inconsistently, including the heart, kidney, liver, lung, spleen, and various lymph nodes. This group had previously reported a similar clinical disease using an i.m. injection, which also resulted in transmission to sentinel animals across the room. Other animals found to be susceptible to MPV infection include sooty mangabeys (Cercocebus atys), domesticated dogs although current evidence remains debated, orangutans, and shrews. Finally, fire-footed rope squirrels (Funisciurus pyrropus) have been proposed as a reservoir for MPV, but the data has not been officially peer-reviewed at the time of writing.

Conclusions

In summary, we detailed the pathogenesis, transmission, and MCMs, where applicable, of the various animal models used in the context of mpox disease studies (Figure 1). As described throughout this review, MPV exhibits a broad host range, from classical laboratory animals (e.g. mice, rabbits, NHPs) to unconventional, wild-caught species (e.g. African pouched rats, prairie dogs, marmots) naturally susceptible to MPV. When investigating infectious diseases, the use of a specific animal model must be adapted to the study’s needs with careful consideration of the logistical advantages and disadvantages associated with the considered species. This includes not only animal species and strain, but animal sex and age, all in relation to the specific viral strain or clade, the dosage, inoculation route and transmission, the target organ and replication, the resulting immune response, clinical signs, pathology, and progression to include lethality. Additionally, reagent availability, project rationale and feasibility related to animal usage considerations, facility support, animal availability, and costs should be factored in.
As highlighted above, multiple rodent species have been evaluated with mixed success. Many species were found to be refractory to clinical disease and lethality, including guinea pigs, deer mice, hamsters, wild-type mice, and rats. However, other factors need to be considered during the selection process. Indeed, the age of the animal is critical, as juvenile mice, but not adults, will succumb to infection uniformly to multiple routes of infection. Wild-type mice, too, are refractory to disease, while immunocompromised mice such as CAST/EiJ became standard as they exhibit weight loss and may succumb to infection should the challenge parameters be stringent enough. For example, the use of a clade I virus given i.n. or i.p. at a sufficiently high dose, with reports of the i.p. route requiring smaller doses of virus, will generate uniform lethality. Ultimately, mice have been successfully used for pathogenesis studies aimed at comparing virulence between various MPV isolates and infection can be monitored using bioluminescence if non-invasive characterization of disease progression is required. Furthermore, evaluation of MCMs in mice can easily be conducted due to an observable reduction in mortality if a lethal model is used, as well as having immunoreagent availability toward characterizing associated immune responses. While other rodent models such as dormice, marmots, and ground and tree squirrels have shown to be highly susceptible to infection, they have been underused most likely due to the logistical issue surrounding procurement of these animals as their commercial availability ranges from minimal to non-existent. Importantly, these species have specific geographical distributions, making them harder to acquire by scientists conducting studies in countries located outside of the natural distribution of these animals. For example, prairie dogs have been extensively characterized as disease model for MPV infection in the United States due to their involvement in the 2003 outbreak, yet their use outside of the US remains limited as a model. Nonetheless, following an i.n. challenge, they exhibit numerous clinical signs, including skin lesions, which are not observed in mice except under very specific challenge conditions, and that vary in intensity depending on the viral strain used. Uniform lethality can be achieved under the correct conditions, making prairie dogs suitable for the evaluation of MCMs. This challenge model also results in a severe respiratory disease, featuring a crusty nose, laboured breathing, nasal discharge, as well as early viral shedding as detected in oral and nasal swabs, making this animal suitable for transmission studies.
Ultimately, as is the case for many infectious diseases, NHPs have been considered the gold standard for mpox studies. Unsurprisingly, given their historical relevance in the discovery of MPV, the model has been refined for multiple applications. Studies evaluating the efficacy of prophylactic and therapeutic options have favoured the more aggressive i.v. challenge model in cynomolgus and rhesus macaques, which results in rapid viremia and viral distribution to key organs. Additionally, the use of a clade I virus at a dose of 5 × 107 PFU is almost always uniformly lethal, a key parameter for achieving statistical significance between control and treated or vaccinated animals, as numbers are often low for each group. However, this model has been criticized by those seeking to study MPV pathogenesis, given the high challenge dose required to achieve this effect, as well as the lack of resemblance to natural routes of exposure in humans. As an alternative, some studies have tried to purify challenge inoculums to reduce the required dose, with limited success. Currently, only marmosets have been shown to be highly susceptible to MPV challenge using low doses of virus, displaying 100% lethality at doses of only 48 PFU. Finally, challenge routes involving mucosal membranes, such as aerosol, intrabronchial, and intratracheal, have been evaluated successfully with varying degrees of lethality, providing alternatives for characterizing MPV pathogenesis in an animal model that more closely mimics the route of exposure and clinical progression observed in humans.
While no animal model is perfect, the optimal choice will vary based on the purpose of the study, as well as the ability of the laboratory to procure and handle these animal species. Naturally-infected or susceptible species are of great value to understand disease ecology, pathogenesis, as well as zoonotic transmission. In a laboratory setting, transmission events have been best documented in prairie dogs and rabbits, but squirrels, multimammate mice, and NHPs may also be considered. For the evaluation of prophylactic and therapeutic options, most studies report data from mice, prairie dogs, and NHPs as lethal models have been highly characterized. However, the availability of immunological reagents for evaluating correlates of protection is higher for mice and NHPs, offering an additional insight into the protective mechanism of action of these MCMs. Finally, while most species discussed in this review are susceptible to infection in the context of pathogenesis studies, marmots, prairie dogs, and NHPs are the models consistently displaying the typical lesions and/or lymphadenopathy observed in humans. The continued development of these animal models, along with novel tools such as organ-on-a-chip systems and artificial intelligence solutions, is paramount to the development of efficacious medical countermeasures and furthering our understanding of mpox pathogenesis and transmission. These pave the way to clinical trials that ultimately result in life-saving therapeutics and vaccines, which have been reviewed elsewhere. This is reinforced by the emergence in 2023 of a novel Clade Ib virus in the DRC. While Clade I viruses have historically been associated with higher disease severity and case fatality rates of up to 10%, this trend remains to be confirmed for Clade Ib as deaths remain limited. Of concern is the reported sustained human-to-human transmission, mainly through sexual contact, as well as the impact of genomic changes associated with this virus, which warrants further evaluation in animal models pertaining to MCMs, pathogenesis, and transmission.
