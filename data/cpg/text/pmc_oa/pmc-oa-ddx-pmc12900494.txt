Geographic Measures of Early Life-Course Social Exposome in the Evaluation of Structural Neuroanatomy: A Scoping Review
Life-course social exposome exposures may influence structural neuroanatomy in early life, thereby altering risk and protective factors implicated in the biological mechanisms of disease. The literature examining geospatially defined early-life social exposome exposures and their association with structural neuroanatomy has not been characterized. A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate), PsycINFO (EBSCO), and Google Scholar. The study team dually screened 4960 records, 35 of which were included in the final review. Most articles identified an association between adverse social exposome exposures and aberrant structural neuroanatomy (n = 32). Studies assessed multiple developmental periods: infancy (n = 4), childhood (n = 27), early adolescence (n = 11), and late adolescence (n = 7). The Area Deprivation Index was the most widely used social exposome metric (n = 21). Future research should seek harmonized approaches to investigating the duration and magnitude of exposures and their association with brain structure and function.
Early life represents a period of tremendous neurodevelopment, during which the developing brain is highly sensitive to environmental influences and insults. Examining early-life exposures and their impact on neurodevelopmental outcomes is critical when thinking about brain health across the life-course. It is increasingly understood that the exposome—life-course exposure to all internal and external chemical, biological, and physical factors —plays a significant role in brain maturation. Moreover, the social exposome, a component of the exposome, referring to all social factors, conditions, and environments across the life-course (i.e., economic disadvantage, educational attainment, segregation, systemic racism), is highly influential in health and disease.
Unique interactions exist between environmental influences and childhood behavioral and brain development, which may go on to influence later-life health. These include proximal factors—individual-level factors such as child- or family-level exposures that act directly in the immediate care environment (i.e., prenatal tobacco or alcohol use, early-life lead exposure, and violence in the home). In addition, there are fundamental social exposome factors, referring to structural and place-based conditions (i.e., neighborhood poverty, air and noise pollution, and redlining). Most of the existing literature examines proximal, individual-level factors. Although these studies highlight the diverse array of exposures that impact health and development, they are not representative of systemic, structural circumstances that shape our health and well-being. Community-based interventions targeted with geospatial indices may provide promise for equitable improvements to health across the life-course at the population level. To effectively target neighborhood-level social exposome factors, it is essential to demonstrate the association between specific modifiable risk factors and brain health across early life.
There is a small body of research examining the relationship between the social exposome and neuroanatomical outcomes among children. The notable brain structures implicated include the hippocampus, amygdala, prefrontal cortex, frontal and temporal gray matter regions, and white matter microstructures. Structural neuroanatomy is primarily assessed using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including specialized acquisition sequences such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). Whittle et al. demonstrated that greater neighborhood disadvantage was associated with altered structural brain development. Similarly, Hackman et al. reported associations between increased disadvantage and poorer neurocognitive performance along with smaller cortical surface area and subcortical volumes. Decreased cortical thickness has been shown to be associated with residence in higher neighborhood disadvantage. Some studies have focused their structural neuroanatomical assessments on prefrontal and hippocampal structures to understand neighborhood-level poverty’s association with differential cognitive functions. In addition to examining macro-level structures, researchers have also looked at microstructural properties and connectivity, with Bell et al. examining disadvantage in relation to white matter pathways connecting the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, and hypothalamus. Together, these studies illustrate that the social exposome can impart alternations in neurotypical neuroanatomical development. These alterations may in turn promote disparate health outcomes in later life in the domains of cognition, epigenetic aging, disease prevalence, and mortality.
The primary goal of this scoping review was to evaluate the extent of the literature examining early-life exposure to the adverse social exposome and its association with structural neuroanatomical differences in a population ≤24 years of age. A secondary goal was to examine the commonalities in brain regions impacted as well as the components of the exposure metrics utilized to study the social exposome. Examining how early-life exposure to social disadvantage impacts structural neuroanatomy is critical for policy development to support equitable brain health outcomes and mitigate the development of later-life psychiatric and neurodegenerative conditions.

METHODS AND MATERIALS
We used the systematic methodology of a scoping review as defined by the Joanna Briggs Institute to conduct this scoping review. This review was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). A scoping review methodology was selected as a means to rigorously and transparently examine the extent of the literature on this topic. This allowed for a broad research question that was less restricted by study design (see Supplemental Materials A for detailed methodology).

Identification of Relevant Studies
The review team collaborated with an experienced research librarian (LC) to develop a search strategy that combined controlled vocabulary and title/abstract terms related to geographic-based social exposome metrics in early-life structural neuroanatomy development (see Supplemental Materials B for full search strategies).

Study Selection
The article selection process is described in Figure 1. Each reference’s title and abstract were independently screened by 2 reviewers. The inclusion criteria required studies to have cognitively normal human subjects with an average age ≤24 years, a small area–based metric of the social exposome treated as an exposure variable, neuroimaging as an outcome, be peer reviewed, be published in English, and be a primary research study (i.e., not a review article or meta-analysis). The cutoff point of ≤24 years was selected because there is a strong neuroscientific understanding that the prefrontal cortex continues to develop up until age 24. We defined a small area–based metric of social exposome as any geospatially measured metric smaller than a state that assessed a component of the social exposome. The neuroimaging outcome was defined as imaging that examined structural neuroanatomy or structural connectivity (i.e., MRI; not functional MRI/electroencephalography/functional near-infrared spectroscopy, or other functional neuroimaging methods).
Articles undergoing full-text review were independently reviewed by 2 trained reviewers to confirm their eligibility. All disagreements were resolved with a consensus meeting with the study team. To be included in the review, articles had to have undergone analyses that independently examined the role of adverse social exposome exposures on structural neuroanatomy. If social exposome exposures were covariates, but their effects were not disaggregated, the study was not included.

Data Extraction and Reporting
All articles included after full-text review were dually abstracted by the study team and underwent a consensus process to confirm reported data. Data charting was conducted using personalized extraction forms in Covidence (Veritas Health Information). Abstraction included documenting the relevant characteristics of the included studies such as study design, population, geospatial metric of adverse social exposome utilized, exposure, timing, duration, imaging modality used, neuroanatomical structures examined, and key findings.

RESULTS
Of the 9937 records identified by our search, 4960 records remained after deduplication. Following title and abstract screening, 41 records were eligible for full-text review. During full-text review, 6 articles were ultimately excluded for not including a geospatial metric of adverse social exposome in the primary analysis (n = 5) or for not being peer reviewed (n = 1) (see Figure 1).
The final review included 35 articles that used an area-based metric of adverse social exposome as an exposure variable and assessed neuroanatomical outcomes with neuroimaging in a population ≤24 years of age (Table 1). See Table 2 for a summary of all included studies by study characteristics. Thirty-two (91.4%) of the 35 articles included in this review found significant relationships between metrics of social exposome exposures and neuroimaging outcomes. Only 3 studies (8.6%) found nonsignificant relationships (see Table 2). See Table 3 for the detailed, study-level characteristics of the social exposome metrics used; see Figure 2 for a concise, summative overview of the social exposome metrics used. Results pertaining to the metrics of social exposome examined within the studies and results of studies involving air pollution and structural neuroanatomy are described in Supplemental Materials C. All other results are detailed below.

Cortical and Subcortical Volumes
Of the 35 studies, 27 (74.11%) investigated structural neuroanatomy through volumetric assessments of either cortical or subcortical regions. Most studies demonstrated a consistent pattern in which higher levels of adverse social exposome exposure were associated with decreased cortical or subcortical volumes in children and adolescents. Similarly, studies examining prenatal exposures to disadvantage found that greater maternal disadvantage was negatively associated with infant cortical gray matter volumes, providing evidence that the exposome’s influence on structural neuroanatomy begins prenatally and continues throughout early life. However, not all studies followed this expected pattern. For example, Adise et al. reported a negative association between 2 subcomponents of the Childhood Opportunity Index (COI) and subcortical volumes, suggesting that individuals exposed to greater disadvantage (lower COI) exhibited greater subcortical volumes compared with individuals with more opportunities. Meanwhile, Tomasi and Volkow did not find any significant effects of neighborhood deprivation on cortical volumes after controlling for family income. The variability in results across studies may be attributed to differences in geospatial metrics, age groups, and brain regions examined. The discrepancy in the direction of these relationships will be explored further in the Discussion section.

Cortical and Subcortical Thickness and Surface Area
Of the 35 studies included in this review, 18 (51.43%) investigated the effects of adverse social exposome exposures on cortical thickness, while 11 (31.43%) focused on the impact on surface area. Consistent with the volumetric measurements, most studies found a negative association between adverse social exposome exposure and neuroanatomical measurements. Hackman et al. reported that higher neighborhood disadvantage was associated with smaller cortical surface areas but did not find effects on cortical thickness or white matter volumes. Similarly, Rakesh et al. examined multiple indicators of socioeconomic status’ unique roles in the brain’s structural development and found evidence that neighborhood disadvantage was negatively associated with cortical thickness of frontal, parietal, and occipital regions. Alnaes et al. lent further support to these associations by demonstrating that higher levels of economic deprivation and poverty were associated with lower cortical thickness, area, volume, and total volume. Karcher et al. similarly found the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) to be negatively associated with total surface area and average cortical thickness. In an examination of cortical thickness, rather than surface area, Miller et al. found that in adolescents, greater neighborhood disadvantage was associated with advanced cortical thinning. This negative association was also identified in early life, with neonates whose mothers had been exposed to social disadvantage exhibiting smaller total cortical surface areas. Ultimately, these studies all support the hypothesis that greater exposure to components of the adverse social exposome is associated with thinner and smaller cortical and subcortical areas beginning early in life.
Although multiple studies show a relatively consistent pattern of negative associations between adverse exposures and thickness/surface area, a few others have shown mixed associations, positive associations, or no significant associations at all. Vargas et al. found that distinct environmental stressors—specifically, stimulation (e.g., high population density, neighborhood crime), discrepancy (e.g., social exclusion, low sense of belonging), and deprivation (e.g., low neighborhood median income)—conferred overlapping and unique patterns of neuroanatomical development. Notably, deprivation exposures were negatively associated with cortical thickness in frontal regions, such as the superior frontal gyrus. Another study by the same group reported that greater neighborhood deprivation was associated with increased prefrontal cortex surface area and a leftward asymmetry in area and thickness. Similarly, Whittle et al. found that neighborhood disadvantage was associated with altered brain development in both early and late adolescence, such that greater neighborhood disadvantage was associated with increased thickness in a multitude of areas. Finally, McKinnon et al. who used the Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation and a sample of U.K. infants and Wigglesworth et al., who examined neighborhood disadvantage and prefrontal cortex thickness, found that there were no significant associations between disadvantage and neurodevelopment.

Brain Microstructure and Connectivity
A minority of studies reported on the microstructural (n = 6; 17.14%) and connectivity (n = 1; 2.86%) properties of white matter tracts. These were determined using a variety of models such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), restriction spectrum imaging (RSI), and Q-Ball in relation to different metrics of geospatially derived adverse social exposome. Bell et al. found that higher neighborhood disadvantage during adolescence was associated with lower quantitative anisotropy (QA), a sign of compromised white matter integrity. Li et al. illustrated that higher disadvantage and lower household socioeconomic status (SES) were independently associated with lower RSI restricted normalized directional diffusion (RSI-RND), greater RSI restricted normalized isotropic diffusion (RSI-RNI), and lower DTI fractional anisotropy (FA) in white matter regions. These different microstructural metrics reflect altered myelin organization (axons and dendrites) and atypical glial/neuronal cell presence. Such a pattern supports the hypothesis that greater disadvantage is associated with reduced myelination and elevated microglial activity. A few studies found a positive association between greater adverse exposures and increased white matter integrity. Lean et al. found that social disadvantage was related to lower mean diffusivity in some regions and higher FA in others. In addition, Alnaes et al. found that higher ADI and higher particulate matter exposure are linked to higher FA, higher neurite density, and lower radial diffusivity (RD) in the parahippocampal cingulate and uncinate fasciculus and higher RD and lower neurite density in the corpus callosum. While the trends reported by Lean et al. and Alnaes et al. may appear to be the opposite of the microstructural trends reported by Bell et al. (low QA) and Li et al. (lower RSI-RND, DTI, FA, and greater RSI-RNI), they may not be comparable given the different ages of participants. Reported differences may stem from age-related developmental differences rather than true differences in the exposure and outcome of interest. Differences may also be explained by developmental and compensatory mechanisms, which will be further examined in the Discussion section.
These studies provide evidence of the association between life-course social exposome and neuroanatomical microstructural integrity and connectivity. However, they are also indicative of a need for further research to more definitively characterize these relationships.

Brain Maturation
Two of the included studies were focused on the question of how neighborhood disadvantage impacts the trajectory of the brain’s natural maturation processes as measured with neuroimaging. Both Rakesh et al. and Gur et al. found evidence of higher disadvantage being associated with accelerated aging. Using neuroimaging metrics, including volume, thickness, and area of 34 cortical regions; volume of 7 subcortical regions; 1 lateral ventricle volume; and intracranial volume, Rakesh et al. used a previously trained model to predict each individual’s age. The difference between chronological age and predicted age, known as the brain age gap estimate, was used to quantify brain aging. The group identified that higher residential disadvantage was associated with accelerating aging during early adolescence but with delayed maturation during later adolescence. The effects were most pronounced in the frontal and temporal lobes. Gur et al. used a trained model to classify a participant’s neuroimaging data as being that of an adolescent (8–17 years) or an adult (≥18 years). These classifications were based on multiple brain regions including white matter, cerebellum, basal ganglia/striatum, limbic, frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, and FA of white matter tracts. Lower spatially defined SES was associated with higher rates of the brain images being misclassified as adults, demonstrating accelerated aging in the imaging profiles. This study was cross-sectional and thus could not examine differences in trajectories between early and late adolescence such as those found in Rakesh et al..
Although this scoping review was not intended to examine brain aging per se, the incorporation of neuroimaging outcomes to assess brain maturation in the context of exposure to disadvantage is relevant to the discussion of the social exposome’s influence on biological processes that influence functional and structural outcomes.

DISCUSSION
This scoping review is the first of its kind to examine studies that characterized the relationship between geographically based metrics of early-life adverse social exposome exposure and patterns of structural neuroanatomy. Our synthesis shows that adverse exposures have been consistently associated with smaller cortical and subcortical volumes, reduced cortical thickness and surface area, and potential alterations in connectivity and maturation. While there are many studies that have examined early-life individual- or family-level characteristics and neurocognitive or behavioral outcomes, only a few have linked the social exposome across early life to neuroanatomical, imaging-based outcomes. Looking broadly at geospatially defined social exposome metrics provides an opportunity to understand the association between community context and neuroanatomy, identify protective and risk factors, and inform population-level interventions that mitigate risks and promote equitable brain health across the life-course.
The convergence of evidence linking early-life adversity to structural brain alterations raises important questions about the underlying biological pathways. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain how adverse social exposures become embedded in the brain. The stress embedding hypothesis suggests that chronic adversity becomes biologically ingrained through neuroendocrine, immune, and epigenetic pathways, leaving lasting signatures on brain development. Importantly, epigenetic modifications can persist across the life-course and may even be transmitted to subsequent generations, thereby extending the biological impact of adversity beyond the individual. The allostatic load model shifts attention from specific biological signatures to the cumulative wear and tear of repeated stress responses, which can disrupt developmental trajectories and increase disease vulnerability. Activation of stress systems such as the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and autonomic nervous system are adaptive in the short term. However, prolonged activation of these stress systems can lead to dysregulated cortisol signaling, immune function, and neural development over time. This dysregulation may heighten the risk for psychiatric and physical disease across the life-course. Complementing these perspectives, the stress acceleration or accelerated maturation hypothesis highlights that adversity may not only dysregulate biological systems but may also actively hasten the maturation of specific brain circuits, particularly those involved in emotion regulation and threat detection. Such accelerated maturation may be beneficial in the short term by enhancing survival in threatening environments but costly in the long term as it reduces plasticity and increases vulnerability to later disease.
These mechanistic hypotheses must be understood within the broader context of the brain’s prolonged developmental trajectory, which unfolds across the first few decades of life in a tightly orchestrated sequence. Each stage lays the foundation for the next, meaning that disruptions that occur early in development can have cascading, lifelong effects. Susceptibility to environmental influence across early life underscores that the impact of adversity depends not only on its presence and magnitude but also on when during the life-course it occurs.
Evidence from studies included in this review provide support for the mechanistic frameworks described above. For example, Botdorf et al. reported that higher deprivation was associated with smaller hippocampal volumes in 9- to 10-year-old children. Importantly, this structural anomaly mediated the longitudinal relationship between disadvantage and poorer memory outcomes. Similar findings were reported across other studies, in which greater disadvantage was linked not only to structural alterations but also to reduced performance on multiple neurocognitive tasks. These associations may be consistent with the allostatic load model, where the cumulative burden of stress disrupts developmental trajectories and erodes cognitive function. Such mediated pathways in young, cognitively unimpaired populations may represent the earliest steps in biological processes that not only contribute to later-life neurodegenerative disease, including Alzheimer’s disease, but also intersect with mechanisms of cognitive reserve. At the same time, a smaller subset of studies reported the opposite pattern: larger volumes, thicker cortices, or greater surface area in disadvantaged populations. These findings highlight that volumetric changes are not inherently good or bad; rather, both underdevelopment and overdevelopment may signal atypical trajectories. Such patterns may reflect stress-induced hypertrophy, delayed pruning, or accelerated maturation during sensitive periods. Alternatively, they could suggest the presence of protective or resiliency factors (e.g., family structure, social support) that buffer the adverse effects of disadvantage. Distinguishing between maladaptive hypertrophy, adaptive acceleration, and resilience will be critical for clarifying how adversity shapes neurodevelopment and for linking anatomy more directly to functional outcomes.
This scoping review is not without methodological and conceptual limitations. From a methodology standpoint, our initial search might not have identified all of the relevant literature. We mitigated this by using articles known to fulfill our inclusion criteria to build our search strings, completing an iterative review of the search terms with pilot reviews, maintaining a broad scope for the review to capture as much of the relevant literature as possible, and searching a multitude of databases without a time restriction. Additionally, the included articles and results are likely subject to selection bias given the scientific publication bias against null results. However, many of our studies reported nonsignificant effects for many of the brain regions examined.
Beyond methodological considerations, there are also important conceptual limitations in this area of research. Much of the existing literature is cross-sectional, limiting causal inference and our ability to assess trajectories over time. Studies have lacked harmonization in their metrics of social exposome exposure, geographic resolution, timing of exposure, and neuroanatomical outcomes, which precludes meta-analysis and constrains comparability across cohorts. Moreover, research has been conducted largely in White, Western populations, raising concerns about generalizability to more diverse groups who may experience distinct forms of adversity. Even widely used geographically defined exposome indices such as the ADI, while powerful, may obscure meaningful dimensionality within community-level disadvantage. For example, 2 neighborhoods may be equally classified as disadvantaged, but one may be defined by food insecurity and limited health care access, while another may be characterized by high violence, systemic racism, or poor educational opportunities. These differences likely influence neurodevelopment through distinct mechanisms. Finally, indices such as the ADI may correlate with unmeasured but biologically consequential factors. Factors such as prenatal alcohol or drug exposure, which are more prevalent in disadvantaged communities, represent important co-occurring factors with direct neurotoxic effects on neuronal proliferation, migration, and connectivity. This complexity complicates efforts to isolate the unique contribution of neighborhood-level disadvantage. Taken together, these conceptual limitations underscore the need for longitudinal, harmonized, and multidimensional approaches that integrate both individual-and community-level exposures across diverse populations
The implications of this work are 2-fold. The first is that it provides a foundation and framework within which the research field can continue investigating the association between adverse environmental exposures and neurodevelopment. Future work should aim to more precisely quantify the effect of exposure to the adverse social exposome on structural and functional outcomes. Simultaneously, research should examine resiliency factors that have shown themselves to be protective and advantageous, such as positive parenting or temperamental effortful control. The second implication lies in community-level interventions. By leveraging the research in tandem with community-level insights, we can begin shaping interventions to foster greater resilience in the face of adverse environments.
Early life represents a period of heightened sensitivity to environmental influences and insults and therefore may predispose individuals to a lifetime of consequences. Examination of early life is critical when thinking about life-course brain health. Understanding, researching, and intervening on modifiable, fundamental causes of brain health disparities in these early-life stages may promote improved cognitive reserves and resilience, while mitigating harmful exposures, all with the goal of improving the health disparities inherent in neurodevelopment and neurodegeneration.

CONCLUSIONS
This scoping review was the first to explore the extent of the literature pertaining to early-life adverse social exposome exposures and structural neurodevelopment. There was significant heterogeneity in the methods, populations, measures, and outcomes examined. However, there was overwhelming support for the notion that greater exposure to components of the adverse social exposome is associated with compromised structural neurodevelopment. Further research exploring these associations across the life-course and more deeply characterizing the timing, duration, and magnitude of exposures will provide significant insight into pathways to promote equitable brain health.
