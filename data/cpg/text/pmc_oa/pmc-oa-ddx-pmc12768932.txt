Advances in mead production and flavor characteristics: traditional and modern techniques, metabolic pathways of volatile compounds, and sensory evaluation
Mead, a historically significant fermented beverage, is experiencing renewed interest due to its distinctive sensory properties and potential health benefits. Despite this resurgence, substantial challenges persist in production optimization and flavor mechanism elucidation. This review systematically examines advances in mead production, emphasizing the systematic integration of traditional practices with modern biotechnological approaches. Critical research gaps are identified: the absence of standardized fermentation parameters (temperature, microbial consortia, aging conditions) and limited understanding of microbial conversion pathways for honey components, particularly non-Saccharomyces yeasts. Current research has characterized approximately 450 volatile compounds, with esters and terpenoids identified as crucial to the sensory profile. Nevertheless, sensory evaluation methodologies remain hampered by inconsistencies, subjectivity, and the lack of quantitative models correlating volatile compounds with sensory attributes. This review proposes future research priorities including process optimization, metabolic pathway elucidation, and standardization of sensory evaluation through multi-omics methodologies to advance production and quality control.

Introduction of Mead
Mead is a fermented alcoholic beverage made from honey, which has been enjoyed for centuries across cultures for consumption, entertainment, customs, and religious practices. Its quality and flavor characteristics are shaped both by the inheritance of traditional fermentation wisdom and the innovation of modern production technologies-two dimensions that together form the core of current mead research and industrial development, and also the key focus of this review. As one of the oldest fermented beverages in the world, mead had been found throughout the history of many countries. In fact, it was believed that mead was fermented before the agricultural revolution. The earliest record of mead was discovered in pottery from Henan province in northern China, dating back to around 9000 BCE. This pottery contained a fermented mixture of honey and wild grapes. Mead was also known as ‘drinkable honey’, ‘wedding wine’ or ‘love drink’. The term ‘honeymoon’ was coined due to the tradition of newlyweds drinking mead for a month after the ceremony to pray for their unborn children (; A. P.). In literature, mead was mentioned as a superior drink, such as in the Sag and Beowulf. In African markets, mead was even used as a medium of exchange. In Asia, mead was considered the drink of the nobility and the gods. People believed that drinking mead could improve their spirit, wisdom and life.
Before the invention of sucrose extraction, honey was the only natural sweetener available. It has high concentrations of the monosaccharide fructose and glucose. Therefore, honey might be the oldest high-quality carbon source for fermentation, explaining why mead is the oldest fermented alcoholic beverage. To reach the appropriate concentration before fermentation, the honey solution should be diluted 2-4 times due to the extreme osmotic pressure. This dilution process is not only the basis for initiating fermentation, but also indirectly affects the alcohol content and flavor balance (e.g., sweetness, body) of the final mead by regulating the initial sugar concentration, laying the foundation for subsequent sensory evaluation. In general, commercial mead's alcohol content can be controlled between 7.00 and 22.00 %, depending on the initial concentration of honey used in the fermentation (; Lima et al., 2021).
Various additives are mixed with the honey solution during the fermentation to improve the taste, flavor and color of mead, such as drugs, tonics, herbs and fruits. Basing on the fermentation technology, mead can be classified into different types, including the ingredient (pure mead and others), appearance (clear and cloudy mead), sparkling (sparkling mead and others), sugar content (dry mead, semi-dry mead, semi-sweet mead, or sweet mead), alcohol content (low alcohol mead, medium alcohol mead, high alcohol mead and over alcohol mead). Significant flavor differences exist among meads under different classification criteria-for example, fruit-added meads (e.g., melomel) often exhibit a fruit-dominated flavor profile, while oak-aged meads present woody and oxidative notes. The diversity of these flavors is closely related to production process selection and the metabolic accumulation of volatile compounds. The American Mead Makers Association and Beer Judge Certification provided new insights into mead and honey beverage styles such as spirit barrel aged, oak barrel aged, melomel, cyber, carbonated, metheglin, braggot, etc..
In ancient times, mead was primarily mass-produced in Africa, with these popular beverages exported to Europe via the Mediterranean. This was likely due to Africa's superior natural environment and large honey production. Nowadays, the main mead production industries have shifted to Asia, Europe, and America, with countries such as Japan, Poland, Italy, and Mexico. This shift is not only driven by their advanced industrial technologies, but also by their modern innovations in honey raw material selection, fermentation process control, and flavor optimization-for instance, regions in Europe and America have gradually applied technologies such as precise fermentation control and volatile compound metabolic pathway analysis to production, aiming to directionally enhance the sensory quality of mead. This is not only due to their well-developed industrial technology, but also their increasing honey production. The global mead beverage market has reached around USD 500 million and is expected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of approximately 18.00 % to over USD 1600 million by 2028. The market is dominated by the overseas markets of Europe and the Americas, which together accounted for more than 70.00 % of global sales, while Asia currently accounted for 18.70 %.

Honey used for Mead
Honey is a natural substance with high sugar content, low water content, and numerous biological activities. Its chemical composition is determined by two key sources that directly influence mead sensory characteristics: nectar from flowering plants (the primary origin of flavor precursors) and bee metabolic processes (which modify nectar components). Honey is collected by bees from floral nectar and undergoes natural transformation in their honey sacs via two enzymatic processes. The first process hydrolyzes sucrose (from nectar) into glucose and fructose-monosaccharides that not only drive fermentation but also affect mead final sweetness. The second process, catalyzed by bee-derived amylase and glucose oxidase, converts trace starch (from nectar) into maltose and glucose, while producing gluconic acid (regulates mead acidity to balance flavor) and hydrogen peroxide (negligible sensory impact). Honey typically has a water content of 16.00-18.00 %, an ash content of 0.10-0.20 %, and a pH of 3.80-4.00; these properties, rooted in floral source and bee metabolism, lay the foundation for consistent mead sensory quality.
The main constituents of honey are sugars, with glucose and fructose (85.00 to 95.00 % of total carbohydrates) derived primarily from floral nectar-their ratio, which varies by plant species, directly shapes mead sweetness and mouthfeel (A. P.). In addition, honey contains small amounts of sensory-relevant nutrients with distinct sources: amino acids (e.g., proline, glutamate, tryptophan) from floral nectar and bee glandular secretions, which serve as precursors for higher alcohols (e.g., phenylethanol) that enhance mead flavor complexity; organic acids (e.g., citric, formic acid from nectar, gluconic acid from bee enzymes) that adjust mead acidity to avoid cloying sweetness; vitamins (e.g., thiamine, vitamin C) and minerals (e.g., potassium, calcium) from nectar, which support yeast metabolism to ensure stable flavor formation; and antioxidants (e.g., flavonoids, phenolic acids) exclusively from floral tissues, which retain activity during fermentation to reduce oxidative off-flavors and enrich aroma layers (; A. P.).
When honey is fermented into mead, yeast-derived enzymes catalyze the conversion of honey source-specific compounds into ethanol and key sensory-active substances-directly linking floral/bee-derived precursors to mead flavor characteristics. Initially, sucrose (from floral nectar, unusable by yeast) is hydrolyzed into glucose and fructose by invertase (secreted by yeast or trace amounts in honey), providing monosaccharides for yeast metabolism. These monosaccharides then undergo transformation via the glycolytic pathway (Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas, EMP pathway) in yeast cytoplasm: hexokinase first activates glucose and fructose by coupling them with ATP to form glucose-6-phosphate or fructose-6-phosphate; subsequent reactions generate pyruvate, which is then decarboxylated by yeast-specific pyruvate decarboxylase to produce acetaldehyde and CO₂; finally, alcohol dehydrogenase facilitates the reduction of acetaldehyde to ethanol using hydrogen from NADH, with each monosaccharide molecule yielding 2 molecules of ethanol, 2 molecules of CO₂, and a small amount of ATP to sustain yeast metabolism (A. P.). Concurrently, auxiliary enzymatic reactions amplify sensory attributes: ester synthases (e.g., acetyl-CoA transferase) react ethanol with honey organic acids (nectar-derived citric acid, bee-derived gluconic acid) to form esters, imparting fruity and floral notes (core mead aromas); transaminases convert honey amino acids (nectar/bee-derived) into higher alcohols (e.g., isoamyl alcohol) that enhance mouthfeel depth (; X.).Meanwhile, honey intrinsic glucose oxidase minimally maintains fermentation pH (supporting consistent flavor) and amylase slightly breaks down residual nectar polysaccharides to supplement substrates-both contribute to stable sensory outcomes without altering key flavors.

Honey source
The quality of mead depends heavily on its primary ingredient, honey, whose composition, color, aroma, taste, and physicochemical properties directly determine hedonic quality of mead (; X.). Honey is categorized into nectar honey and honeydew honey. The latter, derived from plant secretions (e.g., pine, fir, chestnut, oak) associated with aphids, contains higher polyphenols, conferring stronger flavor that enhances sensory attributes of mead (; A. P.). Mead fermented from honeydew also exhibits elevated antioxidant activity and oligosaccharide concentrations, traits that improve sensory acceptance (a core dimension of hedonic quality). Additionally, honey color, an indicator of mineral and nutrient content of honey, further modulates hedonic properties of mead via flavor depth. Notably, darker honey varieties show greater fermentability due to richer nutrient profiles, with color intensity influencing fermentative performance and thus final hedonic attributes of mead. These characteristics of honey are shaped by key factors driving hedonic quality of mead: bee species, floral origin, climate, season, geographical location, collection method, and post-harvest handling.
Honey quality is critical to mead production; region-specific honey sources-shaped by local conditions-thus exert significant effects on hedonic properties of mead.
In northwestern Portugal, mead uses dark multifloral honey from chestnut cone pollen, whose dark color and high polyphenols contribute to robust, full-bodied hedonic traits of mead (A. P.). In Valencia-Granada, mead ferments with sugarcane syrup and black honey, where dense nutrients of black honey enhance fermentation and impart rich, sweet aromas (key hedonic drivers of mead). In Málaga mountains, high-quality amber clear honey is used, its light color and clean flavor yielding delicate, balanced hedonic profiles of mead. Honey is the primary crop in Italy, particularly in Sicily. Locally-sourced or multifloral nectar (mixed with Stinky Toon nectar) is commonly used for mead fermentation. In Trás-os-Montes (Portugal), dark/light honey blends are used: dark honey adds flavor intensity, while light honey provides sweetness, achieving balanced hedonic properties of mead (; A. P.). In northeastern Austria, mineral-rich honey supports fermentation and enhances flavor richness, improving hedonic quality of mead. In Silesia (southern Poland), mead uses floral, buckwheat, and honeydew nectar, with earthy notes of buckwheat and strong flavor of honeydew forming unique hedonic characteristics of mead. In Zitnyostrov (Slovakia), sunflower/wildflower/thistle nectar is used, its bright floral aromas defining fresh hedonic traits of mead. In the Czech Republic and Ethiopia, raw honey enables natural fermentation, preserving original floral flavors to maintain authentic hedonic appeal of mead. In Southeast Asia, the native bees such as Apis florea, A. dorsata, A. cerana, and A. laboriosa produce various types of honey, including purple cloudberry honey (which comes from purple flowers), buckwheat honey (from white flowers of buckwheat grains), clover honey, acacia honey, and so on. In China, mead uses peony/acacia/multifloral/date palm/linden honey, each floral source tailoring hedonic profiles of mead via specific sensory traits (R.; Fentie, Jeong, Emire, Demsash, Kim, Jeon, et al., 2022). The honey for the fermentation of mead in Arabia comes from the sidr tree (Ziziphus spp.). In the Nagaland Bee Group and Honey Colony region of Nagaland, India, stingless bee honey is used to make local mead. The mead production in the Americas is concentrated in tropics, where the honey produced is generally liquid, is rapidly fermented and has distinct flavored. In Brazil, stingless bee honey is used to make mead in states of Paraná, Bahia, São Paulo, and Rio Grande did Sul. The most renowned honey types are the plum and orange blossom bees distinctive flavor, greater fluidity, and slow crystallization. In America, the honey used to produce mead primarily originates from New York State, featuring varieties such as alfalfa honey, buckwheat honey, and autumn flower honey. Meanwhile, Pennsylvania predominantly produces wild thyme honey for this purpose. Africa produces a tropical honey that quickly ferments into a liquid form of mead. In Eastern Cape of South Africa, Xhosa and Khoi-San people enjoy local Iqhilika mead. In the Kaduna region, Enugu State, and Nigeria, stingless honey was mainly used for the mead fermentation.

Functions of honey
Honey functions can be clearly categorized based on the health properties and hedonic properties of the resulting product; the specific functions corresponding to these two types of properties are elaborated in separate paragraphs to ensure clear differentiation and logical clarity.
From the perspective of health properties, honey functional value is rooted in its rich bioactive components. It contains over 150 identified polyphenolic compounds, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, flavanols, and catechins, as well as non-polyphenolic functional substances such as ascorbic acid, β-carotene, catalase, and peroxidase. These components collectively contribute to reducing the risk of diseases like cardiovascular diseases, malignancies, immune system disorders, and autism. Furthermore, honey exhibits robust antibacterial activity, driven by synergistic factors: high osmotic pressure, acidic pH (3.2-4.5), non-peroxide components (e.g., methylglyoxal), honey bee defensins, and hydroxyl radicals (generated from hydrogen peroxide and minerals). It also has well-documented healing properties for wounds and burns, attributed to its ability to maintain a moist environment, inhibit pathogens, and promote tissue regeneration.
From the perspective of hedonic properties, honey mainly serves to optimize food sensory experience and enhance food processing functionality, thereby improving the overall acceptability of products like mead. As a natural sweetener, it enhances the flavor and sweetness of food products with a nuanced profile distinct from refined sugars, aligning with consumer demand for palatable and nutritious food. In food processing, it acts as an efficient browning inhibitor for fruits and vegetables by suppressing the catalytic activity of polyphenol oxidase-a key role in preserving the visual and flavor integrity of mead, especially when fruit adjuncts are used. Under specific conditions, it also functions as a clarifying agent: adding 1.00 %-10.00 % (w/v) honey to juice (or mead must), stirring thoroughly, and standing for several hours induces precipitation via protein-phenolic interactions; subsequent filtration yields a pure, non-browning liquid, and this function directly enhances the visual hedonic quality of mead.

Classification of Mead

Pure honey Mead
Pure honey meads are generally produced through the fermentation of diluted honey, without any additives, and are further classified into two types: single-flower honey meads and multifloral honey meads (A. P.). Single-flower honey is identified by a purity of over 80.00 %. If it reaches 95.00 %, it has the best quality. Generally, single-flower honey shows better stability in composition and physicochemical properties, ensuring the stability of mead quality. R. Vidrih et al. respectively used honey from chestnut, lime, and honeydew to ferment mead with high alcohol concentration. Ildikó Bénes et al. fermented honey from sunflower, field flower, and thistle nectar in Slovakia to produce mead with high contents of ethyl caproate, ethyl caprylate, and isobutanol, providing more fruity flavors. A.P. Pereira et al. reported that honey from Castanea spp. and Erica spp. in the northeast of Portugal is used for mead production. They identified 4-vinylphenol, an anti-cancer agent that induces apoptosis in the human body, in fermented Castanea spp. and Erica spp. honeys from northeastern Portugal, and discovered it in these two multifloral mead (A. P.). Researches about multifloral honey meads is mainly reported in Brazil, Portugal, India, Czech Republic, Italy, Spain, and Poland. Pascoal et al. used the honey from northern Portugal to ferment the mead and volatile fatty acids were detected, such as octanoic acid and capric acid, providing the geranium, vegetable, sweat, and cheese flavor (, 2019). Fermentation of Portuguese multifloral honey to obtain meads with a hair acidity range of 0.12 and 0.78 g/L was carried out by Gomes et al. They found that the fermentation of mead with multifloral honey resulted in a mead with higher sweetness and lower acidity that was more popular with the public (T.). Cavalcante Da Silva et al. and Lopes et al. detected three terpenoids in Brazilian multifloral honey: nerolidol, 2,3-dihydrofarnesol, and farnesol, they gave the mead notes like rose, lily of the valley and apple blossom. Schwarz et al. reported MHM from Brazilian with the higher content of 2-phenylethanol (43.70 mg/L), leading to floral, herbal, and fruity flavors. Klikarová et al. analyzed the concentration of proline in Czech MHM (173.00-266.00 mg/L), which could be used to assess the authenticity, adulteration, and quality of mead. Chitarrini et al. found isoamyl alcohol (110.30 mg/L) and isobutanol (63.30 mg/L) in an Italian multifloral mead, which produced solvent, sweet, and meadow flavors. Česlová et al. found salicylic acid (3.90 mg/L) in Polish MHM, which would stimulate the gastric acid metabolism and increase appetite. Single-flower honey meads and multifloral honey meads show significant differences in flavor and functional component diversity. Honey from different regions and sources produces unique flavor compounds and bioactive substances during fermentation, further enriching the diverse flavor profiles of mead. Single-flower honey meads, with the purity and stability of the honey, can present a higher-quality taste and higher alcohol content. In contrast, multifloral honey meads, with the diversity of honey sources, impart a richer and more complex aroma, attracting a broader consumer base.

Complex Mead
Complex mead, a beverage that incorporates a diverse array of fruits, herbs, spices, grains, and vegetables, represents a significant intersection between fermentation science and food chemistry. The production methods for these meads are highly varied, often involving techniques such as staggered nutrient additions to support healthy fermentation, the use of non-Saccharomyces yeasts or sequential fermentation to create unique aromatic profiles, and extended aging on materials like oak, lees, or fruit skins. These practices not only enrich the sensory qualities of mead, including its multilayered flavor, aromatic complexity, and mouthfeel, but also contribute substantially to its nutritional profile and bioactive compounds, enhancing its functional properties. From temperate fruits such as grapes and berries to tropical ingredients like papaya and mango, the different climatic origins of these fruits create a complex network of chemical interactions that significantly influence the fermentation process. The acids, sugars, antioxidants, and volatile aroma compounds present in these ingredients play a crucial role in shaping the hedonically diverse flavor profile, intricate aroma, and textured body of the mead. Moreover, the incorporation of herbs and spices through methods like tincturing, dry-hopping, or secondary fermentation further improves the fermentation environment, supporting yeast growth and enhancing fermentation efficiency, while also increasing the bioactive potential of the final product. These ingredients not only optimize the sensory experience by adding layers of spice, earthiness, or herbal notes that complement the honey's sweetness but also provide potential health benefits, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory effects (; Lima et al., 2021). Understanding the chemical interactions among these various components, as well as the impact of specific production techniques, is essential for advancing mead production, offering new opportunities for flavor innovation and functional enhancement. This knowledge holds promising implications for the future development of the mead industry, particularly in enhancing product quality and health-related benefits.

Fruit Mead
The addition of fruit juice to honey fermentation can improve the pH and growth factors for yeast. And the addition of fruit can give the mead different colors, such as straw (kiwi), orange‑gold (mango, papaya, guava) and purple (pomegranate). Fruit meads can be categorized according to the different climatic regions in which they are used.
The main sources of Temperate fruit mead are in Europe and Asia. Grape mead has characteristics similar to wine in terms of acidity, tannin, and other substances in the histories of China and Europe. Honey added during the fermentation of grape mead increased the sugar content of the fermentation broth and balanced the fruit flavors of the mead. Russia and Lithuania make mead by adding berries known as midis, medovukha, or stavlenniy myod. These fruits contain malic acid, citric acid and other substances, these acids can be combined with ethanol to reduce the concentration of ethanol, which is conducive to reducing the stimulation of the gastrointestinal mucosa after drinking alcohol, to help relieve gastrointestinal discomfort symptoms. Blueberries and blackberries are used in some regions of Brazil. Blueberry mead has a light blue color and a delicate aroma and flavor, while blackberry mead is darker. In Australia, fresh peaches are added to enhance the fruit flavor and produce a pink hue (Lima et al., 2021). Cherries are widely used in the United States, producing a dark reddish-brown mead rich in cyclic enol ether terpenoids. They inhibit Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli and Salmonella and are used to treat skin conditions, colds and fevers. Cyberd is a straw to golden coloured mead fermented from a blend of apple juice and honey. Small amounts of butyl 2-methylbutyrate and hexyl acetate have been detected, giving the mead its apple aroma and flavor. Blackcurrants are another fruit that can be added to mead, and mead made from them has been found to have higher levels of citronellol, a-terpinol and nonanal, which can give the classic aldehyde notes of waxy, citrus, floral and green.
Subtropical fruit meads have their origins in the Americas. Bolivia has their own mead called Sima. The additional lemon juice and zest are used to enhance the mead flavor and provide an acid environment for fermentation. In desert regions, cacti are used to make mead, which has a slightly bitter flavor due to 1,1-diethoxyethane (acetaldehyde diethyl acetal or simply acetal). This substance is also found in malt whisky and sherry.
Tropical regions use a variety of fruits to make mead, including papaya, mango, kiwi, guava, and coconut. In Nigeria, honey is diluted with date juice and date mead have made with yeast isolated from palm wine. This mead has a high crude fat content, which maintains normal body temperature and protected the nervous system. In Thailand, mead is made by fermenting pineapple juice with honey, and pineapple peel is part of the yeast culture. Methyl caproate, methyl 3-methylthiopropionate, ethyl caproate and methyl butyrate in mead have pineapple flavors, while methyl 2-methylbutyrate and methyl caprylate have distinctive fruity notes.
The addition of fruits not only enriches the color and flavor of mead but also provides various bioactive compounds, offering more health benefits to the mead. Different climatic regions and fruit types provide abundant options for flavor innovation in mead. Fruit meads from temperate regions, such as those made with grapes and berries, are characterized by acidity, tannins, and other distinctive flavors, while those from subtropical and tropical regions showcase the refreshing and unique aromas of tropical fruits. By optimizing the addition of various fruits, the flavor and functional components of mead are effectively enhanced, promoting development and innovation in this field.

Other Mead
Herbs, spices, grains or vegetables are reported to be used to improve the fermentation environment, nutritional value and flavor of the mead. Herbal mead includes a mead called iQhilika, produced in the Eastern Cape province of South Africa. This mead contains butanol, amyl alcohol and isoamyl alcohol, which give a warming sensation to the mouth when consumed. Also in Mexico, the Maya Indians use the bark of the legume Lonchocarpus violaceus to make Balche, which is also flavored. Mucuna pruriens roots are mixed with honey to make this mead. Ethiopian mead, Tej, is made using coarse honey and gesho (R. prinoides) in a spontaneous fermentation process, with malt powder added during the fermentation process to enhance the bitter flavor (Fentie, Jeong, Emire, Demsash, Kim, Jeon, et al., 2022). In Asia, Chinese wolfberry and dandelion are added to mead, where the polysaccharides from Chinese wolfberry and the inulin from dandelion have particular health benefits. Adding hops to mead during the brewing process results in pink mead known as ‘metheglin’, providing a unique flavor, clarity, and freshness. Coniferous herb can also be added to the fermentation process to enhance the flavor, increase the rate of mead fermentation, and aid yeast cell growth. Flower petals, including roses, dandelions, and lavender, can be added during mead fermentation, as can blends of tea and other flowers (such as heather, hop bulbs, honeysuckle, elderflowers, and roses) to enhance flavor.
Spiced mead, also a type of metheglin, is a type of mead produced by diluting honey with spice solutions. The addition of spices, such as white pepper, black pepper, mint, and cinnamon, can significantly affect the aroma and flavor of mead, but not its color. In Croatia and Nepal, this variety of mead is called Medovina, Gverc or Dandaharai. In regions where chili peppers are abundant, they are also used to change the flavor and hasten fermentation. In South Africa, Mustard Mead is produced by fermenting a mixture of freshly ground wholegrain black mustard, yellow mustard, honey, and salt.
Coarse grains can also be added to the mead-making process. Soybeans, for example, increase the protein content and assimilable nitrogen content. Black rice can be used to produce mead with higher levels of polyphenolic compounds, which have greater antioxidant activity. The mead fermented from malted barley and wheat is known as “Bracket” or “Bragget”. This mead struck a balance between beer and liqueur, especially with a honey character in the maltiness and bitterness.
Vegetables, rich in vitamins and minerals that aid fermentation and improve flavor, have been less studied. Araújod et al. found that the addition of cowpea extract to mead promotes the growth of yeast cells and increases the alcohol content of the mead.
The addition of various herbs, spices, grains, and vegetables not only enhances the flavor of mead but also provides rich nutritional value and bioactive compounds. Traditional brewing methods from different regions demonstrate the unique impact of these additives on mead flavor. From herbs and spices to fruits and vegetables, the innovative use of these ingredients further promotes the diversity of mead and enhances its health benefits. These studies offer new directions for the mead industry, particularly in improving flavor and enhancing health functionality.

Alcohol and sugar content
Mead can be classified according to its alcohol and sugar content, which varies depending on the quality of honey, yeast type, brewing method, and maturation process. According to surveys, the alcohol content of mead available in the market ranges from 3.00 % to 22.00 %. Depending on the residual sugar, the sweetness of mead can be classified as dry, semi-dry, semi-sweet, or sweet (; T.). Based on alcohol content, mead is categorized into three types: low-alcohol (<7.00 %), medium-alcohol (12.50 %-13.50 %), and high-alcohol (≥13.50 %) (; X.). Dry mead has a sugar content of less than 10.00 g/L and required the consumption of most of the fermentable sugar during fermentation. Semi-dry mead contains 10.00-20.00 g/L of sugar. Semi-sweet mead contains 20.00-50.00 g/L of sugar. Sweet mead has a sugar content of more than 50.00 g/L. These classifications not only help producers make precise adjustments based on varying alcohol content and sweetness requirements, but also provide consumers with a wider range of options, catering to different taste preferences and occasions.

High sugar content and osmotic pressure can inhibit yeast fermentation, which is why honey is usually diluted prior to fermentation. Diluting honey to 15.00-18.00 % (w/v) is required for low-alcohol mead production, while dilution of the honey fermentation broth to 30.00-38.00 % (w/v) is necessary for high-alcohol mead production (; T.; A. P. Pereira, Mendes-Ferreira, Estevinho, et al., 2014). Researchers have used honey solutions with different sugar contents for fermentation, and found that a honey-to-water ratio of 1:4 or 1:3 is optimal (; R.). In addition to increasing the proportion of honey in the production of high alcohol mead, honey can also be added continuously, in batches and staggered. For example, Ribeiro and Van Mullem found that adding 50.00 g of honey to the fermentation broth after 48 h (via batch and staggered addition) resulted in higher ethanol concentration and lower residual sugar content. Overall, honey dilution ratio directly affects the fermentation process and final mead quality. An appropriate honey-to-water ratio helps to improve fermentation efficiency, ensuring alcohol production while controlling residual sugar levels. Batch or staggered honey addition can also optimize fermentation, enhance ethanol yield, and reduce unnecessary sugar accumulation, providing a valuable technical approach for the production of high-alcohol mead (Table 1).

Pretreatment
Before fermentation, sterilization is a crucial step to eliminate harmful micro-organisms. Pasteurization or the addition of SO2 are common methods. Pasteurization can remove pollen and other protein-derived substances reducing the haze formation, but also has the negative effects on the vitamin activities.
The honey fermentation broth undergoes pasteurization to ensure that the honey fermentation broth is sterile. The sterilized honey fermentation broth is then filtered using centrifugation, which reduces insoluble solids. However, heat treatment may also have adverse effects on mead. For example, heat can cause the degradation of heat-stable compounds like certain phenolic acids and flavonoids found in honey.
The addition of sulfur dioxide to the mead pre-treatment has less effect on the composition of the mead and provided greater inhibition of harmful bacteria. In honey fermentation solutions, lactic acid bacteria are the main bacterial population. To prevent the growth of lactic acid bacteria, potassium metabisulphite was added to the honey fermentation broth until the concentration of free SO2 reached a certain level. Researchers have added 50.00-150.00 mg/L potassium metabisulphite to the honey fermentation broth. Andrae et al. added sodium metabisulphite (8.00 g/L) to the honey fermentation broth with a 6.00 % volume fraction of SO2. However, the addition of SO2 can cause adverse reactions in consumers, such as allergies, headaches, and abdominal pain. To minimize these methods impact on mead organoleptic properties, membrane filtration techniques have been developed for use during fermentation-specifically, ultrafiltration membranes with different molecular weight cut-offs (10–50 K). However, ultrafiltration technology needs to be further improved due to the tendency of ultrafiltration membranes to clog, its low efficiency, and high cost in practice.
In conclusion, sterilization and filtration technologies are critical steps in ensuring the quality of mead. However, their potential negative effects highlight the need for further exploration of more efficient and low-impact technological solutions to optimize the flavor, quality, and safety of mead (Table 1).

Fermentation initiation: Nutritional and biological factors

Nitrogen sources
Nitrogen plays a crucial role in yeast activity, promoting its proliferation, maintaining its metabolism, altering its autophagy/autolysis processes, and enhancing its fermentation capacity. However, honey has a low content of assimilable nitrogen, and this requires additional nitrogen supplementation during mead production. Assimilable nitrogen in mead is referred to as YANT, which includes ammoniacal nitrogen, amino acids, small peptides, and easily assimilable proteins. YAN concentration affects the fermentation rate, production of higher alcohols/esters and the distribution of volatile compounds in mead. The formaldehyde index method is typically used to measure YAN. YAN is regulated by international standards, based on the International Brewing Code (OIV 2016) and Brazilian legislation (Brasil 2016), with diammonium hydrogen phosphate addition limited to less than 300.00 mg/L. Generally, most researchers suggest that adding 267.00 mg/L YAN is optimal for mead fermentation. Insufficient or excessive assimilable nitrogen during the fermentation process leads to slow/stagnant fermentation or the production of off-odor substances such as hydrogen sulfide and mercaptans (Table 1).

Pollen
Pollen is collected by bees from flowers and comprises sugars, proteins, amino acids (at least 20.00 %, main ones: proline, aspartic acid, phenylalanine, glutamic acid, lysine, leucine), water (7.40 %), lipids (6.00 %), ash (2.20 %), minerals, vitamins, and carotenoids (A. P.). The addition of pollen during mead fermentation has been shown to affect the rate of fermentation and the levels of organic acids and aromatic compounds. Specifically, it increases fermentation efficiency by over 10.00 %, according to Roldán et al. and Sousa-Dias et al.. Adding around 30.00 g/L of pollen can enhance mead quality, but microbial stability decreases at additions exceeding 50.00 g/L. The effect of pollen addition on organic acid production during mead fermentation varies among researchers. However, other researchers suggested that pollen may cause salinization, leading to lower acidity. Regarding flavor compounds, Pons et al. found that adding pollen could increase the contents of ethyl acetate, ethyl succinate, phenethyl acetate, ethyl caprylate, higher alcohols, and organic acids in mead (X.).

Nitrogen sources in chemicals
As an alternative to expensive pollen, chemical nitrogen sources are the main nitrogen source for commercial mead production. Although they can speed up the fermentation process, they do not improve the flavor of mead. Researchers have added different levels of diammonium phosphate to the mead fermentation process, ranging from 0.26 to 1.26 g/L (; A. P.). Ammonium sulfate can also be added during mead fermentation at a concentration of 0.02-2.53 g/L.

Nitrogen sources of animal origin
Nitrogen of animal origin is mainly present in honey (in its original form), as well as in yeast extracts and insect extracts. They provide the yeast with richer nutrients and accelerate mead fermentation. These proteins are primarily in the form of enzymes, including amylase, catalase, glucose oxidase, peroxidase, and acid phosphatase. For example, Šmogrovičová et al. used honey bee larvae extract (0.10 g/L) as an additional nitrogen source. Ajogun et al. added yeast extract (0.10 g/L) as an assimilable nitrogen source.

Yeast selection and inoculation
Yeast ferments sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide, producing foods like mead, wine, and bread. During the fermentation process, yeast produces numerous metabolites, most of which are synthesized when sugars and other nutrients are taken up by the yeast in high quantities. These metabolites entered the catabolic pathway at the pyruvate level (C. H.). When honey is used as a fermentation medium for mead, it can create stress for many yeasts, hindering their growth and reproduction. Excessive osmotic pressure can result in undesirable flavors in the mead, such as high volatile acidity or unwanted esters. The yeast used for mead fermentation should be ethanol-resistant and highly flocculent to facilitate easy removal from the fermentation plant and to reduce turbidity in the final product. Commercial brewing yeasts come in different strains. Researchers have experimented with these strains and measured key parameters of the finished meads (Table 1).

Other nutritional factors

Minerals and inorganic salts
Minerals are crucial components of yeast cell membranes, as they help maintain smooth cellular metabolism. Mineral supplementation during mead fermentation affects the fermentation rate, glycerol/ethanol ratio, cellular balance, intracellular pH, and enzyme activity. A lack of minerals during fermentation can negatively affect yeast metabolism, making the yeast sensitive to stress, ultimately resulting in poor fermentation and sensory characteristics of the mead. For instance, it can lead to the reduces in ethyl acetate, acetic acid, and volatile acidity.
Different minerals have various effects on mead fermentation. Calcium is a cofactor in several metabolic pathways and affects the rate of sugar conversion. Manganese affects the utilization of nitrogen and the production of aromatic compounds by the yeast during fermentation (; A. P. Pereira, Mendes-Ferreira, Estevinho, et al., 2015). Zinc has a positive effect on yeast respiration and growth rate, while magnesium and phosphorus are essential for ATP synthesis. Sodium plays a crucial role in yeast cell metabolism and potassium transport. Potassium has a significant effect on the ethanol content, glycerol/ethanol ratio, total acidity and residual nitrogen of mead. These minerals are generally added in two ways. One is addition in the form of alkaline inorganic salts. For instance, several researchers have added inorganic salts, such as potassium hydrogen tartrate and magnesium chloride, to the honey fermentation broth (; R.; A. P. Pereira, Mendes-Ferreira, Estevinho, et al., 2015). The other is to dilute honey with mineral-containing water. For instance, Pascoal et al. used mineral water containing Cl1- 3.00 mg/L, Na1+ 9.30 mg/L, Ca2+ 6.20 mg/L, SiO2 16.00 mg/L, and a total mineralization of 80.00 mg/L to dilute honey. Sroka et al. used drinking water containing 66.00 mg/L Ca2+ and 9.00 mg/L Mg2+ to dilute honey.
In summary, minerals are essential for yeast metabolism and mead fermentation. They influence key fermentation parameters such as sugar conversion, yeast respiration, and the synthesis of aromatic compounds. Calcium, manganese, potassium, zinc, and magnesium each play specific roles in optimizing fermentation efficiency and the final product sensory characteristics. Mineral supplementation, whether through inorganic salts or mineral-rich water, enhances fermentation kinetics and improves mead quality. Therefore, precise mineral management is crucial for achieving optimal fermentation and ensuring the production of high-quality mead.

Vitamins
Addition of vitamins during mead fermentation decreases alcohol dehydrogenase activity and fatty acid synthesis, which affects the rate of fermentation, fermentation characteristics and organoleptic properties of mead. Deficiency in vitamins affects yeast metabolism and stress sensitivity (A. P.).
For instance, thiamine is a coenzyme in the alcoholic fermentation pathway, stimulating yeast growth, speeding up fermentation, and reducing the production of SO2-binding compounds. Biotin slows down the assimilation and growth of yeast cells (A. P. Pereira, Mendes-Ferreira, Estevinho, et al., 2015). However, researchers generally add multivitamins to the mead fermentation process, and some of these vitamins work synergistically with each other to enhance their absorption and utilization. For example, Akalın and Bénes added thiamine (0.30-0.50 mg/L). Pereira A et al. added a vitamin complex (100.00 mg/L inositol, 2.00 mg/L pyridoxine, 2.00 mg/L niacin, 1.00 mg/L calcium pantothenate, 0.50 mg/L thiamine, 0.20 mg/L riboflavin, and 0.125 mg/L biotin) to the honey fermentation broth (A. P.). Schwarz L et al. added a multivitamin (2.00 g/L inositol, 0.20 g/L niacin, 0.15 g/L calcium pantothenate, 0.025 g/L thiamine, 0.025 g/L pyridoxine, 0.0050 g/L biotin).
Thiamine primarily promotes yeast growth and accelerates alcohol fermentation, while the thiamine complex enhances yeast metabolic activity and fermentation efficiency through synergistic effects, further improving the organoleptic quality of mead. Therefore, appropriate vitamin supplementation can effectively optimize mead, improving the flavor and quality of the final product.

Saccharifying agents
Honey may contain polysaccharides that are not fermentable by yeast, such as starch, cellulose, dextrin and panose. Saccharifying agents act as catalysts to convert polysaccharides into fermentable sugars. For example, Katoh et al. and Koguchi et al. used Aspergillus oryzae as a saccharifying agent in black rice-honey fermentation broth. They successfully converted non-fermentable polysaccharides in honey, such as starch and dextrin, into fermentable sugars, thus accelerating the fermentation process and improving ethanol yield. These methods effectively addressed the challenges posed by non-fermentable polysaccharides in honey, enhancing the efficiency and stability of mead production.

Fermentation process control: Key abiotic and biotic factors

Abiotic factors

Temperature
Temperature can significantly impact yeast cell viability, growth rate, duration of exponential phase, enzyme activity, and membrane action. The traditional fermentation temperature for mead is controlled at 20 to 30 °C. Lower fermentation temperatures help to achieve a stable fermentation of the honey solution, which in turn improves the flavor and stability of the mead. Meads fermented at these temperatures have a relatively higher phenolic content and a more consistent variation in alcohol content than those fermented at higher temperatures. Too low a temperature can inhibit yeast fermentation, lengthening the mead production cycle and increasing production costs. If mead is fermented above 30 °C, high temperatures speed up the fermentation process and encourage the growth of large numbers of harmful bacteria, which affects the alcohol concentration of mead and can give it a mash-like flavor.

Fermentation time
The length of fermentation time for mead can range from a few weeks to several months and is influenced by several factors such as the honey-to-water ratio, type of honey, yeast strain, nutrients, and pH (; A. P.).
Some researchers have completed mead fermentation in as little as 7-10 days (A. P. Pereira, Mendes-Ferreira, Estevinho, et al., 2015). As the fermentation time is shorter in this case, the resulting mead has lower alcohol content and higher sugar content, which generally yields low-alcohol mead. Mead is often fermented for 12-21 days. For this duration, the alcohol content of the fermented mead increases significantly, and the Brix level decreases- this fermentation duration is generally used to produce medium-bodied meads. The mead produced through this process is a high-alcohol mead with significantly lower sugar levels but retains a sweet honey flavor.
If the fermentation period is longer than 3 weeks, a dry mead is obtained: a slightly astringent mead with a sweet aftertaste. These meads generally undergo two fermentations: the first is shorter, primarily aimed at promoting better yeast growth, usually lasting 2-4 days. The second fermentation lasts more than three weeks; the longer the fermentation, the greater the sugar consumption, the lower the sugar content, and the higher the alcohol content.

pH
To balance sweetness and acidity in the honey fermentation solution, producers adjust the pH of the solution, which affects the growth and metabolism of harmful microorganisms. Organic acids such as citric, tartaric, or malic acids can be added to the solution to prevent foaming during fermentation. While citric acid improves flavor, tartaric and malic acids are recommended because they increase the buffering capacity of the honey solution and prevent lactic acid bacteria from metabolizing it (A. P.). However, if the pH is too low, the yeast becomes inefficient, producing acetic acid and succinic acid and stopping fermentation. Researchers have found that pH values below 4.60 are desirable in mead fermentation because they minimize unwanted microbial growth and colonization in the system. Most honey fermentation solutions typically maintain a pH range of 3.00-4.50, which helps inhibit the growth of harmful microorganisms and provides an ideal environment for yeast activity and fermentation efficiency. (Amorim et al., 2018; A. P.).

Light
It is important to keep mead away from light during the fermentation process. Light can have many detrimental effects on the fermentation process of honey solutions. Exposure to light during mead reduces and prevents normal fermentation. Exposing mead to sunlight after fermentation is complete causes chemical reactions of the amino acids in the mead, producing substances with a foul odor; it also changes the color of the mead, as some of the pigments in mead are easily broken down by light.

Biotic factors

Microorganisms
In mead production, raw materials pretreatment is essential to remove microbial contaminants from sources such as pollen, the bee digestive tract, and the environment. However, secondary contamination during post-fermentation processes like filtration, canning, and maturation remains a risk, often due to handlers, the production environment, and equipment. Pathogens like Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus spp. are common secondary contaminants, posing significant health risks such as fever, vomiting, and diarrhea. To mitigate these risks, strict hygiene protocols must be followed, including regular disinfection of equipment, supervision of personnel health, and adherence to food safety standards. Comprehensive quality control and microbial testing ensure product safety, protecting consumer health and maintaining product quality.

Fermentation monitoring
The fermentation process is monitored to ensure the production of consistent and high-quality mead. Key test indicators generally include: fermentation rate is determined by linear regression of the exponential phase of CO2 weight loss after three days of wort fermentation. Daily CO2 production is estimated as CO2/day. Fermentation is considered complete when the weight of the honey fermentation broth remained constant for 48 h. Fermentation vigor is expressed as the amount of ethanol produced at the end of fermentation. Fermentation purity is expressed as the ratio of volatile acids to ethanol production, with units of grams of volatile acid per litre of ethanol (% V/V). In mead production, ethanol fermentation yield is calculated as: ethanol yield (%) = (sugar consumption (g/l)/ethanol yield coefficient) × 100. For alcohol content testing, in addition to traditional methods, Cuenca et al. developed proton transfer reaction mass spectrometry and fibre-coupled attenuated total reflection spectroscopy. In addition, a number of other indicators require testing during the fermentation of the mead, including: cell concentration (cells/ml), soluble solids concentration, and reducing sugar content. Monitoring the fermentation process is a crucial step in ensuring the quality of mead, involving the testing of multiple key indicators, such as fermentation rate, ethanol yield, fermentation vigor, and purity. Additionally, novel detection methods, such as mass spectrometry and spectroscopy, are continuously being improved to more accurately analyze the alcohol production process. By monitoring these indicators, better control over fermentation can be achieved, ensuring the stability and high quality of the final product.

Post-fermentation processes: Clarification, aging, and storage

Clarification and filtration processes
Clarification and filtration processes are essential in extending the shelf life of mead. To achieve this, fining agents are required post-fermentatio to ensure its physicochemical stability and clarification. These agents target components like organic acids, nitrogen compounds, and colloidal phenolic substances. Fining agents are classified according to their general properties, such as clays (montmorillonite, bentonite, and kaolinite), animal proteins (gelatin, soya, and casein), vegetable proteins (gluten, soya, and lupin), charcoal (carbon), synthetic polymers, silica (diatomaceous earth), metal chelators, and enzymes (pectinase).
Tannins, bentonite, and silica are commonly used fining agents that offer different benefits and drawbacks. During the winemaking process, the tannins combine with the proteins in the must to form a complex that made the wine cloudy. Tannins effectively increase the astringency and clarity of the mead but may reduce the content of some volatile substances, such as esters and terpenoids. Bentonite, a soft clay mineral, promotes yeast growth, removes proteins, and prevents browning of the mead. However, using it to make sparkling mead can lead to inconsistent bubble sizes and easy bubble breakage, which can affect consumer appeal. Silica sol is a clear solution of silicic acid. In acidic juices and fruit wines, the negatively charged silica sol reacts with the positively charged gelatin to cause flocculation and clarification. Silica, on the other hand, is the best clarifying agent and allowed the mead to retain a higher concentration of aromatic compounds such as esters, volatile fatty acids, lactones, terpenes, and norbornenes. Mead makers should carefully consider the type of mead being produced and choose the most appropriate fining agent for optimal results.
The choice of clarifying agents in mead production directly impacts the final product's quality and sensory characteristics. The use of different clarifying agents can improve the appearance, aroma, and taste of the mead, but in some cases, it may also affect its aromatic components or bubble characteristics. Therefore, mead producers must carefully select the appropriate clarifying agent based on the type of mead, desired flavor profile, and production process requirements. While ensuring clarity and stability, it is also essential to preserve the mead flavor compounds as much as possible to optimize product quality and enhance the consumer experience.

Aging process
The aging process plays a crucial role in the development of mead's flavor profile, contributing significantly to the formation of desirable flavor compounds. Through maturation, the harsh, acidic, and often unpleasant characteristics of young mead are transformed into a smoother, more refined drink with enhanced clarity and a pronounced floral aroma (Table 1) (X.). Typically, mead undergoes aging in oak barrels, with temperatures maintained at 8.00-15.00 °C, and maturation duration ranging from three months to ten years. This prolonged maturation process influences the chemical composition of the mead, notably increasing ethanol concentration while having negligible effects on the levels of glucose and maltose. Additionally, the concentration of certain compounds such as fructose, ethyl lactate, diethyl succinate, diethyl malate, and monoethyl succinate decreases significantly during aging.
This transformation is primarily driven by chemical reactions, such as esterification and Maillard reactions, which occur over time and contribute to the mead smoothness and complexity (A. P.). Furthermore, aging can help reduce undesirable volatile compounds while enhancing the extraction of phenolic compounds from the oak barrels-a process that further refines the final sensory profile (flavor and mouthfeel). The aging process, therefore, is not only essential for flavor development but also for the overall sensory quality and stability of the mead, creating a product that is more appealing to consumers and ensuring the product longevity. Consequently, careful control of maturation conditions is critical to producing high-quality mead with balanced, complex sensory traits (X.).

Storage conditions
Storage conditions play a crucial role in maintaining the quality and longevity of mead. Studies have shown that adding citric acid solution post-aging can effectively inhibit the growth of unwanted bacteria, ensuring the microbial stability of the product. The choice of storage containers is equally important; mead should be stored in sturdy bottles to prevent breakage, and the bottle corks must be secure to avoid excessive internal pressure from secondary fermentation reactions, preventing leakage or spoilage. Additionally, storage temperature and light exposure significantly affect mead quality. Storing mead at lower temperatures (4.00 °C) and in a shaded environment is the optimal choice, as exposure to sunlight or higher temperatures can destabilize its chemical composition, thereby affecting flavor, color, and overall quality. Therefore, mead storage should not only focus on preventing physical damage but also optimize factors like temperature, humidity, and light exposure to ensure the mead flavor, taste, and quality remain at their best over time.

Alcohol compounds
Alcoholic compounds are the primary substances produced by yeast during alcoholic fermentation and are secondary metabolites of the yeast. High concentrations of alcohol can impart a solvent-like taste and a warming sensation to mead, while low concentrations can add complexity to its flavor. Alcohols account for 40.00-60.00 % of the total aromatic compounds in mead samples. The metabolic pathways of alcohols in Saccharomyces cerevisiae include fermentation of glucose to produce ethanol, amino acid metabolism leading to the production of volatile alcohols such as isoamyl alcohol, synthesis of fatty acids and alcohol generation, as well as various metabolic regulation by environmental factors. For compounds with higher concentrations and an OAV greater than 1, we have analyzed their metabolic pathways in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Fig. 1A-C and 2A-B). Alcohol compounds are not only metabolites of yeast but also play a critical role in the aroma and flavor of the beverage. Alcohols are generally divided into two major categories: ethanol and higher alcohols (T.).
In mead, ethanol is typically the most abundant component. However, ethanol is generally considered a compound responsible for the mouthfeel rather than the aroma of mead, imparting a distinctive pungency that is sometimes referred to as a “strong” taste. The ethanol content in mead, as reported in the literature, ranges from 1.00 to 25.00 %. Low-alcohol mead is produced using yeast strains that are not traditionally used for brewing, as these yeasts have lower tolerance to osmotic pressure and are less efficient in utilizing monosaccharides such as glucose and fructose, making them suitable for brewing sweeter meads. The fermentation time for low-alcohol mead is shorter, and the yeast does not consume all the fermentable sugars. High-alcohol mead, on the other hand, is characterized by the use of more than 30.00 % sugar sources, with Saccharomyces cerevisiae as the yeast strain, and the fermentation process is sufficient and nutrient-rich. During fermentation, many high-alcohol meads add fruits and vegetables to provide ample nutrients for yeast growth and fermentation.
Higher alcohols, defined as compounds with more carbon atoms than ethanol, are among the most important volatile aromatic compounds in mead (Lima et al., 2021). When present in appropriate concentrations, they contribute to the sweetness, complexity, and full-bodied mouthfeel of the beverage. However, excessive levels of higher alcohols may result in greasy or heavy sensory impressions and could pose potential health risks.

Aldehyde compounds
The role of aldehyde compounds in mead is predominantly reflected in shaping the flavor and aroma profiles of the beverage. As intermediate products of the fermentation process, these compounds are intricately involved in alcohol synthesis, engaging in synergistic interactions with other substances to modulate the flavor equilibrium of the mead. The concentration and variety of aldehyde compounds are decisive factors in defining the flavor characteristics of mead; elevated concentrations may give rise to undesirable tastes or flavor anomalies. Moreover, aldehyde compounds can undergo transformations during the storage and aging of mead, exerting a profound influence on both the flavor and the aging outcome. Consequently, the regulation of the generation and conversion of aldehyde compounds is pivotal for ensuring the quality of mead.

Acid compounds
Acid compounds play a pivotal role in mead, not only regulating its acidity but also influencing the taste and flavor profile. They further facilitate the fermentation process and enhance the stability of the beverage. An appropriate level of acidity can endow mead with a refreshing sensation, add layers to its taste, and deepen its flavor complexity. Additionally, acid compounds possess antioxidant properties, which help delay oxidation, improve the stability of mead, inhibit the growth of unwanted microorganisms, and preserve the freshness of the beverage. Moreover, during the storage and aging of mead, acid compounds are crucial for maintaining the structural integrity and flavor consistency of the drink (R.). .
Volatile acid compounds are generated through multiple metabolic pathways during mead fermentation, including the oxidation of alcohols, the conversion during acid fermentation, and the degradation of fatty acids. These reactions lead to the formation of various acidic compounds in mead, thereby affecting its acidity, taste, flavor complexity, and overall balance. Volatile acids produced by fatty acid degradation also contribute to the flavor formation of the wine, influencing the sharpness of its taste. A moderate amount of volatile acid compounds can enhance the flavor hierarchy and taste complexity of mead, while excessive amounts may result in unpleasant odors and flavor defects, negatively impacting the quality of the wine and consumer acceptance. Therefore, the generation and regulation of volatile acids are of paramount importance in the quality formation of mead (Sant'Ana et al., 2025). Ester compounds
Ester compounds play a pivotal role in mead, primarily formed through the reaction between alcohols and acids during yeast fermentation. These compounds not only determine the aromatic and flavor profiles of mead but also significantly influence its taste, complexity, and flavor persistence. Characterized by intense fruity aromas, esters endow mead with rich aromatic layers, enhancing the depth and diversity of its flavor. Additionally, the formation of esters improves the mouthfeel of mead, rendering it smoother and rounder, reducing harshness, and balancing the sweet-sour taste. As the wine matures, the accumulation of esters promotes the enhancement of mead's flavor and complexity, imparting deeper layers and long-lasting flavor characteristics.
Ester compounds are generated via esterification reactions between alcohols and organic acids during mead fermentation. This process produces a diverse array of esters, which are crucial for the flavor, aroma, and taste complexity of mead. Fatty acid metabolism also contributes to ester formation, further enriching the aromatic hierarchy of the wine. The selection of yeast strains and fermentation conditions (such as temperature, pH, and oxygen availability) significantly impact ester production, determining the types and concentrations of esters formed. In summary, esters are synthesized through complex metabolic pathways during mead fermentation, playing a vital role in shaping the aroma, mouthfeel, and flavor profile of the final product. Ketone compounds
Ketones play a pivotal role in the production and sensory characteristics of mead, influencing both flavor and aroma profiles. In terms of flavor, ketones are essential contributors to mead's distinctive aromatic complexity, imparting a variety of scents including fruity, floral, creamy, and caramel notes. These aromatic compounds not only enrich the mead's overall sensory appeal but also interact synergistically with other volatile compounds, resulting in a more balanced and harmonious bouquet. In terms of taste, the presence of certain ketones enhances the fullness and richness of the mead, enriching its texture and depth. Furthermore, they help to balance the acidity and sweetness, ensuring a well-rounded and smooth flavor profile. Beyond these sensory attributes, some ketones exhibit notable antioxidant properties, which, in combination with other antioxidant compounds such as phenolics and flavonoids present in mead, contribute to improved oxidative stability. This interaction helps to delay spoilage, extend the product's shelf life, and maintain its quality over time. Additionally, certain ketones provide energy and have been shown to possess biological activities, including anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects, thereby enhancing the nutritional and health benefits of mead. Thus, ketones play a critical role in shaping the overall quality, flavor, and health-promoting properties of mead.
During the fermentation process, the biosynthesis of ketones in mead is closely associated with several metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, amino acid metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism. In glycolysis, glucose is initially converted into pyruvate, which is then further metabolized into acetyl-CoA, a key precursor for the synthesis of a variety of ketones. Additionally, the intermediate product ribose, derived from the pentose phosphate pathway, can also be converted into ketones. In amino acid metabolism, specifically via the Ehrlich pathway, branched-chain amino acids are metabolized into α-keto acids, which undergo decarboxylation to form aldehydes, which are subsequently reduced to higher alcohols and further oxidized to produce ketones. Other amino acids, such as aromatic amino acids, undergo complex metabolic transformations, contributing to the formation of ketones. Fatty acid metabolism also plays a critical role, as yeast cells assimilate fatty acids to produce acetyl-CoA, which participates in ketone biosynthesis, with intermediates like malonyl-CoA also contributing to ketone formation. Aromatic compounds
Aromatic compounds in mead are formed through multiple biotransformation pathways, which include yeast fermentation, oak infusion, and aging reactions. During yeast fermentation, yeast cells metabolize precursor substances from honey, leading to the production of key aromatic compounds such as phenylethanol and vanillin, as well as esters. These compounds play a critical role in enhancing the aromatic complexity of mead. In the oak infusion process, aromatic compounds from the wood, such as vanillin and vanillic acid, dissolve and diffuse into the mead, thereby enhancing the overall complexity and flavor profile of the beverage. Furthermore, the tannins present in oak interact with the polyphenolic compounds in the mead, facilitating the formation and stabilization of aromatic compounds. During the aging process, as influenced by factors such as oxygen exposure and temperature fluctuations, aromatic compounds continue to interact with other components in the mead, such as tannins and anthocyanins, resulting in further development of the wine's aging characteristics, color changes, and refinement of flavor profiles. These diverse biotransformation pathways define the multifaceted role of aromatic compounds in mead, affecting its color, aroma, mouthfeel, and overall aging potential. Terpenoid compounds
Terpenoid compounds are widely distributed volatile organic compounds in nature, and they play a critical role in the fermentation process of mead. These compounds are not only essential components of the mead aromatic profile, contributing complex floral, fruity, and herbal notes, but also influence the color and taste characteristics of the beverage through their interactions with other chemical constituents. Specifically, monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes exert a decisive influence on the sensory properties of mead, modulating mouthfeel attributes such as astringency and bitterness. Additionally, through their antioxidant activities, terpenoid compounds help to retard oxidation and spoilage, thereby extending the shelf life of the mead. Furthermore, terpenoid compounds contribute to the stability and complexity of the mead flavor profile by regulating the formation of other flavor compounds during fermentation, ensuring that the mead maintains its sensory integrity throughout storage and aging. The biosynthesis of terpenoids in brewing yeast primarily occurs through the mevalonate pathway, where acetyl-CoA is converted to isopentenyl pyrophosphate, a precursor for the synthesis of various terpenoids, including monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes. During fermentation, yeast cells not only produce these compounds through enzymatic activity but also release them into the fermentation medium, imparting distinct aromatic qualities to the mead. Other compounds

Sugars
Sugars are an important component of both honey and mead. Fructose and glucose are the most commonly found monosaccharides in honey, with fructose being the dominant sugar. Other sugars in honey include sucrose, maltose, isomaltose, trisaccharides, and tetrasaccharides (; A. P.). There are several methods used to determine sugar content in mead, including the phenol‑sulfuric acid colorimetric method, DNS method, Barber concentration meter method, HPLC, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, USA-ILE, and LC-RI. During fermentation, it has been found that yeasts consume glucose faster than fructose, with fructose commonly remaining in mead at the end of fermentation. Residual sugars in mead are attributed to unfermentable sugars in honey, such as rhamnose, alginose, nigerian sugar, maltotetraose, maltotriose, maltose, maltooligosaccharide, isomaltulose, and cottonseed sugar. The amount of residual sugars has a significant impact on the sensory properties of mead. Sugar concentration in mead varies by type, with sweet meads having higher levels than dry meads.

Sulfur dioxide
Sulfur dioxide (SO2) is commonly added during the winemaking process, but studies have indicated that sulfites could pose a potential health risk. The Ripper rapid method is the primary detection method for measuring total SO2. In a study by Pereira A et al., SO2 concentrations in mead fermented with different yeasts varied. Although no SO2 was added to the honey solution, SO2 concentrations were detected in all meads at the end of fermentation. The concentrations of SO2 in mead fermented with brewer's yeast ICV D47 ranged from 14.50 to 15.80 mg/L, while brewer's yeast QA23 showed significantly higher SO2 concentrations in mead fermented with potassium salts + vitamins (Pereira et al. 2015a, b). They also found that immobilized yeast cells had significantly lower SO2 concentrations than free cells during the brewing of mead, with total SO2 concentrations ranging from 21.12 to 23.68 mg/L (A. P. Pereira, Mendes-Ferreira, Estevinho, et al., 2014; A. P.). Šmogrovičová D et al. measured free and bound SO2 in mead using iodometric titration and found that South African mead had much higher levels of SO2 (184.10-190.10 mg/L) than Slovakian mead (35.60-41.60 mg/L). Cavalcante Da Silva S et al. determined the total SO2 content of dry and sweet mead in the range of 52.93-64.93 mg/L. Senn K et al. found that free and total SO2 concentrations in traditional American mead ranged from 2.00 to 61.00 mg/L and 5.00-275.00 mg/L, respectively.

Physicochemical properties and functions

Specific gravity
The turbidity of mead is closely related to the flocculation ability of yeast during fermentation, which directly influences the specific gravity and clarity of the mead. Among them, clarity, as the core visual sensory attribute of mead, directly affects consumers' initial perception and acceptance of the product; at the same time, the dynamic change of specific gravity will also indirectly affect the mouthfeel richness of mead-if the specific gravity is too high in the late fermentation stage (with more residual sugar), the wine body tends to show a sweet and greasy mouthfeel; if the specific gravity is too low, it may lead to a bland mouthfeel. The yeast's flocculation process and the resulting changes in specific gravity work together to affect the physical and sensory characteristics of the mead. Therefore, controlling yeast flocculation and monitoring specific gravity are crucial for optimizing the quality of mead.
There are various methods to determine the specific gravity of mead, including the specific gravity method, refractometer method, and turbidimetric method. The specific gravity of the mead ranges from 1.00 to 1.50. Chen C et al. found that the specific gravity of Longan mead was 1.45 (C. H.); Peepall C et al. measured the specific gravity of mead fermented with lactic acid producing yeast to be 1.11. Balogu et al. measured the specific gravity of coconut milk mead to be 1.12-1.42. Karabagias et al. measured a specific gravity of 1.16 for cactus mead. The difference in specific gravity of meads made from different raw materials further reflects the differences in their sugar content and wine body structure, which in turn affects the flavor intensity and mouthfeel hierarchy of the product.
Specific gravity is a critical parameter in the fermentation process, serving as an indicator of changes in sugar and alcohol concentrations. By closely monitoring specific gravity, mead producers are able to track the progress of fermentation, estimate the alcohol content, and make necessary adjustments to the fermentation environment. Fluctuations in specific gravity can also signal issues such as incomplete fermentation or the presence of residual sugars, which may negatively impact the flavor, stability, and overall quality of the mead. For example, high specific gravity caused by incomplete fermentation (excessive residual sugar) will lead to sweet and greasy flavor defects in mead, and may also cause subsequent microbial growth due to sugar residue, destroying the stability of the wine body; abnormal low specific gravity may be accompanied by excessive alcohol content, leading to prominent spicy taste that masks the sweet flavor of honey itself. Consequently, maintaining precise control over specific gravity throughout the fermentation process is essential for producing mead of consistent high quality. Given its importance, mead makers should adopt reliable methods for regular measurement and interpretation of specific gravity to optimize the fermentation process and ensure the desired product characteristics-including flavor coordination, mouthfeel balance, and visual clarity-are achieved.

Electrical conductivity
Electrical conductivity (EC) is a crucial parameter in mead production, reflecting the influence of various components such as sugars, organic acids, proteins, and ash. These constituents collectively contribute to the ionic content of the beverage-for example, organic acid ions (e.g., malic acid, lactic acid) affect the sourness intensity of mead, while sugar-derived ions influence the sweetness perception, and protein-related ions impact the mouthfeel smoothness-thereby directly impacting its electrical conductivity (Y.). The measurement of EC is vital in assessing the stability and quality of mead, as it provides insight into the changes in ionic composition throughout the fermentation and maturation stages. Notably, ions like potassium, calcium, and other electrolytes are instrumental in determining the overall conductivity of the mead, and their concentrations further correlate with sensory attributes: higher potassium ion content may enhance the wine body's mellowness, while appropriate calcium ions help maintain the balance between sourness and sweetness, avoiding overly sharp taste. By monitoring EC levels, producers can evaluate the progress of fermentation, anticipate potential issues with the mead's stability, and make necessary adjustments to optimize the product's quality. Given the importance of EC in mead production, it is essential to maintain precise control over this parameter, ensuring that variations in conductivity are carefully interpreted to produce a high-quality and stable product. Fentie et al. and Suzart Araújo et al. measured the average EC of Ethiopian mead at 0.33-0.72 mS/cm using a digital conductivity meter (; Fentie, Jeong, Emire, Demsash, Kim, Jeon, et al., 2022). These variations in EC values are indicative of differences in the composition and fermentation processes of the mead-for instance, higher EC may correspond to more residual organic acids (resulting in a tangier mouthfeel) or unfermented sugars (leading to a sweeter taste), while lower EC may signal excessive sugar consumption (causing a bland flavor)-including factors such as yeast activity, sugar consumption, and the production of various byproducts like organic acids and alcohol. Monitoring EC during mead production can thus offer valuable insights into the progression of fermentation, allowing producers to track the overall stability of the product and make adjustments as necessary to ensure consistent quality.
Additionally, the correlation between EC and other quality parameters, such as pH and sugar content, underscores the relevance of EC as a non-invasive, real-time method for assessing the health of the fermentation process. Specifically, EC correlates with pH (lower pH often accompanies higher EC from organic acid ions) to influence flavor balance-too high EC (with low pH) may result in excessive sourness that masks honey's inherent sweetness, while EC that is too low (with high pH) may lead to a flat, unbalanced taste-and with sugar content to regulate mouthfeel richness, further confirming EC's role in linking physicochemical properties to sensory experience. Thus, measuring EC in mead not only provides a reliable tool for evaluating the stability and composition of the beverage but also contributes to the optimization of fermentation conditions and the overall enhancement of product quality.

Acidity
The pH value affects the color and flavor of the mead and is one of the most important indicators. Specifically, pH regulates the stability of natural pigments (e.g., flavonoids, anthocyanins) in mead: under acidic conditions (pH 2.40-3.50), pigments remain stable and present bright hues (e.g., golden yellow for pure mead, reddish-orange for fruit mead), while higher pH (above 4.00) promotes pigment degradation or browning, leading to dull, dark appearance; in terms of flavor, lower pH enhances the perception of fresh sourness (complementing fruit or herbal notes in specialized meads), while excessively high pH may weaken characteristic flavors and introduce undesirable bitter or musty tastes. If the pH of mead is too high, it will have low levels of active SO2 and be susceptible to oxidative and biological changes. These oxidative changes often cause the loss of volatile aroma compounds (e.g., honey-derived esters) and the formation of stale, cardboard-like flavors, while biological changes (e.g., microbial overgrowth) can produce off-flavors such as vinegar or rotten fruit, directly damaging the sensory quality of mead. Mead pH measurements are generally carried out using a digital pH meter and the CRISON method.
The pH levels of different meads are relatively stable with little variation. This stability is closely linked to consistent sensory performance: fruit meads had a lower pH of 2.40-3.50, where the acidic environment amplifies the brightness of fruit aromas (e.g., berry, citrus) and creates a refreshing, balanced taste; herbal mead had a pH of 2.74-3.70 (Carol L. Wintersteen, 2005), and this pH range helps retain the delicate herbal notes (e.g., mint, chamomile) without being overpowered by sourness; pure mead had a pH of 2.96-3.20 (; Fentie, Jeong, Emire, Demsash, Kim, Jeon, et al., 2022), which optimizes the expression of honey inherent sweet floral flavor while avoiding cloyingness. However, Fentie et al. measured the pH of Ethiopian mead samples and found that 23.00 % of the meads had a pH above 3.80 (Fentie, Jeong, Emire, Demsash, Kim, Jeon, et al., 2022)-these higher pH samples were observed to have less vibrant colors and a higher likelihood of exhibiting flat or off-flavors due to reduced acid-mediated flavor enhancement. Similarly, Senn K et al. tested a range of traditional American meads, some with pH levels above 4.00; meads with pH above 4.00 not only showed significant browning (compared to those with pH <3.50) but also had a higher incidence of undesirable earthy or fermented off-tastes, attributed to increased microbial activity and pigment degradation.
Controlling the pH value during mead production is of paramount importance, as it has a direct impact on both the sensory characteristics, such as color and flavor, and the stability of the mead throughout fermentation and storage. A well-maintained pH range not only ensures the preservation of the mead's original flavor and quality, but also plays a pivotal role in preventing oxidation and spoilage, which could otherwise compromise the product's safety and shelf life. Moreover, the pH value influences the behavior of yeast and other microorganisms, affecting fermentation efficiency and the development of off-flavors. For example, pH below 2.40 may inhibit yeast activity, leading to incomplete sugar fermentation and residual sweetness that disrupts flavor balance, while pH above 3.80 can stimulate the growth of spoilage microbes (e.g., acetic acid bacteria) that produce vinegar-like off-flavors and reduce the mead shelf life. Therefore, precise pH management is essential not only for optimizing the fermentation process but also for ensuring that the final product remains stable and safe for long-term storage. In conclusion, pH control is a critical factor in achieving both high-quality production and prolonged shelf life of mead, and should be prioritized as a fundamental aspect of the brewing process.

Total acidity (titratable acidity)
Total acidity, also known as titratable acidity, is measured in tartaric acid equivalents and has a significant impact on mead. Specifically, it modulates flavor balance (mitigating cloying sweetness via mild tartness), maintains pigment stability (inhibiting premature browning), enhances aroma release (amplifying honey/fruit volatile compounds), and regulates mouthfeel freshness-directly linking to core sensory attributes. It affects the color, aroma, flavor, freshness, microbial stability, product fermentation process, and shelf life of mead. The increase in acidity is mainly due to the synthesis of organic acids, such as acetic and succinic acid, by the yeast. High acidity in mead may indicate fermentation contaminants (e.g., acetic acid bacteria, which produce excess acetic acid leading to undesirable vinegar-like off-flavors). The total acidity of mead is generally determined through colorimetric titration and acid-base titration methods. Several studies have reported different levels of total acidity in mead.
Several studies have reported different levels of total acidity in mead. For instance, Karabagias et al. brewed a new cactus mead with a total acidity of 0.86 %-a value correlating with bright, citrus-like tartness that complements cactus-derived sensory notes. Sottil et al. showed that mead fermented with T. delbrueckii and S. cerevisiae X5 had a total acidity of 2.00 g/L, while mead fermented with S. bayanus had a total acidity of 1.00 g/L-the higher acidity in co-fermented mead contributed to a crisper mouthfeel, whereas the lower acidity in S. bayanus-fermented mead exhibited a milder, more rounded taste. Li and Sun found that the titratable acidity of mead samples ranged from 4.61 to 5.29 g/L (R.). Pereira et al. reported that the titratable acidity of mead increased from 4.00 to 7.60 g/L during the fermentation process, and mead produced with brewer's yeast QA23 had a higher titratable acidity (over 7.00 g/L) compared to mead fermented with brewer's yeast ICV D47 (in the range of 6.70-7.10 g/L)- QA23-derived higher acidity enhanced flavor complexity, while ICV D47 moderate acidity maintained honey inherent sweetness (A. P. Pereira, Mendes-Ferreira, Estevinho, et al., 2015; A. P.). In addition, Fentie et al. studied the change in total acidity of three spontaneously fermented Ethiopian meads during fermentation, with the average acidity increasing from 0.23 to 3.00, 0.23 to 4.01, and 0.38 to 2.25 g/L, respectively. The total acidity of Ethiopian mead ranged from 1.77 to 8.63 g/L- samples with acidity >6.00 g/L showed pronounced sourness, while those <3.00 g/L exhibited bland flavor profiles. Mohammed S et al. analyzed date mead with a titratable acidity of 5.20 %-9.40 %- this range aligned with date sweet-tart sensory balance. Ajogun C et al. studied the total acid content of coconut wine of 0.57-0.92 %. Furthermore, the addition of vitamins during the fermentation process can slightly lower the titratable acidity of mead-a mild reduction that softens sharp sourness without compromising flavor integrity. Schwarz et al. found that total mead acidity ranged of 51.33 - 54.00 meq/L during fermentation with brewer's yeast QA23 and brewer's yeast Spark. In comparison, honey ferment had a value of 12.00 meq/L, indicating an increase of 39.00-42.00 meq/L in total acidity. Cavalcante Da Silva et al. reported the total acidity of stingless dry mead as 7.80 g/L and stingless sweet mead as 6.00 g/L, which falls within the Brazilian legislation on mead (3.40-9.80 g/L)-dry mead higher acidity matched its lean sensory profile, while sweet mead lower acidity complemented its residual sweetness.
In conclusion, the total acidity of mead is a key determinant of its overall quality, influencing not only taste and flavor but also stability and shelf life. Appropriate acidity (3.40-7.00 g/L, per regulatory ranges) enhances sensory layeredness, while deviations (either <3.00 g/L or > 8.00 g/L) cause flavor imbalance (blandness or harsh sourness). This acidity primarily results from organic acids, such as acetic and succinic acid, which are produced by yeast during fermentation. However, elevated acidity levels may also serve as a warning sign of potential contamination, highlighting the need for vigilance in the production process. Furthermore, significant variations in the total acidity of mead can arise from the selection of yeast strains and differences in fermentation conditions. As such, carefully controlling fermentation parameters and selecting appropriate yeast strains are essential strategies for managing total acidity. By doing so, producers can enhance the mead's quality, stability, and shelf life, ensuring a consistent and high-quality final product.

Volatile acidity
Volatile acidity in mead is primarily composed of acetic acid. It is usually produced by the fermentation of yeast and other microorganisms. The acceptable limit of volatile acidity in mead, according to the Polish standard PN-A-79123, is 1.40 g/L; exceeding this threshold significantly degrades sensory quality by intensifying acetic acid off-notes. A high concentration of volatile acidity found during mead storage may indicate the conversion of ethanol to acetic acid by stray bacteria (e.g., acetic acid bacteria), a process that directly leads to harsh vinegar flavors and reduced product acceptability. Steam distillation with a phenolphthalein indicator and titration with a 0.10 N sodium hydroxide solution are the common measurement methods for volatile acidity.
During the fermentation process, the volatile acidity acetic acid content in mead ranges from 11.00 to 17.00 meq/L, as determined by Schwarz L et al.. Brewer's yeast AWRI-R2 ferments mead with over 1 g/L acetic acid, a level approaching the Polish standard upper limit and posing a slight risk of mild vinegar notes. Volatile acidity in South African mead ranges from 0.75 to 0.79 g/L acetate equivalent, consistent with fresh, non-offensive flavor profiles; while Slovakian mead has a volatile acidity of 0.99-1.49 g/L, with values near 1.49 g/L showing marginal vinegar-like hints. The effect of different yeasts on volatile acidity was studied by Pereira et al. The volatile acidity of free and immobilized QA23 yeast was 0.57 g/L and 0.69 g/L, respectively, which was higher than that of mead produced with ICV D47 yeast at 0.51 g/L and 0.54 g/L; QA23-derived mead exhibited slightly more pronounced acid notes, while ICV D47 yielded milder, sweeter-centric flavors (A. P., 2019; A. P.). Fruit and herbal meads fermented by Kawa-Rygielska J et al. had an average volatile acidity of 0.96 g/L (corrected from mg/L, consistent with unit consistency); this concentration remained within acceptable limits, and aging did not cause significant volatile acidity changes-ensuring stable sensory quality. Cavalcante Da Silva S et al. fermented stingless dry mead and stingless sweet mead with acetic acid values of 1.60 and 1.00 g/L, respectively; the dry mead 1.60 g/L exceeded the Polish standard, resulting in distinct vinegar off-flavors, while the sweet mead 1.00 g/L maintained flavor balance. Portuguese meads have volatile acidity concentrations ranging from 0.51 to 0.67 g/L, a range associated with well-balanced, honey-dominant flavors (Pereira et al. 2014b, a).
In summary, volatile acidity in mead primarily arises from acetic acid produced by yeast and other microorganisms during fermentation. The concentration of volatile acidity is shaped by a range of biochemical factors, including the metabolic profiles of various yeast strains, the composition of raw materials, and the aging process. These factors interact in complex ways, leading to significant variations in volatile acidity across different regions and production methods. Consequently, volatile acidity not only serves as a crucial indicator of the fermentation dynamics but also reflects the overall quality of the mead-its level directly dictates sensory outcomes, with concentrations 0.5-1.4 g/L ensuring desirable flavor, and > 1.4 g/L inducing vinegar-like defects. Its level provides valuable insight into both the success of the fermentation process and the potential impact of the aging process on the final product. Given its importance, careful management of fermentation conditions and yeast selection is essential to controlling volatile acidity, ensuring that it remains within desirable limits and contributes positively to the mead's final character and stability.

Residual assimilable nitrogen
The amount of residual nitrogen in honey may correspond to the quantity of proline that couldn't be assimilated by yeast-excess residual nitrogen may indicate incomplete yeast metabolic efficiency, while insufficient levels signal nutrient limitation that risks halted fermentation. Formaldehyde titration can determine the assimilable nitrogen in honey solutions and mead, providing critical data for pre-fermentation nutrient adjustment to optimize yeast activity (; A. P.; A. P.). Cavalcante Da Silva et al. reported residual assimilable nitrogen levels of 24.16-32.74 mg/L in dry and sweet stingless mead, respectively; this range balances microbial stability (avoiding nitrogen excess that favors spoilage bacteria) and minimal off-flavor risk (preventing amine-like compounds from residual nitrogen degradation). Pereira et al. found that the amount of residual nitrogen is not affected by strain or mead formulation, and all meads had residual nitrogen concentrations of 30.00-40.00 mg/L (Pereira et al. 2014a, b); this consistency supports batch-to-batch flavor uniformity, as strain-independent nitrogen levels reduce variability in yeast metabolic byproducts. When using two groups of high-density brewer's yeast strains, fermented mead had residual nitrogen ranging from 29.17 to 42.00 mg/L, with no significant inter-strain differences (A. P.); this further confirms that residual nitrogen stability is driven by inherent yeast proline assimilation pathways rather than strain-specific traits.
In conclusion, residual nitrogen in mead is tightly linked to yeast capacity for proline assimilation during fermentation. While yeast strain and mead formulation have minimal impact on residual nitrogen content, this consistency reflects stable metabolic regulation of nitrogen utilization. Residual nitrogen levels (24-42 mg/L) serve as a key quality indicator: values within this range ensure complete fermentation (avoiding nutrient shortage) and limit off-flavors (preventing microbial nitrogen breakdown), while deviations may signal suboptimal yeast activity or contamination risk. For producers, monitoring residual nitrogen enables precise fermentation control-supporting consistent yeast performance and reducing quality fluctuations. Thus, understanding residual nitrogen dynamics is essential to optimize fermentation outcomes and maintain high-quality mead production standards.

Adulteration comparison
Adulteration of mead typically occurs through the substitution of authentic ingredients with cheaper alternatives. The most common adulterants are sweeteners such as sucrose, refined beet sugar, corn syrup, high fructose corn syrup, and maltose syrup, which are used to replace genuine honey or other natural sweeteners. This practice reduces production costs but compromises the authenticity and quality of mead by destroying the inherent complex sweetness hierarchy of honey (e.g., floral, fruity secondary notes) and inducing a single, cloying mouthfeel that deviates from authentic mead sensory profile. Another prevalent adulteration method involves mixing honey with ethanol, which not only alters the brewing characteristics (e.g., disrupting yeast normal metabolism to cause incomplete fermentation) but also generates undesirable flavor compounds (e.g., fusel oils) and distorts the balanced alcohol aroma, directly deteriorating sensory acceptability.
Additionally, flavorings such as vanillin are often added to mask the taste of low-quality mead. While vanillin is naturally present in honey and propolis, its concentration is typically low. Therefore, if high levels of vanillin are found in mead, it is often considered an indicator of adulteration, as excessive vanillin masks honey natural floral and herbal aromas, producing a pungent artificial fragrance that breaks sensory harmony. These adulteration practices not only undermine the quality and purity of mead but also pose potential risks to consumer health-long-term intake of excess artificial sweeteners may trigger metabolic disorders, and impurities in low-quality ethanol (e.g., methanol) can cause liver damage-and violate consumer rights to authentic products. As such, ensuring transparency in mead production and the authenticity of raw materials is crucial for maintaining product quality and market fairness.
To ensure the authenticity of mead and prevent adulteration, various targeted detection methods can be employed: isotope ratio analysis distinguishes carbon isotope compositions between honey and industrial sweeteners, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) techniques realize qualitative and quantitative detection of exogenous ethanol/vanillin, near-infrared spectroscopy enables rapid screening of sugar adulterants, and stable isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) accurately identifies raw material origins. These methods provide effective means of detecting adulteration, ensuring that consumers receive products of genuine quality.

Sensory analysis
The primary factors affecting the sensory characteristics of mead include the honey source, dilution ratio, additives (such as adjuncts or fruit juices), yeast strains, fermentation conditions, alcohol content, residual sugars, and acidity (e.g., temperature). The organoleptic quality of mead can be assessed by the OAV of a compound. OAV represents the ratio of the concentration of a particular compound to its sensory threshold, thus establishing a connection between chemical data and sensory attributes. OAVs provide a more accurate reflection of the human olfactory system's response to compounds than their absolute concentrations. When the OAV of a compound exceeds 1, it significantly contributes to the flavor of the beverage. If the OAV exceeds 0.20, the compound subtly alters the beverage flavor (Pereira et al. 2015a, b). Moreover, individual OAVs can estimate each compound's potential contribution to the overall flavor, though synergistic or antagonistic effects between different molecules are not considered.
In addition to quantitative analysis, the main sensory characteristics of a beverage are evaluated by experts or consumers based on its aroma, taste, and (to a lesser extent) color, appearance, and other visual features (A. P.). For example, the Polish standard PN-79120-02:1990 evaluates mead based on its taste, aroma, color, and clarity; similar ISO methods, such as ISO 4121 (ISO, 2003) and ISO 6658 (ISO, 2005), are used for mead evaluation. The sensory attributes assessed are divided into three main categories: appearance (color and cloudiness), taste (sweet, sour, and astringent), and aroma (fruity, honey, botanical, alcoholic, and chemical) (A. P.). Additionally, researchers have investigated the general acceptability of mead, off-flavors (such as rancidity, oxidation, mouldiness), and aftertaste.
In conclusion, the sensory evaluation of mead is a multifaceted process that integrates both quantitative analysis, such as the calculation of odor activity values (OAV), and qualitative assessments conducted by expert panels or consumer groups. A thorough understanding of the various factors influencing sensory outcomes-such as honey source, yeast strain, and fermentation conditions-is essential for producing high-quality and consistent mead. Additionally, adhering to established sensory evaluation standards provides a robust framework for quality control, ensuring that producers can monitor and maintain product excellence throughout production. By doing so, mead makers can align their products with consumer expectations, enhancing both market appeal and acceptability. Ultimately, a deep and nuanced understanding of mead's sensory characteristics not only supports rigorous quality assurance practices but also fosters a stronger connection with consumers, contributing to the overall success of the product in the market.

Functions
Honey, a rich source of essential nutrients (e.g., vitamins, minerals) and bioactive substances (e.g., phenolic compounds, methylglyoxal), contributes to the functional properties of mead-fermented using honey as its primary fermentable substrate, where honey bioactive components are retained or moderately transformed during fermentation. The antioxidant capacity of mead, derived from honey-derived phenolic compounds (e.g., flavonoids, gallic acid, p-coumaric acid), not only plays a significant role in neutralizing free radicals (reactive oxygen species, ROS) and supporting human antioxidant defense systems but also enhances mead sensory stability by inhibiting oxidative browning (pigment degradation) and the formation of stale off-flavors (e.g., cardboard-like notes) during storage.
Additionally, mead has demonstrated antiviral activity, with honey-derived bioactive components (e.g., methylglyoxal, hydrogen peroxide, polyphenols) exerting effects by interfering with viral replication cycles (e.g., blocking viral attachment to host cells) and modulating immune cytokine production-potentially enhancing innate immune function and offering targeted protection against common viral pathogens (e.g., influenza, herpes simplex virus). Furthermore, mead gastrointestinal effects are notable: its mild antimicrobial activity (targeting pathogenic bacteria like Helicobacter pylori) and prebiotic-like properties (promoting proliferation of beneficial gut flora such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus) help maintain gut microbiota balance, alleviate mild digestive discomfort (e.g., bloating), and support digestive health.
Together, these honey-derived functional components position mead not only as a beverage of sensory pleasure (e.g., honey inherent floral aroma, balanced sweetness) but also as a functional product that may provide multiple health benefits-aligning with consumer demand for “nutraceutical beverages” that integrate sensory and physiological value.

Antioxidant capacity
Honey, one of the primary ingredients in mead, is known to contain a high concentration of antioxidant compounds. The level of antioxidants in honey can vary depending on factors such as the source of pollen, the type of honey, production environment, and processing techniques. However, the antioxidant activity of mead is not solely dependent on honey but is also influenced by other factors, including the type of additives (e.g., fruits, herbs, or spices), fermentation processes, and aging. The primary antioxidant components in mead include ascorbic acid, α-tocopherol, catalase, β-carotene, peroxides, flavonoids, tannins, and phenolic acids (, a). These components are associated with the inhibition of free radicals, which are linked to cellular damage and the onset of chronic nervous system diseases, as well as the prevention of tumors and atherosclerotic lesions. Several commonly used methods to assess the antioxidant activity of mead include DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, ORAC, BHT, and DMPD.
Fruit, vegetable, and herbal meads typically exhibit higher antioxidant activity. For example, Kawa-Rygielska et al. found a significant increase in DPPH% scavenging activity when grape seeds were added to mead. The pure mead sample exhibited an antioxidant activity of 0.16 μmol TE/mL, while the mead containing grape seeds had an antioxidant activity of 0.42 μmol TE/mL. The antioxidant activity of the honey fermentation solution was determined using the ABTS⋅ + assay. The antioxidant activity of dandelion mead was 0.29 μmol TE/mL, while the antioxidant activity of the grape seed mead sample was 0.89 μmol TE/mL. Adamenko et al. added red fruit juice to mead and fermented it with SM yeast, resulting in mead with high antioxidant activity. The antioxidant activity measured by DPPH was 6.20 mmol TE/mL, by ABTS was 7.10 mmol TE/L, and by FRAP was 8.20 mmol TE/L. Cavalcante Da Silva et al. measured the EC50 values using the reducing power method and found values of 0.61 mg/mL for dry mead and 0.42 mg/mL for sweet mead. The EC50 values measured by the DPPH inhibition method were 1.51 mg/mL and 1.42 mg/mL, respectively. Sweet meads exhibited higher antioxidant capacity, possibly due to higher ethanol concentrations and residual sugars. Fentie et al. tested the antioxidant activity of Ethiopian mead, finding DPPH and ABTS values ranging from 37.90 % to 81.00 % and 27.40 % to 73.10 %, respectively. The main source of antioxidant activity in Ethiopian mead is honey, with some contribution from the addition of “gesho”. Czabaj et al. fermented multifloral mead with an average phenolic compound content of 303.02 mg GAE/L, while mead made from honeydew honey had an average phenolic compound content of 236.68 mg GAE/L.
The antioxidant activity of mead is a crucial component of its sensory quality and health benefits, and its antioxidant properties are influenced not only by the type of honey but also by fermentation processes, additives, and aging conditions. Research has shown that the types and concentrations of antioxidant components in mead are closely related to the type of honey, fermentation conditions, and the additives used (e.g., fruits, herbs). The use of different additives, such as grape seeds and red fruit juice, can significantly enhance the antioxidant capacity of mead, providing theoretical support for the nutritional improvement and functional enhancement of mead. Furthermore, understanding and optimizing the antioxidant characteristics of mead is essential for enhancing its health benefits, improving product quality control, and meeting the increasing consumer demand for functional foods. With further research, more precise standards and technologies are expected to be developed, which will promote the healthy and sustainable development of the mead industry.

Antiviral activity
Recent studies have indicated that certain compounds in mead may possess antiviral properties, offering new perspectives on its potential in immune modulation and disease prevention. Asama et al. isolated terpenoids from mead and demonstrated their prophylactic effect against influenza virus (IFV) infection in female BALB/c mice. Compared to the control group, IFV-infected mice exhibited significantly higher survival rates, a marked increase in IgA secretion from airway epithelial cells, a significant reduction in interleukin-6 (IL-6) secretion, and fewer alveolar infiltrating cells at the same airway site. These findings suggest that mead and its constituents may serve as potential natural antiviral agents, demonstrating notable immune-modulatory and antiviral effects.

Gastrointestinal effects
Recent research has highlighted that mead contains several bioactive compounds that may offer therapeutic benefits for individuals with chronic gastrointestinal disorders. Notably, acetic acid, a key component of mead, has been shown to enhance colon peristalsis and improve digestion, suggesting its potential role in gastrointestinal health. Acetic acid promotes intestinal motility, which aids digestion and nutrient absorption, and may provide relief in conditions such as chronic constipation and gastrointestinal dysfunction. Additionally, polyphenols and organic acids in mead may exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, further supporting gastrointestinal well-being. Thus, mead presents potential as a functional beverage for regulating digestive processes and alleviating gastrointestinal discomfort. However, further investigation is needed to fully elucidate its therapeutic mechanisms.

Challenges and future prospects
Despite its unique flavor profile and growing popularity, mead, a fermented beverage with significant market potential, still faces numerous research challenges. Future advancements in mead research and its industrial development will hinge on breakthroughs in several key areas, including the optimization of brewing processes, the elucidation of the formation mechanisms of flavor compounds, the refinement of sensory evaluation systems, and the exploration of its functional health benefits.

Challenges
(1) Bottlenecks in Standardization and Intelligentization of Brewing Processes.
Currently, mead brewing encompasses diverse technical approaches, ranging from traditional natural fermentation to modern bioreactor-based fermentation and enzymatic hydrolysis. Although these processes significantly influence the flavor and quality of mead, the lack of unified standards for brewing parameters (such as fermentation temperature, pH value, yeast inoculation amount, etc.) has led to substantial flavor discrepancies among products produced by different processes, thereby hindering large - scale industrial production. Traditional fermentation processes, which typically rely on empirical control, suffer from issues such as long fermentation cycles, low efficiency, and unstable flavor profiles. While the introduction of modern technologies has enhanced production efficiency and stability, maintaining traditional flavor characteristics remains a significant challenge. Moreover, the mechanisms underlying honey pretreatment, as well as the flavor differences between solid - state and liquid-state fermentation, remain insufficiently studied and lack theoretical support. There is an urgent need to develop multi-parameter collaborative regulation models during process optimization to improve standardization and intelligentization levels.
(2) Complexity of Microbial Community Regulation.
Mead fermentation predominantly relies on a single - microorganism consortium dominated by Saccharomyces cerevisiae. However, the roles of natural microbiota in honey, including wild yeasts, lactic acid bacteria, and acetic acid bacteria, in flavor formation have not been adequately explored. Current research primarily focuses on the fermentative functions of brewer's yeast, potentially underestimating the contribution of natural yeast metabolites (such as terpenoids) to flavor. Certain wild yeast strains can produce specific flavor compounds (e.g., geraniol), but their limited fermentation efficiency has restricted their widespread application. Additionally, acetic acid bacteria in honey may cause wine acidification, yet their functions during fermentation remain unclear, and existing studies lack a systematic analysis of the interactions between dominant strains and flavor compounds. Therefore, achieving targeted flavor regulation through microbial strain combination and community regulation remains a major challenge in mead research.
(3) Fragmentation of Flavor Compound Metabolic Pathways.
The flavor of mead is determined by the interactions of various volatile compounds (such as esters, alcohols, aldehydes, etc.) and non - volatile components. Nevertheless, there are significant gaps in the understanding of the metabolic pathways of these volatile compounds and their interaction mechanisms. Although researchers have demonstrated that volatile substances in mead are mainly produced through glycolysis and amino acid metabolism (such as the Ehrlich pathway), how factors such as differences in honey sources, yeast strain selection, and fermentation conditions affect the formation mechanisms of volatile compounds remain unknown. For instance, the conversion of natural components in honey (such as flavonoid precursors) into volatile phenols during fermentation, as well as the contribution of the Maillard reaction to aldehyde formation during aging, have not been systematically investigated. The fragmented nature of flavor compound metabolic pathways undermines the theoretical basis for current process optimization. Therefore, further elucidation of the metabolic networks of flavor compounds and their regulatory mechanisms is of paramount importance for enhancing mead flavor.
(4) Weak Correlation Mechanisms between Sensory Evaluation and Flavor Compounds.
Sensory evaluation is one of the core indicators for assessing mead quality. However, the current sensory evaluation system suffers from insufficient standardization and methodological discrepancies. Despite the identification of over 300 volatile compounds in mead, the specific contributions of these compounds to sensory quality remain unclear. The current screening of key odor - active compounds predominantly relies on aroma extraction dilution analysis, which is subject to significant subjective differences and unable to quantify the sensory contribution of each compound. For example, the actual concentration of phenethyl acetate in mead varies widely, and its sensory manifestations of “fruity” and “sweet” aromas show significant discrepancies across studies. Additionally, the thermodynamic effects of non-volatile components (such as honey polysaccharides) on aroma release have not been fully elucidated, resulting in an unclear correlation mechanism among “compounds-release-perception”. Thus, establishing quantitative correlations between flavor compounds and sensory evaluation represents a critical issue that urgently needs to be addressed in current research.

Future prospects
(1) Innovation and Intelligent Development of Brewing Processes.
Future research should focus on the transition from traditional empirical control to intelligent and precise regulation. By establishing a core parameter database for mead brewing, integrating sugar compositions, amino acid contents, and fermentation kinetics data from different honey sources, and leveraging modern technologies such as machine learning, process prediction models can be developed to achieve precise control. Researchers can explore the impact of different fermentation temperature profiles on product quality and optimize fermentation processes using these models. Furthermore, the promotion of “mixed - strain fermentation” technology will enable the screening of yeast strains through metagenomics and strain modification, optimizing yeast metabolic pathways and enhancing the synthesis of flavor compounds (such as the expression of terpenoid synthases). Such process innovations will contribute to the efficient, standardized, and personalized production of mead, meeting the market demand for unique flavors and high - quality products.
(2) In-depth Exploration of Flavor Mechanisms.
With the development of metabolomics and isotope labeling techniques, the metabolic pathways of volatile compounds in mead will be more accurately elucidated. Future research can utilize 13C isotope tracing technology to map the metabolic network of mead, with a particular focus on the metabolic flow of sugars and amino acids and their roles in flavor formation. By constructing metabolic pathways from “precursors-intermediate products-volatile end products”, the transformation of honey components into flavor substances through microbial metabolism can be further revealed. Additionally, the use of gas chromatography - mass spectrometry (GC - MS) in combination with electronic nose technology to study the synergistic effects of volatile compounds and quantify their contributions to sensory evaluation will provide a theoretical basis for mead flavor regulation.
(3) Improvement and Application of Sensory Evaluation Systems.
Future research should aim to establish a more scientific and standardized sensory evaluation system for mead, facilitating the transition from qualitative description to quantitative correlation. By introducing sensomics approaches, integrating volatile compound profiles with consumer preference tests, characteristic flavor markers of mead can be screened, and their contributions to different flavor characteristics can be quantified. Meanwhile, the development of in vitro screening models based on olfactory receptors to predict the binding affinities of volatile compounds with olfactory receptors will further uncover the structure-activity relationships between “compound structure-olfactory perception”. This will help establish mathematical models between volatile compounds and sensory attributes, enabling precise prediction and control of mead quality.

Conclusion
This review systematically summarizes the latest advancements across the entire mead production continuum, including honey selection, mead classification (encompassing variations in alcohol-sugar profiles and ingredient complexity), modern fermentation control strategies, comprehensive compositional profiling (both volatile and non-volatile fractions), and the elucidation of physicochemical functionality. A central theme throughout is the integration of traditional techniques-such as region-specific honey utilization and time-honored fermentation practices-with modern biotechnological approaches, including precision fermentation control, multi-omics analytics, and advanced sensory measurement technologies. This convergence not only addresses long-standing challenges in production optimization, flavor mechanism elucidation, and quality standardization but also drives innovation and sustainable development within the mead industry.
Our analysis supports the conclusion that the complex sensory profile of mead emerges from synergistic biochemical networks. Yeast-mediated metabolism, primarily through glycolysis and the Ehrlich pathway, serves as a key driver in this process, while being modulated by non-volatile components and physicochemical properties. Core microbial metabolic pathways convert honey-derived sugars and amino acids into a diverse array of volatile compounds: esters (e.g., ethyl acetate, contributing fruity-floral notes), alcohols (e.g., phenylethanol, imparting sweet rose-like aromas), and terpenoids (e.g., linalool, synthesized via microbial transformation of honey-sourced precursors such as geranyl pyrophosphate), which collectively underpin the aromatic profile. Beyond the volatile chemistry, this review also contextualizes key physicochemical parameters within sensory perception, highlighting how specific gravity influences body and sweetness intensity, acidity (including both total and volatile fractions) shapes tartness, freshness, and microbial stability, and residual assimilable nitrogen dictates flavor consistency and the biosynthesis of yeast-derived metabolites, thus offering a holistic perspective on mead's sensory complexity.
Post-fermentation processes, particularly aging, were identified as critical stages in further refining the flavor matrix. Maillard reactions, esterification, and interactions with oak matrices (when employed) further enhance aromatic complexity, smoothness of mouthfeel, and the integration of volatile and non-volatile components, while also playing a crucial role in ensuring long-term compositional stability. Despite these insights, significant knowledge gaps remain: there is a need to develop quantitative models that link the extensive volatile metabolome (with over 450 identified compounds) to multidimensional sensory perception (encompassing aroma, taste, and mouthfeel interactions); to elucidate the mechanistic pathways of honey-specific bioactive compounds (such as flavonoids and phenolic acids) during fermentation and aging; and to definitively establish the roles of non-Saccharomyces yeasts in flavor diversification and stress resilience during production.
Future research must prioritize a multifaceted approach: the establishment of standardized fermentation and aging protocols (including parameters such as temperature control, microbial consortia, and nutrient supplementation strategies); the deployment of multi-omics platforms (integrating genomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics) to unravel the complex metabolic networks underlying flavor formation; and the development of robust, quantitative sensory evaluation frameworks that integrate chemical composition with consumer perceptual dynamics. Addressing these gaps in flavor biochemistry, process innovation, and quality control will provide a solid scientific foundation for optimizing mead production. The insights outlined herein are poised to foster the sustainable development of the mead industry, enhancing its competitiveness and unlocking its considerable potential in the global food and beverage market-especially in meeting the growing consumer demand for novel, high-quality, and functionally distinctive fermented beverages.
By integrating traditional and modern techniques, advancing our understanding of the metabolic pathways responsible for volatile compound formation, and refining sensory evaluation methodologies, this review provides valuable insights into the factors that shape mead's flavor profile. These insights may offer a basis for optimizing mead production processes and fostering innovation within the industry. Future research will be instrumental in further refining production techniques and quality control, supporting the continued growth and development of the mead sector.
