Comparative evaluation of efficacy and safety of nitrous oxide and midazolam for conscious sedation in pediatric dental patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Children’s fear of dental treatment can create challenges for dentists in managing their behavior, which may hinder the provision of proper dental care. The use of sedative agents can alleviate anxiety and control behaviors in pediatric dental patients. Nitrous oxide and midazolam are the two most commonly used pharmacological agents for behavioral management in pediatric dental care. However, evidence supporting their efficacy and safety is unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to consolidate the most robust evidence on the efficacy and safety of nitrous oxide versus midazolam for conscious sedation in pediatric dental patients aged 3 to 16 years. As per the predefined protocol, a systematic review methodology was employed to search electronic databases. Titles and abstracts were screened, and the full text was reviewed by two authors. The included trials compared nitrous oxide and midazolam as single sedatives in children aged 3 to 16 undergoing dental treatment. These trials compared nitrous oxide with oral, intravenous, transmucosal, or intranasal midazolam. Data on the methods, participants, interventions, outcomes, and results were extracted, and each trial was assessed for risk bias. Of the seven articles, two had a moderate risk of bias and five had a high risk. The overall results showed no statistically significant differences between the two interventions (P = 0.64). Despite the limitations of this systematic review and meta-analysis, the present study provides important information regarding the efficacy of both sedatives. However, well-designed and well-documented clinical trials are still required. In addition, guidelines for the standardization of the criteria and definition of success in procedural sedation need to be developed.

INTRODUCTION
Behavioral management is crucial for effective dental care in pediatric patients because fear and behavioral issues are common challenges. These issues may stem from previous traumatic experiences (objective fear) or may be influenced by family, friends, and caregivers (subjective fear). Additionally, children's limited maturity and intellectual capacity can lead to communication difficulties. Because cooperation is essential for the success of pediatric dental treatments, various behavioral management techniques are necessary.
Most pediatric patients can be managed using non-pharmacological strategies; however, pharmacological methods are sometimes required. Traditionally, general anesthesia was used, but it is costly, requires specialized personnel and facilities, and carries potential risks. Consequently, alternative sedation techniques have been developed for managing these patients.
Conscious sedation is a technique in which the patient verbally communicates with the dentist during sedation, thereby providing a safer alternative with fewer potential risks. ADA has stated conscious sedation as: “A minimally low level of consciousness slightly impairs cognitive function and coordination, while leaving ventilatory and cardiovascular functions unaffected. This is considered moderate sedation”.
An Ideal sedative should reduce anxiety, improve behavior, facilitate a smooth procedure, and foster a positive attitude toward the patients. They should have a rapid onset, wear off quickly, and cause minimal adverse effects. Over time, various sedative drugs have been used, including barbiturates, inhalation agents, opioids, and short-acting benzodiazepines. Benzodiazepines primarily induce muscle relaxation, hypnosis, amnesia, and anxiolysis, and have anticonvulsant effects. These drugs are classified based on their elimination half-lives.
Midazolam, a short-acting benzodiazepine, is widely used because of its rapid onset, short duration, multiple administration routes, and minimal side effects. The recommended dosage ranges from 0.25–1.0 mg/kg, with a maximum dose of 20 mg. When administered orally, it can be mixed with a sweetening agent, such as a simple syrup, and placed in the retromolar area. After oral intake, midazolam undergoes decarboxylation in gastric juice before absorption. Its high first-pass metabolism contributes to its short duration, with sedative effects typically worn off within 20–30 min. When administered intramuscularly or intravenously, midazolam does not irritate the tissues and remains stable in aqueous solutions. Its high lipophilicity enables rapid penetration across the blood-brain barrier, leading to quick sedation. Oral, intravenous, intramuscular, and rectal routes have shown promising results; however, their limitations, such as low bioavailability and fear of needles, restrict their use. By contrast, the intranasal route offers faster induction because of the high vascularity and large surface area of the nasal mucosa. It also requires minimal patient cooperation and does not require strictly sterile techniques for drug administration. However, challenges remain, such as the potential for irritation or discomfort in the nasal passages and variability in drug absorption, which can depend on factors such as nasal mucosa health and the specific formulation of the drug. Overall, it is a compelling option when considering alternatives to injection-based methods for conscious sedation or other medical interventions.
Benzodiazepine levels in the cerebrospinal fluid closely resembled those of free drugs in the plasma. These drugs bind to GABA-A receptors, thereby enhancing the inhibitory effects of GABA and increasing neuronal resistance to excitation. Midazolam may cause side effects, such as excessive drowsiness, cognitive impairment, ataxia, and slurred speech. Respiratory depression is a major concern requiring immediate attention. Flumazenil, a competitive benzodiazepine antagonist, effectively reverses sedation, but has a very short duration of action. For safe and effective reversal, 0.2 mg should be administered every 3–5 min, up to a maximum dose of 1 mg.
Nitrous oxide (N2O) is a widely used inhalational sedative owing to its combined analgesic and anxiolytic properties, which make it particularly suitable for mild sedation during dental procedures. It is a colorless, odorless, non-combustible gas, and the least potent inhalation anesthetic. N2O has a rapid onset of action, typically taking effect within 2–5 min. Once inhaled, it quickly diffuses into systemic circulation via the pulmonary vein, bypassing first-pass metabolism. Factors such as respiratory physiology and particle size influence delivery. The rapid diffusion of N2O can also contribute to the second-gas effect, accelerating sedation onset. Its minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of 105% indicates a lower potency than that of other inhalational anesthetics.
Nitrous oxide exerts its effects by inhibiting NMDA receptors, thereby contributing to anesthesia, whereas its analgesic properties result from the release of endogenous opioids. Its anxiolytic effects stem from GABA-A receptor activation, and gas is primarily eliminated through the lungs. N2O is considered safe for asthmatic patients but is contraindicated in individuals with obstructive pulmonary diseases. Prolonged exposure may lead to megaloblastic changes in the bone marrow, making it unsuitable for individuals with a methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase deficiency. N2O is easy to administer and allows precise titration. It has a high safety margin, and nausea and vomiting occur in approximately 1–10% of cases. The nasal hood may interfere with the anterior maxillary injections because the patient must wear the nasal hood throughout the procedure.
In clinical dentistry, nitrous oxide concentrations should not exceed 50% during routine procedures, although adjustments can be made based on the complexity of the procedure. Abrupt cessation of high N2O concentrations can lead to diffusion hypoxia, in which alveolar oxygen is diluted. To prevent this, 100% oxygen should be administered after discontinuing N2O. Additionally, residual nitrous oxide exposure may pose occupational hazards, emphasizing the importance of proper ventilation and equipment maintenance in clinical settings.

Need for this review
This review systematically evaluated the safety and efficacy of midazolam-based sedation versus nitrous oxide sedation in pediatric dental patients aged 3–16 years. To the best of our knowledge, no existing systematic reviews or meta-analyses have specifically compared and synthesized data on the effectiveness and safety of these sedatives in pediatric dentistry.
By integrating data from multiple studies and conducting a thorough risk-of-bias assessment, this meta-analysis provided a detailed comparison of N2O and idazolam for conscious sedation in pediatric dental patients. As the first meta-analysis to focus on this topic, it fills a critical gap in the literature and offers valuable evidence-based insights. These findings will help guide clinical decision-making and improve sedation practices in pediatric dentistry.

METHODS

1. Protocol and registration
This review follows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and is registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024523253).

2. Selection criteria for studies

1) Types of studies included
This systematic review included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on pediatric dental patients undergoing conscious sedation with N2O or midazolam. The eligible trial designs included split-mouth, parallel-group, crossover, prospective, single-blind, non-blind, single-center, multicenter, and pilot studies. In vitro, case-control, cross-sectional, quasi-randomized studies, case reports, case series, narrative reviews, non-English literature, animal studies, commentaries, interviews, and updates were excluded. Seven studies were analyzed based on primary and secondary outcomes.

2) Types of participants
Inclusion criteria
1. RCTs involving children (3–16 years) requiring sedation for dental procedures, including those with medical or behavioral conditions.
2. Children undergoing restorative treatment, pulpal therapy with local anesthesia, extraction (deciduous or permanent teeth), or dental trauma management.
Exclusion criteria
1. Children undergoing complex surgical procedures.

3. Types of interventions
Test group: Midazolam was administered via various routes, doses, and timings before dental treatment.
Control group: Nitrous oxide delivered via inhalation for sedation before treatment.

4. Types of outcome measures

1) Primary outcomes:
• Anxiety/behavior assessed through various indices.
• Safety measured via physiological parameters at different treatment stages.

2) Secondary outcomes:
• Duration of sedation.
• Sedation level.
• Adverse events.
• Patient preference.

5. Search strategy

1) Information sources
1. Electronic searches: A literature search was conducted in the PUBMED, GOOGLE SCHOLAR, K-HUB, PROQUEST, and COCHRANE REVIEW databases by two independent investigators. Studies published between 2002 and 2023 were included.
2. Additional sources: A manual search ensured the inclusion of relevant published and unpublished studies. Experts in the field were consulted regarding unpublished data. For comprehensive evidence collection, the search was restricted to English-language publications.

2) Data collection and analysis

6. Selection of studies and search strategy
Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of articles for initial exclusion. A search strategy focusing on RCTs was used. Full-text articles that met the inclusion criteria were retrieved. Discrepancies were resolved by consultation with a third examiner.
The search strategy incorporated various combinations of titles, abstracts, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), and keywords, including "Conscious sedation," "Pediatric patients," "Children," "Nitrous oxide," "Midazolam," and "Randomized controlled trials."
The research question, framed using the PICO approach, was: "Is midazolam sedation as effective and safe as nitrous oxide sedation in pediatric patients aged 3–16 years before a dental procedure?"

7. Selection process
a) Identification: Two independent reviewers screened the databases and selected articles based on their titles and abstracts. Duplicate records were excluded from analysis.
b) Screening: Publications were screened in three stages:
1. Titles were reviewed.
2. Abstracts were examined.
3. Full texts were assessed if the abstract lacked sufficient details.
In addition, the full texts and reference lists of the primary articles were reviewed to identify relevant studies.

8. Data extraction and management
Data extraction was performed using a structured form with both reviewers blinded to each other’s data. Manual searches and citation tracking supplemented the computerized search. Gray literature and unpublished studies were included to minimize the publication bias. However, the reviewers were not blinded to journal names or study authors.

9. Data items
Each study is categorized by publication year in ascending order. The extracted data were standardized and recorded in a structured form.
Assessment of Risk of Bias in the Included Studies Assessing bias is essential for evaluating study reliability. Eligible studies were assessed using the Revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool for Randomized Trials (ROB 2). Two independent reviewers classified the bias risk as low, unclear, or high for each domain, and the overall risk rating was determined accordingly.

10. Handling missing data
Only the available data were analyzed. The authors of the included studies were contacted to clarify missing information when necessary.

11. Synthesis of results
Data were analyzed by comparing the numeric values of the measured variables. Results were presented narratively, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. The assessed outcomes included sedation level, adverse events, appointment duration, and patient preference. Sensitivity analysis was performed to compare the results based on the risk of bias.

RESULTS

1. Study description and search results
Following the search strategy, the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1) summarizes the findings. A total of 356 records were identified, of which 224 were screened based on title and abstract. After full-text assessment of the 20 articles, seven met the inclusion criteria.

2. Characteristics of included studies

1) Study design
Six studies employed a randomized, controlled, split-mouth, crossover design, and one used a randomized, controlled, crossover approach. Publications ranged from 2002 to 2023. Five studies were conducted in the UK and two in India. Ethical approval was obtained for all cases. Two studies included sample size calculation and five specified randomization methods, except two (Table 1).

2) Participant characteristics
Participants ranged in age from 3 to 16 years, with an average age of 9.1 years. The trials enrolled 276 subjects (39.42 per study), with 31 dropouts. One study included only ASA I patients, while six included both ASA I and II. One trial assessed baseline anxiety using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and two used Frankl’s Behavior Rating Scale. Preoperative fasting (2 h) was reported in five studies. (Table 1)

3) Intervention details
All studies compared nitrous oxide and midazolam. Most (n = 5) used the Quantiflex MDM relative analgesia, while one used Matrx Porter Digital. Nitrous oxide was titrated in 10% increments up to 30%, followed by 100% oxygen post-procedure. One study used 50% nitrous oxide.
Midazolam administration varied: oral (n = 4), intravenous (n = 1), transmucosal (n = 1), and intranasal (n = 1). Dosages ranged from 0.2–0.5 mg/kg depending on the administration route. Treatments included bilateral orthodontic extractions (n = 4), primary teeth extractions (n = 2), and bilateral pulp therapy (n = 1) (Table 1).

4) Outcome measures
Outcomes included behavior, anxiety, physiological parameters, sedation level, adverse effects, and patient preference. Behavior was assessed using the Frankl–Houpt Behavior Rating Scale. Anxiety was measured using Spielberger’s State Anxiety Inventory, the Children’s Fear Survey Schedule, and the VAS. Sedation levels were evaluated using the Brietkopf and Buttner classification, Ramsay sedation scale, and Ellis sedation score. The patient preferences were recorded in six trials. Time to peak sedation, recovery duration, and total appointment length were also analyzed. Nitrous oxide resulted in faster onset and recovery, whereas midazolam led to longer appointments (Table 2, 3).

3. Synthesis of findings

1) Oral midazolam vs. nitrous oxide (n = 4)
A total of 154 children participated in the study, with nine withdrawals. Three studies used the Frankl and Houpt Behavior Rating Scales, which showed no significant differences in behavior. Anxiety levels were significantly reduced in both groups. Two studies noted significant differences in arterial oxygen saturation; however, the values remained within the safe limits. Higher sedation levels were observed with midazolam, leading to longer appointment durations. Adverse events were significant in one trial. Two studies reported a higher preference for nitrous oxide, while one favored midazolam (Table 2, 3).

2) Intravenous midazolam vs. nitrous oxide (n = 1)
Among 42 participants (aged 12–16 years), 13 declined participation due to cannulation concerns, and two withdrew. No significant differences were found in behavior or sedation scales. Physiological parameters remained stable. Adverse effects were observed with midazolam, although preference was slightly higher. (Table 2, 3)

3) Transmucosal midazolam vs. nitrous oxide (n = 1)
A study of 36 children compared buccal midazolam (0.2 mg/kg) to nitrous oxide for orthodontic extractions. No significant behavioral differences were noted. Anxiety levels decreased significantly in both groups. The physiological parameters were within normal limits. Patients in the midazolam group had longer appointments and fewer side effects. Nitrous oxide was preferred by 20 patients, whereas midazolam was preferred by 10 (Table 2, 3).

4) Intranasal midazolam vs. nitrous oxide (n = 1)
A trial involving 35 children (aged 4–7) compared intranasal midazolam (0.3 mg/kg) to nitrous oxide. No significant behavioral differences were found. The physiological parameters were stable, except for a higher heart rate at T2 with midazolam. Minor adverse reactions, including sneezing and hiccups, were experienced by four participants. (Table 2, 3).

4. Risk of bias assessment
The Revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (ROB 2) was used to evaluate bias in the included studies. Five studies had a high risk, and two had a moderate risk.
• Randomization Process: three low risk, four some concerns.
• Allocation Concealment: one low risk, four some concerns, two high risk.
• Blinding of Participants: two low-risk, four some concerns, and one high-risk.
• Blinding of Outcome: three low-risk, three some concerns, and one high-risk.
• Missing Outcome Data: four low-risk, one concerned, and two high-risk.
• Selection of Reported Results: three low-risk, two high-risk, and two some concerns.

DISCUSSION

1. Summary of main results
Similar to other systematic reviews, the studies included in this review were principally poorly designed and reported. Most showed a mix of low, unclear, and high risk of bias: none of the participants had a low risk, two studies (29%) were rated as moderate risk, and five (71%) were considered high risk. Evidence is insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of nitrous oxide versus midazolam. Most trials found that both treatments were similarly effective, except for one. Both treatments showed comparable effectiveness in terms of behavioral scores, sedation levels, and treatment completion, although midazolam typically required more time. None of the trials reported significant respiratory depression, and excitatory behavior was rare. Both techniques were deemed safe, with any desaturation remaining within acceptable limits.
This meta-analysis examined the comparative effects of Midazolam and Nitrous Oxide on SpO2, heart rate, and anxiety levels.
1. SpO2: Both interventions had similar effects on oxygen saturation, with no significant changes noted. This suggests that both agents are safe for respiratory function when used appropriately.
2. Heart Rate: No significant differences in heart rate were observed between the two groups. Both interventions appeared to be equally safe in terms of the cardiovascular response.
3. Anxiety: There was a trend favoring Midazolam for anxiety reduction; however, the result was not statistically significant because of the high variability among the studies. However, more standardized trials are required to confirm this potential benefit.
Overall, Midazolam and Nitrous Oxide were comparable in terms of safety (SpO2 and heart rate) and effectiveness (anxiety reduction), although high heterogeneity suggests caution when generalizing these findings. Further research using standardized protocols may provide definitive conclusions.

2. Overall completeness and applicability of evidence
After a comprehensive search using an advanced search builder, this systematic review included seven RCTs, which are regarded as the paramount evidence of quality. To the best of our knowledge, these seven articles met the inclusion standards. According to Khan et al., no minimum number of articles is required for a systematic review. The search was limited to English-language articles published between 2002 and 2023.
This review included several crossover trials. Lim et al. noted that crossover designs enhance statistical efficiency by comparing treatments within the same subject, achieving an accuracy similar to that of parallel designs with smaller samples. However, they are often excluded because of the need for a washout period, ethical concerns, and potential dropouts. Ashley et al. recommended excluding these studies from systematic reviews.
Effective behavior management is essential in pediatric dentistry, particularly for children aged 3–7 years, who tend to be more anxious and less cooperative than older children. This review included patients aged 3–16 years old. Kapur A et al. (2018) suggested conscious sedation is suitable for children over 1 year. The AAPD (2023) considers N2O safe for all ages, especially for children who can follow nasal breathing instructions and respond to verbal commands, with parental consent recommended for very young children. Ashley P et al. advised against midazolam for infants under 1 year or weighing less than 10 kg.
A thorough systemic assessment is crucial before administering sedation. According to the ASA classification, ASA Class I and II patients are typically suitable for outpatient moderate sedation, while ASA Class III and IV patients require medical supervision in a hospital.31 This review included only ASA Class I and II patients. Five trials adopted a 2-hour preoperative fasting period. For patients at high aspiration risk, the 2–4–6 fasting rule is recommended, with food and fluid intake documented on the day of sedation. The AAPD (2023) states fasting is unnecessary for N2O/O2 analgesia/anxiolysis, though a light meal may be advisable. Ashley et al. noted that treatment type influences patient behavior and anxiety. This review included RCTs on various dental procedures, with extractions in five trials and pulp therapy in two.
The route of drug administration has a significant impact on effectiveness. Four trials included in this review administered oral midazolam at 0.3–0.5 mg/kg. Karishma et al. (2022) found similar sedation levels at 0.3 and 0.5 mg/kg, recommending 0.5–1 mg/kg for children. To mask its bitter taste, trials used sugar-free orange or blackcurrant cordial. Shabbir et al. noted that oral delivery is preferred in pediatric dentistry due to fear of injections, although Cheng et al. found both oral and IV routes effective.
Wilson et al. (2003) administered midazolam intravenously at a mean dose of 2.8 mg (range, 1–5 mg), whereas Rosen et al. recommended 0.01–0.05 mg/kg IV. One trial by Srinivasan et al. 27] used intranasal midazolam at 0.3 mg/kg, with Fuks et al. stating 0.2 mg/kg was equally effective. However, Shabbir et al. reported nasal irritation and poor absorption, whereas Srinivasan et al. observed sneezing in 11.4% of children, likely due to the drug trickling into the oropharynx. Wilson KE et al. (2007) found transmucosal midazolam (0.2 mg/kg) had high bioavailability.
All trials in this review used titration techniques for nitrous oxide at 30–40% concentration, typically achieving effective analgesia and anxiolysis; Guelmann et al. found no added benefit in continuous administration. The effectiveness of nitrous oxide is influenced significantly by psychological reassurance; therefore, traditional behavior guidance techniques should be maintained to enhance its impact.
Effective behavioral management is essential in pediatric dentistry. Behavior was assessed using validated scales. These scales are integral to understanding and addressing children's behavior at each dental appointment. The effectiveness of the drugs in the trials was gauged by assessing either a decrease in anxiety or an improvement in behavior. According to Guinot et al., behavior and dental anxiety are related. Therefore, it is imperative that dentists assess behaviors to identify patients who require specialized care because of anxiety.
Frankl Behavior Rating Scale: Used in three trials, this scale was considered reliable for grading children’s behaviors. It is considered a highly dependable asset for behavior grading of children in a dental context. Jaikaria et al. advocated Frankl’s scale for assessing pre-operative behavior of patients.
Houpt Behavior Rating Scale Five trials were used to measure the overall treatment outcomes. The overall behavior and outcome of treatment were measured using the Houpt Behavior Rating Scale in five trials. Hosey et al. suggested that the Houpt Scale is a dependable instrument for evaluating a patient’s reaction to particular aspects of treatment. Lima AR et al. in 2003 and Mozafar et al. in 2018 also used the same scale to assess behavioral improvement.
Behavior Style Questionnaire (BSQ) and Emotionality-Activity-Sociability (EAS) Scale: Used by Janiani et al. (2023) for behavioral assessment.
Anxiety was measured using various scales, including the Corah Dental Anxiety Scale (DAS), Spielberger’s State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, and Anxiety Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Mukundan D et al. (2023) used the facial image scale, whereas Venchard et al. employed the Corah Anxiety Questionnaire. Two trials measured general and dental anxiety using Spielberger’s State Anxiety Inventory and Children’s Fear Survey Schedule. Visual analogue scale was used in a trial by Mhatre et al in 2018.
The analysis of stress-related findings is complex because of their physiological and psychological aspects. Anxiety assessment is challenging because it depends on subjective judgment. An objective measure currently used is cortisol level in the blood/saliva. Studies, such as those by Pereira-Santos et al., have shown that salivary cortisol is an effective indicator of stress response. They concluded that a combination of midazolam (7.5 mg), N2O, and 5% O2 effectively reduced salivary cortisol levels.
Shapira et al. found consistently high behavioral scores with midazolam. Mhatre et al. reported superior anxiolysis with nitrous oxide compared to oral midazolam. Luhmann et al. found that nitrous oxide minimized discomfort, with fewer side effects and quicker recovery than midazolam. Veerkamp et al. also observed a significant reduction in anxiety with nitrous oxide. Srinivasan et al. found intranasal midazolam as effective as N2O in reducing anxiety.
Sedation was evaluated using the Brietkopf and Buttner Sedation Scale (five trials), Ramsay Sedation Score (one trial), and Ellis Sedation Score (one trial). The Houpt Sedation Rating Scale, which is widely used for its reliability, was applied by Al-Zahrani et al. and Ilasrinivasan et al.. According to Mhatre et al., no children progressed to deep sedation. Wilson et al. found that all children remained conscious throughout their treatment, regardless of the type of sedation used. Additionally, none of the children were over-sedated. As per Wilson et al., none of the patients in either group reached the highest level of sedation, and verbal communication was maintained throughout.
Physiological safety markers included arterial oxygen saturation and respiratory rates. For midazolam, Wilson et al. reported oxygen saturation between 90% and 100%, with safe respiration rates. Fraone et al. found no significant effects on the vital signs at 0.5 mg/kg. Fraone et al. reported that oral midazolam at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg had no significant impact on heart rate, oxygen saturation, or blood pressure. Mhatre et al. (2018) reported no cases of respiratory depression, with respiratory rate changes remaining within acceptable limits. Nitrous Oxide: Wilson KE et al. reported median oxygen saturation of 98%. However, Litman et al. found mild respiratory depression when combining 0.5 mg/kg midazolam with 60% N2O in young children. Both are considered safe for sedation, with midazolam having minimal impact on respiratory function compared to other agents.
The authors measured the time taken for sedation onset, recovery, and the total appointment time. Nitrous oxide has a quicker onset and recovery than midazolam, reducing the overall appointment length. Keidan et al. also found that N2O resulted in shorter hospital stays.
Mhatre et al. reported that 70% of the children preferred oral midazolam for future sedation, whereas 30% preferred nitrous oxide. The stronger preference for inhalational sedation in the study by Wilson et al. in 2006 differs from that in a comparable study conducted by Wilson et al. in 2002, on children aged 10–16 years, which found that oral midazolam was the preferred sedation technique, likely due to better emotional regulation.
Overall, both midazolam and nitrous oxide were safe and effective, and their selection was influenced by patient preference, procedure type, and behavioral factors.

3. Quality of the evidence
The quality assessment used the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, which is known for its rigorous evaluation. The included studies showed a high overall risk of bias due to inadequate blinding of operators, outcome assessors, and participants, affecting intervention implementation and evaluation. Of the seven studies, two had a moderate risk, none had a low risk, and five had a high risk.
Domain-wise assessments varied:
• Domain 1: Low risk in three studies, concerns in four.
• Domain 2: Low in one, moderate in four, high in two.
• Domain 3: Low in two, concerns in four, high in one.
• Domain 4: Low in three, concerns in three, high in one.
• Domain 5: Low in four, moderate in one, high in two.
• Domain 6: Low in three, high in two, concerns in two.
These inconsistencies highlight the need for a better methodology. The study quality varied, with QATSDD scores ranging from 22 to 40 (average: 25.6/42). Many trials lacked details regarding allocation concealment, sequence generation, and participant characteristics. Sample size estimations were often missing, possibly reducing the statistical power. Variability in statistical methods further complicates data analysis.
The findings of this study align with prior reviews, emphasizing the limited evidence for pediatric dental sedation; however, bias from inadequate blinding remains a concern, influencing treatment delivery and assessment. Recommendations have been made to mitigate these biases. Standardizing operator communication can reduce the bias in nitrous oxide effectiveness. Despite these limitations, the data remain valuable for our conclusions.

4. Strength of this review and meta-analysis
The strengths of this systematic review and meta-analysis include adherence to the PRISMA guidelines, methodological rigor, and transparency. A comprehensive literature search across multiple databases minimized the risk of missing relevant studies, whereas the use of the ROB 2 tool ensured robust risk of bias assessments. As the first meta-analysis to compare the efficacy and safety of nitrous oxide and midazolam for conscious sedation in pediatric dental patients aged 3–16 years, it addresses a significant gap in the literature. The inclusion of both safety and efficacy outcomes, along with quantitative synthesis through meta-analysis, strengthened the reliability of the findings. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses further enhanced the robustness of the results, offering valuable guidance for evidence-based clinical decision-making.

5. Potential biases in the review process
Most studies included in the review were crossover trials. According to Higgins (2011), previous treatment experiences have a well-accepted influence on dental anxiety, particularly in children. It is due to the carryover effect that modifies the direct effects of the treatment. The latest Cochrane study on paediatric sedation excluded crossover experiments, but then later they were included in National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidelines (NICE).
A significant bias may stem from this review, which included only English-language studies and full-text articles, potentially reducing the representation of the findings. Limited access to unpublished and gray literature, along with a 10-year search window, might have introduced publication bias. Variations in medication administration routes and dosages across studies also contributed to potential confounding biases. Agreements and disagreements with other studies or reviews:-
The findings of this systematic review support those of a systematic review conducted in 2023. Only nitrous oxide and midazolam conscious sedation were used during tooth extraction in the linked review. Restrictions on the treatment administered were not considered in the current review. Additionally, their main outcomes were time, anxiety, and degree of sedation. Whereas behavior and safety were the primary objectives of the review.
Another related review published previously in 2018 considered nitrous oxide and midazolam sedation in pediatric patients. Both reviews have applied GRADE approach to assess the quality of evidence included in the systematic review. This review used the Quality Assessment Tool for Studies with Diverse Signs (QATSDD). The tool demonstrates strong reliability and ease of use for application to multi- or mixed-methods health services research. Another review by Janiani et al. also reported that Midazolam and nitrous oxide inhalation were not statistically different in terms of the success of treatment and behavior modification. However, midazolam results in a deeper level of sedation and amnesia. All the included studies were found to have a high risk of bias.

Author’s conclusion

Implications for practice
Determining the optimal sedation regimen is complex due to variations in drug use and administration. Nitrous oxide and midazolam are effective and safe for managing anxiety during dental procedures. Midazolam can cause fatigue and affect heart rate, requiring close monitoring. Generally, 30-50% nitrous oxide is used for sedation, and a 0.5 mg/kg oral dose of midazolam is recommended. Midazolam tended to extend appointment times compared to nitrous oxide, but both were within acceptable limits. No severe respiratory depression or significant adverse effects were reported. These drugs are safe with proper medical supervision, suggesting the need for further research in community settings.

Implications for research
The reviewed studies have several limitations. The following recommendations were made to enhance future research quality.
1. Adherence to CONSORT guidelines
Clinical trials should follow the CONSORT guidelines for designing, conducting, and reporting to ensure transparency and rigor (Moher 2001).
2. Improved reporting
Studies should provide detailed reports on factors such as sex, weight, time to onset of sedation, dropout rates, reasons for treatment failure, baseline anxiety and behavior, physiological parameters, and consistency in scales and outcomes.
3. Blinding
Ideally, the surgeon, participant, and assessor should be blinded to the sedatives used. If full blinding is not feasible, the assessors should be blinded. Blinding can be partially achieved using control agents such as O2 during midazolam administration or plain orange cordial prior to nitrous oxide.
4. Sample size and standardization
Therefore, accurate sample size estimation and reporting should be prioritized. Many studies lack standardization, which leads to variability. Trials should focus on specific age groups, as methods effective for younger children may not be effective for older children.
5. Medical status of participants
Most studies involve healthy or minimally diseased children (ASA I and II). Further research is needed to explore the effectiveness of sedation strategies in medically compromised patients.
6. Study design
Parallel designs are preferred over crossover designs because of the impact of the first treatment phase on the second phase. Future studies should use well-designed, parallel, and randomized controlled trials, preferably in community settings.
7. Baseline and outcome variables
Standardize the evaluation of key outcomes, including behavior and anxiety. Ensure that treatment completion and other outcome measures are statistically analyzed and documented, focusing on both clinician- and patient-centered indicators.
