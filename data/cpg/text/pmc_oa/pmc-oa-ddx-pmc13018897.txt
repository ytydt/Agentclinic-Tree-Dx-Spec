Mpox and the one health approach
Objective
To synthesize updated epidemiological, genomic, clinical, and policy evidence on mpox, with emphasis on the emergence of Clade I sub-lineage “Clade Ib” and its implications for One Health-based preparedness.
Methods
This narrative, non-systematic state-of-the-art review draws on literature indexed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase, alongside official reports from WHO, Africa CDC, CDC, ECDC, and FAO. Searches covered January 2000 to May 2025, with epidemiological data included through 31 May 2025. Human observational studies, outbreak reports, genomic analyses, clinical trials, and guidance documents were included. Evidence was synthesized in alignment with SANRA principles.
Key findings
Since the 2022 global outbreak driven by Clade IIb, mpox epidemiology has shifted with sustained transmission of Clade I variants in Central Africa and the emergence of a proposed Clade Ib lineage characterized by APOBEC3-associated mutational signatures. Pediatric predominance and household transmission patterns distinguish recent outbreaks from earlier sexual-network–associated spread. Vaccine effectiveness of JYNNEOS varies by population and dosing regimen, while randomized trials (PALM007, STOMP) have not demonstrated statistically significant clinical benefit of tecovirimat on primary endpoints. Environmental surveillance and genomic sequencing remain critical to tracking transmission dynamics.
Implications
Even amid sustained human-to-human transmission, integrated One Health strategies-linking human, animal, and environmental surveillance are essential for outbreak containment, equitable vaccine deployment, and long-term prevention.

Article type and methodological approach

Article type and review approach
This manuscript is a narrative, non-systematic state-of-the-art review. It provides a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence on Mpox epidemiology, virology, diagnostics, treatment strategies, vaccination, and One Health integration, with a particular focus on the evolving Clade Ib epidemiology (2023–2025). The objective of this review is not to perform quantitative meta-analysis or formal systematic evidence grading, but rather to integrate emerging genomic, epidemiological, clinical, and policy-level evidence to inform preparedness and public health strategy.

Methods
A structured literature search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase, alongside major public health repositories including World Health Organization (WHO) Disease Outbreak News (DON), Africa CDC situation reports, U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Mpox dashboards, European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) reports, and Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) One Health surveillance documents. Search terms included combinations of “Mpox,” “Monkeypox virus,” or “MPXV” with “Clade I,” “Clade Ib,” or “Clade IIb,” as well as topic-specific terms such as “APOBEC3 mutations,” “Tecovirimat,” “Brincidofovir,” “Vaccinia Immune Globulin,” “JYNNEOS,” “ACAM2000,” “Vertical transmission,” “One Health,” “Environmental surveillance,” and “Genomic epidemiology.” Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied to refine the search strategy. Primary literature coverage spanned January 2000 to May 2025, with particular emphasis on outbreak-related evidence published between January 2022 and May 2025. The final literature search was updated on 30 May 2025, and epidemiological data were included up to a cut-off date of 31 May 2025.

Reporting guideline
This narrative, state-of-the-art review was developed in accordance with the SANRA (Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles) framework to enhance transparency, methodological rigor, and reporting quality. The review addresses SANRA core elements including (1) justification of the review's importance, (2) clearly stated objectives, (3) structured literature identification strategy, (4) transparent selection and synthesis of evidence, and (5) balanced, evidence-informed interpretation. Given the integrative and policy-oriented aim of this manuscript, a systematic review framework (e.g., PRISMA) was not applied.

Sex and gender considerations
This review applies principles consistent with the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines. The terms “sex” and “gender” are used in accordance with source reporting. Where studies reported biological categories (male/female) based on clinical or surveillance data, the term “sex” is used. Where behavioral or identity-based descriptors (e.g., men who have sex with men) were reported, the terminology is retained as presented in the original sources. Given variability in outbreak surveillance systems, distinctions between sex assigned at birth, gender identity, and behavioral risk categories were not consistently available across studies. Accordingly, interpretations of pediatric predominance, pregnancy outcomes, and transmission within sexual networks are presented with acknowledgment of reporting heterogeneity and potential misclassification.

Introduction
Monkeypox virus, also known as mpox virus or MPXV, is a double-stranded DNA virus that is part of the Orthopoxvirus genus and is the cause of a zoonotic illness. As of August 5, 2022, there have been over 28,700 documented cases of the virus in 93 countries. Mpox virus (MPXV), a double-stranded DNA Orthopoxvirus, is the causative agent of a re-emerging zoonotic disease with global implications. While first identified in 1958 in captive monkeys in Denmark, it is now recognized that rodents are the primary reservoir species. Since its first confirmed human case in 1970 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, MPXV has caused periodic outbreaks, initially linked to zoonotic spillovers. Monkeypox can cause fever, rash, and pustules, which are clinical signs that are comparable to smallpox. But compared to smallpox, monkeypox usually has a lower death rate and is less severe. Importation of animals and migration from African nations were the primary causes of the illness epidemic. MPX instances were then periodically reported globally. Still, in 2022, the MPX virus spread internationally, and as a result, regardless of travel restrictions, it was deemed a worldwide health emergency. According to recent estimates, the 2022 mpox outbreak increased post-COVID-19 public health burdens and highlighted the need for integrated surveillance across global health frameworks. Outside of Africa, cases have been recorded since May 2022 from nations where mpox transmission has not previously been established. The monkeypox virus has been divided into two separate clades: Clade I, formerly called the Congo Basin (central African) clade, and Clade II, formerly known as the old west African clade. In accordance with current WHO nomenclature, mpox virus (MPXV) is classified into Clade I and Clade II, with Clade II further subdivided into Clade IIa and Clade IIb as part of a revised, neutral naming system for virus variants. Recent genomic surveillance reports note the growing detection of a Clade I sub-group referred to as Clade Ib, including outbreaks in eastern regions of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where sustained human-to-human transmission has been documented. Historical indicators of mpox infection, such as recent visits to endemic regions, contact with imported wild animals, and tending to an infected person or animal, aid in the differential diagnosis; nonetheless, clinical symptoms are crucial. In 2022, the World Health Organization changed the sickness to conform to contemporary nomenclature conventions. These recommendations state that illness names should minimize needless negative consequences on commerce, travel, tourism, or animal welfare and avoid upsetting any ethnic, national, regional, professional, or cultural groups. Still, the historical moniker for the virus that causes it exists. In accordance with WHO nomenclature recommendations, the term “mpox” is used consistently throughout this manuscript to refer to the disease, while “mpox virus (MPXV)” refers to the causative pathogen.

More than 100 nations reported over 150 fatalities and approximately 90,000 verified cases. Europe experienced about 25,000 cases during this time, while North America saw about 30,000. The case-fatality rate (CFR) was less than 1%. In mid-2023, the WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) to be over because of a decrease in the number of cases. Around 29,000 suspected cases and 812 fatalities have been reported since January 2024 (CFR approximately 2.7–3.6% (confirmed cases; DRC reports)) as a result of the new sub-lineage, a proposed Clade I sub-lineage, provisionally termed ‘Clade Ib’ in recent genomic studies, emerged in the DRC. In 2024, there were 461 recorded deaths and more than 15,000 confirmed cases in Africa. Due to the fast spread of Clade Ib, the WHO re-declared PHEIC by August 2024. Approximately 66% of cases and 84% of fatalities in the DRC were in children under the age of 15, indicating a trend away from sexually transmitted diseases and toward domestic transmission. By March 2025, there were confirmed Clade Ib cases in Burundi, Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda, and Sweden, Thailand, the United States, Canada, Germany, Belgium, France, the United Kingdom, India, Pakistan, the United Arab Emirates, Oman, and China. Mpox, which is caused by the emerging Clade Ib subclade, has spread from the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) to at least 13 African nations as of mid-2025. Since September 2023, a novel clade Ib MPXV lineage has emerged in eastern DRC (South Kivu) and expanded within and beyond Central Africa. Genomic analysis reveals a predominance of APOBEC3-type mutations, indicating sustained human-to-human transmission rather than isolated zoonotic spillover. By early 2025, clade Ib had been detected across multiple African countries (e.g., Burundi, Tanzania, Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda, Zimbabwe, Zambia) and as far as Thailand, Sweden, Germany—documented via metagenomic sequencing. Recent genomic surveillance studies show that new MPXV lineages, especially those within Clade Ib and sub-lineages of Clade IIb, have a marked rise in APOBEC3-type mutational signatures, such as C-T and G-A transitions. According to Ochieng et al., for instance, APOBEC3 editing was detected in 90% of non-synonymous mutations and 88% of synonymous mutations among Clade IIb viruses, particularly in lineage B.1 and its progeny. Similar findings were published by Masirika et al., who found that APOBEC3-type mutations are strongly linked to persistent human-to-human transmission across geographically heterogeneous clusters. In the South Kivu outbreak, sexual transmission was a crucial factor in maintaining chains of spread, as evidenced by the fact that 29% of PCR-confirmed cases were sex workers and 52% of cases were female. Etiology and virology
Life cycle of the Mpox virus, detailing cellular entry mechanisms, cytoplasmic replication, virion formation (IMV/EEV), and immune evasion processes.
Fig. 1
A life-threatening zoonotic disease with a broad host range, MPXV is constantly appearing and reemerging. Considering their strong host-pathogen interaction with other Orthopoxvirus members, rodents might serve as potential MPXV reservoirs. While mpox's cutaneous and systemic manifestations are widely known, its neurological complications have recently come to prominence. Both African and American case series have reported neurological symptoms, such as encephalitis, seizures, and neuro-inflammatory sequelae. Huhn et al. found that 12% of hospitalized patients had encephalitis and confusion in a 2004 U.S. outbreak investigation. Similarly, seizures and post-encephalitic sequelae in pediatric mpox survivors confirm Orthopoxvirus neurotropism. Research has shown that mpox is an evolutionary illness. It is a zoonotic virus disease caused by a dsDNA virus belonging to the Poxviridae family, a group of orthopoxviruses that also includes smallpox. Primarily, mpox is transmitted by direct animal contact by bodily fluids, blood, aerosol, or infected wounds. It can also be spread by close human-to-human contact or respiratory secretions, with clinical signs and the development of serologic. Containing an approximate 200 kb genome, MPXV is a large double-stranded DNA virus containing inverted tandem repeats (ITS) and a covalently closed hairpin on both the 5′ and 3′ ends. Its huge size, broad host range, and tissue tropism make it the biggest known human virus. Many cell lines in mammals allow viruses to enter them. For viruses belonging to the genus Orthopoxvirus, no particular host cell receptor has been found too yet. Mainly, interactions with cell surface ligands (such as chondroitin sulphate, heparan sulphate, and glycoasaminoglycans) and membrane fusion are responsible for viral entrance. Both the external enveloped virus (EEV) and intracellular mature virus (IMV) are infectious virions produced by cytoplasmic replication. Immune evasion and intrahost transmission are functions of the exterior envelope of EEV, while interhost transmission is the responsibility of IMV. Recent genomic studies, such as the thorough analysis by Kinganda Lusamaki et al., have shown that Clade Ib MPXV strains, particularly those from the Kamituga (South Kivu) region in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, have a significantly higher burden of APOBEC3-mediated mutational signatures (C → T and G → A transitions) than Clade Ia. This suggests that human-to-human transmission is more likely than multiple zoonotic spillover events. This genetic characteristic is consistent with more general research showing that APOBEC3-driven mutation patterns are a defining characteristic of MPXV adaptability in human populations. Collectively, our observations highlight Clade Ib's evolutionary path toward effective interhuman transmission, highlighting the necessity of attentive genomic monitoring and flexible public health measures. Transmission
The primary way that mpox transmits from person to person is through close contact with an infected individual, such as a household member. As well as mouth-to-mouth or mouth-to-skin contact (like kissing), close contact also encompasses being face-to-face with a person with mpox, which may generate infectious respiratory secretions. Additionally, it can spread by coming into touch with an infected person's body fluids, including blood, saliva, and semen. The virus enters the human body through the respiratory system, breaks in the skin, or mucous membranes found in the mouth, nose, and eyes during transmission. Environmental sampling studies have demonstrated the presence of mpox virus DNA on high-contact household surfaces, including porous materials such as bedding and clothing, for several days following exposure. However, detection of viral DNA by PCR does not necessarily indicate the presence of viable, infectious virus. While some studies have successfully recovered replication-competent virus from select surface samples under controlled conditions, most environmental investigations report DNA detection without confirmation of viral infectivity. Reported persistence durations—such as detection up to approximately 15 days—primarily refer to PCR-based identification of viral genetic material rather than sustained recovery of infectious virus. Therefore, environmental contamination should be interpreted cautiously, with distinction between molecular detection and demonstrated transmission risk. Mpox DNA was found in placental tissue and umbilical cord blood in multiple case clusters from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Nigeria, where vertical transmission has been documented. Perinatal mortality was close to 60%. These results emphasize the necessity of prenatal screening and maternal-fetal separation regimens during current epidemics. Timely isolation, thorough contact tracing, and communication between the human and animal health sectors are essential to containment, according to thorough risk-assessment studies. On March 8, 2024, the Ministry of National Health Services in Pakistan verified the country's first case of the virus, stating that the patient originated from Saudi Arabia. According to health experts, sequencing is being done to identify the precise viral strain that the individual was exposed to. The first incidence of mpox in Sweden was announced on Thursday 2024. Clade 1 was confirmed to have been contracted there, and the patient was claimed to have contracted the infection in Africa and was presently undergoing treatment.

Diagnosis

Fig. 2
Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) on lesion material (swabs/crusts) and, when necessary, oropharyngeal tissues is used for laboratory confirmation of mpox. Due to their excellent analytical sensitivity and specificity, validated assays that target conserved MPXV/Orthopoxvirus loci (such as F3L, B6R, G2R, E9L, and TNF-receptor genes) continue to be the gold standard for diagnosis. qPCR is routinely positioned as the first-line diagnostic in clinical and outbreak contexts by review syntheses and bench evaluations. Lesion swabs produce the most, but when skin samples are not available, alternate collections (saliva, oropharyngeal swabs, EDTA plasma) can help detection, albeit with lesser sensitivity; comparative studies in confirmed cases quantify these trade-offs and inform sampling algorithms. The use of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) in diagnostic processes to verify ancestry and monitor transmission is growing. Sequence-level analysis reveals APOBEC3-type C → T mutational signatures that indicate sustained human-to-human transmission and distinguishes Clade I, IIb, and emerging Clade Ib. These findings complement qPCR for case confirmation, cluster investigation, and public health response, according to recent datasets from the DRC and surrounding regions. During field research, near-real-time typing is made possible by portable nanopore processes. Immunofluorescence assays (IFA) and serology (ELISA/IgM-IgG) may offer supporting evidence when molecular capacity is restricted, but their application in acute confirmation is limited due to the crucial warning of cross-reactivity across Orthopoxviruses (e.g., vaccinia). These benefits and limitations for programmatic use are listed in current reviews. With turnaround times of ≤30–60 min and sensitivities that are comparable to qPCR, emerging isothermal and CRISPR-based assays (such as RPA/LAMP with Cas12 readouts) provide useful alternatives for decentralized screening and surge testing during epidemics. Risk assessments can be informed by environmental sampling. Several investigations have found MPXV DNA on household and high-touch surfaces, with recovery—when the virus is viable—from porous items like clothing and bedding, highlighting fomite control and the importance of environmental monitoring in One-Health surveillance. Validated cartridge-based qPCR platforms and emergency-listed assays (such as Xpert Mpox, Cobas MPXV, and Abbott Alinity m MPXV) have increased testing availability during major outbreaks; nevertheless, programs should still give priority to lesion sampling and confirmatory algorithms in accordance with national guidelines. Treatment and vaccine strategies
JYNNEOS (MVA-BN): According to real-world data, which included a sizable U.S. trial with 2193 cases and 8319 controls, two doses of JYNNEOS were 66% (95% CI: 47–78%) effective in preventing Mpox, whereas one dosage was 36% (95% CI: 22–47%) effective. Its safety has been thoroughly documented; no major occurrences, such as myocarditis, have been recorded in vaccinated persons, and moderate side effects have been noted. According to German case-control data, the total one-dose efficacy varied from 58% to 84% among people without HIV. The rapid-design potential and good immunogenicity of novel vaccination platforms, like mRNA-based constructions, are being investigated for Orthopoxvirus prophylaxis. Additionally, by improving antigen presentation, nano-vaccine platforms hold promise for long-lasting, cross-clade immunity.
ACAM2000: It produces a powerful immune response, but it is not recommended for immunocompromised people due to replication-related risks. It is kept in stocks for use in an emergency. The Vaccine Comparison Summary: JYNNEOS vs ACAM2000 is given in Table 3.
Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP): Crucially, Tecovirimat is not advised for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP); according to international standards, the key intervention is still JYNNEOS immunization within 4 days of exposure, and Tecovirimat is only needed to treat symptomatic or high-risk individuals. Global modeling assessments examine logistical and equitable issues with strategic ring-vaccination, which is still a key component of outbreak containment.
Vaccinia Immune Globulin Intravenous (VIGIV): The hyperimmune antibody preparation known as Vaccinia Immune Globulin Intravenous (VIGIV) is made from the plasma of people who have received a live vaccine. Its main use has been not for Mpox itself, but for the treatment of replication-competent smallpox vaccination side effects, including eczema vaccinatum, progressive vaccinia, and severe generalized vaccinia. Despite the absence of official clinical trial evidence for Mpox usage, VIGIV was made available under Emergency Access guidelines for severe or high-risk cases during the 2022–2023 epidemic, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, because of its cross-protective potential against orthopoxviruses.

Public health and prevention
It's advised to avoid interacting with animals, such as rats and primates, that could be infected. Given that there is evidence linking sexual contact to the latest epidemic of Mpox, people should be aware of the steps they may take to reduce their risk of contracting the disease during sexual activity. If sharing utensils for food or personal hygiene is necessary, wash and disinfect them after each use. Avoid sharing razors and other such products. Refrain from exposing others to lesions; if a person with mpox lesions has to be in communal or public areas, then wearing gloves, bandages, or clothes to cover all lesions can help stop the spread of the illness. To help protect you and your community, if you were exposed to mpox or are at increased risk of contracting it, take preventative measures and get vaccinated. The current state of Mpox control depends on focused public health measures that address vertical distribution, sexual transmission, and post-recovery care. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) states that the best way to lower the risk of transmission in outbreak situations is to avoid close or sexual contact, especially with new or numerous partners. Pregnant women with suspected or proven MPXV should undergo specialized isolation, intensive fetal monitoring, and risk mitigation counselling in order to prevent perinatal transmission, according to WHO's 2025 clinical recommendation. Additionally, Komakech et al. stress the urgent need for long-term studies on Mpox sequelae among African survivors, promoting organized community-based follow-up initiatives to treat both psychological and medical sequelae. In conclusion, coordinated approaches involving sexual health education, maternal-fetal protections, and extensive survivor support networks are necessary to lower Mpox transmission and lessen its long-term effects.

One health and Mpox: integrated perspectives
As a real zoonotic concern, the Mpox virus calls for an all-encompassing One Health approach that incorporates the fields of environmental, animal, and human health. Human exposure to Mpox occurs through forest encroachment and wildlife commerce, and wildlife reservoirs, particularly rodents and non-human primates, have been repeatedly connected to Mpox outbreaks. Additionally, domestic animals have shown signs of reverse zoonosis, or human-to-animal transfer, raising the possibility of creating new reservoirs. Global epidemic studies have shown that the mpox crisis is a fresh zoonotic alarm after COVID-19, highlighting the need for One-Health-integrated wildlife-trade surveillance. Land-use changes, deforestation, and inadequate veterinary surveillance all increase the likelihood of spillover incidents. The emergence of the emerging Clade Ib Mpox lineage in Nigeria and the Democratic Republic of the Congo has been linked to irregular veterinary–public health coordination and inadequacies in animal health surveillance, shows that integrating human and animal surveillance is essential for early detection of community epidemics. This was confirmed by a 2024 comprehensive review published in Scientific Reports, which found that recurrent zoonotic spillover in Nigeria is a result of underfunded One Health infrastructures. Globally, One Health frameworks are being adopted by important agencies. Orthopoxvirus circulation is being detected in animal reservoirs and environmental samples through programs such as WHO environmental viromic initiatives and FAO's GLEWS+. The Clade Ib-driven Mpox epidemic in Africa was declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern by the WHO on August 14, 2024, highlighting the critical need for coordinated surveillance. To lower zoonotic transmission and reservoir circulation, preventive measures should focus on high-risk animal-related groups, such as veterinarians, bushmeat handlers, and wildlife traders, in addition to human vaccination. Early detection, epidemic preparedness, and response effectiveness are improved when environmental, veterinary, and human health data are synchronised under the One Health banner. For thorough mpox control, the One Health paradigm is still essential, even though the current Clade-Ib outbreak highlights the importance of human-to-human transmission. Setting-based monitoring systems, which cover both human and animal populations in their shared ecosystems, improve responsiveness to emerging risks and support early identification of zoonotic spillover, especially in endemic areas where Clade-Ib originated. Incorporating ecosystem and reservoir monitoring, such as wildlife and rodent surveillance, also yields important information about the dangers of viral persistence and revival, which helps guide public health forecasts and preparedness measures. Climate-related ecological changes increase spillover risk even more, hence One-Health architectures must incorporate environmental-variable monitoring. To effectively contain mpox upstream of human transmission, human, animal, and environmental health systems must intersect. The Democratic Republic of the Congo's mpox response, which includes multisectoral coordination and environmental risk assessment, is a prime example of the strategic application of One Health principles.

Comparative analysis: Mpox, Smallpox & Cowpox
The genus Orthopoxvirus includes the cowpox virus, smallpox virus (Variola virus), and mpox virus (MPXV). MPXV and variola share about 96% of their genomic sequences. Cross-protection is made possible by the vaccine virus's great genetic similarity. Because Variola exclusively infects people, it spreads widely for a long time before being eradicated in 1980. Following a febrile prodrome, all three viruses cause a centrifugal rash that progresses through many lesion phases (macules, papules, vesicles, pustules, and scabs). About 90% of cases of mpox have lymphadenopathy, whereas smallpox does not. Usually, cowpox results in localized pustular lesions with little systemic symptoms. The prevention of mpox depends on vaccinia-based vaccinations, such as ACAM2000 and non-replicating MVA (JYNNEOS); tecovirimat and other antivirals work well. Limitations and future directions

Limitations
The evidence that is currently available about maternal-fetal outcomes in mpox is scarce and mostly comes from pooled analyses and small case series. The pooled rates of unfavorable outcomes, such as vertical transmission (79%; 95% CI: 6–151%), congenital anomalies (50%; 95% CI: 21–80%), and miscarriage or fetal death (62%; 95% CI: 21–102%), were calculated in a recent systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis of 11 reported pregnancies. But these estimates are based on relatively small cohorts, mostly from Clade I outbreaks in environments with limited resources, and they have large confidence intervals that show significant statistical uncertainty. Variations in healthcare access, gestational age at infection, and diagnostic confirmation further restrict generalizability. Furthermore, new genomic information reveals APOBEC3-type mutational signals in Clade Ib, which are suggestive of host-mediated editing and persistent human transmission in areas like South Kivu, DRC. Sex- and gender-disaggregated data were inconsistently reported across outbreak settings, limiting the ability to conduct formal sex-based comparative analyses. Behavioral risk classifications were not uniformly defined, and gender identity data were rarely available in surveillance datasets.

Future research directions
Future study should involve longitudinal monitoring of survivors to identify long-lasting physical, neurological, or psychosocial consequences, as well as prospective cohort studies to assess immunological durability after infection or vaccination in order to fill in these information gaps. Establishing maternal-fatal registries in various geographical areas would also aid in measuring the risk of vertical transmission and developmental outcomes. How APOBEC3-driven mutation patterns in Clade Ib relate to disease severity and dissemination may be further clarified by combining genomic and clinical data.

Alignment with WHO strategy and objectives
The WHO's Strategic Framework for Enhancing Prevention and Control of Mpox, which places a high priority on outbreak control, the development of medical countermeasures, and the mitigation of zoonotic spillover risk, is in line with this publication. The review supports the fundamental objectives of epidemic preparedness and aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-Being), which prioritizes universal access to affordable, inclusive healthcare, by promoting greater surveillance, One Health partnerships, and equitable vaccine access.

Transferability and generalizability
Though much of the data derives from urban or well-resourced contexts, the insights remain relevant to underserved populations—particularly in endemic regions of Central and West Africa were resource constraints impact intervention implementation. High rates of pregnancy complications in the DRC underscore structural health system vulnerabilities. Future implementation research should focus on community-based models—such as decentralized diagnostic platforms, culturally adapted vaccination outreach, and survivor care networks—to enhance feasibility and equity in low-resource settings.

Conclusion
The re-emergence and global expansion of Mpox, particularly with the increasing prominence of Clade Ib, highlight the evolving dynamics of zoonotic diseases in the 21st century. Traditionally limited to endemic regions of Central and West Africa, Mpox now represents a transboundary threat, with outbreaks reported in multiple non-endemic countries since 2022. These developments underscore gaps in surveillance, delayed diagnoses, vaccine hesitancy, and uneven access to antiviral therapeutics. Therapeutic options remain limited, with Tecovirimat being the primary antiviral under expanded access protocols. While vaccines such as JYNNEOS and ACAM2000 are available, their deployment has been constrained by supply limitations, adverse event profiles, and insufficient coverage in high-risk populations. The role of Vaccinia Immune Globulin (VIG) remains investigational, lacking robust clinical trial data in Mpox-specific contexts. This manuscript not only consolidates the current virological, clinical, epidemiological, and therapeutic knowledge of Mpox but also emphasizes the essential role of the One Health approach in long-term disease control. Zoonotic spillovers are often precipitated by anthropogenic activities, including deforestation, wildlife trade, and habitat encroachment, necessitating integrated surveillance at the human-animal-environment interface. Strengthening veterinary infrastructure, real-time environmental monitoring, and cross-border coordination are critical components of this approach. By presenting updated data on Clade Ib epidemiology, treatment strategies, and One Health frameworks, this review contributes a novel, policy-relevant perspective to the Mpox discourse. It calls for investment in multisectoral preparedness systems, equitable access to medical countermeasures, and proactive surveillance to mitigate future outbreaks and minimize global health risks.
