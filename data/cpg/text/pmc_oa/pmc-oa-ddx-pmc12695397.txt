Correlation of serum uric acid with cancer: a systematic evaluation and dose-response meta-analysis of 43 cohort studies
Objective:
The association between serum uric acid (SUA) and cancer incidence and mortality remains controversial. In addition, increasing attention has been paid to its potential role in cancer prognosis. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the correlation between SUA and cancer incidence, mortality, and prognosis through a meta-analysis.
Methods:
A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library was conducted from inception to 28 February 2025. Cohort studies reporting odds ratios (ORs), hazard ratios (HRs), or relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were included to assess the association between SUA and cancer incidence, mortality, and prognosis.
Results:
A total of 43 cohort studies involving 3 697 941 participants were included. High SUA levels were not significantly associated with cancer incidence (RR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.16). However, a stronger correlation was observed in females at the time of the sex-stratified study (female RR = 1.20, male RR = 1.10). In addition, high SUA was positively associated with prostate cancer incidence but inversely associated with lung cancer incidence. According to 7 studies, high SUA levels were not associated with total cancer mortality (RR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.58, 1.48). Conversely, 11 studies reported that elevated SUA levels were significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS) (RR: 1.81; 95% CI: 1.37, 2.37), especially in breast cancer and non-small cell lung cancer.
Conclusion:
Elevated SUA was not significantly associated with the incidence of cancer in the total subject population. Although SUA was not associated with cancer mortality, it was significantly associated with poorer overall survival.

Introduction

HIGHLIGHTS
The relationship between SUA and cancer incidence, mortality and prognosis was systematically evaluated for the first time.
Compared with men, elevated SUA levels were significantly associated with a higher cancer incidence rate in women, but there was no overall association in the total population.
High SUA is associated with an increased incidence of prostate cancer but a reduced risk of lung cancer, showing opposite effects.
SUA was independently associated with poorer OS in cancer patients, especially in the subgroups of breast cancer and non-small cell lung cancer.
Serum uric acid (SUA) is the end product of purine metabolism, primarily synthesized in the liver, intestine, and vascular endothelium, and excreted via the kidneys and intestines. Impaired purine metabolism or reduced uric acid excretion can lead to elevated SUA levels, resulting in hyperuricemia (HUA). HUA is a chronic, progressive condition with no obvious symptoms, often associated with gout, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and nephropathy. Several studies have demonstrated that SUA levels are associated with cancer incidence and mortality. Elevated SUA levels have been positively associated with colorectal and renal cancer risk. However, SUA levels are inversely associated with prostate cancer (PCa) risk in certain populations. A large cohort study also showed a U-shaped relationship between SUA levels and cancer, suggesting that extreme SUA levels (either too low or too high) increase the risk of death, with a cancer HR of 3.776 in the high SUA group. Further studies have identified gender differences in the association between SUA levels and mortality. High SUA levels have been associated with poorer prognosis in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, suggesting that SUA may serve as a biomarker for CRC risk and prognosis. Overall, the relationship between SUA and cancer remains uncertain.
SUA exhibits a paradoxical role in carcinogenesis, with both pro-tumorigenic and anti-tumorigenic effects mediated through distinct molecular mechanisms. On the one hand, SUA can increase the risk of cancer development and death through oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. Serum xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), which disrupt redox homeostasis and, in turn, affect biological processes such as inflammation, cell proliferation, and tumor transformation. It has also been found that SUA upregulates NF-κB activity, induces the expression of COX-2 and iNOS, and promotes tumor angiogenesis. Urate crystals can activate NLRP3 inflammasomes, induce the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-18, and create a chronic inflammatory microenvironment, thus promoting cancer cell proliferation and metastasis. On the other hand, the antioxidant properties of SUA may have a protective effect in certain cancers, helping to inhibit cancer development . Studies have also shown that SUA may slow the progression of certain solid tumors (e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma) by regulating glutathione metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and inhibiting ferroptosis (an iron-dependent programmed cell death). This mechanistic duality underscores the importance of systematically assessing the relationship between SUA and cancer risk.
There is a lack of systematic evaluation of the relationship between SUA and cancer across key clinical outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively synthesize current evidence on the associations between SUA and cancer incidence, mortality, and prognosis. Given the global cancer burden and the complex, dualistic role of SUA in tumor biology, a better understanding of these relationships may provide new insights for risk stratification, prognostic evaluation, and individualized patient management.

Methods

Reporting and protocol registration
This article has reported this work in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) and AMSTAR (Methodology Quality for Evaluating Systematic Reviews) guideline. The study protocol was registered with the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration number: CRD420251022858). This study adheres to the principles of transparency in the use of AI as outlined in the TITAN guidelines.

Search strategy and study selection
A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases was conducted, covering literature up to 28 February 2025. A total of 43 cohort studies were included. Relevant literature was obtained through manual screening, using keywords such as: “uric acid,” “serum uric acid,” “gout,” “hyperuricemia,” “cancer,” “tumor” and “malignancy.” Keywords were derived from titles, abstracts and MeSH terms, the search was performed without any language restrictions. A detailed search strategy is reported in Supplement Digital Content 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/E932. Reference lists of relevant literature on the topic were also examined to avoid missing other relevant studies. Two independent reviewers (XFL and HXC) performed literature screening, with disagreements resolved through discussion or by consulting a third investigator (SRW). Titles and abstracts were first screened to identify potentially eligible studies, followed by full-text review to determine final inclusion.

The PICOS model (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design) was used to define the inclusion criteria for this study (Table 1). Inclusion criteria: (1) cohort design studies; (2) studies examining the relationship between SUA and cancer; (3) studies providing ORs, RRs or HRs along with their 95% CIs. Exclusion criteria: (1) studies not related to cancer incidence, mortality and prognosis; (2) as well as case reports, reviews, animal studies and studies with duplicate data; (3) study lacks available data for meta-analysis.

Data extraction
Data were independently extracted by two researchers, XFL and HXC, Disagreements were resolved by consulting a third author (SRW). The relevant data extracted included the first author, year of publication, source, study design, cancer type, sex, age, sample size, time to baseline, follow-up duration, adjustment variables cancer incidence, mortality and prognostic risk (RRs, HRs or ORs 95% CI). All data extraction and study selection procedures were conducted independently and in duplicate to ensure methodological rigor.

Assessment of research quality
Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which is applicable to both case-control and cohort studies. The scale evaluates the quality of the literature based on eight items related to selection, comparability, and exposure. Each criterion is scored as “1” if the answer is “yes” and “0” if the answer is “no” or “unclear.” NOS scores range from 0 to 9, with a score of 6 or higher indicating high quality and a score of 3 or lower indicating low quality. XFL and HXC independently assessed the risk of bias for each domain and for the study as a whole, discussing any conflicts to reach a final consensus.

Dose-response analysis
In the dose-response meta-analysis, studies that provided at least three SUA categories (e.g., quartiles Q1-Q4), RR, dose range, total number of patients and number of cases were included. The dose-response meta-analysis was divided into two parts: linear and nonlinear analysis. In the linear dose-response meta-analysis, a linear relationship between SUA levels and cancer risk was assumed. The Greenland and Longnecker method were used to extract RRs and 95% CI from different SUA levels to calculate study-specific RRs (linear slopes) and 95% CIs. To estimate the linear trend, the following approximations were made: the midpoint of the SUA range for each category was assigned to the corresponding RR; for open-ended highest categories, the length of the interval was assumed to be the same as that of the neighboring categories; and for open-ended lowest categories, the lower limit of the SUA was assumed to be zero, with the width of the highest category assumed to be the same as that of the neighboring intervals. Study-specific RRs and variances were then pooled using the DerSimonian–Laird random effects model, and pooled RRs and 95% CIs were calculated. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using the Cochran Q test and the I2statistic.
In the nonlinear dose-response meta-analysis, nonlinear trends were estimated using the RR distribution of cancer incidence for at least three quantitative SUA exposure categories. Generalized least squares regression was used to calculate combined study-specific estimates using a restricted cubic spline method model with four nodes at the 3rd, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentile nodes of the distributions (these knot locations follow standard recommendations for spline modeling in dose-response meta-analyses, allowing sufficient flexibility while avoiding overfitting, particularly in datasets with limited exposure categories), and maximum likelihood in the random-effects meta-analysis. The median or mean SUA concentration for each category was assigned to each corresponding RR with a 95% CI, and the lowest reported SUA level was set as the reference value (0). A two-tailed P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical methods
The included literature was organized and categorized into three groups: SUA and cancer incidence, SUA and cancer mortality, and SUA and cancer prognosis. Basic information was then extracted. The RR for SUA and cancer incidence was analyzed for the total population, male population, and female population. Due to high heterogeneity, further subgroup analyses were conducted. SUA and cancer mortality were analyzed for the total population, male and female populations, and different cancer types. SUA and cancer prognosis were analyzed for both univariate analysis (U) and multivariate analysis (M) of OS across different cancer types. If an article included both M-OS and U-OS, M-OS was selected. If only one of U-OS or M-OS was reported, it was also included. Subgroup analyses were then performed based on year, intervention, sample size, and cancer type.
The strength of the association between SUA levels and cancer was assessed by calculating the RRs with 95% CI. The Cochran Q-statistic test and I2 test were conducted to evaluate inter-study heterogeneity, with P <0.05 and I2 >50% indicating significant heterogeneity across studies. When inter-study heterogeneity (I2) was less than 50%, the fixed-effects model using the Mantel–Haenszel approach was applied. Otherwise, the random-effects model using the DerSimonian and Laird approach was used. Stratified analyses by gender and cancer type were also performed. To test the robustness of the estimates, sensitivity analyses were conducted using the leave-one-out method. Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test were used to assess the risk of publication bias. All analyses were performed using STATA 17.0 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Selection and identification of studies
PRISMA of evidence search and selection of articles.
A total of 10,737 records were identified through a systematic search for studies on the association between SUA and cancer incidence, mortality, and prognosis (Fig. 1). After screening for duplicates and ineligible literature, 43 studies that met the inclusion criteria were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Of these, 16 studies addressed morbidity, 13 addressed mortality, 13 explored prognostic relationships, and 1 addressed both morbidity and mortality. All procedural steps were performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020 Checklist) (Supplement Digital Content 2, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/E932) .

A total of 43 cohort studies from around the world were included in this systematic evaluation and meta-analysis (Table 2). Of these, 32 (74.4%) were conducted in Asia (China, Korea, Japan), and 11 (25.6%) were from Europe and the United States (United States, Germany, United Kingdom, etc.). These studies included 3 697 941 participants, with sample sizes ranging from 99 to 444 462, and all employed a cohort design. Of the 43 studies, 33 (76.7%) were mixed-sex studies, 7 focused on males, and 3 focused on females. Twenty studies focused on solid tumors (3 colorectal, 2 lung, 2 breast, 1 renal, and 2 hepatocellular carcinomas), while 23 (53.5%) involved multiple cancer types. Although this review included 43 cohort studies, their geographical distribution was markedly imbalanced, with 74.4% of studies conducted in Asia (mainly China, Korea, and Japan), and no studies originating from Africa. This lack of representation from African populations may limit the generalizability of our findings across diverse global populations. In addition, heterogeneity in outcome indicators – encompassing incidence, mortality, and prognosis – necessitated subgroup analyses to enhance the interpretability and applicability of the results. Seventeen studies were related to cancer incidence, 14 to cancer mortality, and 13 to cancer prognosis. One study addressed both incidence and mortality, bringing the total number of included studies to 43. To ensure the methodological rigor of the included studies, we evaluated study quality using the NOS, which assesses selection, comparability, and outcome domains. All 43 cohort studies scored between 7 and 9, indicating generally high methodological quality. Specifically, 20 studies (46.5%) received a maximum score of 9, while the remaining 23 studies (53.5%) scored 7 or 8, reflecting moderate to high quality. No study was rated as low quality. The full distribution of NOS scores is presented in Table 2. These results suggest a low overall risk of bias and support the robustness of the synthesized evidence in this meta-analysis.

Pooled results from 32 studies indicated that higher SUA levels were not significantly associated with cancer risk in the total population (RR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.16), with a high degree of heterogeneity (I2 = 66.2%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Visual inspection of the funnel plot for the meta-analysis of the SUA-cancer risk association suggested symmetrical distribution. However, the Egger test result (P = 0.045) contradicted the visual inspection, suggesting a potential risk of publication bias (Fig. 3A). To address this, the “trim-and-fill” method was applied to adjust the combined effect size for publication bias. The adjusted results did not change (RR = 1.075; 95% CI: 0.991, 1.168), indicating that publication bias did not significantly affect the pooled estimate (Fig. 3B). Subgroup analyses by cancer type revealed that prostate cancer (n≥3) was positively associated with incidence (RR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.09, 1.46), whereas lung cancer (n≥3) was negatively associated (RR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.85, 0.99) (Table 3).
Subgroup analysis by region indicated variability in associations: Japan showed a significant positive association (RR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.12, 1.42; I2 = 0%), whereas studies from the UK showed a negative trend (RR = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.78, 1.02; I2 = 77.8%). Chinese studies also indicated a positive but non-significant association (RR = 1.19; 95% CI: 0.91, 1.55; I2 = 68.8%). Regarding sample size, studies with ≥10 000 participants showed a similar effect estimate (RR = 1.09; 95% CI: 0.99, 1.16; I2 = 76.8%) compared to those with <10 000 participants (RR = 1.08; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.16; I2 = 44.9%). As for follow-up duration, the association was more evident in studies with unknown follow-up time (RR = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.82, 0.98) but less consistent across follow-up subgroups of ≥10 years (RR = 1.08; 95% CI: 0.99, 1.16) and <10 years (RR = 1.07; 95% CI: 0.97, 1.19). Finally, meta-regression analysis suggested that cancer type (P = 0.019) and follow-up duration (P = 0.001) might explain part of the observed heterogeneity, while region (P = 0.142) and sample size (P = 0.216) were not statistically significant contributors (Table 3). After combining data from stratified and single-sex studies, the meta-analysis grouped by sex revealed a stronger association in females (RR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.11, 1.29; I2 = 87.2%; P < 0.001) compared to males (RR = 1.10; 95% CI: 1.05; 1.16; I2 = 71.8%; P < 0.001). Notably, the Egger test for female data indicated publication bias (t = 2.52; P = 0.016). Sensitivity analyses, which excluded one study at a time, did not significantly alter the results, suggesting that the overall findings were not substantially influenced by any single study.
Nonlinear dose-response meta-analysis of SUA levels and OR or RR of cancer incidence in the total subject population in cohort studies.
A linear dose-response analysis of 144 studies revealed a 4% increase in cancer incidence for every 50 μmol/L increase in SUA levels (RR = 1.04; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.06). Additionally, a significant nonlinear association between SUA concentration and cancer incidence was observed (P-nonlinear < 0.001). As SUA concentration increased from 0 μmol/L to 400 μmol/L, the RR gradually increased from a baseline of 1.00 to approximately 1.75, with the risk increase accelerating as SUA concentration rose. Specifically, in the low concentration range (0–120 μmol/L), the RR increased slowly from 1.00; in the mid-to-high concentration range (120–400 μmol/L), the RR rose more rapidly, and the risk surged (RR = 1.75) when SUA concentration exceeded 200 μmol/L, suggesting the presence of a potential threshold effect (Fig. 4).

Serum uric acid and cancer mortality
Forest plot of the relationship between SUA and cancer mortality.
A total of 30 studies examined the association between SUA and cancer mortality. Of these, 7 studies focused on the overall population (without differentiating by cancer type), 7 studies were conducted in men, 4 in women, and the remaining 12 studies investigated unspecified type cancer types. There was no significant difference in the risk of death between patients in the high SUA group and those in the low SUA group (RR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.58, 1.48). Since fewer than 10 studies were included, a funnel plot was not used to assess publication bias. However, Egger’s test revealed no publication bias (t = 0.28; P = 0.792). Subgroup analysis indicated that the results were not influenced by gender: for males, the RR was 1.06 (95% CI: 0.85, 1.32; I2 = 82.5%; P < 0.001), and for females, the RR was 1.33 (95% CI: 0.98, 1.80; I2 = 74.3%; P = 0.009) (Fig. 5).

A total of 13 studies examined the association between SUA and OS. Due to the high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 89.7%; P < 0.001), a random-effects model was used. The results showed no significant association between high SUA levels and poorer OS or disease-free survival (DFS) (OS: RR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.99, 1.03; DFS: RR = 1.00; 95% CI: 0.62, 1.60). Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed significant asymmetry between SUA and cancer OS. Additionally, the Egger test results (t = 4.32; P = 0.001) confirmed the visual assessment, indicating a substantial risk of publication bias. Further sensitivity analyses showed that the studies by Wang and Yang had significantly different effect estimates compared to the other studies, which provided visual evidence of between-study heterogeneity (Fig. 6). After excluding the Wang and Yang studies, the pooled effect estimate increased significantly, with a new RR of 1.81 (95% CI: 1.37, 2.37), and heterogeneity (I2 = 79.4%; P < 0.001) decreased. This suggests that removing these two studies resulted in a substantial increase in the effect size, indicating that they were a source of heterogeneity. Therefore, this result suggests a potential significant association between high SUA levels and poorer OS. Additionally, meta-regression analysis showed that covariates such as sample size (N < 300 or N ≥ 300), cancer type (e.g., non-small cell lung cancer, locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma of the larynx in men, hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal carcinoma, etc.), analysis method (univariate vs. multivariate analysis), treatment type (surgery, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy), and year of study (before or after 2020) were not significant sources of heterogeneity (P > 0.05) (Table 4) (Fig. 7).

Discussion
This study is the first comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of the association between SUA levels and cancer incidence, mortality, and prognosis. The results revealed no significant association between higher SUA levels and cancer incidence when compared to lower SUA levels in the total cancer subject population (RR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.16). However, combining data from both stratified and single-sex studies showed that SUA levels were significantly associated with cancer incidence in both males and females, with the association being stronger in females (males: RR = 1.10; 95% CI: 1.05, 1.16; I2 = 71.8%; P < 0.001; females: RR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.11, 1.29; I2 = 87.2%; P < 0.001). These findings suggest that gender may influence the relationship between SUA levels and cancer incidence. Subgroup analyses also indicated that high SUA levels were positively associated with prostate cancer incidence and negatively associated with lung cancer incidence. Regarding cancer mortality, the results of seven included studies showed no significant association between high SUA levels and mortality in the total cancer population, and no significant findings were observed in sex-stratified analyses. Additionally, results from 11 included studies indicated that elevated SUA levels were significantly associated with poorer OS in the total cancer population.
To date, only two meta-analyses have examined the association between SUA levels and cancer incidence and mortality. Yan et al conducted a meta-analysis on SUA and cancer risk, showing that higher SUA levels in the total cancer population were associated with an increased risk of cancer incidence (N = 5; RR = 1.03; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.05) and mortality (N = 12; RR = 1.17; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.32). In particular, high SUA levels were linked to increased cancer mortality, especially among female patients. Conversely, the study by Dovell et al which included 11 cohort studies, found no significant association between SUA levels and cancer mortality (RR = 1.11; 95% CI: 0.94, 1.27). Overall, the results of these two studies were divergent, with small sample sizes. This study builds upon these findings by incorporating, for the first time, an analysis of the association between SUA levels and cancer prognosis.
In this study, we found that the association between SUA levels and cancer incidence was significantly stronger in women (RR = 1.20) than in men (RR = 1.10). The observed sex-specific differences in the association between SUA and cancer risk may be attributed to both hormonal and metabolic factors. In women, particularly after menopause, declining estrogen levels are associated with altered adipose tissue distribution, insulin resistance, and increased oxidative stress – all of which may modulate uric acid metabolism. Estrogen also promotes uric acid excretion by upregulating renal urate transporters, such as URAT1 and GLUT9; thus, its decline may lead to higher SUA levels in postmenopausal women. Additionally, differences in xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) activity, inflammation profiles, and immune responses between sexes may influence the biological effects of SUA on cancer initiation and progression. These mechanistic disparities could partly explain the stronger association observed in women compared to men in our meta-analysis.
Our study found that high SUA levels were positively associated with prostate cancer risk (RR = 1.26) and inversely associated with lung cancer risk (RR = 0.92). Although biologically plausible mechanisms exist for both associations – such as SUA-induced activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes and ROS signaling promoting prostate cancer, or SUA’s antioxidant properties potentially exerting a protective role against lung cancer – these findings should be interpreted with caution. Notably, the number of studies contributing to these subgroup analyses was limited (n =3 for prostate cancer and n =5 for lung cancer), which may reduce the robustness and generalizability of the conclusions. Therefore, these opposing associations are better viewed as hypothesis-generating observations rather than definitive evidence of causal relationships. Future large-scale, site-specific prospective studies and mechanistic investigations are needed to verify these associations and clarify the underlying biological pathways.
In examining the relationship between SUA and cancer prognosis, we found that high SUA levels were associated with poorer OS, particularly in breast cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma. Increasing evidence suggests that SUA activates tumor-associated macrophages through the NLRP3/IL-1β axis, which in turn promotes angiogenesis and metastasis. Specifically, IL-1β inhibits the expression of PD-L1 on macrophages, impairing their immunosuppressive effect on T cells, and indirectly promoting a decline in tumor immunity. Furthermore, renal cancer cells induce macrophage IL-1β expression through SUA secretion, which activates the STAT3 pathway and promotes angiogenesis, making SUA a potential biomarker for cancer prognosis. To further explore the prognostic significance of SUA in different tumor types, we conducted a meta-analysis of eligible studies.
To identify potential sources of heterogeneity, this study examined the influence of study characteristics (region, sample size, cancer type, etc.) on effect sizes through meta-regression analysis. The observed heterogeneity in the association between SUA and cancer incidence may be partly explained by differences in cancer type and follow-up duration. Various cancers differ markedly in their pathophysiology and metabolic microenvironments, which may influence the biological role of SUA. For instance, SUA may act as a pro-oxidant in certain cancers (e.g., prostate or colorectal cancer), promoting tumorigenesis via inflammatory pathways, while in others (e.g., lung cancer), it may exhibit antioxidant properties and confer a protective effect. These mechanistic divergences across tumor types could contribute to inconsistent associations observed in subgroup analyses. Furthermore, follow-up duration plays a critical role in detecting incidence outcomes, particularly in prospective cohorts. Studies with longer follow-up are more likely to capture true incident cases and long-latency effects of SUA, whereas shorter follow-up periods may underestimate associations due to insufficient case accrual. Therefore, variability in follow-up time across studies may lead to differential effect sizes and contribute to the overall heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis.
Notably, the sensitivity analysis identified two studies – by Wang et al and Yang et al – as significant sources of heterogeneity in the prognostic analysis. Upon exclusion of these studies, the pooled effect size for OS increased markedly (RR from 1.01 to 1.81), while heterogeneity decreased (I2 from 89.7% to 79.4%). Several distinctive characteristics of these studies may account for their outlying influence. The study by Wang et al focused exclusively on patients with small-cell lung cancer (SCLC), a tumor subtype with high malignancy and unique metabolic demands, which may influence SUA dynamics differently from other solid tumors. Additionally, their study employed a relatively high SUA cutoff value (296.45 μmol/L), and their findings suggested only a modest prognostic effect of SUA on OS and PFS. Yang et al’s study, on the other hand, investigated patients with advanced gastric cancer and incorporated both SUA and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) into their prognostic model. Importantly, they excluded individuals with hyperuricemia or gout, likely narrowing the SUA distribution and attenuating its prognostic signal. Moreover, their study applied a time-dependent ROC approach to determine an optimal SUA cutoff (228 μmol/L) and constructed a nomogram for survival prediction, which differs methodologically from most other studies included in our analysis. These differences in cancer type, sample characteristics, and analytical strategy likely contributed to their divergent results and elevated their influence in the pooled model. As such, the exclusion of these studies may have enhanced the homogeneity and generalizability of our findings, supporting a stronger association between elevated SUA and poor cancer prognosis across broader populations.
Overall, it is crucial to monitor the relationship between dynamic changes in SUA levels and cancer progression, particularly to assess its prognostic value during treatment. The molecular mechanisms (e.g., NLRP3/IL-1β axis) through which SUA affects specific cancers (e.g., colorectal cancer) should be further elucidated through animal models or mechanistic studies. Although the exact role of SUA in cancer prognosis remains incompletely understood, this study found that elevated SUA levels were significantly associated with poorer survival. Consequently, SUA levels should be closely monitored in cancer patients in clinical practice. For patients with hyperuricemia, especially those with advanced cancer, a combination of uric acid-lowering therapy and antitumor treatment may be necessary. However, given SUA’s antioxidant-protective effects, interventions should be implemented with caution and moderation.

Conclusion
This systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that elevated SUA levels were not significantly associated with cancer incidence in the overall study population. Although SUA levels were not associated with cancer mortality, they were significantly associated with poorer overall survival. Heterogeneity between studies, partly due to differences in cancer type, follow-up time, and adjustment for confounders, needs to be interpreted with caution. To further validate the relationship between SUA and cancer, future studies need to be conducted prospectively to elucidate the temporal dynamics of SUA during cancer progression and treatment, as well as the role of SUA in specific cancers, in order to provide more reliable evidence for cancer prevention in clinical management.
