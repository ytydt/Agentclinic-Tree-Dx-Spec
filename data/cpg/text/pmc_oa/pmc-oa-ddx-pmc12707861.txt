Navigating Disparities and Overcoming Barriers to Access Diabetic Kidney Disease Therapies: A Proposed Multifactorial Approach
Abstract
Background
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a serious complication arising from long-term diabetes, disproportionately affecting historically marginalized populations, such as racial and ethnic minority groups, populations with low socioeconomic status, or a lower level of education. This review explores the causes of these disparities and barriers to accessing DKD therapies and proposes solutions.
Summary
Socioeconomic factors like lack of health insurance, low income, and limited health literacy significantly hinder access to DKD therapies for marginalized communities. Additionally, inadequate access to healthcare services further exacerbates the issue. Clinical factors also contribute to the inequity. Under-recognition of DKD, under-prescription of effective medications, and a lack of a patient-centered healthcare environment are prominent concerns. Implicit bias among healthcare professionals may also play a role. Patient factors include limited awareness of DKD, challenges with treatment adherence, and frequent healthcare interruptions. Policy interventions like the Inflation Reduction Act, expanding health insurance coverage, and increasing reimbursement for DKD therapies can improve affordability and access. Additionally, a shift toward preventive care models is crucial. Clinical interventions focus on improving early detection, accurate diagnosis, and proper management of DKD. Educating healthcare providers about the benefits of DKD therapies and implementing value-based kidney care programs are essential steps. Patient interventions involve raising awareness about DKD, implementing culturally appropriate educational programs, and fostering community-based support systems.
Key Messages
Addressing these disparities requires a multipronged approach involving policy changes, improved healthcare delivery, and patient education. This collaborative effort can ensure equitable access to DKD therapies and improve health outcomes for all populations.
Plain Language Summary
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a serious health problem that can happen to people who have uncontrolled diabetes for a long time, and it tends to affect historically marginalized populations. A key problem is that not everyone has the same access to medicines that can help. There are many reasons why some people cannot get the medicine they need. For example, some people lack health insurance or struggle financially, which makes affording the necessary medications a major hurdle. Others might not have the knowledge or resources to manage their diabetes effectively, increasing the risk of developing DKD. Finding a doctor and/or a healthcare setting who understands their specific needs and cultural background can be an additional obstacle. Even within the healthcare system, disparities exist. Sometimes, doctors may not be able to recognize DKD early, or they might struggle to recommend the most appropriate treatment due to a lack of specific training or resources. Unconscious biases, where certain groups are unintentionally treated differently, can also affect who receives the care they deserve. By working collaboratively, we can bridge this gap by advocating for policies that expand health insurance coverage, support preventive and lifestyle modification programs, and make medication more affordable. Additionally, it is crucial to educate both healthcare providers and patients about DKD prevention, early detection, and culturally competent care.

Introduction
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a complex condition that arises due to long-term diabetes, particularly when treatment targets have not been achieved. It can affect up to 20% of people who have diabetes and is a leading cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) and premature death worldwide. The slow-progressing disease is asymptomatic initially, presenting a challenge for early diagnosis. It is alarming to note that a vast majority, approximately 90%, of people with chronic kidney disease (CKD) are not aware of their condition, and about 40% of adults remain unaware of having advanced kidney disease. This lack of awareness underscores the importance of regular screening for early detection and treatment of DKD.
Globally, kidney disease outcomes are heavily influenced by socioeconomic, cultural, and political factors, leading to significant inequities in disease burden and care. Many countries still lack access to basic diagnostics, a trained nephrology workforce, universal primary healthcare, or affordable renal replacement therapies, which contributes to poor DKD outcomes in those regions. Diabetes exhibits a disproportionate impact on racial/ethnic minority groups within the USA, with higher incidences observed in specific groups: 14.7% in American Indian/Alaska Native people, 12.5% in Hispanic people, and 11.7% in non-Hispanic Black people. In contrast, lower diabetes prevalences are reported among non-Hispanic Asians (9.2%) and non-Hispanic Whites (7.5%). Moreover, Black and Hispanic Americans exhibit a significantly higher prevalence of ESKD in comparison with non-Hispanic White Americans, with Black Americans demonstrating a 3.4-fold higher prevalence and Hispanic Americans showing a 1.3-fold higher prevalence. In another study, Black, Hispanic, and Asian patients exhibited higher rates of ESRD compared to their White counterparts, with incidences of 6.8, 4.3, and 4.5 per 1,000 person-years, respectively, against 3.2 per 1,000 person-years in White patients. Also, Black Americans had consistently higher cardiometabolic mortality rates compared to White Americans. Black women had the highest mortality rate ratios compared to White women due to diabetes (2.09), and Black men had the highest compared to White men due to hypertension (2.18).
In Europe, diabetes disproportionately affects many racial and ethnic minority groups – often with a two- to six-fold higher risk compared to the host majority groups – particularly among South Asian, African-Caribbean, sub-Saharan African, Middle Eastern/North African, and some migrant. For instance, UK data show standardized prevalence rates of 1.7% in White populations versus 5.3% in African-Caribbean and 8.9% in Pakistani/Bangladeshi groups, and long-term incidence estimates of 14% for Europeans compared with 33% for Indian Asians and 30% for African Caribbeans. Beyond higher prevalence, these groups also experience elevated risks of diabetes-related microvascular complications such as nephropathy and retinopathy, reflecting complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, socioeconomic deprivation, and differential access to care.
This alarming trend in diabetes prevalence and its complications among minority groups not only highlights the urgent need for targeted healthcare interventions but also points to significant disparities in the management and treatment outcomes of the condition, as evidenced by the inequity in prescription rates for disease-modifying medications, such as sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), between White patients and their Black and Latino/Hispanic counterparts. Black patients were 22% less likely to receive SGLT2i and 25% less likely to receive GLP-1RA in comparison with White patients after adjusting for patient- and system-level characteristics. Similarly, Hispanic patients were 24% less likely to receive SGLT2i and 33% less likely to receive GLP-1RA than non-Hispanic White patients.

Causes of Inequities and Barriers to Access DKD Therapies

Socioeconomic Factors
Socioeconomic factors are a major cause of inequalities and limited access to DKD therapies, particularly for historically marginalized communities. Lack of health insurance is a significant hurdle for individuals seeking regular healthcare services, including access to healthcare providers, laboratory tests, medications, or dialysis treatments. In addition, they may face difficulties in getting referrals to specialists or enrolling in transplant programs. The financial burden of these essential medical services can be overwhelming for those without adequate insurance coverage, leading to untreated illnesses and worsening medical conditions.
Global estimates indicate that annual per-patient costs escalate sharply with CKD severity from a mean of USD 3,060 at stage G3a to USD 57,334 for hemodialysis and USD 49,490 for peritoneal dialysis for uninsured or underinsured households. Out-of-pocket expenses for medications, transport, and home adaptations compound this burden, forcing trade-offs between essential needs and treatment adherence (“cost-saving nonadherence”) in socioeconomically deprived communities. The cost of a physician’s visit without insurance varies widely, typically ranging from USD 300 to USD 800, with factors such as location and the HCP specialty playing key roles. Disease-modifying therapies, crucial for managing chronic conditions like diabetes, are part of the significant healthcare expenses, with glucose-lowering drugs costing the USA an estimated USD 57.6 billion annually from 2015 to 2017. This accounts for 15–20% of the total yearly cost for all prescription drugs. Insulin prices in the USA have increased sharply, tripling over the past decade, with the net price rising by 57% and the list price by 252% from 2007 to 2016. Historically, drug costs have caused inequities and barriers. However, in 2023, the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) announcement of the maximum fair price for the first ten drugs selected under the Drug Price Negotiation Program (DPNP) represents a major step in the rollout of this historic drug pricing reform. Stakeholders will be closely monitoring the results and adapting to the new negotiation process in the coming years.
Although insulin is not included in the first set of 10 drugs included in the DPNP, the IRA separately caps insulin costs at USD 35 per month for Medicare beneficiaries starting in 2023, even before the negotiated prices for the first 10 drugs take effect in 2026. While the USA has instituted negotiated price caps under the IRA, similar international reforms remain sparse. In Europe, annual treatment costs for CKD stages vary substantially by country: dapagliflozin adds EUR 665 in the UK, EUR 898 in Germany, and EUR 477 in Spain to standard care, reflecting heterogeneity in pricing and reimbursement policies, which might change after loss of exclusivity. On the other hand, across low- and middle-income countries, lack of comprehensive universal health coverage and limited healthcare funding means that even basic DKD care and diagnostics may be inaccessible or unaffordable for large parts of the population.
Furthermore, individuals with a low income face significant financial constraints when seeking to meet out-of-pocket expenses related to the treatment of DKD. Such expenses may include co-payments, deductibles, transportation, and home modifications. Due to the inadequacy of their resources, they may experience a lack of affordability in these expenses. This, in turn, results in forced choices between essential needs such as food, housing, etc., and DKD care. The phenomenon is known as cost-saving nonadherence. It is, therefore, imperative for policymakers and healthcare providers to recognize these financial barriers and to develop initiatives aimed at reducing their impact on the low-income population.
People with low education may have lower health literacy, presenting challenges in understanding medical information, instructions, or options related to DKD care. Health literacy is the ability to obtain, process, and use health information to make informed decisions about one’s health. In addition to low health literacy, cultural factors and a lack of trust in the healthcare system, which exist in some populations, affect the willingness of some to make changes toward allopathic medicines and instead utilize community homeopathic means. For instance, some cultures may have a strong tradition of using natural or homeopathic remedies, which can lead to a preference for these methods over medicines. Additionally, cultural perceptions of illness and wellness, as well as the role of family and community in healthcare decisions, can impact how individuals manage their health conditions like DKD.
Low health literacy can affect the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of DKD in several ways. For example, people with lower levels of knowledge about diabetes and its complications, including DKD, may have less awareness of the symptoms and signs of DKD, such as albuminuria. As a result, they may find it more challenging to follow self-care recommendations, such as monitoring blood glucose, controlling blood pressure, adhering to medications, and making dietary and lifestyle changes. Furthermore, they may feel less confident and lack the skills to communicate with healthcare providers or ask questions about their condition and treatment options.
Trust in the healthcare system is a cornerstone for effective healthcare delivery. However, historical injustices, perceived discrimination, and negative personal experiences can erode trust in the healthcare system. Studies examining trust and preventive health service use among older adults found that Black participants were less likely than White participants to trust their own doctors, and they had a higher mean of distrust score (3.1 vs. 1.8, p < 0.01), respectively. The absence of trust can lead to skepticism about conventional treatments and a hesitancy to seek care, potentially leading to the underutilization of beneficial therapies and unfavorable health outcomes.
Inadequate access to healthcare services is a significant challenge for individuals with low socioeconomic status. Such individuals may reside in regions with limited or substandard healthcare facilities or providers, facing various obstacles when seeking healthcare services, such as traveling long distances, lack of transportation, language barriers, or cultural differences. These challenges may have severe consequences, including delayed diagnosis and treatment, worsened health outcomes, and/or increased healthcare costs. It is imperative to address limited access to healthcare services for individuals with low socioeconomic status as a critical public health priority.
The high cost and overall affordability of therapies for DKD are a significant concern for patients. Specifically, therapeutic options such as GLP1-RA, SGLT2i, and finerenone can be prohibitively expensive for many DKD patients, particularly those from historically marginalized populations. In the USA, for patients without insurance coverage, the retail pharmacy price for a DKD therapy ranges from USD 530 to USD 1,300 per month. Contrastingly, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASi) are widely available as low-cost generics (e.g., lisinopril often costs ∼USD 30–40 per month), so their cost is generally much less of a barrier compared to the newer agents. This highlights the need for affordable treatment options that can improve outcomes for DKD patients and reduce disparities in care.

Clinical Factors
People with DKD may not receive adequate or timely diagnosis, monitoring, or treatment for their condition due to various clinical factors. The under-recognition of DKD presents a significant challenge in the healthcare industry. Owing to its insidious and progressive nature, DKD may remain asymptomatic until its advanced stages. Individuals from historically marginalized populations are at risk of not receiving regular screening for renal function, which includes estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) or a formal diagnosis of DKD. In the USA, only 42% of primary care physicians utilize uACR and eGFR to diagnose DKD. This marks the need for a more comprehensive approach to recognizing and diagnosing DKD, particularly as it pertains to societal health equity.
Healthcare providers may refrain from prescribing medications known to be effective, such as GLP1-RA, RAASi, SGLT2i, and finerenone, to patients with euglycemic diabetes with persistence of albuminuria or controlled blood pressure. This practice is often due to a lack of familiarity, confidence, or evidence on the healthcare professional’s part. Research has indicated that under-prescription of these medications can lead to missed opportunities to improve patient outcomes. Furthermore, clinicians may not appropriately prescribe or optimize disease-altering therapy. For example, in a prospective, population-based cohort study including 10,400 patients with CKD and T2D, discontinuing RAASi due to eGFR decline (eGFR<30 mL/min/1.73 m2) was noted in 17% of the patients. In approximately 2% of cases, serum creatinine levels can rise above 30% of the baseline after starting RAASi therapy, which is a signal to consider holding the medication according to guidelines. However, it is important to note that continuing the drug in the remaining 98% of cases should still be done with regular monitoring, as individual responses to RAASi can vary and may require dosage adjustments or even discontinuation based on the patient’s condition and concurrent medications.
Moreover, there is a lack of a conducive environment to effectively identify and treat chronic diseases like CKD in most healthcare settings. This includes the limited time HCPs have to interact with patients, the lack of multidisciplinary teams, limited opportunities for patients to receive culturally and linguistically oriented care and education, and the limited emphasis on prevention strategies. Currently, the majority of the healthcare system operates in a “reactive model” rather than a “proactive model” of health care.
A sensitive but important area to tackle among healthcare professionals is implicit bias. It is common for clinicians to create stereotypes about patients based on their race/ethnicity, age, socioeconomic status, gender, sexual orientation, presence of disabilities, etc. These biases may lead to decreased efforts in maximizing opportunities to reduce the progression of underlying medical conditions.

Patient Factors
People with DKD may not receive adequate access to DKD therapies due to the following patient-related factors: limited awareness and understanding of DKD remains a prevalent issue among patients. In many cases, patients are unaware of their condition and its subsequent implications. The root causes, symptoms, complications, and treatments of DKD often elude their comprehension. This issue is further compounded by the fact that only 9% of US adults diagnosed with moderate to severe CKD possess knowledge of their condition. At early to advanced stages of CKD, the condition is nearly symptomless. Data on awareness of CKD indicate that fewer than 10% of individuals at minimal risk (<2%) and 50% of those at high risk of developing kidney failure within 5 years (≥15%) were aware of their condition. This may also explain why a substantial proportion of the population tends to “crash into dialysis” at ESKD. This holds especially true for African American or Black and Latino/Hispanic patients with CKD, who exhibit low awareness and high levels of fatalism toward the disease.
In addition, patients may also lack the commitment to undertake recommended lifestyle modifications, including adopting healthy behaviors such as maintaining a balanced diet, increasing physical activity, avoiding tobacco smoking, moderating alcohol consumption, and sustaining a healthy body weight. Such lifestyle changes are critical in managing DKD, and inadequate adherence to these recommendations can have significant negative consequences, such as worsening DKD, leading to irreversible damage, poly medications, increasing the risk of drug interactions, and reduced quality of life.
Nonadherence to DKD therapies is a crucial challenge that can arise due to various factors, such as side effects, complexity of treatment, and cultural beliefs. Patients may fail to comply with their prescribed medications, dietary restrictions, or appointments. This can lead to poor health outcomes and increased healthcare costs. Often, it is not that patients are unwilling to follow their treatment plans but rather struggle to incorporate the treatment plans into their daily lives due to various social, medical, psychological, and financial factors. Unfortunately, healthcare providers and the healthcare system do not always address these factors effectively. Therefore, healthcare professionals must work closely with patients to identify and address any barriers to adherence and develop strategies to support patients in adhering to their treatment plans. It is also important to evaluate system-based support, which does not rely on the manual contributions from healthcare providers who are also very burned out in the current time of healthcare delivery. Patients who lack insurance, transportation, social support, or trust can experience more frequent healthcare interruptions or loss of follow-up care, which poses significant challenges to managing their health conditions effectively. This underscores the crucial need for access to regular healthcare services and resources to help patients stay informed, maintain trust in their providers, and manage their conditions effectively.

Potential Solutions for Reducing Inequities and Barriers to Access DKD Therapies

Policy Interventions
Policy interventions can involve changing the rules, regulations, or incentives that affect the behavior of healthcare providers, patients, payers, or other stakeholders. Expanding health insurance coverage, reducing cost-sharing, and increasing reimbursement for DKD therapies can substantially ameliorate patients’ affordability and accessibility to the most appropriate and efficacious treatment options for their condition. Moreover, this can foster a conducive environment for healthcare providers to prescribe and administer these therapies without pecuniary restraints or disincentives.
Payers have implemented a “fail first policy” that mandates the patients to attempt cheaper or older therapies before accessing newer or more expensive alternatives. This policy aims to regulate the costs and utilization of prescription drugs. However, it may also result in delayed or denied access to optimal therapies, leading to decreased prescription rates and increased discontinuation rates of these drugs among patients with DKD. Additionally, the administrative burden placed on healthcare providers to advocate for the approval of medications for patients significantly affects prescription behavior.
Furthermore, the IRA significantly impacts drug pricing in the USA, particularly for Medicare beneficiaries, through provisions such as the Medicare DPNP and inflation price caps. The negotiation program enables Medicare to directly discuss and set prices with manufacturers for some high-priced medications, including some SGLT2i inhibitors like Jardiance and Farxiga. This has resulted in significant price reductions of 66% and 68%, respectively, from their 2023 list prices. These prices will take effect starting from January 2026.
Patient assistance programs developed by pharma companies must also be expanded and better utilized by healthcare providers and patients to help those who are struggling financially to cover their medications. The increased focus on preventive care and patient outcomes may provide financial incentives for institutions to increase the utilization of disease-altering therapies. In 2019, the treatment of Medicare beneficiaries with CKD cost USD 87.2 billion, and treating those with ESKD cost an additional USD 37.3 billion. Value-based care models such as Accountable Care Organizations (ACOs) allow healthcare providers to share in any savings from preventing such high-cost outcomes. This creates a direct financial incentive for institutions to invest in preventive DKD care and to increase utilization of therapies that slow disease progression. In practice, some health systems have joined ACO programs and explicitly prioritized DKD prevention and care because reducing CKD progression can yield significant cost savings. Aligning payment incentives (bonus payments, shared savings, etc.) with improved kidney outcomes encourages healthcare organizations to implement comprehensive DKD management programs.

Clinical Interventions
Improving the quality and delivery of healthcare services for people with DKD, such as early identification, accurate diagnosis, and proper management of DKD, is crucial to prevent or delay its progression and associated complications. A combination of eGFR and uACR can be used for efficient screening and management of DKD. Also, in order to facilitate the use of effective medications for DKD, healthcare providers need to be educated about the safety, indications, and benefits of SGLT2i and finerenone, as well as the benefits of GLP1-RA on CKD beyond their blood glucose-lowering activity.
To ensure equitable access and delivery of quality kidney care, health systems that serve historically marginalized populations can implement a value-based kidney care program. It is anticipated that this program aligns incentives and accountability among healthcare providers, payers, and patients to promote standardized protocols, performance measures, feedback mechanisms, care coordination, patient engagement, and payment models. In doing so, it may improve the overall efficiency, quality, and outcomes of kidney care.
Additionally, healthcare providers and healthcare systems may consider shifting from a reactive model of care to a robust, proactive model. Implementation of science programs for GDMT in DKD can address disparities by providing equitable access to specialist care and integrating care pathways sensitive to socioeconomic and regional determinants of health. These programs often establish multidisciplinary care pathways that account for socioeconomic and regional determinants of health. For example, embedding pharmacists, dietitians, or care coordinators into diabetes and kidney clinics can help ensure patients get appropriate DKD therapies and follow-up. There should also be greater emphasis on preventive measures such as blood pressure control, glycemic control, and use of renoprotective agents before advanced CKD develops.
As a concrete example, a pharmacist-led intervention program called “Positive Kidney Health” was implemented to improve care for patients at risk of CKD progression. This program, involving proactive medication management and patient education by pharmacists, demonstrated improved risk factor control and guideline implementation in a high-risk population. Such models highlight how specialized care interventions can enhance DKD management. Similarly, some institutions have introduced nephrologist consultation services embedded in primary care for diabetic patients with CKD, ensuring earlier specialist input and optimization of therapy.

Patient Interventions
Patient intervention includes increasing awareness and knowledge of DKD and its therapies, implementing effective nutritional interventions and providing physical activity programs among historically marginalized populations. It is important to use culturally appropriate materials and methods to increase the awareness and knowledge of DKD among historically marginalized populations.
Language barriers can pose significant challenges for individuals with DKD who seek access to health information and services related to their condition. This may be remedied by providing health information and services in languages spoken by the patient receiving the services and using digital features and translated content in scientific publications to facilitate understanding them. Furthermore, engaging and involving the target population and relevant stakeholders in the design, delivery, and evaluation of interventions and programs is critical to their success. This is particularly vital in the field of nephrology, where racial and ethnic minoritized populations face a stark paradox: they are significantly underrepresented in clinical trials yet disproportionately bear the burden of adverse kidney disease outcomes. Moreover, involving minoritized populations in the planning stages of trials and ensuring that investigative teams are diverse in terms of language, race, and ethnicity can significantly enhance the representation and, consequently, the efficacy of clinical trials.
This collaborative approach to interventions may increase adoption within the community to meet specific needs, improve health outcomes, and enhance quality of life. Community programs represent a valuable platform for raising awareness, providing education, and delivering screening and referral services for DKD in local settings. For instance, culturally competent community health workers, peer educators, or faith-based leaders can provide relevant information and support to people with DKD in their communities. Additionally, community workshops offer an excellent opportunity to educate and empower DKD patients or those at risk of developing the condition in their communities. These interactive sessions or events can augment knowledge and skills, assist patients in managing their condition, reduce the risk of complications, and provide a support network by connecting patients with similar experiences.
Notably, evidence from a community-wide CKD management program in the UK demonstrates the impact of such proactive interventions. Following the introduction of a region-wide systematic CKD program (which included primary care education, patient engagement, routine eGFR reporting, and early referral strategies), the population-adjusted incidence of renal replacement therapy (dialysis or transplant start) declined. The proportion of patients initiating dialysis with an optimal modality (transplant, peritoneal dialysis, or fistula for hemodialysis) increased significantly.

Conclusion
DKD is a major complication of diabetes that affects millions of people worldwide and leads to increased morbidity and mortality. However, there are significant disparities and barriers to accessing DKD therapies among historically marginalized populations, such as racial and ethnic minority groups, populations with low socioeconomic status, or a lower level of education. These are caused by socioeconomic factors (such as cost, insurance coverage, availability of healthcare services, and transportation), clinical factors (such as comorbidities, medication adherence, and provider bias), and patient factors (such as knowledge, beliefs, preferences, and trust). These disparities and barriers result in worse outcomes and health inequities for marginalized populations with DKD.
To address this issue, a multifactorial approach involving policies, clinical, and patient interventions is proposed, such as educating healthcare providers and patients, improving screening and diagnosis, implementing value-based kidney care programs as well as expanding health insurance coverage, reducing out-of-pocket cost, and/or increasing reimbursement. Ultimately, medications must be more affordable for all patients through combined efforts of government, healthcare systems, insurance companies, and the pharma industry.
These interventions may help reduce the gap and improve the access and quality of DKD therapies for historically marginalized populations. In doing so, the burden of DKD and its complications may be alleviated, and the health outcomes and quality of life for these marginalized populations with DKD may be improved.
