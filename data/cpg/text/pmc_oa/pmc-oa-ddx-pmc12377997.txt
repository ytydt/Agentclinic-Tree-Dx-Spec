Minimally invasive approach versus open approach in the management of necrotizing acute pancreatitis: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Acute pancreatitis is one of the most common gastrointestinal diseases, with necrotizing pancreatitis affecting 10% to 15% of patients. Over recent years, the management of pancreatic necrosis has evolved significantly, with a growing shift towards minimally invasive approaches. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of minimally invasive approach compared to open surgical approach in managing necrotizing acute pancreatitis. A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted, including 22 studies. Both fixed-effect and random-effect models were applied to analyze nine outcomes evaluated. Homogeneity among studies was assessed using χ2 tests, I2 statistics, and p-values. The risk of postoperative mortality, intra-abdominal bleeding, pancreatic and enteric fistulas, perforation, new-onset diabetes, and postoperative sepsis was significantly lower in the minimally invasive approach group than in the open surgical approach group. A minimally invasive approach to necrotizing acute pancreatitis might be associated with a lower risk of complications compared to an open surgical approach. However, most of the included studies were observational studies. Additional randomized trials are needed to further confirm these findings.

INTRODUCTION
The management of “walled-off” pancreatic necrosis has undergone significant evolution over the past three decades, resulting in changes to various treatment approaches. Currently, both open surgery and laparoscopic techniques are used, alongside the development of step-up approaches that combine percutaneous or endoscopic drainage with minimally invasive debridement. However, there is no universal standard approach for all patients due to considerable differences in clinical status, composition of the pancreatic collection (which can range from liquid to solid), and the anatomical location of the collection. As such, treatment timelines and strategies should be tailored to each patient’s specific needs to minimize morbidity and mortality while improving their overall prognosis.
The aim of this study was to compare the effectiveness and outcomes of minimally invasive approaches (including percutaneous drainage, endoscopic necrosectomy, and minimally invasive transgastric or retroperitoneal necrosectomy) and an open surgical approach.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Systematic review and meta-analysis were carried out in adherence with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The protocol for this study was registered on PROSPERO.

Search methods for identification of studies
A comprehensive search was performed across PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar databases to identify relevant studies by two authors (RC and MM). Keywords used for identifying the study population included pancreatitis, acute pancreatitis (AP), severe pancreatitis, necrotizing pancreatitis, walled-off pancreatic necrosis, infected pancreatic necrosis, and pancreatic collections. These were combined with terms related to therapeutic treatments such as necrosectomy, conventional surgery, open, minimally invasive, endoscopy, transgastric endoscopy, endoscopic necrosectomy, laparotomy, laparoscopy, laparoscopic necrosectomy, retroperitoneum, retroperitoneal necrosectomy, retroperitoneal surgery, drainage, endoscopic drainage, percutaneous drainage, and retroperitoneal approach.
No restrictions were applied concerning language, country, year of publication, sample size, or study design. The final search was completed in November 2024. Additional strategies included reviewing references of all included articles for further relevant materials.

Data extraction and management
All included studies were reviewed by two independent reviewers (RC and MM). Extracted data encompassed the country of study, study design (retrospective observational study, prospective study, or randomized clinical trial), cohort enrollment period, number of patients treated with open surgery vs. minimally invasive approaches, types of procedures performed, timing of interventions, and outcomes of interest.
Patient characteristics such as sample size, sex, average age, etiology of AP, number of patients with sepsis at baseline, number of patients with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome at baseline, preoperative Ranson score, preoperative APACHE II score, CT-severity index (CT-SI), white blood cell count, and C-reactive protein level (in mg/dL) were also collected and reported in tables. Outcomes examined included postoperative mortality, development of sepsis, intra-abdominal hemorrhage, pancreatic fistula, enteric fistula, gastrointestinal perforation, surgical reinterventions, and long-term outcomes (such as new-onset diabetes, need for pancreatic enzyme use, and pseudocyst formation).

Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis for dichotomous variables was performed using risk ratio (RR) calculated with the Mantel-Haenszel method. For continuous variables, the analysis utilized the ‘mean difference’ (absolute mean difference) calculated by the inverse variance method. Both fixed-effect and random-effect models were applied to all outcomes.
Homogeneity among studies was evaluated with χ2 tests, I2 statistics, and p-values. χ2 < 0.05 and I2 < 50% indicated no heterogeneity, for which the analysis was conducted using the fixed-effect model. The random-effect model was used when these criteria were not satisfied. p-values greater than 0.1 and I2 values less than 50% indicated an absence of heterogeneity. I2 values between 50% and 75% suggested moderate heterogeneity, while its values greater than 75% indicated high heterogeneity. All statistical analyses were carried out using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 from The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020.

RESULTS

Identification of studies
A systematic search in PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar yielded 690 results and 6 records through other sources. After removing 293 duplicates, titles and abstracts of the remaining 403 articles were reviewed and 308 articles that failed to meet our inclusion criteria were excluded. We evaluated 95 full-text articles and included 22 studies in this meta-analysis. The search strategy is illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart shown below (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of studies
Included studies were published between 1998 and 2024. Geographically, two studies were conducted in France, three in the United Kingdom, two in the Netherlands, three in the USA, three in Germany, one in India, one in Latvia, three in China, one in Romania, two in Poland, and one in Croatia. Of these studies, one was a randomized controlled trial, fifteen were retrospective observational studies, and three were prospective observational studies (Table 1–3). The duration of patient enrollment spanned from a minimum of 3 years to a maximum of 16 years.
Each study provided descriptions of surgical procedures performed. In the conventional surgery group (open necrosectomy), all studies utilized transperitoneal laparotomy for necrosectomy. In the minimally invasive group, 15 studies employed a retroperitoneal approach, two studies conducted transgastric endoscopic necrosectomy, and two studies utilized both retroperitoneal and endoscopic techniques (Fig. 2).
Sixteen studies reported the timing of intervention expressed as the number of days or weeks from admission or the onset of the disease. All studies reported outcomes of interest as shown in Table 4. Baseline (preoperative) characteristics of patients from each study are presented in Table 2, 3. These tables included sample size, the male-to-female ratio, average age, and etiology (indicating the number of patients with biliary pancreatitis, alcoholic pancreatitis, or other causes). Additionally, they reported the number of patients experiencing sepsis and those with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, along with the Ranson score, APACHE II score, CT-SI score, white blood cell count (in units of ×109/L), and the level of C-reactive protein (in mg/L).
Regarding outcomes of interest, postoperative mortality within 30 days was reported in 21 studies. Postoperative intra-abdominal bleeding appeared in 19 studies. The formation of pancreatic fistulas was noted in 17 studies. Enteric fistula formation was reported in 15 studies. Visceral organ perforation was identified in seven studies. Pseudocyst formation was documented in five studies. Postoperative sepsis occurred in 11 studies. Newly diagnosed diabetes was present in ten studies. The use of pancreatic enzymes was mentioned in seven studies.
In our meta-analysis, nine outcomes of greatest clinical interest were analyzed. Results are summarized in the following chart created with GraphPad. They will be discussed in subsequent sections.

Quality assessment of included studies
For each included study, two reviewers (RC and MM) independently assessed the risk of bias. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. We used the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool I for classifying the risk of bias into three categories (low, high, or moderate). Criteria and classifications outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions were used to assess each bias item. The risk of bias graph and summary are shown in Fig. 3 and 4, respectively. Regarding the risk of bias due to confounding, 7 included studies showed a moderate risk of bias, while other studies showed a low risk of bias. Regarding the bias due to selection of participants, in classification of interventions and measurement of bias, only two studies showed a moderate risk of bias. Regarding “bias due to deviations from intended interventions” and “bias due to missing data,” all included studies showed a low risk of bias. Ten included studies showed a moderate risk of bias in the area regarding reported results.

Results of systematic review

Postoperative mortality
Meta-analysis of 21 studies with 2,470 patients revealed that the postoperative mortality rate in the minimally invasive group (12.02%) was significantly lower than that in the open necrosectomy group (27.77%) (RR: 0.47, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.38–0.57; p < 0.00001). There was no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 5).

Intra-abdominal bleeding
The meta-analysis of 20 studies involving 2,058 patients showed that the rate of postoperative intra-abdominal bleeding in the minimally invasive group was 9.7%, which was lower than that (21.19%) in the open necrosectomy group (RR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.52–0.96; p = 0.003). There was a moderate heterogeneity (I² = 37%) (Fig. 6).

Pancreatic fistula
The meta-analysis of 17 studies with 1,836 patients showed that the minimally invasive group had a significantly lower postoperative risk of pancreatic fistula (17.36%) than the open necrosectomy group (36.25%) (RR: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.49–0.81; p = 0.0003). There was a low heterogeneity (I² = 33%) (Fig. 7).

Enteric fistula
A meta-analysis of 15 studies encompassing 1,636 patients indicated that the postoperative risk of enteric fistula formation in patients of the minimally invasive group (7.85%) was significantly lower than that in the open necrosectomy group (11.34%) (RR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.42–0.84; p = 0.003). The analysis demonstrated no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 8).

Visceral perforation
A meta-analysis of 7 studies with 395 patients revealed that the rate of postoperative visceral organ perforation was lower in the minimally invasive group (6.51%) than in the open necrosectomy group (17.77%) (RR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.21–1.00; p = 0.05). The heterogeneity was low (I² = 25%) (Fig. 9).

Pseudocysts
A meta-analysis of 5 studies with 401 patients found that the postoperative risk of pseudocyst formation in the minimally invasive group (5.86%) was lower but not significantly lower than that in the open necrosectomy group (13.28%) (RR: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.29–1.10; p = 0.09). There was no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 10).

Postoperative sepsis
A meta-analysis of 10 studies encompassing 1,103 patients revealed that the minimally invasive group had a significantly lower postoperative risk of sepsis (17.51%) than the open necrosectomy group (38.31%) (RR: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.37–0.66; p < 0.00001). The analysis also demonstrated a moderate heterogeneity (I² = 36%) (Fig. 11).

New diabetes diagnosis
The meta-analysis of 9 studies involving 977 patients revealed that the postoperative risk of newly diagnosed diabetes in the minimally invasive group (7.59%) was significantly lower than that in the open necrosectomy group (24.3%) (RR: 0.37, 95% CI: 0.23–0.61; p < 0.0001). Results also demonstrated a moderate heterogeneity (I² = 44%) (Fig. 12).

Use of pancreatic enzymes
The meta-analysis of 6 studies involving 529 patients indicated that postoperative risk of using pancreatic enzymes showed no statistically significant differences between the minimally invasive group (24.8%) and the open necrosectomy group (22.84%) (RR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.46–1.76; p = 0.75). Results also showed a high heterogeneity (p = 0.004; I² = 76%) (Fig. 13).

DISCUSSION
Acute necrotizing pancreatitis (ANP) is a severe form of AP with a mortality rate exceeding 30%. It is often due to infections. Surgical interventions, including open and minimally invasive approaches, are crucial for removing necrotic tissues and draining fluid collections.
For treating necrotic tissues, various techniques have been proposed, including percutaneous drainage, transgastric endoscopic or laparoscopic necrosectomy, laparoscopic necrosectomy with retroperitoneal access, and open necrosectomy. However, no single method is considered universally superior to others as heterogeneity among patient samples complicates direct comparisons.
According to International Association of Pancreatology and American Pancreatic Association (IAP-APA) guidelines, primary indications for intervention in necrotic collections include the following:
• Clinical suspicion of infected necrotizing pancreatitis with clinical deterioration, preferably when the necrosis has become walled-off, ideally after 4 weeks.
• In the absence of documented infected necrotizing pancreatitis, ongoing organ failure for several weeks after the onset of AP, ideally when the necrosis has become walled-off.
• Sterile necrotizing pancreatitis, later in the course after necrosis has walled-off, with ongoing gastric outlet, intestinal, or biliary obstruction due to mass effect; persistent symptoms (pain, unwellness); or a disconnected pancreatic duct with ongoing symptoms.
Minimally invasive strategies have been shown to be able to reduce perioperative stress, new-onset organ failure, and long-term complications such as fistulas, exocrine and endocrine pancreatic insufficiency, and incisional hernias.
Historically, immediate open approaches with necrosectomy and debridement were the first choice for infected and symptomatic necrosis. However, as medical practice has evolved, minimally invasive strategies have been proposed for treating pancreatic necrosis. The term “step-up method” was coined with the PANTER trial by the Dutch Acute Pancreatitis Group. It refers to minimally invasive procedures that can be escalated to include drainage of infected pancreatic necrosis if necessary.
Treatment strategies should be individualized for each patient. However, goals of therapy have remained consistent. The primary objective is to decrease systemic inflammatory response syndrome and reduce the risk of multi-organ damage and failure. Secondary goals include reducing the risk of complications associated with AP, such as pancreatic fistulas or exocrine and endocrine pancreatic insufficiency. Current consensus recommends that the initial management of infected necrosis in clinically stable patients should include a 2- to 4-week course of antibiotics before surgery to allow inflammatory reactions to become better organized. A combined multimodal approach integrating minimally invasive surgery and endoscopic drainage appears to be more effective than the open approach.
Our meta-analysis of 22 studies found that minimally invasive approaches significantly reduced postoperative mortality and complications compared to open necrosectomy. Benefits of minimally invasive approaches, such as percutaneous drainage and transgastric endoscopic necrosectomy, include lower rates of pancreatic or enteric fistulas, visceral perforation, pseudocyst formation, sepsis, and newly diagnosed diabetes. However, no significant differences were found in intra-abdominal bleeding or pancreatic enzyme use between minimally invasive approach and open approach groups. These results were consistent with those of another meta-analysis by Xiao et al., which demonstrated that the approach could reduce the risk of complications associated with AP.
Despite these positive findings, this meta-analysis has several limitations, including statistical heterogeneity, selection biases, variations in surgical techniques, differences in patient characteristics, and variations in intervention timing. Many included studies were conducted when open surgery was still routine. The lack of randomized controlled trials made it difficult to compare similar patient groups. Moreover, our study did not incorporate some studies in literature regarding the potential use of robotic approaches to overcome limitations of endoscopic and minimally invasive techniques. However, there are only a few studies on the robotic approach. These studies have demonstrated its safety and efficacy in performing pancreatic necrosectomy and internal drainage in patients with pancreatic necrosis.
Other important methodological limitations are due to overlap in treatment approaches between minimally invasive and open access. This affects the clarity of a comparative analysis between step-up approach and open access. The lack of strict protocol separation introduces the potential for confounding variables, making it challenging to assess the direct impact of each treatment strategy. Future studies should ensure clearer delineation between treatment groups to improve validity and provide more robust conclusions. Despite these limitations, findings of this study offer valuable insights into the treatment of patients with necrotizing pancreatitis and highlight the need for standardized protocols to enhance their clinical outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS
Minimally invasive approach offers reduced mortality and postoperative complications in ANP treatment. More high-quality randomized studies are needed to confirm results of this study and address existing research gaps.
