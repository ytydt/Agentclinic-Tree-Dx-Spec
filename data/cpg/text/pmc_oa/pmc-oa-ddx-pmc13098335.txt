Regulatory integration of new approach methodologies for human-relevant developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) assessment: A systematic cross-sector review
Developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) assessment is a critical component of regulatory safety evaluation across pharmaceutical, chemical, environmental, and consumer product sectors. Traditionally, DART testing has relied on animal-based studies designed to detect adverse effects on fertility, embryonic development, and postnatal growth. However, these conventional approaches present increasing scientific and ethical challenges, including high costs, extended study durations, limited mechanistic insight, and interspecies differences that may reduce their predictive value for human health outcomes. Concurrently, regulatory frameworks increasingly emphasize the principles of Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement (3Rs), driving the development of alternative testing strategies. This systematic review examines the evolving regulatory landscape for DART assessment and evaluates the scientific and regulatory progress associated with emerging New Approach Methodologies (NAMs). A structured literature search was conducted across major scientific databases following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, identifying relevant scientific and regulatory publications from January 2005 to February 2026. The review analyzes key international regulatory frameworks, including OECD Test Guidelines, ICH S5(R3), the European Union REACH regulation, and guidance from U.S. and European regulatory authorities. Particular emphasis is placed on mechanistic frameworks such as Adverse Outcome Pathways (AOPs) and Integrated Approaches to Testing and Assessment (IATA), which facilitate the integration of diverse data streams within weight-of-evidence–based regulatory decision-making. Overall, NAMs offer significant potential to enhance the efficiency, mechanistic understanding, and human relevance of DART assessment. Nevertheless, challenges related to method validation, regulatory harmonization, and integration into existing safety assessment frameworks remain, highlighting the need for continued scientific innovation and international regulatory collaboration.

Introduction
Developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) testing is a fundamental component of regulatory safety assessment for pharmaceuticals, industrial chemicals, pesticides, environmental contaminants, and consumer products. Adverse exposures during critical windows of development may result in infertility, spontaneous abortion, congenital malformations, neurodevelopmental impairment, and long-term disease susceptibility later in life. Consequently, regulatory risk assessment frameworks require robust preclinical evidence to identify potential hazards, characterize dose–response relationships, and establish exposure limits that protect vulnerable populations such as pregnant individuals and developing embryos or fetuses. Within the European regulatory landscape, chemical safety legislation under the REACH Regulation places substantial emphasis on reproductive and developmental toxicity endpoints as part of comprehensive human health risk assessment.
Historically, regulatory DART assessment has relied primarily on in vivo animal studies designed to detect embryo-fetal toxicity, fertility impairment, and developmental abnormalities. Standardized experimental approaches have been harmonized internationally through testing programs established by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, which provides detailed recommendations for study design, endpoint selection, and interpretation of toxicity findings through the OECD Test Guidelines framework. In the pharmaceutical sector, regulatory requirements for reproductive toxicity testing are defined by the ICH S5(R3) Guideline, which outlines the nonclinical studies required to support clinical development and marketing authorization of human medicinal products. These frameworks have improved consistency in safety evaluation; however, uncertainties remain regarding human relevance and extrapolation of animal findings .
Animal models have played a central role in regulatory toxicology, with rodents and rabbits as standard species in prenatal and multigenerational studies . However, historical reliance on animal testing—reinforced by regulatory reforms following public health events such as the Elixir Sulfanilamide disaster  is increasingly being re-evaluated in light of scientific and ethical considerations.
Despite their longstanding contributions, conventional animal-based DART studies face increasing scientific, ethical, and practical challenges. Ethical concerns are addressed through the principles of the Three Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), which promote minimization of animal use. In addition, species-specific differences in placental biology, metabolism, and developmental timing may limit the predictive value of animal models for human outcomes. Moreover, conventional multigenerational toxicity studies are resource-intensive and time-consuming, limiting their applicability for large-scale chemical safety assessment.
These challenges have stimulated the development of innovative testing strategies collectively referred to as New Approach Methodologies (NAMs). NAMs encompass non-animal approaches including advanced human cell-based in vitro systems, organoids, microphysiological systems, high-throughput screening technologies, and computational toxicology models such as QSAR and machine learning-based approaches. Increasingly, these technologies are organized within mechanistic frameworks such as Adverse Outcome Pathways and Integrated Approaches to Testing and Assessment, which facilitate structured, weight-of-evidence–based regulatory decision-making.
Recent advances in systems biology, high-throughput screening, and computational modeling have accelerated the development of NAM-based strategies for evaluating developmental toxicity endpoints, including applications in developmental neurotoxicity and endocrine disruption. Nevertheless, regulatory acceptance of NAMs remains variable across sectors, and animal studies continue to serve as the primary evidentiary standard in most frameworks. Differences in validation requirements, evidentiary thresholds, and legislative mandates further complicate harmonized integration of NAMs into regulatory decision-making.
While numerous reviews have examined individual NAM technologies or ethical aspects of animal reduction, relatively few studies have systematically evaluated their regulatory integration across sectors.

Methodology of the systematic review

Review design
This study was conducted as a systematic review to evaluate the regulatory landscape governing developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) testing and to assess the emerging role of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) in regulatory toxicology. This review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines, incorporating structured literature identification, transparent selection criteria, and reproducible search strategies to ensure methodological rigor.
The review protocol was developed in accordance with PRISMA 2020 methodological recommendations, and study selection, data extraction, and evidence synthesis were conducted using predefined criteria to minimize bias and ensure methodological transparency.
The primary objective of this review was to examine the evolution of international regulatory requirements for DART assessment and to critically evaluate the scientific and regulatory readiness of NAMs as complementary or alternative approaches to traditional animal-based toxicity testing. To accommodate both regulatory documents and experimental toxicology studies, the review followed a structured systematic–narrative hybrid approach.

Literature search strategy
A comprehensive literature search was conducted using three major scientific databases: PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. These databases were selected because they provide extensive coverage of biomedical, toxicological, and regulatory science literature.
The search included studies published between January 2005 and February 2026, ensuring coverage of both foundational regulatory developments and recent advances in mechanistic and predictive toxicology.
The final literature search was completed on 28 February 2026.
The search strategy combined keywords and controlled vocabulary related to developmental and reproductive toxicity, regulatory toxicology, and alternative testing approaches.
Representative search terms included developmental toxicity, reproductive toxicity, embryo-fetal toxicity, OECD Test Guidelines, ICH S5, REACH regulation, New Approach Methodologies, in vitro toxicology, in silico toxicology, adverse outcome pathways, and integrated approaches to testing and assessment.
Boolean operators were used to combine search terms. An example of the full PubMed search strategy used is provided below to ensure reproducibility:
("developmental toxicity" OR "reproductive toxicity" OR "embryo-fetal toxicity") AND ("New Approach Methodologies" OR NAMs OR "in vitro toxicology" OR "in silico toxicology") AND ("adverse outcome pathway" OR AOP OR "integrated approaches to testing and assessment") AND ("regulatory toxicology" OR OECD OR ICH OR REACH OR FDA OR EMA) Filters applied included publication years 2005–2026 and English language.
In addition to peer-reviewed articles, regulatory documents and official guidance materials were retrieved from authoritative sources including OECD, ICH, ECHA (REACH), FDA, and EMA to ensure accurate representation of regulatory testing requirements and policy developments.

Study selection process
The study selection process followed the PRISMA systematic review framework, which includes four stages: identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion. Title/abstract screening and full-text assessment were performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. A total of 1482 records were initially identified through database searches, including 612 records from PubMed, 421 from Web of Science, and 449 from Scopus. After removal of 327 duplicate records, 1155 unique articles remained for title and abstract screening.
During the screening stage, 894 records were excluded because they were not relevant to regulatory toxicology, developmental or reproductive toxicity endpoints, or NAM-based testing approaches. The full texts of 261 articles were subsequently assessed for eligibility.
Following full-text evaluation, 169 studies were excluded due to lack of regulatory relevance, exclusive focus on basic developmental biology, or absence of translational toxicological applicability. Ultimately, 92 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the qualitative synthesis and regulatory analysis presented in this review.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Eligibility criteria were defined prior to the literature screening process to ensure that the selected publications were directly relevant to regulatory toxicology and DART risk assessment.
Studies were included if they described regulatory frameworks governing developmental and reproductive toxicity testing, investigated New Approach Methodologies applicable to regulatory toxicology, addressed mechanistic toxicology frameworks such as adverse outcome pathways (AOPs) or integrated approaches to testing and assessment (IATA), or evaluated the regulatory acceptance and implementation of non-animal testing strategies.
Publications focusing exclusively on basic developmental biology without regulatory relevance, clinical reproductive outcomes unrelated to preclinical toxicology, or experimental models lacking translational applicability to regulatory safety assessment were excluded. Editorials, commentaries, and opinion articles without substantial scientific analysis were also excluded.

Data extraction and evidence synthesis
A formal risk-of-bias assessment was not performed due to the heterogeneous nature of included sources, which comprised experimental studies, regulatory guidelines, and mechanistic toxicology reports. This approach is consistent with systematic reviews in regulatory toxicology where conventional bias assessment tools are not directly applicable. Instead, emphasis was placed on methodological quality, scientific robustness, and regulatory relevance during qualitative synthesis. Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers using predefined extraction criteria, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion to ensure consistency and accuracy. The extracted information was synthesized using qualitative thematic analysis, enabling identification of major research trends, emerging methodologies, and regulatory challenges associated with the implementation of NAM-based testing strategies in developmental and reproductive toxicity assessment.

Protocol registration
No formal review protocol was prospectively registered (e.g., PROSPERO), as this study integrates regulatory document analysis with scientific literature. However, the review methodology was predefined and applied consistently in accordance with PRISMA 2020 principles to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Regulatory framework analysis
To provide a comprehensive regulatory perspective, this review examined developmental and reproductive toxicity testing requirements across major international regulatory frameworks governing pharmaceuticals, industrial chemicals, environmental contaminants, and consumer products.
The analysis focused on the OECD Test Guidelines for chemical safety assessment, the pharmaceutical safety guidelines developed by the International Council for Harmonisation (particularly ICH S5(R3)), the European Union REACH regulation, and regulatory guidance issued by the FDA and EMA.
Comparative evaluation of these frameworks enabled assessment of testing requirements, regulatory expectations, and the evolving integration of mechanistic and alternative testing strategies within regulatory safety evaluation systems.

Conceptual framework for NAM integration
Recent developments in regulatory toxicology emphasize the adoption of mechanistic and human-relevant testing strategies that can complement or partially replace traditional animal-based studies. One of the most widely adopted conceptual frameworks supporting this transition is the Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) concept, which describes the causal linkage between a molecular initiating event and downstream biological key events leading to an adverse outcome of regulatory concern.
Within regulatory risk assessment, AOP-based knowledge can be incorporated into Integrated Approaches to Testing and Assessment (IATA). These approaches combine multiple lines of evidence—including in vitro assays, in silico predictive models, exposure data, and targeted in vivo studies—within a weight-of-evidence framework to support hazard identification and risk characterization while reducing reliance on animal testing.

Historical evolution of animal-based developmental and reproductive toxicity testing

Early foundations of regulatory DART testing
The scientific and regulatory evaluation of developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) has evolved considerably over the past several decades. Early toxicological safety assessments primarily focused on acute toxicity and general systemic effects, while reproductive and developmental outcomes received comparatively limited attention. However, increasing awareness of the vulnerability of developing organisms to chemical exposure led to the gradual recognition that adverse effects on fertility, embryonic development, and postnatal growth represent critical endpoints in toxicological risk assessment.
A major turning point in the history of reproductive toxicology occurred following the thalidomide tragedy of the early 1960s, which revealed that pharmaceutical exposure during pregnancy could result in severe congenital malformations. This event profoundly influenced global regulatory policy and underscored the necessity of systematic testing for developmental toxicity prior to the approval of pharmaceuticals and other chemicals intended for human use. Consequently, regulatory authorities began requiring dedicated reproductive toxicity studies in laboratory animals to evaluate potential hazards associated with prenatal exposure.
Early animal studies in reproductive toxicology primarily assessed endpoints such as fertility, implantation success, fetal viability, and gross morphological abnormalities in offspring. Although these studies provided important safety insights, they often lacked standardized methodologies and consistent experimental designs. Variability in study protocols across laboratories and regulatory jurisdictions made interpretation and comparison of data challenging, highlighting the need for harmonized testing strategies capable of producing reliable and reproducible results for regulatory decision-making.

Development of international testing guidelines
To address inconsistencies in experimental design and improve the comparability of toxicological data, international organizations began developing standardized testing guidelines for reproductive and developmental toxicity assessment. Among the most influential initiatives was the establishment of the test guideline program by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which aimed to harmonize toxicological testing procedures across member countries.
The OECD Test Guidelines provide detailed protocols describing study design, animal selection, dosing regimens, and evaluation endpoints for a wide range of toxicological assessments. Within the field of reproductive toxicology, several guidelines were introduced to standardize the evaluation of fertility and developmental toxicity. For example, OECD Test Guideline 414 focuses on prenatal developmental toxicity and assesses fetal growth, viability, and structural abnormalities following maternal exposure to a test substance during pregnancy. Similarly, OECD Test Guidelines 421 and 422 provide screening approaches for evaluating reproductive and developmental effects within a combined study design.
The implementation of standardized guidelines significantly improved the reproducibility and regulatory acceptance of reproductive toxicity studies. Furthermore, the OECD framework for Mutual Acceptance of Data (MAD) enabled toxicity data generated in one member country to be accepted by regulatory authorities in others, reducing duplication of studies and facilitating international regulatory harmonization.
In addition to core reproductive toxicity guidelines, OECD has expanded testing strategies to include specialized endpoints such as developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) and endocrine disruption. Test guidelines such as OECD TG 426 (developmental neurotoxicity) and internationally coordinated endocrine disruptor screening programs reflect increasing regulatory attention toward complex and mechanistically driven developmental outcomes, further highlighting the limitations of traditional apical testing approaches and the need for complementary NAM-based strategies.

Multigenerational and extended one-generation studies
As the field of reproductive toxicology matured, researchers recognized that some toxic effects may only manifest over multiple generations or during later stages of development. To capture these long-term outcomes, multigenerational reproductive toxicity studies were introduced as part of regulatory testing frameworks. Traditional two-generation studies evaluate reproductive performance, fertility, and developmental outcomes in both parental and offspring generations, providing comprehensive insight into potential intergenerational effects of chemical exposure.
Although two-generation studies provide extensive information, they require substantial numbers of animals, long experimental durations, and considerable financial resources. Subsequent analyses indicated that evaluation of the second generation often contributed limited additional regulatory value in many cases. In response to these concerns, the OECD introduced the Extended One-Generation Reproductive Toxicity Study (EOGRTS; OECD Test Guideline 443). This approach incorporates a modular study design that allows investigators to examine specific developmental or reproductive endpoints depending on regulatory needs and existing knowledge regarding the substance under investigation.
The EOGRTS represents an important refinement of traditional reproductive toxicity testing by improving study efficiency while maintaining the ability to detect key developmental and reproductive effects. At the same time, the guideline reflects increasing attention to the ethical principles of animal research, including reduction in animal use and optimization of experimental design.

Harmonization in the pharmaceutical sector
In addition to chemical regulation frameworks, the pharmaceutical industry has established harmonized international guidelines for reproductive and developmental toxicity testing through the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH). The ICH guidelines were developed to ensure consistent safety evaluation standards for pharmaceuticals intended for global markets.
The ICH S5 guideline outlines recommended approaches for assessing fertility effects, embryo–fetal development, and pre- and postnatal development in support of clinical development and marketing authorization. These studies are typically conducted in two relevant animal species and are designed to identify potential reproductive hazards associated with pharmaceutical exposure prior to human clinical trials.
More recently, the updated ICH S5(R3) guideline has introduced greater flexibility in study design and acknowledges the potential role of mechanistic and non-animal data within a weight-of-evidence framework. While animal studies remain the primary regulatory requirement for reproductive toxicity evaluation in pharmaceutical development, the revised guideline reflects growing recognition that complementary scientific approaches may enhance the overall assessment of reproductive safety.

Integration into chemical and environmental regulation
Developmental and reproductive toxicity testing has also become an essential component of chemical safety assessment within environmental and consumer product regulation. One of the most comprehensive regulatory frameworks in this area is the Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals (REACH) regulation implemented in the European Union. REACH requires manufacturers and importers to generate safety data for chemicals placed on the market, including information related to reproductive and developmental toxicity endpoints.
Under REACH, testing requirements are determined by production volume and potential exposure scenarios. Higher production volumes generally require more extensive toxicological evaluation, including reproductive toxicity studies conducted according to standardized OECD guidelines. Importantly, the regulation also promotes the use of alternative approaches to reduce unnecessary animal testing. Registrants are encouraged to employ strategies such as data sharing, read-across between structurally related chemicals, and quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling whenever scientifically justified.
These regulatory developments illustrate the growing emphasis on efficient and ethically responsible safety assessment strategies within chemical regulation, while maintaining a strong commitment to protecting human health and the environment.

Scientific limitations of animal-based DART testing
Despite their long-standing role in regulatory toxicology, traditional animal-based DART studies possess several scientific limitations that affect their predictive value for human health outcomes. One of the primary challenges arises from interspecies differences in reproductive physiology, placental structure, developmental timing, and metabolic processes. Such biological differences can influence the manifestation of toxic effects and complicate the extrapolation of animal study results to human risk assessment.
In addition to species differences, conventional reproductive toxicity studies often rely on apical endpoints such as fertility outcomes, fetal viability, and gross morphological abnormalities. While these endpoints provide valuable information regarding overt toxic effects, they offer limited insight into the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms responsible for developmental toxicity. As a result, traditional testing approaches may fail to detect subtle mechanistic perturbations that could contribute to long-term adverse health outcomes.
Animal-based reproductive toxicity studies also present substantial logistical and ethical challenges. These experiments require large numbers of animals, extended study durations, and considerable financial investment. Given the increasing number of chemicals requiring safety evaluation globally, reliance solely on traditional animal testing approaches is becoming increasingly impractical and resource-intensive. Consequently, the toxicology community has begun exploring alternative strategies capable of providing more mechanistically informative and human-relevant data.

New approach methodologies for developmental and reproductive toxicity

Scientific drivers for NAM development
The increasing recognition of scientific and logistical limitations associated with traditional animal-based developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) testing has accelerated the development of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs). These methodologies encompass a diverse range of experimental and computational approaches designed to improve the human relevance, efficiency, and mechanistic understanding of toxicological assessments. NAMs typically include in vitro cell-based models, organoid systems, high-throughput screening technologies, computational toxicology tools, and systems biology approaches that collectively aim to supplement or eventually replace animal testing in regulatory safety evaluation.
Several scientific drivers have stimulated the rapid advancement of NAMs in toxicology. First, traditional DART studies require extensive animal use, long experimental durations, and significant financial resources, limiting their feasibility for evaluating the large number of chemicals requiring safety assessment. Second, conventional animal studies frequently rely on apical endpoints that provide limited insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying toxicity. In contrast, NAMs enable the investigation of early cellular and molecular perturbations associated with developmental toxicity pathways, thereby improving mechanistic interpretation and predictive capacity.
In addition to scientific considerations, ethical and regulatory initiatives aimed at reducing animal experimentation have also contributed to the expansion of NAM-based approaches. Regulatory agencies increasingly promote the 3Rs principle—replacement, reduction, and refinement of animal use—within toxicological research and risk assessment frameworks. Consequently, NAMs are now being explored not only as screening tools but also as components of integrated regulatory decision-making strategies.

In vitro models for developmental and reproductive toxicity
Among the most widely investigated NAMs for DART assessment are human cell–based in vitro systems, which provide mechanistically informative platforms for evaluating chemical effects on developmental processes. Advances in stem cell biology have enabled the generation of human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) that can differentiate into a wide range of tissue-specific cell types, including neural, cardiac, hepatic, and reproductive cells. These systems allow researchers to investigate how chemical exposures influence cellular differentiation, proliferation, and organogenesis during early developmental stages.
Stem cell–based assays have been applied extensively in developmental toxicity testing. For example, pluripotent stem cell differentiation models can recapitulate key developmental processes and have been used to detect disruptions in pathways involved in embryonic development. These assays provide valuable insights into the mechanisms of developmental toxicity and may serve as early screening tools during chemical and pharmaceutical safety evaluation.
More recently, three-dimensional organoid systems have emerged as highly promising models for toxicological testing. Organoids are self-organizing multicellular structures derived from stem cells that mimic the architecture and functional characteristics of human tissues. Brain, liver, kidney, and cardiac organoids have been used to investigate the toxic effects of environmental chemicals and pharmaceuticals on organ development, enabling more physiologically relevant assessment of developmental toxicity compared with conventional two-dimensional cell cultures. For example, neural organoid models have been successfully applied to study neurodevelopmental toxicity mechanisms and to evaluate chemical effects on neuronal differentiation and synapse formation.
Another rapidly evolving technology is the development of microphysiological systems (MPS), commonly referred to as organ-on-a-chip platforms. These systems integrate living cells within microfluidic devices that replicate aspects of tissue microarchitecture and physiological interactions. MPS technologies allow dynamic monitoring of cellular responses under controlled experimental conditions and may provide improved predictive models for developmental toxicity pathways.

Computational and in silico approaches
In parallel with advances in experimental models, computational toxicology has emerged as a critical component of NAM-based safety assessment. In silico models utilize chemical structure information, biological data, and computational algorithms to predict potential toxicological outcomes without the need for laboratory experimentation. These models are particularly valuable for prioritizing chemicals for further testing and for supporting hazard identification within regulatory frameworks.
One widely used computational approach is quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling, which predicts biological activity based on chemical structure and physicochemical properties. QSAR models can identify structural features associated with developmental toxicity and provide rapid screening tools for evaluating large chemical libraries. Similarly, read-across methodologies allow toxicological data from well-characterized substances to be extrapolated to structurally related compounds, thereby reducing the need for additional experimental testing.
Another important computational tool in toxicology is physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, which describes the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of chemicals within the human body. PBPK models can be integrated with in vitro toxicity data to estimate internal exposure levels and to perform in vitro–to–in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE), thereby improving the interpretation of experimental results within a human health risk assessment context.
Recent advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence have further enhanced the predictive capabilities of computational toxicology. These approaches can integrate large datasets derived from chemical structures, biological pathways, and high-throughput screening assays to generate predictive models capable of identifying potential developmental toxicants with increasing accuracy.

Omics technologies and mechanistic profiling
The integration of omics technologies—including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics—has significantly expanded the mechanistic resolution of toxicological investigations. These approaches enable comprehensive characterization of molecular perturbations induced by chemical exposure and provide valuable insights into the biological pathways involved in developmental toxicity.
For example, transcriptomic profiling can identify gene expression signatures associated with chemical-induced disruption of developmental pathways, while proteomic and metabolomic analyses can reveal downstream biochemical alterations linked to toxicological outcomes. The integration of multi-omics datasets with computational modeling approaches allows researchers to construct detailed mechanistic frameworks describing the progression from molecular initiating events to adverse developmental outcomes.
Importantly, omics-based analyses also support the identification of potential biomarkers of developmental toxicity, which may facilitate earlier detection of adverse effects and improve the predictive performance of NAM-based testing strategies.

Adverse outcome pathways and integrated testing strategies
A key conceptual framework that supports the integration of NAMs into regulatory toxicology is the Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) concept. AOPs describe the sequence of biological events linking a molecular initiating event (MIE) triggered by chemical exposure to a measurable adverse outcome at the organism or population level. This framework provides a structured approach for organizing mechanistic knowledge and linking experimental data to regulatory endpoints.
In the context of developmental and reproductive toxicity, AOP networks have been developed to map complex biological pathways involved in processes such as endocrine disruption, neurodevelopmental impairment, and reproductive dysfunction. These networks integrate diverse data sources and enable the systematic evaluation of mechanistic evidence supporting toxicity predictions.
The AOP framework also facilitates the development of Integrated Approaches to Testing and Assessment (IATA), which combine multiple lines of evidence—including in vitro assays, computational models, and existing toxicological data—within a structured decision-making framework. IATA strategies enable regulators to evaluate chemical safety using a weight-of-evidence approach that integrates mechanistic data with exposure information.

Current regulatory acceptance of NAMs
Although NAM technologies have advanced rapidly, their integration into regulatory safety assessment remains an evolving process. Regulatory agencies worldwide increasingly recognize the potential value of NAMs for improving toxicological evaluations and reducing reliance on animal testing. For example, the United States Environmental Protection Agency has articulated a strategic vision for incorporating NAMs into chemical safety assessments and promoting the transition toward non-animal testing approaches.
Within the pharmaceutical sector, recent revisions to international regulatory guidelines have acknowledged that NAM-derived data may contribute to safety assessment within a weight-of-evidence framework. However, most regulatory authorities currently consider NAMs primarily as supporting tools rather than direct replacements for animal studies, particularly in the context of complex endpoints such as developmental and reproductive toxicity.
The broader regulatory acceptance of NAMs will likely depend on continued improvements in assay validation, demonstration of predictive reliability, and the development of standardized frameworks for integrating multiple evidence streams into regulatory decision-making processes.

Summary of NAM categories and regulatory status
The expanding portfolio of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) reflects the multidisciplinary transformation occurring in modern toxicology. These approaches integrate advances in cell biology, bioengineering, computational modeling, and systems biology to generate mechanistically informative data relevant to developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART). Although many NAM technologies were initially developed for early screening and research applications, their role in regulatory toxicology is rapidly evolving as validation efforts, international collaborations, and mechanistic frameworks such as adverse outcome pathways (AOPs) and integrated approaches to testing and assessment (IATA) mature.
Different NAM categories contribute distinct types of evidence for hazard identification, mechanistic understanding, and exposure-based risk assessment. For example, in vitro cellular models enable the investigation of key developmental processes, while computational toxicology tools support chemical prioritization and prediction of toxicological outcomes. In parallel, emerging bioengineered systems such as organoids and microphysiological platforms offer opportunities to model tissue-level responses with improved human relevance. When integrated through AOP-informed IATA frameworks, these complementary data streams can support weight-of-evidence–based regulatory decision-making.

Remaining scientific and implementation challenges
Despite the significant promise of NAM-based approaches, several challenges must be addressed before these methods can be widely adopted in regulatory DART assessment. One major challenge is the complexity of developmental biology, which involves intricate interactions among multiple tissues, signaling pathways, and temporal processes that are difficult to replicate fully in simplified experimental systems.
Another critical issue is the need for robust validation frameworks capable of demonstrating the predictive reliability of NAMs across diverse chemical classes and exposure scenarios. Regulatory acceptance requires confidence that alternative methods can accurately identify developmental toxicants while minimizing false positive and false negative outcomes. Consequently, validation efforts often require extensive reference datasets and collaborative international evaluation initiatives.
Furthermore, effective integration of diverse NAM datasets into regulatory decision-making requires standardized frameworks for evidence integration and uncertainty evaluation. Continued collaboration among scientists, regulators, and industry stakeholders will therefore be essential to establish consensus methodologies and to accelerate the responsible adoption of NAMs in developmental and reproductive toxicology.
While New Approach Methodologies offer promising opportunities to modernize developmental and reproductive toxicity testing, their broader regulatory implementation requires careful consideration of validation frameworks, evidentiary standards, and decision-making paradigms. The following section therefore examines the regulatory challenges associated with the adoption of NAM-based approaches in safety assessment.

Regulatory challenges and validation paradigms

Regulatory risk aversion in DART assessment
Regulatory decision-making in developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) assessment has historically been characterized by a high degree of caution due to the severe and often irreversible consequences associated with adverse developmental outcomes. As a result, regulatory frameworks have traditionally relied on established animal testing paradigms that are perceived as providing a conservative basis for hazard identification and risk assessment. This inherent regulatory risk aversion has slowed the adoption of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs), despite growing scientific evidence supporting their potential utility in toxicological evaluation.
A key factor contributing to this cautious approach is the requirement that regulatory decisions maintain a high level of protection for human health and the environment. Consequently, regulatory authorities often require extensive evidence demonstrating that alternative methods provide predictive performance comparable to, or better than, traditional animal studies before considering their adoption. This evidentiary threshold can create significant barriers for emerging methodologies that have not yet accumulated extensive validation data across diverse chemical classes and exposure scenarios.
Furthermore, regulatory frameworks governing chemical safety assessments were largely developed at a time when animal testing represented the primary available methodology. Many existing legislative instruments explicitly specify particular animal-based test guidelines, which can limit the flexibility needed to incorporate innovative non-animal approaches. As a result, even when NAM-based evidence is scientifically compelling, regulatory implementation may remain constrained by existing legal requirements and institutional practices.

Validation paradigms for NAMs
Validation represents a central requirement for the regulatory acceptance of any new toxicological testing methodology. Traditional validation frameworks typically involve multi-laboratory ring trials designed to demonstrate the reproducibility, reliability, and predictive capacity of a method relative to established reference standards. However, applying these conventional validation paradigms to NAMs can be challenging because many NAM approaches generate mechanistic data rather than directly measuring apical toxicological outcomes.
International organizations such as the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) have developed guidance documents to support the validation and regulatory acceptance of alternative testing methods. These frameworks emphasize several key criteria, including reproducibility across laboratories, biological relevance of the measured endpoints, and the ability of the method to accurately predict adverse outcomes relevant to regulatory decision-making. Nevertheless, the diversity of NAM technologies—including in vitro assays, computational models, and systems biology approaches—means that a single standardized validation pathway may not be appropriate for all methodologies.
To address these challenges, the concept of fit-for-purpose validation has gained increasing attention in regulatory toxicology. Under this approach, the validation requirements for a given method are defined according to its intended regulatory application rather than applying a uniform validation standard across all methodologies. For example, a NAM used for early chemical prioritization may require a different level of validation than a method intended to replace an established regulatory test guideline. This flexible approach may facilitate more efficient integration of NAMs into regulatory assessment frameworks while maintaining scientific rigor.

Sector-specific differences in regulatory acceptance
The regulatory acceptance of NAMs varies considerably across industrial sectors due to differences in legislative frameworks, risk assessment paradigms, and historical testing practices. In the pharmaceutical sector, regulatory requirements for reproductive toxicity testing remain largely anchored in traditional animal studies due to the high stakes associated with drug safety evaluation. However, mechanistic data derived from NAM approaches are increasingly used to support weight-of-evidence assessments and to inform study design during early drug development.
In contrast, the cosmetics sector has experienced more rapid adoption of NAMs following legislative bans on animal testing in several jurisdictions. For example, the European Union’s prohibition of animal testing for cosmetic ingredients has stimulated substantial investment in alternative methods and integrated testing strategies capable of supporting regulatory decision-making without the use of animal models. These regulatory initiatives have played an important role in accelerating methodological innovation and international collaboration in the development of NAM-based testing approaches.
Chemical regulation frameworks such as REACH represent an intermediate scenario in which NAMs are increasingly encouraged but are often used in combination with traditional toxicological data. In these contexts, alternative methods are frequently applied within integrated testing strategies that combine computational predictions, in vitro assays, and existing toxicological information to support hazard identification and risk assessment.

Weight-of-evidence and uncertainty integration
A critical challenge in integrating NAM-derived data into regulatory risk assessment involves the systematic evaluation of multiple evidence streams generated by diverse methodologies. In this context, weight-of-evidence (WoE) frameworks provide a structured approach for integrating experimental, computational, and observational data to support regulatory conclusions regarding chemical safety.
Within WoE frameworks, each line of evidence is evaluated according to predefined criteria such as data quality, biological plausibility, and consistency across independent studies. The integration of these evidence streams allows regulators to evaluate the overall strength of scientific evidence supporting a particular hazard hypothesis. This approach is particularly relevant for NAM-based assessments, where mechanistic data derived from different experimental platforms must be combined to infer potential adverse outcomes.
However, WoE integration also introduces new challenges related to uncertainty characterization and data interpretation. Because NAM datasets may vary widely in terms of experimental design, biological complexity, and measurement endpoints, regulators must carefully evaluate the confidence associated with each line of evidence. The development of transparent and standardized frameworks for uncertainty analysis therefore represents an important priority for advancing the regulatory acceptance of NAM-based testing strategies.

International harmonization efforts
International harmonization plays a critical role in facilitating the regulatory acceptance of new toxicological methodologies. Organizations such as the OECD, the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH), and various national regulatory agencies collaborate to develop guidance documents, validation frameworks, and test guidelines that support the adoption of innovative testing strategies. These collaborative initiatives aim to ensure that toxicological data generated using NAM approaches are recognized and accepted across multiple regulatory jurisdictions.
One of the most significant developments in this area has been the establishment of Integrated Approaches to Testing and Assessment (IATA) frameworks. IATA strategies integrate diverse sources of information—including in vitro assays, computational predictions, and existing toxicological data—to support hazard identification and risk assessment in a structured decision-making framework. These approaches are designed to improve efficiency and reduce reliance on animal testing while maintaining high standards of scientific rigor in regulatory evaluations.
In addition, international cooperation initiatives such as the International Cooperation on Alternative Test Methods (ICATM) facilitate collaboration among validation organizations worldwide, including the European Union Reference Laboratory for Alternatives to Animal Testing (EURL-ECVAM), the U.S. Interagency Coordinating Committee on the Validation of Alternative Methods (ICCVAM), and related institutions in Asia and other regions. These partnerships promote data sharing, methodological standardization, and coordinated evaluation of emerging testing approaches.

Regulatory barriers and enabling factors
Despite substantial progress in the development of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs), several regulatory and scientific barriers continue to limit their widespread implementation in developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) assessment. One of the most significant challenges is the lack of clearly defined regulatory acceptance criteria for many NAM-based approaches. In several regulatory frameworks, authorities encourage the use of NAM-derived evidence to support hazard identification or prioritization; however, traditional animal-based studies are often still required to confirm safety conclusions. This dual requirement can limit the practical regulatory impact of alternative methods and reduce incentives for their broader adoption.
Another important barrier relates to the integration of diverse datasets generated by NAM platforms. Differences in biological models, experimental conditions, and analytical approaches can complicate the interpretation of results and hinder the translation of mechanistic findings into regulatory decision-making. Furthermore, regulatory authorities frequently require robust historical datasets and well-characterized reference chemical libraries to evaluate the predictive performance and reliability of new methodologies. For many emerging NAM technologies, such datasets remain limited, slowing the validation and acceptance process.
In parallel with these challenges, several enabling factors are accelerating the transition toward NAM-based toxicological assessment. Advances in systems biology, computational toxicology, high-throughput screening technologies, and mechanistic pathway analysis are improving the ability to link molecular perturbations to adverse developmental outcomes. In addition, increasing collaboration among regulatory agencies, academic researchers, and industry stakeholders is supporting the development of internationally harmonized validation frameworks and decision-support tools for regulatory toxicology.

Global initiatives driving the transition to NAM-based DART testing

OECD frameworks and initiatives
The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has played a central role in advancing the development and regulatory implementation of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) in toxicology. Through its internationally recognized Test Guidelines Programme, the OECD has historically provided standardized protocols for toxicological testing that facilitate regulatory harmonization and mutual acceptance of data among member countries. In recent years, the organization has expanded its focus toward supporting alternative testing strategies that reduce reliance on animal experimentation while maintaining high standards of scientific rigor in chemical safety assessment.
One of the most influential OECD initiatives supporting the integration of NAMs is the development of Integrated Approaches to Testing and Assessment (IATA) frameworks. IATA strategies combine multiple sources of information—including in vitro assays, computational modeling, exposure data, and existing toxicological evidence—within a structured decision-making framework. These approaches allow regulators to evaluate chemical hazards using weight-of-evidence principles while minimizing unnecessary animal testing.
In addition, the OECD has been instrumental in promoting the Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) framework, which provides a mechanistic structure linking molecular initiating events to adverse biological outcomes relevant to regulatory risk assessment. The OECD-supported AOP Knowledge Base facilitates the collaborative development and evaluation of AOP networks and serves as a repository for mechanistic information that can support the interpretation of NAM-derived data. By providing internationally harmonized frameworks for data interpretation and integration, OECD initiatives are helping to establish the scientific and regulatory foundations necessary for broader adoption of NAM-based toxicological assessments.

ICH Initiatives in pharmaceutical safety assessment
Within the pharmaceutical sector, the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) has played a key role in establishing globally harmonized safety assessment guidelines. Although traditional animal studies remain central to regulatory evaluation of reproductive toxicity in drug development, recent ICH initiatives increasingly acknowledge the potential value of mechanistic and alternative data sources in supporting safety assessments.
The revision of the ICH S5 guideline (S5(R3)) represents a significant development in this context. The updated guideline introduces greater flexibility in the design and interpretation of reproductive toxicity studies and recognizes that mechanistic data generated from in vitro systems or computational models may contribute to the overall weight-of-evidence evaluation during drug development. This shift reflects broader recognition within the pharmaceutical regulatory community that advances in modern toxicological science can enhance the predictive capacity of safety assessment strategies.
Furthermore, the pharmaceutical industry has invested heavily in the development of human-relevant experimental models and high-throughput screening technologies that support early identification of potential reproductive hazards during drug discovery. These initiatives aim to improve the efficiency of pharmaceutical development pipelines while reducing reliance on extensive animal testing programs.

U.S. regulatory programs supporting NAMs
Regulatory agencies in the United States have implemented several strategic initiatives designed to accelerate the development and adoption of alternative testing methodologies. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has been particularly active in promoting the use of NAMs through programs aimed at modernizing chemical safety assessment and expanding the application of computational toxicology tools.
One notable initiative is the EPA’s Strategic Plan to Promote the Development and Implementation of Alternative Test Methods, which outlines a long-term vision for reducing reliance on animal testing in regulatory evaluations. This strategy emphasizes the use of high-throughput screening technologies, computational modeling approaches, and integrated testing frameworks to enhance the efficiency and scientific basis of chemical risk assessment.
Similarly, the U.S. National Institutes of Health and the National Toxicology Program have supported major research programs focused on advancing alternative testing methods, including the development of human cell-based assays and organ-on-chip technologies. These initiatives have generated extensive datasets that support the evaluation of chemical effects on biological pathways relevant to developmental and reproductive toxicity.

European union strategies
The European Union has emerged as a global leader in promoting the development and regulatory acceptance of NAMs. European policy initiatives have increasingly emphasized the importance of transitioning toward human-relevant testing approaches that reduce animal use while maintaining robust chemical safety assessments. One of the most significant policy frameworks in this area is the European Chemicals Strategy for Sustainability, which aims to modernize chemical risk assessment through increased use of innovative scientific methods and digital technologies.
Within the European regulatory system, agencies such as the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) actively support the development and evaluation of alternative testing approaches. In addition, the European Union Reference Laboratory for Alternatives to Animal Testing (EURL-ECVAM) plays a critical role in validating and promoting new methodologies that can contribute to regulatory decision-making.
European regulatory initiatives have also stimulated substantial investment in collaborative research programs aimed at developing advanced toxicological models. Projects funded under the Horizon Europe research framework support interdisciplinary research in systems toxicology, computational modeling, and advanced in vitro methodologies, further strengthening Europe’s leadership in NAM innovation.

International collaboration and strategic roadmaps
The transition toward NAM-based toxicological assessment is inherently a global challenge that requires coordinated collaboration among regulatory agencies, research institutions, and industry stakeholders. Several international initiatives have therefore been established to facilitate cooperation and knowledge exchange in the development and validation of alternative testing strategies.
One prominent example is the International Cooperation on Alternative Test Methods (ICATM), which brings together validation bodies from multiple regions to coordinate evaluation efforts and promote international acceptance of alternative testing approaches. By sharing data, harmonizing validation procedures, and conducting joint scientific assessments, ICATM partners aim to accelerate the regulatory adoption of innovative toxicological methodologies.
In addition to institutional collaborations, several international strategic roadmaps have outlined long-term visions for the modernization of toxicological testing. These roadmaps emphasize the integration of mechanistic toxicology, systems biology, computational modeling, and high-throughput screening technologies to create predictive frameworks capable of evaluating chemical safety with greater efficiency and human relevance. Such collaborative initiatives are expected to play a pivotal role in shaping the future landscape of developmental and reproductive toxicity testing.
Collectively, these global initiatives demonstrate a growing international commitment to modernizing toxicological assessment through the integration of innovative scientific approaches and collaborative regulatory frameworks. Building upon these developments, the following section explores future directions for advancing human-relevant developmental and reproductive toxicity testing.

Future directions for human-relevant developmental and reproductive toxicity testing

Transition toward predictive regulatory toxicology
The field of developmental and reproductive toxicology is undergoing a gradual transformation from traditional hazard identification toward predictive, mechanism-based toxicology. Advances in molecular biology, computational modeling, and systems toxicology have enabled researchers to investigate the biological pathways underlying chemical toxicity with increasing precision. This transition is expected to support the development of predictive frameworks capable of identifying potential reproductive hazards earlier in the chemical evaluation process and with greater relevance to human biology.
Predictive toxicology emphasizes the identification of early molecular perturbations that may ultimately lead to adverse developmental outcomes. By integrating mechanistic data obtained from in vitro models, high-throughput screening platforms, and computational tools, researchers can construct predictive models capable of evaluating chemical effects on critical developmental pathways. Such approaches have the potential to significantly improve the efficiency of safety assessment while reducing reliance on large-scale animal testing programs.
Moreover, predictive toxicology frameworks may enable regulators to prioritize chemicals for further evaluation based on biological activity and exposure potential. This targeted approach to risk assessment could improve regulatory decision-making by focusing resources on substances that present the greatest potential risk to human health.

Human-relevant experimental models
One of the most promising developments in modern toxicology is the rapid advancement of human-relevant experimental models capable of replicating key aspects of human physiology and development. Technologies such as human stem cell–derived organoids and microphysiological systems are increasingly used to model complex biological processes that were previously difficult to investigate using conventional experimental approaches.
Human organoid models derived from pluripotent stem cells have demonstrated particular promise for studying developmental toxicity mechanisms. These systems can mimic important structural and functional features of developing tissues, including neural, cardiac, hepatic, and reproductive systems. As a result, organoids provide valuable platforms for investigating chemical effects on tissue development, cellular differentiation, and signaling pathways associated with embryogenesis.
Similarly, organ-on-chip technologies integrate living cells with microfluidic devices that simulate aspects of human tissue microenvironments. These systems allow dynamic monitoring of cellular responses under physiologically relevant conditions and enable the study of complex interactions among multiple cell types. As these technologies continue to evolve, they may provide increasingly accurate models for evaluating the developmental toxicity potential of environmental chemicals and pharmaceuticals.

Artificial intelligence and data integration
The rapid expansion of toxicological datasets generated by high-throughput screening assays, omics technologies, and computational modeling has created new opportunities for applying artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning approaches to chemical safety assessment. These data-driven methods can analyze complex datasets to identify patterns and relationships that may not be readily apparent through conventional statistical analysis.
Machine learning algorithms have been successfully applied to predict chemical toxicity based on structural features, biological pathway interactions, and experimental assay results. In the context of developmental and reproductive toxicology, AI models can integrate diverse datasets to identify potential developmental toxicants and predict adverse outcomes associated with chemical exposure.
In addition, AI-driven data integration platforms may facilitate the interpretation of large multi-omics datasets generated during mechanistic toxicology studies. By combining transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic information, these systems can generate comprehensive molecular profiles describing how chemicals perturb biological pathways relevant to development and reproduction. Such integrated analyses have the potential to enhance mechanistic understanding and improve the predictive accuracy of NAM-based toxicological assessments.

Strengthening mechanistic frameworks and regulatory capacity
The continued development of mechanistic toxicology frameworks will play a critical role in advancing the regulatory implementation of NAM-based testing strategies. Concepts such as Adverse Outcome Pathways (AOPs) provide structured frameworks that link molecular initiating events to adverse biological outcomes through a sequence of key biological events. As AOP networks become more comprehensive and better validated, they may serve as central tools for integrating data derived from diverse experimental and computational methodologies.
In parallel with scientific advancements, strengthening regulatory capacity will be essential for facilitating the transition toward modern toxicological assessment approaches. Regulatory agencies must develop expertise in evaluating complex datasets generated by NAM technologies and establish transparent frameworks for integrating mechanistic evidence into decision-making processes. Training programs, collaborative research initiatives, and international regulatory partnerships will therefore play an important role in building the scientific capacity required to implement innovative testing strategies effectively.

Vision for post-2030 regulatory toxicology
Looking toward the future, many experts envision a toxicological testing paradigm that relies far less on traditional animal studies and instead emphasizes integrated, human-relevant testing strategies supported by advanced computational tools. In such a framework, chemical safety assessment would combine multiple sources of information—including in vitro assays, computational modeling, mechanistic pathway analysis, and exposure data—within predictive frameworks capable of evaluating toxicity with improved accuracy and efficiency.
This future regulatory paradigm would likely involve the routine application of integrated testing strategies that combine NAM-derived evidence with exposure modeling and epidemiological data to support risk assessment. Advances in digital technologies and data analytics may further enable real-time integration of toxicological information, allowing regulators to evaluate chemical safety more dynamically and proactively.
Although significant scientific and regulatory challenges remain, ongoing research and international collaboration continue to advance the development of predictive toxicology frameworks that prioritize human relevance and mechanistic understanding. These efforts are expected to play a transformative role in shaping the next generation of developmental and reproductive toxicity testing.
The evolution of developmental and reproductive toxicity assessment reflects a broader transformation occurring within toxicological science, where traditional animal-based testing strategies are increasingly complemented by innovative methodologies that provide deeper mechanistic insight and improved relevance to human health. The concluding section synthesizes these developments and outlines key considerations for the future integration of New Approach Methodologies into regulatory toxicology.

Conclusion
Developmental and reproductive toxicity (DART) assessment has traditionally relied on animal-based testing frameworks that have played a critical role in safeguarding human health. However, these approaches are associated with important limitations, including interspecies differences, high resource requirements, and limited mechanistic insight. Advances in New Approach Methodologies (NAMs)—including in vitro models, computational toxicology, and mechanistic pathway analysis—are transforming toxicological science by enabling more human-relevant and efficient safety assessment strategies.
Recent progress in stem cell–derived models, organoid technologies, omics-based profiling, and artificial intelligence has expanded the capacity to investigate developmental toxicity mechanisms and predict potential hazards earlier in the chemical evaluation process. At the same time, international initiatives led by regulatory organizations are promoting the integration of NAMs within weight-of-evidence and integrated testing frameworks. Although challenges related to validation, regulatory acceptance, and data integration remain, continued scientific innovation and global collaboration are expected to accelerate the transition toward predictive, human-relevant toxicology. Ultimately, the responsible incorporation of NAMs into regulatory assessment has the potential to improve chemical safety evaluation while reducing reliance on traditional animal testing.
