Tailoring the Surgical Approach to Chiari I Malformation with Intraoperative Ultrasounds: Advantages, Limitations, and Controversies
Abstract
Introduction
Decompression of posterior cranial fossa remains the mainstay of surgical treatment of Chiari malformation type I, though several surgical maneuvers have been differently combined to bony decompression aiming to increase the efficacy of surgery. In this context, intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) claims to offer real-time feedback of adequacy of surgical decompression, thus accordingly tailoring the aggressiveness of surgery. The present review aims to highlight benefits, limitations, and areas in need of further investigation.
Materials and Methods
A comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar was performed for studies published in English in the last 30 years. The following keywords, including MeSH terms, were used to retrieve eligible papers: “Chiari Malformation Type I,” “posterior fossa decompression,” and “ultrasonography.”

A total of 1,598 papers were screened, and 1,531 records were excluded through title and abstract screening; 65 studies were considered relevant to our research question and were assessed for eligibility. Finally, 23 articles were included in the review.
Conclusions
IOUS has been frequently used to evaluate the adequacy of surgical decompression, though this qualitative assessment remains subjective with obvious inter-operator variability. On the other side, a quantitative assessment has been more rarely used through the literature, as this approach is difficult to reproduce. Despite the obvious advantages of IOUS, further investigation is required to standardize this approach.

Introduction
The heterogenous etiology of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is nowadays recognized and reflects into the surgical paradigm to treat this condition. Etiological treatment is reserved to cases with fairly recognized secondary etiology. Similarly, when hydrocephalus is concomitant, it is widely accepted to treat first hydrocephalus. In other cases, foramen magnum decompression remains the mainstay of treatment.
However, posterior fossa decompression may combine different surgical maneuvers, including bony decompression, dural splitting or scoring, expansive duraplasty, and finally, arachnoid opening with coagulation or aspiration or resection of the cerebellar tonsils. These technical differences obviously may affect the analysis of results but the main factor consists of opening the dura, as well reflected by a large number of meta-analyses addressing the relative merits of bone-only decompression of the posterior fossa and decompression with duraplasty.
Almost invariably, the comparison between the two techniques shows that the bony decompression alone has a very low rate of complications but a lower success rate; conversely, the duraplasty ensures better results but with a higher rate of complications, in particular related to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. On these grounds, controversy regarding the best operative management of CMI still exists, despite the efforts to reach a consensus and draw guidelines.
The use of intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) has been proposed to tailor the surgical approach of CMI, by offering immediate functional feedback and guiding intraoperative decision-making, thus limiting unnecessary surgical steps. Nevertheless, limited data and a lack of standardized assessment protocols currently constrain its broader application. We present a comprehensive analysis of the use of IOUS in CMI surgery, highlighting its benefits, limitations, and areas in need of further investigation.

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy and Selection Process
The systematic review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar was performed for studies published in English in the last 30 years. The following keywords, including MeSH terms, were used to retrieve eligible papers: “Chiari Malformation Type I,” “posterior fossa decompression,” “ultrasonography.”
After searching the three databases, all results were collected. Duplicates were removed using Rayyan software. Three authors (F.Z., R.L.R., and I.T.) independently screened titles and abstracts. Additional publications were retrieved from the reference list of relevant articles. Three reviewers (F.Z., R.L.R., and I.T.) independently screened the full texts of selected articles, with a strong inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s kappa 0.8 agreement); a senior author (P.F.) resolved discrepancies. PRISMA flowchart.

Eligibility Criteria
To meet the inclusion criteria, articles had to: (1) include patients with CMI and (2) report on the use of IOUS. Exclusion criteria were: (1) gray literature (i.e., conference abstracts, dissertations) and (2) animal-based studies.

Data Collection and Criteria Appraisal
Data extraction was performed from the text, tables, and figures of the included articles using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) framework. The study population (P) included patients affected by CMI. The intervention (I) involved the use of IOUS; where applicable, the comparison group (C) included CMI patients who underwent surgery without the use of IOUS. The primary outcome (O) to be extracted among studies was postoperative clinical improvement and a need for reoperation.
Additional data extracted included title, year of publication, study design, demographics of the study population, and main findings. Three investigators (F.Z., R.L.R., and I.T.) conducted the data extraction, organizing the information into structured tables within Excel spreadsheets to facilitate descriptive analysis of each study. Subsequently, a senior investigator (P.F.) independently reviewed and double-checked the extracted data from all articles. Any discrepancies between the investigators were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data Synthesis
A narrative synthesis was performed. A quantitative meta-analysis was not conducted due to substantial heterogeneity among the included studies due to a lack of quantitative data and methodological variability in IOUS assessment which precluded meaningful pooling of results.

Results
The search of the literature yielded a total of 3,189 results from three databases (PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholars). Duplicate records were then removed (n = 1,591). A total of 1,598 papers were screened, and 1,533 records were excluded through title and abstract screening; 65 studies were considered relevant to our research question and were assessed for eligibility (online suppl. File. 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000550058). Upon full-text review, 23 articles were included in the review which reported on the use of IOUS in the surgical management of CMI patients, of which 8 were cohort studies, 9 cross-sectional studies, 4 case series, and 2 case reports. The publication date range of the included studies spans from 1996 to 2024.

Demographic Data
The included studies collectively examined a heterogeneous cohort comprising 2003 patients, with the mean age ranging between 2 months and 77 years. In particular, 12 were exclusively pediatric, 5 included mixed-age populations, and 6 focused on adult cohorts. Pediatric studies exclusively used qualitative IOUS assessments, as a tool to minimize duraplasty when intraoperative CSF flow appeared adequate. In contrast, quantitative parameters, such as CSF flow velocity and cisternal volume, were proposed in some adult and mixed-age studies to guide surgical decisions. A direct comparative analysis between pediatric and adult populations is not feasible due to the considerable heterogeneity in study design and reported data.
Sample sizes ranged significantly, from as few as single case report to 315 participants. Several studies focused specifically on pediatric populations, while others included mixed or adult-dominant samples. Syringomyelia was reported in 51% of the patients (n = 1,032). Follow-up duration, when reported, varied from 6 to 135 months, indicating variable long-term assessment. Detailed data are reported in online supplementary file 2.

IOUS: Assessment, Goals, and Related Outcomes
Across all studies, ultrasound was brought in after suboccipital craniectomy, in order to decide whether to continue with more invasive surgical steps, namely, increase in craniectomy size, C1 laminectomy (if not performed as primary procedure), C2 laminectomy, dural opening and duraplasty, and cerebellar tonsillar shrinkage. IOUS was employed to visualize the following parameters: (a) pulsations of the cerebellar tonsils, (b) CSF flow dynamics across the craniocervical junction (CCJ), (c) subarachnoid spaces ventral and dorsal to the cerebellum and tonsils. In nearly all studies, the aforementioned parameters were assessed qualitatively based on surgeon experience. Only 4 studies reported quantitative use of IOUS, assessing volumetric changes in the cisterna magna, CSF flow velocity at the CCJ, and amount of tonsillar descent.
The use of IOUS was consistently associated with low complication rates and favorable clinical and radiological outcomes. Only in 13 studies, patients reported postoperative complications, including de novo syrinx, transient worsening of a preexistent syrinx, occipital neuralgia, posterior fossa epidural hematoma, CSF leak, surgical site infection, and meningitis.
In terms of clinical outcomes, nearly all studies reported clinical improvement following IOUS-guided surgery. Radiological improvement, particularly in the resolution of syringomyelia, was also a key finding, though some authors reserved an IOUS-based approach to CMI without syringomyelia. Increase in size and/or appearance of a de novo syrinx was reported only in 3 studies. Reoperation rates were generally low among the studies, with Salomao et al. reporting the higher rate (20%).
To conclude, despite differing approaches, the outcomes were generally favorable across all age groups, thus leading to optimistic conclusions on the role of IOUS. However, a meta-analysis is biased by the heterogeneity of data, so that the present review did not aim to draw statistical comparisons but rather to describe trends and methodological differences across the included literature. Furthermore, the definition of surgical success/failure was largely inconsistent through the papers, and this represents a major bias for defining the real impact of IOUS.

Discussion
CMI management remains one of the most debated topics in pediatric neurosurgery due to the multiple options available coupled with surgeons’ preferences and fear of complications. Because no preoperative imaging modality can fully predict surgical response, a flexible, case-by-case approach is recommended; this might eliminate any needless steps and their associated risks. In this context, IOUS has gained increasing attention as a noninvasive guide that permits the neurosurgeon to tailor operative steps according to patient-specific variables on the basis of real-time anatomical and physiological measurements. IOUS allows surgeons to dynamically assess CSF flow, tonsillar pulsatility, and subarachnoid spaces ventral and dorsal to the brainstem. However, there are several controversial issues to face. First, as highlighted by our review, to date only four studies have provided quantitative assessment of IOUS findings, allowing for an objective evaluation of the adequacy of PFD. All other authors describe the adequacy of decompression based on the surgeon’s impression of cerebellar tonsillar position or CSF pulsation at the CCJ.
Another critical aspect of IOUS assessment is the impossibility to assess the obtained “gain” as IOUS may not be acquired prior to bone decompression. The gain may be assessed by comparing IOUS with preoperative MRI, with obvious limitations. On the other hand, acquiring IOUS after bone decompression and after dural scoring or splitting may provide an idea of the partial “gain” of this maneuver. In the personal experience, we assess the gain comparing IOUS after bone decompression and after the removal of the epidural “ring” at the bulbo-cervical junction represented by a thickened posterior atlanto-occipital membrane remnant (Fig. 2). Despite these limitations, the IOUS and the ultrasound modification after surgical maneuvers may possibly stratify CMI cases and guide the surgical treatment.

IOUS Qualitative Assessment
Qualitative assessment of decompression using IOUS is more widely used to guide the decision-making in CMI surgery. Several studies have demonstrated its efficacy in visualizing cerebellar tonsillar pulsations, CSF flow through the CCJ, and subarachnoid space around the brainstem. Yeh et al. performed durotomy and tonsillar coagulation in patients with abnormal IOUS findings such as inadequate CSF volume or piston-like tonsillar motion, whereas those with satisfactory flow after bone-only decompression were managed conservatively. At a mean 20-month follow-up, 95% of 124 patients had favorable outcomes, and none of the 40 patients treated with bony decompression alone required reoperation. Narenthiram et al. retrospectively evaluated 19 pediatric patients (11 with syringomyelia) using IOUS during PFD: a subjective assessment was made on whether there was adequate cerebellar tonsillar and/or CSF pulsation following suboccipital craniectomy. If there was adequate pulsation, the dura was not opened. Based on IOUS, 8 patients underwent dural decompression and 11 patients bony decompression only. Only 2 patients, one from each group, required reoperation. Syringomyelia improved more frequently in the IOUS group (86% versus 64%), though the result does not reach statistical significance. Similar IOUS-guided decision-making was reported in other series, further highlighting the use of IOUS findings in avoiding unnecessary maneuvers, while not compromising on the outcome.
Despite these positive experiences, some authors have questioned the utility of IOUS in most severe cases, either with syringomyelia or with severe tonsillar herniation. Indeed, the evolution or even the appearance of syringomyelia after posterior fossa decompression despite adequate surgical decompression suggests that a deeper understanding of its pathophysiology is required (Fig. 3).

Despite adequate bony decompression based on IOUS findings (d) with resolution of symptomatology, postoperative MR showed appearance of cervicothoracic syringomyelia (e).
Indeed, IOUS has proved particularly effective in guiding surgery for patients without syringomyelia, while a more aggressive approach has been advocated in patients with syringomyelia, regardless of IOUS assessment. Shamji et al. stratified their CMI patients into three groups based on MRI and IOUS findings. In patients without syringomyelia and borderline cisterna magna volume, IOUS was used to determine the necessity of additional intradural procedures. If normal CSF flow and subarachnoid patency were observed, surgery was limited to bone decompression. If not, the procedure was extended to include duraplasty and arachnoid lysis. No significant difference in clinical outcome was observed between groups.
This somehow reflects the consensus statements of the international meeting on CMI held in Milan, Italy, in 2019. Indeed, bony decompression is suggested in children without syringomyelia (and without severe symptoms), taking into account a possible risk of symptom recurrence. On the other hand, duraplasty is preferable when syringomyelia (and marked symptoms) is present, taking into account a certain risk of complications. The agreement on this is high but not complete (80%) because some panelists prefer to perform the most radical treatment as the first step (duraplasty with or without tonsil coagulation) to avoid reoperations and to favor a quick resolution of syringomyelia, while others do not accept the surgical risk of duraplasty and acknowledge the low improvement provided by bony decompression alone.
The only prospective study available on this topic (82 adolescents, 40 treated by bony decompression and 42 by duraplasty) shows a similar clinical outcome and syrinx improvement between the two techniques, with longer operation times and hospital stay and higher risk of complication by duraplasty. The severity of tonsillar herniation has been also proposed as a factor limiting the prognostic utility of IOUS. McGirt et al. found IOUS as a good predictor of outcome in children with tonsillar herniation rostral to the lower border of the C1 lamina. However, in children with tonsillar herniation extending caudal to the C1 lamina, despite intraoperative ultrasonographic evidence of adequate hindbrain decompression, suboccipital bone decompression alone compared with decompression and duraplasty was associated with a twofold increase in the risk of symptom recurrence, suggesting that in patients with moderate and severe tonsillar herniation, subjective interpretation of intraoperative ultrasonography may not be effective in selecting patients in whom decompression alone is sufficient. A shortcoming is that other anatomical features such as the presence of spinal cord syrinx were not evaluated in the analysis.
In turn, the limitations of IOUS in patients with syringomyelia and severe tonsillar herniation question the utility of IOUS. Indeed, we know that in pediatric cases without syringomyelia and with mild tonsillar herniation, bony decompression is effective and safe.

IOUS Quantitative Assessment
While surgeons’ impression remains indispensable, quantitative and objective metrics obtained via IOUS could represent a more reliable factor that may increase reproducibility and support evidence-based decisions. Milhorat et al. were among the first to propose quantifiable intraoperative color Doppler ultrasound (CDU) criteria for adequate decompression including a retrocerebellar space volume of 8–10 cm3 and optimal CSF flow at the CCJ as defined by: (a) a peak velocity of 3–5 cm/s, (b) bidirectional movement, and (c) a waveform exhibiting vascular and respiratory variations. Also, they present objective measurements to guide surgical steps. They first used CDU after suboccipital craniectomy to establish the true position of the tonsillar tips, which was typically 3–6 mm lower than predicted by MRI. A laminectomy was not performed for herniations above C1. In patients with intermediate herniations (12–15 mm), the superior aspect of the C1 arch was sometimes underbitten; herniations of more than 15 mm underwent a standard C1 laminectomy. Then, cisterna magna volume and CSF flow velocity were assessed to guide the next surgical decisions. If cisterna magna volumes were of less than 0.5 cm3 and CSF flow velocities were in the range of 0–0.8 cm/s anterior and posterior to the CMJ, durotomy and duraplasty were performed. A simple duraplasty without additional steps was performed in patients who met the following criteria: cisterna magna volume of at least 4 cm3; CSF velocity/flow of at least 2 cm/s; and CSF tracings demonstrating bidirectional movement with vascular and respiratory variations. Patients not meeting these criteria underwent internal decompression with lysis of the arachnoid and, in some cases, tonsillar shrinkage based on direct inspection of the obex area and the presence of pulsatile flow from the fourth ventricle into the dorsal cervical theca. Postoperative cine phase contrast MRI confirmed the presence of unrestricted CSF flow anteriorly and posteriorly through the foramen magnum in most patients. However, no specific data on long-term outcomes of the cohort were reported.
Nishikawa et al. later confirmed these criteria, using quantitative IOUS to evaluate decompression effectiveness intraoperatively. In cases where target CSF flow velocities (peak of 3–5 cm/s) or cisternal volumes (at least 8 cm3) were not met, additional steps such as extended craniectomy, dural opening, or tonsillar shrinkage were performed. Postoperative cine-MRI confirmed successful decompression in most cases. In another series by Cui et al., authors used IOUS with color Doppler flow imaging to assess CSF flow velocity and direction and confirmed that bidirectional flow with peak velocities above 4–13 cm/s correlated with symptom resolution. A cutoff of 3 cm/s was used for duraplasty decision by Brock et al. in a prospective series including 49 CMI patients. Among these, 36 patients (73%) had CSF flow above 3 cm/s and did not undergo duraplasty. In 13 (27%) patients with initial flow <3 cm/s, a dural opening was performed together with duraplasty. All patients showed clinical improvement, and no significant difference was noted between both groups. However, complication rates were lower in the bony decompression group, reinforcing the role of IOUS as a tool to minimize unnecessary intradural manipulation. Overall, these findings suggest that IOUS is not merely a qualitative adjunct but a quantifiable, physiologically grounded modality that can inform individualized surgical plans in CMI treatment, though this approach should be standardized.

Reliability and Reproducibility of IOUS
Despite its increasing integration into CMI, concerns remain regarding IOUS reliability, reproducibility, and interpretative value. One major limitation lies in the inability to perform a true “before-and-after” comparison during surgery. Because ultrasound cannot be employed prior to bone removal, it becomes impossible to directly quantify the physiological “gain” achieved by PFD. This absence of baseline data inherently restricts objective intraoperative assessment.
This limitation is underscored by the findings of Bond et al., who used intraoperative MRI to evaluate 15 CMI patients undergoing surgery. Remarkably, 93% of patients exhibited a significant increase in dorsal CSF flow simply from surgical positioning (neck flexed to approximately 1–2 fingerbreadths between the chin and chest). In some cases, this improvement was so pronounced that any subsequent enhancement due to bone decompression was difficult to discern. These observations highlight the dynamic nature of CSF flow and caution against overinterpreting intraoperative findings as direct indicators of the adequacy of decompression.
The questionable role of IOUS in patients with syringomyelia further highlights the inability of IOUS to provide a comprehensive interpretation of CSF dynamics (Fig. 4). Nonetheless, Heiss et al. demonstrated that when used within a multimodal framework, including preoperative cine-MRI and intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, IOUS findings correlate with both imaging and clinical outcomes in most cases.

In addition to interpretative challenges, IOUS also poses several technical limitations, particularly in the use of CDU for assessing CSF flow. Unlike blood, CSF is acellular, low in protein content, and circulates at low, nonhomogeneous velocities within irregular subarachnoid spaces. Moreover, CSF motion often involves bidirectional to-and-fro movement, which differs significantly from vascular flow. As Milhorat et al. emphasized, standard CDU settings are ill-suited to capture these subtle flow dynamics. Accurate imaging requires meticulous adjustment of multiple machine parameters, including B-scan gain, Doppler velocity scales, filter sensitivity, time gain compensation, and real-time focus. Even with these optimizations, artifact recognition and avoidance of aliasing artifacts demand considerable operator experience. In summary, while IOUS provides valuable real-time feedback during CMI surgery, its limitations, both technical and interpretative, must be carefully considered. Future improvements in ultrasound technology and cross-validation with other intraoperative modalities may enhance its reliability and clinical utility.
Aside of reliability and reproducibility of IOUS, additional biases are common to the studies included in the present analysis. The definition of surgical success/failure was largely inconsistent through the papers, thus limiting the definition of the real impact of IOUS.
On these grounds, definition of success/failure is a critical point, as radiological outcome could be less rewarding than clinical outcome, in particular after bony decompression, and could change over time particularly in children (Fig. 5). Finally, the role of IOUS may be overestimated, as in the case that the dural opening is performed based upon IOUS findings, we have not the possibility to define if this surgical maneuver was unnecessary.
Sagittal MR showing symptomatic complex Chiari in an 8-year-old girl (a) who received bony decompression based on IOUS findings (b). c Radiological outcome on postoperative MR was questionable despite resolution of preoperative symptoms. At 6-year follow-up, MR showed spontaneous resolution of tonsillar herniation along with growth spurt (d).
Last, the “intention to treat” was never considered. We think that the utility of IOUS should be defined by assessing in how many cases the IOUS changed the initial intention to treat, considering either case as the candidate to bony decompression who received also duraplasty or case candidate to duraplasty who finally received only bony decompression based upon IOUS findings. Without this analysis, the IOUS seems a modality to merely corroborate the intention to treat of the surgeon rather than to truly guide and tailor the extension of surgery.

Conclusions
CMI remains a complex and heterogenous condition with no universally accepted surgical strategy. While posterior fossa decompression with or without dural opening remains the mainstay of treatment, the extent of intervention is still largely driven by institutional experience and surgeon preference. In this context, IOUS has been used as a real-time tool to guide surgical decision-making based on patient-specific anatomy and CSF dynamics. Quantitative assessment may provide more reliable criteria, but this approach is not standardized and remains difficult to reproduce. Qualitative approach is more diffused but still relies on the subjective impression of adequacy of surgical decompression. Current evidence does not support the use of IOUS as a tool to tailor the surgical treatment of CMI, due to several limitations, such as variability in interpretation, lack of standardization, and the absence of large-scale outcome studies using objective benchmarks.
On these grounds, future efforts should aim to establish consensus-driven protocols for IOUS use and validation through prospective multicenter studies. Incorporating IOUS into the surgical workflow may hopefully help bridge the gap between radiological findings and intraoperative realities, ultimately fostering a more personalized, data-driven approach to CMI treatment.
