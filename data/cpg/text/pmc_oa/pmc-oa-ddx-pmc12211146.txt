Alleviating effects of probiotic supplementation on biomarkers of inflammation and oxidative stress in non-communicable diseases: a systematic review and meta-analysis using the GRADE approach
Background
Inflammation and oxidative stress are key risk factors in noncommunicable diseases (NCDs). Probiotics have been suggested to be beneficial in mitigating inflammation and oxidative stress; however, the evidence remains inconsistent due to variations in study design, dosage, and patient populations.
Methods
Studies were included following a systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and EMBASE, provided they fulfilled the eligibility criteria. This systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) aimed to clarify the effects of probiotics on oxidative stress and inflammation in non-communicable diseases (NCDs).
Results
A review of 18 studies revealed that probiotic supplementation significantly reduced CRP (SMD = -1.33, 95% CI: -1.84, -0.82; p < 0.001, high certainty), TNF-α (SMD = -1.10, 95% CI: -1.66, -0.55; p < 0.001, low certainty), and MDA levels (SMD = -1.38, 95% CI: -2.08, -0.69; p < 0.001, high certainty). Additionally, while probiotics increased GSH levels (SMD = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.06, 1.23; p < 0.001, high certainty), they did not change the levels of IL-6 (SMD = -1.05, 95% CI: -2.21, 0.11; p < 0.001, low certainty), NO (SMD = 0.47, 95% CI: -0.54, 1.48; p = 0.363, low certainty) and TAC levels (SMD = 0.24, 95% CI: -0.27, 0.74; p = 0.357, moderate certainty).
Conclusion
The supplementation of probiotics may have mitigated biomarkers associated with inflammation and oxidative stress.

Introduction
The gut microbiota, which comprises bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other microorganisms, inhabits the human gastrointestinal tract and is regarded as a “secondary organ system” due to its essential performance of physiological functions for the host. This complex microbial ecosystem can be influenced by multiple factors: host age, genetic background, dietary patterns, disease states, and medications have all been shown to alter gut microbiota composition. Within this community, certain probiotic species (e.g., Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium spp.) are strongly associated with beneficial health outcomes, such as enhanced disease resistance and reduction of comorbid conditions. In contrast, dysbiosis—a pathologically perturbed microbiota—is characterized by a decline in microbial diversity and the loss of beneficial commensals. Dysbiotic states are typically marked by a relative overgrowth of potentially pathogenic taxa, for example, members of the Enterobacteriaceae family (class Gammaproteobacteria). Among the most prominent beneficial microorganisms contributing to gut homeostasis are bacteria from the genera Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Streptococcus, which are often present in high colony-forming unit (CFU) counts within a healthy gut microbiota. As a result, the administration of probiotic formulations—comprising live, non-pathogenic microorganisms with health-promoting properties—has become increasingly prevalent. These probiotics are utilized both as preventative agents and as adjuncts in the management of various diseases.
Dysbiosis, or the exacerbation of underlying pathological conditions, has been associated with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), as well as increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Sustained elevations in ROS can result in oxidative stress—a state in which the generation of oxidants surpasses the organism’s antioxidant defense capacity, despite the presence of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and reduced glutathione (GSH). If unmitigated, oxidative stress may lead to significant damage to cellular biomolecules, including lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.
Despite increasing scientific interest in the therapeutic potential of probiotics, current evidence regarding their impact on oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers in individuals with non-communicable diseases (NCDs) remains inconclusive. In the context of inflammation, findings from meta-analyses have been mixed: some report no significant effects of probiotic supplementation in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and multiple sclerosis (MS), while others suggest a measurable reduction in inflammatory markers. These discrepancies may be attributable to methodological differences, including statistical approaches and the heterogeneity of included studies. Similar inconsistencies are observed for antioxidant biomarkers such as glutathione (GSH), with findings varying across investigations.
This meta-analysis aims to systematically assess the effects of probiotic supplementation on key biomarkers of oxidative stress, including malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and nitric oxide (NO), as well as inflammatory markers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP), in populations affected by non-communicable diseases (NCDs). The study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of how probiotics may help mitigate oxidative and inflammatory issues in individuals with NCDs.

Methods

Register
This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was registered in the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews.

Search strategy
Relevant study published up to March 2025 were searched in PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science and EMBASE using the following keywords: (“Probiotics” OR “Saccharomyces” OR “Lactobacillus” OR “Bifidobacterium”) AND (“Oxidative Stress” OR “Total Antioxidant Capacity” OR “TAC” OR “Antioxidant” OR “Oxidant” OR “Reactive oxygen species” OR “Malondialdehyde” OR “MDA OR “Glutathione” OR “GSH” OR “Nitric Oxide” (Supplementary Table 1). Our search was not limited by language or publication date. Additionally, the reference lists of included studies and review articles were examined to ensure no relevant publications were overlooked. Two independent reviewers evaluated the eligibility of the studies, resolving any discrepancies through discussion and, if necessary, with the involvement of a third reviewer. Inter-rater agreement during the study selection phase was assessed using the kappa (κ) coefficient, with a value above 0.80 considered acceptable; in this analysis, agreement was 0.92, indicating excellent concordance.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria
The study selection adhered to the following PICOS criteria: Population (P): Adults aged 18 years and older with NCDs; Intervention (I): Supplementation with probiotics; Comparison (C): Placebo or a control group; Outcomes (O): Biomarkers for oxidative stress, which include MDA, TAC, GSH, and NO, as well as inflammatory markers like IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP; Study design (S): Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that follow a clearly defined treatment protocol, specifying the type of probiotic, dosage, and duration of treatment. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) studies involving pregnant or lactating women; (2) in vivo and in vitro experimental studies; (3) observational study designs; and (4) studies lacking a control group. In addition, grey literature sources—including dissertations, conference abstracts, and patents—were not considered for inclusion in this review.

Data extraction and collection
Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation of eligible studies. Extracted data included study characteristics (author, publication year, location), participant details (sample size, age, health status), intervention specifics (probiotic type, dosage, and duration), and outcomes (mean and SD of outcomes for MDA, GSH, TAC, NO, IL-6, TNF-α and CRP). Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Quality of studies and the GRADE approach
Two reviewers independently assessed the methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2). The quality of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) framework, which evaluates overall confidence in effect estimates based on key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and potential publication bias.

Statistical analysis
The statistical analysis and meta-analysis were conducted using STATA software (version 14.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). All data were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) for each variable to estimate the pooled effects. A random-effects model was used to calculate the standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The between-study heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistics. A subgroup analysis was carried out to detect possible sources of heterogeneity among the studies. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed by systematically excluding each primary study one at a time to assess the robustness and reliability of the findings. The Begg’s rank correlation and Egger’s linear regression tests were conducted to assess the small-study effect. Furthermore, a visual inspection of the funnel plot was employed to evaluate publication bias. A p-value of less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

After screening the titles and abstracts, 577 records were excluded, and 20 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. All 18 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The characteristics of the studies included in this review are shown in Table 1. Effect of probiotics on CRP
Probiotic supplementation significantly reduced CRP levels (SMD = -1.33, 95% CI: -1.84, -0.82; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 90.1%, p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the overall findings with SMD (p < 0.05). Subgroup analysis by study population revealed the most potent effects in patients with MS (SMD = -1.78, 95% CI: -3.21, -0.34; p < 0.001, I² = 92.5%, p < 0.001), followed by diabetic (SMD = -1.58, 95% CI: -2.42, -0.74; p < 0.001, I² = 92.6%, p < 0.001). Further analysis based on intervention duration showed more reductions in long-term (≥ 12 weeks: SMD = -1.45, 95% CI: -2.01, -0.89; p < 0.001) interventions (Table 4).

Effect of probiotics on TNF-α
Probiotic supplementation significantly reduced TNF-α levels (SMD = -1.10, 95% CI: -1.66, -0.55; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Heterogeneity among the included studies was high (I² = 83.6%, p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis confirmed that the pooled results were stable and not influenced by any individual study.

Effect of probiotics on IL-6
The IL-6 level did not significantly decrease following probiotic supplementation (SMD = -1.05, 95% CI: -2.21, 0.11; p < 0.001) with a substantial heterogeneity (I² = 95.8%, p = 0.995) (Fig. 4). Sensitivity analysis showed that the combined results remained robust and were not affected by any single study.

Effect of probiotics on MDA
Probiotic supplementation led to a significant reduction in MDA levels (SMD = -1.38, 95% CI: -2.08, -0.69; p < 0.001), though heterogeneity was high (I² = 94.3%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5). The sensitivity analysis findings reinforced the reliability of the outcome derived from the SMD analysis (p < 0.05). Subgroup analysis revealed consistent reductions across different studied populations, notably in patients with Alzheimer’s (SMD = -3.39, 95% CI: -4.95, -1.82; p < 0.001). Further analysis based on intervention duration showed more reductions in long-term (≥ 12 weeks: SMD = -1.74, 95% CI: -2.55, -0.93; p < 0.001) interventions (Table 4).

Effect of probiotics on GSH
The improving effect of probiotics on GSH levels was significant (SMD = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.06, 1.23; p < 0.001), although high heterogeneity was observed (I² = 92.0%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6). Sensitivity analysis verified the robustness of the primary findings based on the SMD analysis. Subgroup analyses showed that the most significant increases in GSH were observed in patients with diabetes (SMD = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.11, 1.79) and those with a mean age of less than 50 years (Table 4).

Effect of probiotics on NO
Probiotic supplementation did not significantly increase NO levels (SMD = 0.47, 95% CI: -0.54, 1.48; p = 0.363), with no heterogeneity (I² = 95.2%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 7). The conclusions remained stable, as verified by the sensitivity analysis on the SMD analysis.

Effect of probiotics on TAC
Probiotic supplementation did not significantly increase TAC levels (SMD = 0.24, 95% CI: -0.27, 0.74; p = 0.357) with high heterogeneity (I² = 90.2%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 8). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the results for SMD metrics, with consistent findings across different analyses. Subgroup analyses showed that the most significant increases in TAC were observed in patients with diabetic (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.19, 1.19) and a mean age of less than 50 years (Table 4).

Publication bias
Small-study effect was assessed using Egger’s and Begg’s tests, with no significant indications of bias for CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, NO, and GSH (p > 0.05). Egger’s and Begg’s tests indicated a significant publication bias in the analysis for MDA and TAC (p˂0.05). The trim and fill test was conducted due to the observation of an uneven distribution of studies across all markers following a visual inspection of the funnel plot assessment. Consequently, after executing the correction analysis along with TAC (six imputed studies) and MDA (two imputed studies), the results remained unchanged.

Discussion
In this systematic review and meta-analysis, probiotic supplementation was found to have a significant anti-inflammatory effect on key biomarkers. Specifically, levels of CRP and TNF-α were significantly reduced by probiotics, whereas levels of IL-6 were not significantly altered. These findings are consistent with several prior analyses; for example, a recent umbrella review found that probiotics significantly lowered CRP and TNF-α in diabetic patients (CRP SMD − 0.79, TNF-α SMD − 1.35) while IL-6 showed a non-significant trend (SMD − 0.29, 95%CI crossing zero). Following the majority of previous meta-analyses, our aggregated findings similarly suggest a substantial decrease in acute-phase reactants (CRP) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α); however, a more variable effect on IL-6 is observed.
The observed pattern suggests that probiotics exert a broad anti-inflammatory effect, yet their influence may vary across different mediators. Probiotics play a mechanistic role in influencing the gut-immune axis through various means. They can alter the composition of gut microbiota and enhance the integrity of the intestinal barrier, which helps lower the translocation of systemic endotoxins (lipopolysaccharides) and subsequent immune activation. Probiotics limit toll-like receptor activation and innate immune pathways via the gut, ultimately decreasing secretion of downstream cytokines and acute-phase protein secretion. In parallel, specific probiotic strains stimulate regulatory T cell (Treg) activity and other anti-inflammatory immune responses. For example, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains have been shown to increase Treg populations and inhibit Th1/Th17-driven inflammation, directly suppressing pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6. Probiotics also enhance the production of gut-derived metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids) that signal through immune cells to further dampen inflammation. Collectively, these mechanisms plausibly explain why CRP and TNF-α, which are downstream markers of innate inflammation, are reduced with probiotic use.
The lack of a significant effect on IL-6 warrants closer consideration. IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine with both pro- and anti-inflammatory roles, and its regulation can be complex. Unlike CRP, a stable acute-phase protein synthesized in the liver, IL-6 levels may be more acutely responsive and subject to tight homeostatic control. Probiotic effects on IL-6 may be more modest or variable than on CRP and TNF-α, leading to the non-significant pooled result. Indeed, many individual trials report only small or inconsistent changes in IL-6 after probiotic intervention. High between-study heterogeneity may also mask a true effect, especially given the diverse assays and patient populations used. Notably, sensitivity analyses in some meta-analyses suggest a potential IL-6 reduction after excluding outlier studies, but the overall evidence remains inconclusive. It is also plausible that IL-6 regulation involves additional pathways (e.g., exercise, adiposity, circadian rhythms) that probiotic supplementation alone does not sufficiently modulate. Therefore, while probiotics attenuate specific inflammatory pathways, the stimulus for IL-6 production in chronic conditions may require longer or more targeted interventions.
Our subgroup analyses provide further insight into these findings. Probiotic interventions lasting longer than 12 weeks yielded more pronounced anti-inflammatory effects than those of shorter duration. This observation aligns with other reviews reporting that only prolonged supplementation (e.g., ≥ 10 weeks) significantly lowers markers like CRP and TNF-α. Probiotics must first colonize or alter the host microbiome, which is a gradual process; thus, a longer duration allows cumulative modulation of gut flora and barrier function. In addition, chronic inflammatory processes take time to change measurably. The pooled data indicated that short-term studies often showed little to no change in CRP or TNF-α, whereas sustained use of probiotics (beyond three months) produced steady declines. This time-dependence suggests that future trials and therapeutic recommendations should consider at least 3–4 months of probiotic administration to observe meaningful anti-inflammatory benefits.
Subgroup analysis revealed a significantly greater reduction in CRP levels among individuals with MS who received probiotic supplementation, suggesting a potentially more prominent anti-inflammatory effect in this population. Elevated CRP levels have been associated with disease activity, progression, and a pro-inflammatory environment that may exacerbate neuroinflammatory responses. The observed effect may be attributed to well-documented changes in the gut microbiota, including reduced microbial diversity and overexpression of pro-inflammatory species. Given that individuals with MS often have elevated baseline inflammatory markers, particularly during active disease phases, the significant reduction in CRP following probiotic intervention may reflect a combination of higher baseline inflammation and a greater response to microbial modulation. These findings suggest that probiotic supplementation may serve as a valuable adjunctive strategy in reducing systemic inflammation in MS, which warrants further investigation in larger, controlled clinical trials.
Interestingly, the anti-inflammatory effects of probiotics were particularly pronounced in individuals over 50 years of age. This may reflect the concept of “inflammaging”, wherein aging is accompanied by low-grade chronic inflammation. Older adults frequently exhibit elevated baseline levels of CRP and TNF-α, which offers a greater opportunity for reduction. This means that when baseline inflammation is high, interventions such as probiotics can lead to a more significant decrease in markers. A recent study involving seniors over 70 years old indicated at least a trend toward lower CRP levels with probiotic supplementation. Although age-related changes in immunity could theoretically blunt probiotic responses (e.g., lower gut microbial diversity, weakened T-cell function), our findings suggest that the net benefit remains substantial. It is also possible that older adults have accumulated dysbiosis or leaky gut issues over time, making them exceptionally responsive to microbial therapy. Altogether, the age-subgroup result implies that probiotics might be especially useful in ameliorating the heightened inflammatory milieu seen in mid-to-late life.
The pooled results demonstrated that probiotics significantly reduced MDA levels and increased GSH concentrations, indicating a favorable effect on oxidative stress. However, no significant changes were observed in NO and TAC, suggesting a selective influence on specific oxidative pathways.
MDA, a key indicator of lipid peroxidation, was consistently reduced following probiotic supplementation. Subgroup analyses revealed that this effect was more pronounced in individuals with Alzheimer’s disease, during interventions longer than 12 weeks, and in populations aged over 50. These findings align with the understanding that neurodegenerative conditions and aging are associated with elevated oxidative stress, providing a greater scope for measurable improvements. Longer intervention durations may be necessary for probiotics to effectively modulate gut microbiota and enhance systemic antioxidant responses, thereby yielding more significant decreases in oxidative markers such as MDA.
Conversely, probiotics significantly increased GSH and TAC levels in individuals with diabetes and in participants under the age of 50. Diabetes is characterized by chronic hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress and impaired endogenous antioxidant systems. Probiotic supplementation may support redox homeostasis in this population by enhancing GSH biosynthesis and reducing reactive oxygen species through gut-derived metabolites such as butyrate. Younger individuals may exhibit a more responsive antioxidant system and improved metabolic flexibility, which could explain the greater increases in GSH and TAC observed in this subgroup.
The minimal impact on NO and overall TAC in the general population could be due to differences in assay methods, individual baseline antioxidant levels, and the various mechanisms that control NO production, such as those mediated by endothelial and immune pathways. Furthermore, TAC represents the total antioxidant activity and might not be sufficiently sensitive to identify specific alterations resulting from microbial modulation.
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review. Firstly, the heterogeneity in probiotic strains, dosages, and intervention durations across the included studies may have contributed to variability in outcomes, particularly concerning biomarkers such as GSH and NO. Secondly, the absence of power analyses in most studies raises concerns about the statistical validity and reliability of their findings. Thirdly, the predominance of short-term interventions limits our understanding of the long-term effects of probiotics on inflammation and oxidative stress markers. Fourthly, due to the limited number of studies, subgroup analyses for SMD, as well as for specific biomarkers like NO, IL-6, and TNF-α, were not feasible. Lastly, inconsistencies in biomarker assay methods across studies may have introduced additional variability in effect sizes, potentially affecting the comparability and generalizability of the results. The main strength of our study was the first meta-analysis in patients with NCDs to use the GRADE assessment in this area, which indicated the certainty of the evidence obtained.

Conclusion
This meta-analysis highlights the significant potential of probiotic supplementation in modulating biomarkers of inflammation and oxidative stress. Notably, probiotics were associated with reductions in CRP, TNF-α, and MDA, alongside enhancements in GSH levels. These effects were particularly evident in individuals with diabetes, MS, and Alzheimer’s disease, suggesting that probiotics may serve as a valuable adjunctive therapy in managing these conditions. However, the absence of significant effects on IL-6, TAC, and NO indicates variability in probiotic efficacy, which may be attributable to differences in strain specificity and individual patient characteristics.
