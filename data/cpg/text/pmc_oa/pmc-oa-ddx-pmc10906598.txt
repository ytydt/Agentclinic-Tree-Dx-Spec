A Systematic Review of the Orthoplastic Approach in Adult Lower Extremity Soft Tissue Sarcoma Flap Reconstruction
Background:
The orthoplastic approach to patient care has changed the way patients with a wide variety of lower extremity pathology are treated. Through a systematic review, we aim to analyze outcomes in adult patients with lower extremity soft tissue sarcomas who undergo an orthoplastic flap management approach to their care.
Methods:
A systematic review of adult lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma excision with plastic surgery flap reconstruction was conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines searching the Pubmed, Embase, and Web of Science databases from inception to April 2023.
Results:
After removal of duplicates, title and abstract screening, and full-text review, 26 articles were accepted for inclusion. The total mean follow-up duration was 32.0 ± 24.3 months. Reconstruction used microvascular free flaps in 65.5% (487/743), while 34.5% (256/743) were local flaps. 85.8% (307/358) of patients ambulated postoperatively. Revision surgery was required in 21% of patients during their respective follow-up periods. The limb salvage rate was 93.4% (958/1,026). Among pooled surgical outcomes, 22.2% (225/1,012) of patients experienced a perioperative complication.
Discussion:
Our study demonstrates that although complication rates in lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma reconstruction may be further optimized, a multidisciplinary flap reconstructive approach provides high rates of limb salvage and functional postoperative ambulation.
Soft tissue sarcomas (STSs) are rare malignancies with an annual incidence of approximately five in 100,000. Although heterogenous in subtype and location, a majority of cases affect the extremities, most commonly the thigh. Incidence increases with age, with a median age of 66 years and equal distribution among sexes. Surgical excision is the mainstay of treatment, with management historically involving radical amputation with wide margins. As treatment paradigms have shifted to limb-sparing approaches, the literature has emerged that describes comparable survival rates and superior functional outcomes in limb-sparing surgery compared with amputation. Standard of care now involves a multidisciplinary approach with excision by orthopaedic or surgical oncology teams and soft tissue reconstruction frequently performed by plastic surgeons.
The benefit of plastic surgery involvement in soft tissue reconstruction after sarcoma excision is documented in many studies, describing decreases in complication rates, secondary procedures, and duration of hospital stay. Conversely, other studies have described poorer outcomes with plastic surgery involvement, possibly due to recruitment in more complex cases, namely, patients with larger defects and higher grade or recurrent tumors. However, studies investigating flap reconstruction in particular have described comparable functional and clinical outcomes in patients with high grade and recurrent tumors compared with lower grade and primary tumors reconstructed with less complex reconstructive techniques. Ultimately, these findings emphasize the importance of optimizing closure with robust and well-vascularized soft tissue in limb salvage procedures.
Despite the ample literature describing plastic surgery flap reconstruction involvement after lower extremity STS excision, no studies describe consolidated outcomes. Although systematic reviews and large-scale database queries have been done to describe flap reconstruction outcomes of plastic surgery involvement for the upper extremity and trunk, outcomes for the lower extremity are limited to case series and single-institution series. Given the tendency to involve plastic surgeons in lower extremity STS flap reconstruction, it is important to clarify lower extremity–specific benchmarks to aid with patient counseling, guideline-based standards, and treatment advancement. Our study aims to investigate the state of orthoplastic collaboration in lower extremity STS flap reconstruction by performing a systematic review of patient characteristics, oncological indications, procedural data, and outcomes.

Methods
A systematic search of the PubMed, Ovid Embase, and Web of Science databases was done according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines from inception until April 2023 (Table 1). Studies were included if they reviewed adult patients with resected soft tissue sarcoma of the lower extremity with plastic surgical flap reconstruction and for which incidence of revision surgery, limb salvage, or surgical/perioperative complication rate was reported. Groin malignancies, tumors of bony origin, pediatric cases, and case studies were excluded. If the study described patients outside of our inclusion criteria, study-specific data must have been clearly available. Articles must have been available in full-text English language and represent original research data. If not explicitly stated, plastic surgery involvement was ascertained by author departmental affiliations. If it was unclear whether reconstruction involved vascular or plastic surgery, only those patients with flap coverage were included.
Search Terms
(sarcoma* OR sts OR “soft tissue neoplasm*” OR “connective tissue neoplasm*”) AND (“tissue transfer*” OR plastic* OR “reconstructive surgery” OR flap*) AND (“lower extremity*” OR “lower leg*” OR le OR lower limb*)
Screening was independently conducted using Covidence systematic review software by two authors (M.L. and S.D.). Results from the initial search were compiled, with duplicate studies removed. After title and abstract screening, full-length articles were reviewed with reasons provided for each exclusion. Conflicting inclusion decisions at any point throughout screening were discussed until the authors reached a consensus. Data points extracted for the cohort meeting our inclusion criteria included study design, country of origin, follow-up duration, age, gender distribution, available comorbidities, perioperative radiation therapy or chemotherapy, functional scores (Toronto Extremity Salvage score, Musculoskeletal Tumor Society Scoring System, or other scales), postoperative ambulation, revision surgery incidence, limb salvage rate, and wound complications, such as flap failure, hematoma formation, and infection. When differentiated as major versus minor, only rates of major complications according to established definitions were included. Studies excluding cases of amputation were not included in our pooled amputation rate. Additional data points collected were presenting status of primary versus locally recurrent tumor, tumor surface area, flap origin, flap type, timing of plastic surgery involvement, tumor grade and stage, and tumor location.

Results
The primary search strategy yielded 1,592 articles. After removal of duplicates, 778 independent records were identified across the databases. Subsequent screening of the title and abstract excluded 715 records, leaving 63 studies eligible for full-text review (Figure 1). The most common reason for exclusion was the lack of differentiated analysis of the lower extremity. Ultimately, 26 articles were accepted for inclusion, published between 1992 and 2023, with an eligible cohort ranging from 1 to 181 (40.8 ± 54.2) and totaling 1,060 patients across all data points. Patient Characteristics
Among patients included in our study, reported mean age was 55.2 ± 6.3 and 51.6% (397/770) of patients were male. Although most studies did not report differentiated comorbidities for their eligible cohort, the most common comorbidities were diabetes in 33% (26/79) and active smoking in 30.7% (70/228). 44.1% (437/991) of patients received preoperative radiation therapy, and 21.8% (216/991) of patients received postoperative radiation therapy. Perioperative chemotherapy was received by 26.9% (268/995). The mean duration of hospital stay was 23.4 ± 19.8 days, and 85.8% (307/358) ambulated postoperatively. Functional scores were only reported for included patients in eight studies, with the most common being the Lower Extremity Musculoskeletal Tumor Society tally, Toronto Extremity Salvage Score, and Lower Extremity Functional Score, with mean scores of 26.8, 82.6, and 69.4, respectively.

Surgical Outcomes
The mean follow-up duration was 32.0 ± 24.3 months. 20.6% (77/374) of patients required revision surgery during their respective follow-up periods. All but one study included amputation rates, and the pooled limb salvage rate was 93.4% (958/1,026). A 22.2% (225/1,012) pooled complication rate was observed among studies which reported perioperative outcomes.  Of 553 patients with specific outcomes provided, infection occurred at 11.9%, wound dehiscence at 8.0%, seroma formation at 5.8%, delayed wound healing at 5.6%, partial flap necrosis at 5.6%, total flap loss at 4.2%, and hematoma formation at 2.0% (Table 3).

Surgical Information
The most common locations for reconstruction of the lower limb were the leg (37.4%), thigh (34.1%), knee (17.4%), and foot (11.1%). Regarding reconstruction, there were a total of 487 free flaps (65.5) and 256 local or pedicled flaps (34.5%). The most common flap choice was the latissimus dorsi (42.0%), all of which were mobilized as free flaps. The second and third most common flaps were the anterolateral thigh (22.3%) and rectus abdominis (15.6%), each of which were transferred using a free flap approach in 96.7% and 35.7% of flaps, respectively (Figure 3). Reconstruction by plastic surgery was immediate in all but 1.2% of cases (12/1,034) for which plastic surgery involvement was delayed.

Discussion
Orthoplastic surgery encompasses a collaborative approach between orthopaedic surgery and plastic surgery to achieve functional limb restoration and avoid amputation in a wide variety of extremity pathologies. Although standard of care for lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma frequently involves plastic surgery reconstruction in limb-sparing approaches, a dearth of literature exists to describe the encapsulated effect of orthoplastic flap management for this patient population. Furthermore, existing studies lack consolidated information on patient demographics, oncological characteristics, and procedural data for cases managed with this multidisciplinary approach. Our investigation addresses this gap in knowledge with a global systematic review focused on adult lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma flap reconstruction and the orthoplastic approach.
Overall, plastic surgery involvement and the orthoplastic approach were consistent with low amputation rates of 6.6%. This is similar to other limb-sparing reports generally, but extremity salvage overall is less commonly attempted without plastic surgery expertise. However, patients experienced revision surgery rates of 21% and a pooled complication rate of 22%, which we consider to be notable, and could possibly be attributed to increased recruitment of plastic surgeons in larger and more complex cases. To provide some benchmark for these percentages, we found that Houdek et al published a series of 653 patients treated with a limb-sparing approach for lower extremity STS with indications similar to our pooled cohort. All flap reconstruction within that study was done independently by orthopaedic surgery without plastic surgery involvement and, thus, without the orthoplastic approach. The authors reported a major wound complication rate of 40% and a revision surgery rate of 32%, with the most common wound complications being delayed wound healing or dehiscence. Furthermore, postoperative radiation therapy was used in 7% of their cohort compared with the rate of 22% in our pooled review, which may also highlight the value of plastic surgery involvement given the negative effect of postoperative radiation on flap outcomes. Additional studies describing outcomes in flap-related STS management without plastic surgery intervention reported major wound complication rates of 17%, 24%, 26%, and 27%. Although there is a paucity of literature which has attempted to directly quantify the benefit of plastic surgery in STS management, our comparatively low rates demonstrate a suggested benefit. Although excluded from our review due to the inclusion of pediatric and hip cases, Agrawal et al also performed a 2013 analysis comparing outcomes before and after plastic surgery involvement became standard of care for extremity STS limb-sparing management at their institution. Their results were so compelling that the authors argued plastic surgery should be an essential component of STS care, with amputation rates decreasing from 56% to 39% and major infections decreasing from 43% to 26%. Furthermore, in addition to decreasing complication rates of index operations, plastic surgery involvement adds the benefit of a team specialized in wound care in the event of wound complications.
Our investigation also implies good functional results with plastic surgery involvement, with high postoperative ambulation rates of 86%, consistent with other limb-sparing approaches generally. To further resolve functional progression, reliable, accurate, and clinically meaningful quantitative functional scoring protocols have been developed but were included in only eight of our studies. Of those, five different systems were used, most commonly being the Lower Extremity Musculoskeletal Tumor Society tally reported in six studies, followed by the Toronto Extremity Salvage Score reported in three studies. This heterogeneity has been previously discussed in a systematic review of the topic by Kask et al who found that of 37 queried studies, 10 different previously reported measurement tools were used and 10 included new measures developed by the authors. Of the previously validated measures, the Toronto Extremity Salvage Score was most common, used in 43%, followed by the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society tally, used in 32%. Further exemplified by our results showing a different representation of scoring techniques, these authors also argue that investigation of functional outcomes would likely benefit from standardization of reporting measures.
The specific flaps used in lower extremity STS reconstruction were frequently detailed in the literature discovered through our review. The most frequently used was the latissimus dorsi muscle, which was used exclusively as a free flap, as well as the anterolateral thigh and rectus abdominis flaps, for which both microvascular and pedicled techniques were used. These flaps have been established as reliable options for coverage of many defects and confer an ability to cover large wounds. Interestingly, the rectus abdominus being the most commonly used flap from the lower extremity is notable given our exclusion of groin tumors and the most common tumor sites being in the lower leg. Although most of the pooled surgical data demonstrated wide utilization of free flap techniques, many included studies also offered comparisons with local/pedicled flaps. In 2018, Kapoor et al found that the combined occurrence of hematomas, seromas, infection, and abscesses was markedly higher for pedicled flaps at both donor and recipient sites compared with free flaps for extremity sarcoma excision reconstruction. However, they also found that in their cohort, the size of tumors treated with pedicled flaps was markedly larger, which may explain increased complication rates. In 2023, Novak et al found that free flap reconstruction was associated with increased length of stay but resulted in fewer cases of prolonged wound healing and seroma, as well as decreased recurrence and higher functional scores for larger tumors. However, to further elaborate, Slump et al found no notable differences in rates of extremity wound dehiscence, hematomas, seromas, or infection between pedicled flaps and free flaps, nor did their cohort have markedly different tumor sizes between the two approaches. Taken together, these findings may suggest that pedicled and free flaps represent similarly viable surgical approaches with smaller tumors, but free flaps may be indicated in select patients with the greater risk for complications associated with larger tumors.
Timing of reconstruction relative to tumor excision is another important surgical consideration. Two studies comparing immediate versus delayed reconstruction by plastic surgeons have found that delayed reconstruction is associated with higher complication rates. Specifically, Reece et al reported a complication rate of 63% in their delayed cohort compared with 36% in the immediate group. They also reported that their single case requiring revision surgery occurred in the group treated with delayed reconstruction and that the hospital course was longer at 40.6 days in the delayed group, compared with 13 days in the immediate group. Similarly, Zhou et al found overall complications to be markedly higher in their delayed group, occurring in 100% of cases compared with 28% of those undergoing immediate reconstruction. However, they also found tumor size, depth, and extent of bone involvement to be greater in the delayed group. Taken together, these findings may be explained by selection bias where more complicated patients at inherently higher risk of complications tend to require larger reconstruction performed over multiple surgeries. Alternatively, another study found that of their patients developing wound infections, a markedly higher proportion underwent immediate reconstruction, but this group also had more aggressive tumors and comorbidities, suggesting that this finding may reflect a single institution's practice pattern in deciding between immediate and delayed surgery. Another selection bias may be seen in reconstruction after sarcoma excision generally, with patients requiring flap reconstruction often having higher-grade and larger tumors, recurrent disease, and more likely to receive adjuvant radiation and extensive excision. Ultimately, the effect of free tissue reconstruction on outcomes after sarcoma excision may be best evaluated in the context of a collaborative prospective randomized trial.
We did find that a markedly larger proportion of our included patients received perioperative radiation compared with chemotherapy. This is expected given that sarcomas are less likely to be responsive to systemic chemotherapy and are frequently treated with radiation. However, both radiation and chemotherapy are known to disrupt cellular processes important to wound healing and therefore may potentially compromise wound healing. As a purely prognostic indicator, several studies have noted neoadjuvant radiation and chemotherapy to be associated with worse postoperative healing courses. The ability of plastic surgeons to incorporate nonradiated tissue during reconstruction to address soft tissue defects not only benefits wound healing but can help to facilitate the delivery of higher radiation doses in cases of recurrent or invasive tumors. Notably, our included studies primarily focused on reconstructive outcomes, making it impossible to thoroughly determine the effect of management options on oncological outcomes such as recurrence. It is possible that access to plastic surgeons for flap reconstruction could positively affect oncological outcomes by allowing oncologists to be more aggressive in their excisions knowing they have a secondary team who specializes in reconstructing defects involving all tissue types.
Our study has several limitations. First, pediatric cases were excluded from our study because they differ from adult sarcomas regarding epidemiology, management, and prognosis. Soft tissue sarcoma of the groin was also excluded from our study because it represents a distinct anatomical region with different characteristics and management considerations. Studies that mentioned groin and pediatric cases were evaluated for potential inclusion of only a subset of their cohort, but we were only able to exclude those that explicitly stated their cohort demographics and lower extremity definition. Our determination of the included patient subset based on flap usage may also not account for the proportion of local flaps, which could be done by orthopaedic surgeons, but in all these cases, at least some portion of the case still had a plastic surgeon involved in some capacity. Systematic reviews are also subject to inherent limitations based on the fidelity of data collection and analysis, as well as risks of bias in each individual study. Furthermore, no single data point was reported across all 26 studies, and this heterogeneity extends to important factors such as follow-up duration, which ranged from 3 months to more than 6 years. In addition, poor resolution regarding tumor characteristics, specifically size, is also a limiting factor for generalizable comparisons. Our study also focused only on the scope of plastic surgeon involvement within flap management, but steps should be taken to assess the reported literature on functional reconstruction, and additional techniques such as lymphovenous bypass and nerve grafting, as well as primary closure and skin grafting. Importantly, we are also unable to directly compare outcomes with and without plastic surgery involvement, which is a critical next step in future research to definitively determine the benefits of orthoplastic collaboration in flap reconstruction.
Our study demonstrates that although complication rates in lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma flap reconstruction can be optimized, a multidisciplinary reconstructive approach involving plastic surgery may provide lower complication rates with comparably high rates of limb salvage and functional outcomes. This study provides an early benchmark to delineate patient characteristics, management strategies, and clinical outcomes of orthoplastic collaboration in soft tissue sarcoma flap reconstruction.
