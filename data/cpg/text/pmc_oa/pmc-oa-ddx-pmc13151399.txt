Economic evaluation of diversion programs for people with drug-related legal involvement: a systematic review
Background
Implementing community-based diversion programs (DPs) as an alternative to incarcerating people who use drugs has yielded potentially beneficial, yet conflicting evidence regarding cost-effectiveness. This study systematically reviewed the economic evaluations (EEs) of DPs by synthesizing findings on cost-effectiveness and recidivism, while strengthening the appraisal of causal validity, risk of bias, and reporting quality to inform future research and policy development.
Methods
A systematic literature review of EEs in the DPs was conducted using seven databases. We extracted characteristics including country, EE type, perspective, cost-effectiveness, recidivism, and limitations. We assessed causal validity and evaluated risk of bias using ROBINS-I, reporting quality using the Quality of Health Economic Studies (QHES) and Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS), and strength of evidence on recidivism using the Maryland Scientific Methods Scale (SMS).
Results
Of the 2,246 records, 16 studies covering 12 DPs were included. Most originated in the US (n = 9), with five from Australia and two from the UK. Over half adopted a societal perspective, followed by a taxpayer perspective, and most were funded by nonprofit organizations. The study types included cost-utility (n = 3), cost-effectiveness (n = 4), cost-minimization (n = 4), and cost-benefit analyses (n = 5), predominantly incorporating criminal justice and healthcare costs. ROBINS-I indicated a moderate to serious risk of bias in most studies, driven primarily by confounding and selection issues, and three-quarters cited data-related limitations. The QHES and CHEERS scores showed a wide variability in reporting quality. Evidence from the three SMS-assessed studies among prison releasers suggested that DPs may lower recidivism and be cost-effective; however, confidence in these findings was limited by a moderate-to-serious risk of bias, mainly due to confounding, selection, and author-reported data limitations (75%), including heterogeneity and inconsistency in cost components and measurement.
Conclusions
DPs demonstrate heterogeneous cost-effectiveness due to inconsistent analytical approaches, high methodological bias, and pervasive author-reported data limitations. These issues, particularly confounding and selection bias, undermine confidence in causal and economic conclusions. Standardized, bias-minimized cost-utility analyses with rigorous designs are urgently required to inform policy decisions.

Background
In 2021, an estimated one in seventeen people worldwide used drugs, a 23% increase over the past decade. Approximately 2% of the global population was living with drug use disorders, with some countries exceeding 5%. These patterns impose substantial societal costs, particularly on healthcare and criminal justice systems (CJSs). In Europe, drug use and drug use disorders have been estimated to cost up to 4.7% of gross national product, and in the US, the 2017 economic burden of opioid use disorders and overdoses approached USD 1.0 trillion, including productivity losses, healthcare, and criminal justice expenditures. People who inject drugs also disproportionately contribute to the transmission of HIV and viral hepatitis, further increasing public health costs. Historically, many jurisdictions have relied on imprisonment to respond to drug use and to handle cases involving people with drug-related legal involvement, but non-incarceration alternatives—such as diversion, treatment, and harm-reduction approaches—have recently gained prominence as more health-oriented policy responses. Yet, in several settings, even modest public-health-oriented diversion remains contested, as NIMBY-type concerns and preferences for punitive rather than service-oriented responses have been documented in debates on overdose prevention centers in the US and non-custodial educational programs in South Korea.
Diversion, in its broad conceptualization, refers to a spectrum of institutional practices that redirect individuals away from the conventional punitive trajectory of the CJS. The term “diversion programs” (DPs) has often been used in this broad sense to refer to measures that divert individuals from incarceration, a quintessential instrument of retributive criminal justice (Fig S1). From a criminal justice perspective, probation and court-supervised models (e.g., drug courts) are recognized as non-custodial alternatives to conviction or punishment; however, they function mainly as justice-sector gateways that may route individuals to care rather than constitute community-based treatment. From a community perspective, we define community-based DPs as non-custodial, mostly outpatient interventions—such as case management, brief education, and linkage to routine health and social services—that are integrated into routine health and social care rather than delivered in specialized courts or residential settings. This usage follows the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC)/WHO guidelines on community-based treatment as integrated, primarily outpatient care embedded in community systems. Throughout, we distinguish these community-based DPs from other non-custodial justice-sector or court-based interventions. In line with our conceptual focus on brief, outpatient, community-embedded DPs as a feasible entry point for broader harm-reduction and rehabilitation systems, we focus on programs that divert individuals from criminal court jurisdictions. Venue-dependent models (such as drug courts and residential therapeutic communities) are treated as contextual comparators and are excluded from the pooled quantitative synthesis because their implementation and resource profiles are not directly comparable to outpatient community diversion.
Existing economic evaluations (EEs) suggest that diversion can be cost-effective even when some public-sector costs increase. For example, in Maryland, buprenorphine diversion increased pharmacy spending by USD 987 per patient but reduced other medical costs, yielding net annual savings of USD 4,554 compared to no treatment. Prior reviews by drug courts have also reported net benefits, including estimated savings of approximately USD 5,680 per participant per year. However, this evidence largely reflects intensive court-based models and may not generalize to the lower-intensity community diversion approaches that many jurisdictions are only beginning to implement.
However, findings are not uniformly positive. The Law Enforcement Assisted Diversion (LEAD) program in the US has shown reductions in recidivism and criminal justice costs, but these benefits have not been consistently replicated, and nationwide versus Wirral-level evaluations of diversion in the UK have produced conflicting results. To date, no systematic review has specifically examined the EE of community-based DPs. Accordingly, this review systematically synthesizes the EEs of community-based DPs for people who use drugs, with the dual aim of summarizing the incremental economic outcomes of community-based DPs (and their variation across program types and comparators where data permit) and appraising the overall strength and causal validity of the evidence through risk-of-bias and reporting-quality assessment, including recidivism findings. By concentrating on early-stage, community-embedded diversion, we sought to provide policy-relevant evidence to inform scalable, non-custodial public health responses in settings where such approaches remain contested.

Method

Search strategy and data sources
We conducted a systematic review of the EEs of community-based DPs for people who use drugs, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (Table S1). The core methodological procedures were preregistered on an online registry (#AsPredicted C1W_9Y1), and the final protocol—refining the scope to community-based EEs—was subsequently registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251186011). Because PROSPERO registration occurred after the initial search, it should be regarded as retrospective. We searched PubMed/Medline, Cochrane Library, Embase, ProQuest Library, SCOPUS, EBSCOhost, and Web of Science databases up to November 2023. All records were managed in EndNote, extracted into a standardized Excel form, and screened and extracted manually without automation tools. The keywords or medical subject headings included drug abuse, drug addiction, substance-related disorders, and diversion. We also investigated the search strategies associated with “economic evaluation,” “cost-benefit,” “cost-effectiveness,” and “cost-utility.” The detailed search procedures are described in Table S2.

Study selection
Using predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria, we investigated the literature to determine whether the articles met the criteria. First, we included studies covering DPs that were explicitly described as community-based and non-custodial, delivered primarily in outpatient or routine community settings, including programs initiated or supervised by criminal or juvenile courts or probation, when the core intervention consisted of community-based treatment or case management rather than court processing. Accordingly, we operationally defined “diversion programs” as community-delivered (outpatient) diversion in which the primary intervention is treatment and/or case management rather than court adjudication. Program types included and excluded from this review are summarized in Table 1 (see also Figure S1). Because DPs vary across countries, venue-dependent interventions such as drug courts or residential therapeutic communities were excluded unless the EE clearly focused on a programmatic outpatient diversion component. Second, articles were included according to the defined population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes. We targeted non-incarcerated people who used illicit or controlled drugs and compared the cost-effectiveness of community-based DP with that of non-DP and control groups.

Additionally, recidivism outcomes were extracted when they were incorporated into the EE. Third, we excluded studies that were (1) not EEs (e.g., those that focused only on costs, disease burden, or budget impact); (2) focused on legal drugs only (e.g., alcohol, tobacco, and caffeine); (3) did not cover DP (e.g., medical detoxification, inpatient treatment, and court-based programs where the intervention consisted solely of specialized drug court processing without a distinct community-based treatment or case-management component); (4) reviews, commentary, conferences, abstracts, letters, and news articles; (5) less than 50% of the study population was drug addicted in mixed studies (e.g., when mental illness and gambling addiction are more common); (6) lacked sufficiently clear and complete per-capita economic outcomes to allow robust comparative analysis; (7) Quality of Health Economic Studies (QHES) < 50 (very low-quality EE); and (8) not in English. Two independent researchers conducted title- and abstract-level selection procedures (MKH and KAN). Three researchers (MKH, KAN, and SKP) analyzed the full texts of the remaining articles. When disagreements occurred, all the collaborating researchers reached a consensus through discussion.

Extraction of study data
Two independent reviewers (KAN and SKP) extracted the data from the DP, and a third researcher (MKH) addressed any discrepancies. We extracted the following characteristics and results of the study: (1) type of economic analysis (e.g., cost-utility analysis [CUA], cost-minimization analysis [CMA], cost-benefit analysis [CBA], cost-effectiveness analysis [CEA]); (2) location (country and province); (3) intervention or comparator; (4) DP and baseline year; (5) perspective (e.g., taxpayer or society); and (6) conclusion of cost-effectiveness. Additionally, we extracted the following information from the study method: (1) eligibility criteria for DP; (2) study design; (3) decision model; (4) cycle length; (5) time horizon; (6) discount rate; (7) cost items (e.g., CJS, health-related, victimization, DP, and earnings); (8) items included in the outcome (e.g., employment and recidivism); and (9) author-reported limitations.
We standardized all reported costs to 2023 US dollars using country-specific consumer price index data and exchange rates (World Bank) and converted outcomes to net monetary benefit for cross-study comparability. For countries without an official willingness-to-pay threshold, we used 1× GDP per capita as a proxy; full conversion and net monetary benefit formulas are provided in Table S3.

Study quality evaluation
We used four complementary tools to assess study quality, reporting, and risk of bias. First, we evaluated the quality of the EE methods using the QHES, which is a 100-point quality assessment scale with 16 criteria used to stratify systematic reviews and identify superior cost-effectiveness data. Second, we assessed the completeness and transparency of EEs using the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS 2022) checklist. Third, because all included studies were non-randomized evaluations of interventions, we used the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool to appraise causal validity and risk of bias across seven domains (confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results). Fourth, we used the Maryland Scientific Methods Scale (SMS) to evaluate recidivism prevention in the included studies.
The SMS was divided into Levels 1–5 to assess the internal validity of the study design. Level 5 studies, such as a random assignment or randomized controlled trial, have the highest internal validity. Level 3 studies meet minimal requirements for assessing program efficacy on recidivism, such as similar before-and-after assessments of the control and treatment groups. When applying the SMS, we used simple, pre-specified decision rules (e.g., regarding loss to follow-up, follow-up duration, and precision of effect estimates) to ensure consistent and transparent scoring; detailed thresholds and examples are presented in Table S4. We define a recidivism prevention effect as “suggestive evidence” in at least two studies with a Level ≥ 3 on the SMS. In addition, if there is one study with a Level of ≥ 3 on the SMS, it is defined as “preliminary indication” in preventing recidivism. For example, in the LEAD program, if there is one SMS Level 3 and one SMS Level 2, and positive recidivism, the conclusion is “preliminary indication” because there is only one SMS Level 3 or higher. Prison-released DP has one SMS Level 4 and two Level 3s, and as all of them are positive for recidivism, it is deemed “suggestive evidence” by satisfying at least two conditions of three or more. Recidivism rates applied from the original studies.

Results

Literature search
Of the 2,246 studies identified, 310 were included after removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts. Finally, 16 articles were selected after full-text review (Fig. 1). We used 12 types of DP data and excluded studies that used the same basic DP data. Therefore, studies that used data from the same DP and had the same EE methods and results are listed together in the results table (e.g., the New Mexico Sentencing Commission [NMSC], Shanahan et al.) whereas studies that used different EE methods are listed separately (e.g., Cowell et al.. and Lattimore et al.).

Methodological quality and risk of bias
QHES scores ranged from 57 to 100, with model-based evaluations generally scoring higher than quasi-experimental or cohort designs; however, most evaluations showed important gaps in subgroup analyses, explicit discounting, justification of model structure, and incremental analysis (Table 4, Table S5, Fig. S2). Similarly, the CHEERS-based assessment indicated variable reporting completeness, particularly for model structure, handling uncertainty, and justification of perspectives and time horizons. According to ROBINS-I, no study was judged to have an overall low risk of bias; almost all were at moderate to serious risk, mainly due to confounding and selection of participants, with additional concerns about missing data and outcome measurements in several evaluations. SMS ratings for recidivism outcomes ranged from Levels 1 to 4 (Table S4.B, Fig. S2): a small number of prison-release diversion evaluations provided Levels 3 to 4 evidence with consistently favorable recidivism results and were classified as “effective,” whereas LEAD and cannabis DPs were supported only by single Level 3 studies and were, therefore, considered “preliminary indication” (Table S3). Notably, despite these stronger QHES/CHEERS and SMS profiles in some designs, ROBINS-I ratings remained largely moderate to serious, underscoring persistent identification limits. Furthermore, program types, comparators, outcome definitions, and follow-up periods differed substantially across the evaluations, and there were no sufficiently comparable data to support a formal meta-analysis.

Cost and outcome components included in the evaluations
Figure S3 and Table S6 summarize the cost and outcome components captured by the 16 EEs. Almost all studies included criminal justice, healthcare, and DP costs (93.8%), whereas victimization and other societal costs were incorporated far less frequently (31.3% and 18.8%, respectively). Recidivism was reported in nearly all evaluations (93.8%), followed by drug use (75.0%), health-related outcomes (68.8%), and employment (43.8%); other social outcomes were reported in half of the studies (50.0%). Consistent with this, author-reported data bias was common (75%), further underscoring limitations in causal inference. Taken together with the high risk of bias, these patterns suggest that the existing evidence base focuses heavily on criminal justice and healthcare costs and on recidivism, while broader societal costs and outcomes remain underrepresented.

Discussion
Overall, this review indicates that the economic and recidivism effects of DPs are only preliminary indications and remain context-bound and methodologically uncertain. This study identified the EEs of DPs in only three countries (the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom) based on a small, heterogeneous set of studies conducted in specific legal, service, and population contexts. Across the few prison-release DPs evaluated in the US and Australia, recidivism and cost outcomes were generally favorable, with SMS Levels of 3 to 4 in some studies, but residual confounding and selection bias remain important concerns. Taken together, these findings provide suggestive rather than definitive evidence that prison-release DPs can reduce reoffending and generate net cost savings,. consistent with broader evidence that diversion-oriented approaches can outperform traditional criminal justice responses in selected setting.
In contrast, evidence on the LEAD program and cannabis-related police diversion remains preliminary. LEAD evaluations generally reported favorable recidivism and economic results, but only one SMS Level 3 microsimulation CUA suggested potential cost-effectiveness; most other studies used weaker designs or partial cost data, and prior reports note that recidivism reductions are not consistent across sites. Adult cannabis DPs in Australia provide suggestive evidence of reduced recidivism with cost-saving or cost-neutral findings, whereas juvenile cannabis diversion reduced cannabis use but did not clearly achieve cost savings or cost-effectiveness. The UK DIP evaluations also showed context-dependent effects, with a national study finding no benefit and a regional Wirral study reporting dominant results. Overall, prison-release DPs currently have the most consistent—albeit context-specific—support, whereas evidence for LEAD and cannabis diversion remains tentative and requires further rigorous replication across diverse legal and service settings.
The EE methods applied to DPs were diverse and not standardized. The DP evaluations in our review used all four major EE types. Four of the five CBAs adopted a set of cost items similar to those used in US drug court evaluations but did not employ explicit decision-analytic models or discounting and showed relatively low EE quality (QHES/CHEERS). In contrast, Zarkin et al. combined CBA with a dynamic simulation model and appropriate discounting, achieving relatively high EE quality and an SMS Level of 3, providing methodologically robust assessments of prison-based diversion. However, its lifetime horizon relies on extrapolating effects well beyond the observed follow-up. Given the uncertainty about long-term effect persistence, lifetime modelling may overstate the net benefits. Reporting and interpreting more conservative fixed-horizon scenarios (e.g., up to 10 years) alongside lifetime estimates would provide a more policy-defensible range of economic value.
To better inform public health policy, our findings highlight several methodological gaps across studies. A minority of EEs incorporated decision-analytic modelling or discounting, which limits the robustness and comparability of long-term cost estimates. CUAs were conducted in only three evaluations, providing the clearest—though still scarce—basis for comparing DPs with other health interventions using a common quality-adjusted life year (QALY) scale. Most studies relied on observational or quasi-experimental designs and were judged to be at serious risk of bias on ROBINS-I for confounding and participant selection, which means that both cost and recidivism findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive.
Against this backdrop, future EEs of DPs should move towards a more coherent analytical framework. Where feasible, model-based CUAs with explicit discounting and clearly justified structures could serve as a reference approach, supplemented by robust CEAs when QALY estimation is not realistic. Health outcome measures should be selected with comparability in mind. QALYs derived from routinely collected clinical or administrative data would allow direct comparison with other public health interventions, while validated mental health or functioning scales can play a useful role if their links to long-term health and social outcomes are made explicit. In addition, results should be reported separately for key sectors (for example, criminal justice and healthcare) alongside an overall societal summary so that decision-makers can see how DPs may reduce criminal justice expenditure while shifting costs to health and community services rather than focusing on a single budget.
Finally, strengthening the underlying study designs is essential to generate more policy-ready evidence. Given the practical and ethical constraints on randomized trials in this field, future work will likely continue to rely on cohort and quasi-experimental designs, but these can be improved through clearer comparison groups, longitudinal follow-up with appropriate counterfactuals, pre-specified analytic plans, and systematic use of methods such as propensity scores or other causal-inference techniques. Building and maintaining routine cross-sector data infrastructures that link criminal justice, health, and community records while respecting privacy would also facilitate timely and comprehensive evaluations. Together, methodological standardization and stronger causal designs are needed for policy-ready evidence.
From a policy perspective, the available evidence supports prioritizing community-based DPs—especially prison-release and police-led schemes—as part of a broader shift from purely custodial sanctions towards more health-oriented responses to drug use. Governments should invest in routine, linked justice–health–social care data systems and pre-planned economic evaluations so that new or scaled-up DPs can be continuously monitored, compared on a common value-for-money scale, and adapted for youth and other priority groups.

Limitations and future research
This systematic review was conducted to provide a structured synthesis of EEs of DPs that can better inform public health and cross-sector policy. However, its findings should be interpreted with caution because of several important limitations. First, by restricting the inclusion to four standard EE methods (CUA, CEA, CMA, and CBA) and English-language publications, other relevant or non-English evaluations may have been missed. Second, substantial heterogeneity in program types, comparators, outcome definitions, follow-up periods, and analytic methods precluded meta-analysis and formal pooling of effect sizes. Future synthesis may be feasible if more comparable evaluations from additional countries become available. Third, none of the included studies explicitly examined polydrug use or polypharmacy despite their growing importance in contemporary drug markets. Most evaluations categorize individuals primarily by the main drug type or offence, which may underestimate the complexity of real-world patterns of use. Fourth, QALY-based CUAs were rare, with only one study explicitly modelling disease incidence to derive QALYs. Compared with other areas of health economics, where QALYs are standard, the DP literature remains underdeveloped, limiting cross-program comparisons. Finally, all included evaluations were conducted in high-income settings (the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom). There is an urgent need for context-specific EEs of DPs in countries without specialized drug courts, such as South Korea, Japan, and Saudi Arabia, where the patterns of drug use, legal frameworks, and service systems differ markedly.

Conclusion
This systematic review suggests that DPs can reduce criminal justice costs and may be cost-effective or cost-saving. However, the evidence remains limited in quantity, quality, and generalizability, particularly outside a small number of high-income settings and specific models, such as prison-release, LEAD, and cannabis DPs. By expressing the results as net monetary benefits and relating them to GDP-based willingness-to-pay thresholds as an illustrative benchmark, future evaluations can compare alternative diversion models on a common public health value-for-money scale. Future research should prioritize well-designed quasi-experimental or longitudinal evaluations that report transparent sector-specific and societal perspectives and apply QALY-based or other robust health outcome measures broadly particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Such evidence would enable more reliable judgments regarding the scalability and value-for-money of DPs as humane alternatives to traditional criminal justice responses.
