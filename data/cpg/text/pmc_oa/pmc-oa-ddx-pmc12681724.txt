Risk Factors, Clinical Features and Diagnostic Workup of Sclerosing Mesenteritis: a Single-Center Study of 219 Patients
Sclerosing mesenteritis (SM) is a rare, idiopathic, non-neoplastic condition characterized by chronic fat necrosis, inflammation, and fibrosis most commonly affecting the mesentery of the small intestine. This can result in mass effect and associated nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms. Additionally, severe gastrointestinal luminal and mesenteric vascular compression may occur. While historically SM was diagnosed via abdominal imaging with confirmatory histopathology, practice has shifted away from the latter to limit invasive possibly unnecessary biopsies, unless the diagnosis is unclear or a strong clinical suspicion for malignant involvement of the mesentery is present. However, this workup can become nuanced given malignant (eg lymphoma) and nonmalignant (eg inflammatory or infectious conditions) involvement of the mesentery can have similar characteristics on imaging (ie “mimics”).
Given the disease prevalence of 0.18%–3.14%, much of what is known regarding SM is based on smaller studies and expert opinion. The largest nonimaging study of SM was conducted at our institution including patients diagnosed between 1982 and 2005. Increased frequency and improvement of imaging have led to an increasing number of newly diagnosed cases which warrants further investigation to improve our understanding of SM. In this retrospective investigation, we aimed to better characterize the associations, clinical presentation, and diagnostic workup of SM.
After protocol approval, adult patients diagnosed with SM between January 1, 2010, and January 4, 2023, based on imaging interpretation at our center with or without histopathological confirmation were included. Supplementary material summarizes patient selection and inclusion/exclusion criteria. A standardized data abstraction form was utilized, and descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize characteristics.
Of the 600 charts reviewed, 219 patients met the inclusion criteria and 94% were evaluated by gastroenterologists. Of those excluded, 56 cases were an imaging mimic of SM (Figure A1), including lymphoma (3.2%), metastatic carcinoid tumor (1.2%), other carcinoma/neoplasm (1.7%) and other etiologies (3.3%).
The median (interquartile range) age at diagnosis was 63 (54–70), with a male predominance (59.8%) and 94.5% were White (Table A1). The most common associated factors for SM were a history of abdominal surgery (63.9%), concurrent abdominal pathology (43.4%), autoimmune disease (31.1%), irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) diagnosis (20.5%), and malignancy (13.2%). Of the latter, the most common were skin cancer (37.9%), followed by lymphoma (27.6%) and genitourinary cancer (27.6%).

While 15.1% of patients were asymptomatic, the most common symptoms on initial presentation for SM included abdominal pain (76.7%), followed by nausea or emesis (31.5%; Table 1). When stated, clinicians attributed presenting symptoms to SM in only 48 (21.9%) cases. In the remaining cases, the symptoms were thought to be only possibly related to SM (39.7%) or not attributed to SM (21.9%). While many patients had a normal abdominal exam (41.1%), the most common finding was abdominal tenderness (42.9%) with only 6.4% having a palpable abdominal mass. Disease complications occurred in 45 (20.5%) cases, most commonly small bowel obstruction (44.4%), mesenteric artery/vein compromise without thrombosis (44.4%) followed by chylous ascites (24.4%).
A minority (7.8%) of cases were initially misdiagnosed as another condition (Table 1), primarily metastatic carcinoid tumor (47.1%), but showed SM on biopsy. With regards to laboratory testing, when available, only 24.0% had an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate and/or C-reactive protein. Of 56 cases who had serum immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) measured, 89.3% were normal (<135 mg/dL). The most common initial diagnostic modality was computed tomography (90.9%). While the majority of patients (59.4%) were diagnosed purely based on imaging, 40.6% underwent biopsy (image-guided = 11.9%, laparoscopic = 17.8%, laparotomy = 11.0%) for definitive diagnosis. Of the 34 cases that underwent IgG4 immunohistochemistry, 7 (20.6%) had a prominent IgG4 infiltrate on pathology that would be consistent with IgG4-related disease.
In accordance with the literature, abdominal surgery history was the most prevalent associated factor for SM. Our study is the first to report IBS diagnosis in relation to SM, an important consideration given symptom overlap. Indeed, it is difficult to determine whether the nonspecific symptoms are due to IBS or SM (or both), particularly retrospectively. There is lack of consensus in the literature regarding malignancy as a risk factor for SM or SM being a paraneoplastic condition. Some studies report coexisting malignancy in 69.4% of patients whereas in others it is <10%. Overall, higher quality evidence is needed to better characterize the relationship between SM and malignancy.
Similar to previous studies, the majority of patients were symptomatic (84.9%) but with predominantly nonspecific symptoms and signs. This nonspecific presentation engenders difficulty in attributing symptoms to SM or another condition, such as IBS. However, this distinction is of clinical importance given that management of IBS is quite different from SM.
Lab workup was also equivocal. For instance, our results suggest that the serum IgG4 level is a poor marker alone when assessing for IgG4-related SM given that only 1/7 cases with a prominent IgG4+ plasma cell infiltrate on histology had corresponding serum IgG4 elevation. This is similar to a case series of 12 patients with IgG4-related SM where only 50% had an elevated serum IgG4 level. Our results showed that a large portion of patients (40.6%) are still undergoing biopsy. However, this is a drastic change when compared to the study by Akram et al. conducted nearly 2 decades ago where 100% of patients underwent biopsy, and the majority (65%) was via laparotomy, suggesting a shift toward a less invasive diagnostic approach. Notably, 20% of individuals who underwent IgG4 staining on pathology were found to have IgG4 infiltrate. This suggests that when biopsy is undertaken, IgG4 tissue staining could be considered to provide additional diagnostic clarity.
Distinguishing SM from mimics and vice-versa can pose a diagnostic challenge on imaging, as demonstrated by our results, given there is no validated diagnostic imaging criteria. Prior imaging studies have suggested that at least one lymph node >10 mm, abdominopelvic lymphadenopathy in another region or a SUVmax ≥3.0 on positron emission tomography are suggestive of malignancy, predominantly lymphoma.
In summary, SM is typically a benign condition that can cause nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms but also complicated disease in 20.5% of cases. The diagnosis becomes nuanced as malignancy, most frequently lymphoma, can mimic SM on imaging. Future studies are warranted to identify distinguishing imaging characteristics between known SM cases and mimics to facilitate targeted biopsy.
