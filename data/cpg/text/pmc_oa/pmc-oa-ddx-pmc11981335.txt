Supraorbital keyhole approach (SOKHA) versus endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) for suprasellar meningioma (SSM) resection: systematic review and meta-analysis
Background:
Complete surgical resection of suprasellar meningiomas (SSMs) is challenging due to their proximity to neurovascular structures. Minimally invasive transcranial approaches such as the supraorbital keyhole approach (SOKHA) and the endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) have emerged with the advancement of neuroendoscopy and neuromicroscopy.
Objective:
To compare the SOKHA and EEA for the management of SSM in terms of patient characteristics, surgical efficacy, and safety.
Materials and methods:
A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive search of the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases was performed, identifying studies comparing SOKHA and EEA for SSMs. Data extraction and quality assessment were conducted independently by two reviewers.
Results:
Out of 1678 identified studies, six retrospective studies met the inclusion criteria. Visual improvement was significantly higher with EEA compared to SOKHA (81.00% vs 62.12%, respectively, I2 = 0%, P = 0.03), but EEA had a statistically significantly higher risk of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak (risk ratio = 0.30, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.09–0.94) with a fixed-effect model. The rate of gross total resection (GTR) and major complications did not differ significantly between the two approaches. A serious overall risk of bias across all included studies was identified. No significant publication bias was detected.
Conclusion:
While both SOKHA and EEA show comparable GTR rates and safety profiles, however EEA is associated with better visual outcomes but higher CSF leak rates. The surgical approach can be tailored to the individual case without affecting the GTR. Further larger-scale studies are needed to accurately assess long-term outcomes and optimize surgical strategies for SSM.

Introduction

Highlights
Minimally invasive transcranial approaches like the supraorbital keyhole approach (SOKHA) and endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) have emerged with the advancement of neuroendoscopy and neuromicroscopy.
We compare the SOKHA and EEA for the management of SSM in terms of patient characteristics, surgical efficacy, and safety through systemic review.
After analyzing 1678 studies, we found that both SOKHA and EEA show comparable GTR rates and safety profiles, EEA is associated with better visual outcomes but higher CSF leak rates.
Suprasellar meningiomas (SSM) account for approximately 5%–10% of all intracranial meningiomas and arise in midline anterior skull base locations, such as the tuberculum sellae (TS) and planum sphenoidale (PS). Complete surgical resection is a significant challenge given their close proximity to neurovascular structures. Conventional frontotemporal (FT) and subfrontal (SF) approaches may lead to suboptimal cosmetic results and carry a risk of frontal lobe retraction injuries. Minimally invasive transcranial approaches (MITCAs), including the supraorbital keyhole approach (SOKHA), have emerged as promising alternatives. Concurrently, the endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) has expanded the spectrum of available surgical techniques and is gradually becoming the preferred choice for midline skull base lesions.
Comparative studies between EEA and the traditional transcranial approach (TCA) have indicated superior visual outcomes with EEA, with no significant difference in gross total resection (GTR). Nonetheless, EEA is associated with an elevated risk of postoperative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. Single-arm studies comparing MITCA with EEA have reported no significant differences in terms of GTR and visual outcomes.
However, the debate surrounding the risk profile of complications associated with EEA persists; there is no suitable comparative review in the literature that highlights the differences between SOKA and EEA in the case of SSM. This study aims to conduct a comparative meta-analysis approach to compare the EEA and SOKHA in the management of SSM, including TS meningiomas (TSM) and planum sphenoidale meningiomas (PSM), in terms of patient characteristics, surgical efficacy, and safety.

Methodology

Literature search
A systematic literature review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive full-text search was performed across the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases, encompassing publications up to 21 July 2023. The search strategy employed the following keyword string: (Tuberculum Sellae Meningioma OR Planum Sphenoidale Meningioma OR Meningioma Tuberculum Sellae OR Tuberculum Meningioma OR Suprasellar Meningioma OR Tuberculum Sella Meningioma OR Anterior Skull Base Meningioma OR Midline Meningioma) AND (Transsphenoidal OR Endoscopic OR Endonasal OR Extended Endoscopic OR Supraorbital OR Supraorbital keyhole OR SOKHA OR EEA OR EEEA OR minimally invasive).

Study selection
PICOS criteria were applied for the study selection.
Population (P): Cases of suprasellar meningioma.
Intervention (I): Endoscopic endonasal approach.
Comparison (C): Supraorbital keyhole approach.
Outcomes (O): Rate of major complications, rate of visual improvement, rate of visual worsening after surgery, GTR, CSF leak, and/or length of postoperative hospital stay.
Studies (S): Comparative studies including interventional and observational studies.
Studies comparing the efficacy and safety of SOKHA with EEA for SSM, including TSM and PSM, were included. Noncomparative observational studies, case reports, literature reviews, animal studies, conference papers, non-English manuscripts, publications on neoplasms other than SSM and/or patients with a history of prior surgery and/or radiotherapy for meningioma were excluded. Non-English articles with English abstracts only were initially reviewed but were eventually excluded from the review because of the lack of linguistic expertise. Each study was independently reviewed for inclusion by at least two reviewers and discrepancies were resolved by a third independent reviewer. The screening process involved title/abstract review followed by a full-text review of identified studies.

Data extraction
Two reviewers independently conducted the data extraction process. Data were extracted from the studies under specific headings, including: “Author,” “Year of publication,” “Country,” “Study design,” “Surgical approach,” “Mean age,” “Proportion of females,” “Sample size,” “Follow-up duration,” “Mean maximum tumor diameter,” “Mean tumor volume,” “Arterial encasement,” “Major complications,” “Visual improvement,” “Visual worsening,” “Extent of resection” (GTR/Simpson grade 1 or 2), “Occurrence of CSF leak,” and “Length of hospital stay.”

Data synthesis and quality assessment
This review primarily focused on three primary outcomes: (1) the rate of major complications after surgery (including new vision loss or deterioration, new hypopituitarism, new palsies, stroke, hemorrhage, vascular injuries, CSF leak, and death); (2) visual improvement after surgery; and (3) visual worsening after surgery. Secondary outcomes included: (4) GTR, defined as Simpson grade 1 or 2 resection; (5) CSF leak; and (6) length of postoperative hospital stay. The two groups were compared for potential confounders including “mean age,” “mean tumor volume,” “mean tumor diameter,” “rate of vascular encasement” but these variables were not adjusted for in the analysis of the outcomes due to inconsistent reporting of these variables across the included studies. The risk of bias in studies was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias in non-randomized studies – of interventions (ROBINS-I) tool across seven domains. Each outcome was rated for the certainty of evidence from “very low” to “high” by using the Grader tool and assessed for overall risk of bias, inconsistency, precision, indirectness, and publication bias.

Statistical analysis
Data analysis was conducted using the Cochrane web-based Review Manager (RevMan). The data were analyzed using the Mantel–Haenszel (M–H) method with the risk ratio (RR) as an effect size measure for categorical variables, and the inverse variance method with the mean difference as an effect size measure for continuous variables. A random-effects model was used for analysis and reporting. Heterogeneity was assessed using Higgins’ I-squared (I2), with I2 > 50% considered significant. Sensitivity analysis was performed for each outcome by altering the analysis model to a fixed-effect model and by omitting each study one by one. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Egger’s intercept, and Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill analysis.

All studies included in the analysis were of a retrospective design and compared outcomes between SOKHA and EEA. The two surgical approaches were evaluated across various covariates, as detailed in Table 1. The median-of-mean (MoM) age of patients in the included studies was 49.5 years for SOKHA and 51.1 years for EEA. The MoM tumor volume was consistently greater for SOKHA compared to EEA in most studies, with an MoM volume of 15.8 cm3 for SOKHA and 8.7 cm3 for EEA. The most pronounced difference in mean tumor volume between the two groups was observed in the study by Linsler et al, with mean volumes of 14.9 cm3 for SOKHA and 2.1 cm3 for EEA. Furthermore, the rate of vascular encasement by the tumor was higher in the SOKHA group (56%), compared to the EEA group (34%). It should be noted that not all studies provided data on these covariates, and these were consequently excluded from the descriptive analysis. Major complications
Forest plot showing the risk ratio (RR) in major complication rates in the supraorbital keyhole approach (SOKHA) vs endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) groups. RRs were calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method to combine summary statistics, and data were pooled using a random-effects model.
All six studies were included in the analysis. A total of 41 major postsurgical complication (25.00%) were reported in 164 patients; 14 (21.00%) for SOKHA and 27 (28.00%) for EEA. There was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of major complications between SOKHA and EEA (RR = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.42–1.61, P = 0.56), with no significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) observed (Fig. 2). Sensitivity analysis did not reveal any statistical significance.

Visual improvement
Forest plot showing the risk ratio (RR) in the visual improvement rates in the supraorbital keyhole approach (SOKHA) vs endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) groups. RRs were calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method to combine summary statistics, and data were pooled using a random-effects model.
All six studies were included in the analysis. Visual improvement was reported in 62.12% (41/66) of cases with preoperative visual deficits in the SOKHA group, as compared to 81.00% (81/100) in the EEA group. SOKHA had a statistically significant risk of a lower visual improvement rate after surgery, compared to EEA (RR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.65–0.98, P = .03), with no significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3). However, the statistical significance was lost in the sensitivity analysis by excluding Jiang et al, Sankhala et al, or Fatemi et al. Nevertheless, it remained statistically significant after changing the analysis to the fixed-effects model.

Visual worsening

All six studies were included in the analysis. The incidence of postoperative visual worsening, including new visual loss, was 8.6% in the SOKHA group, compared to 6.3% in the EEA group. However, the pooled effect estimate was statistically insignificant (RR = 1.51, 95% CI = 0.56–4.07, P = .41), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), as depicted in Fig. 4. The results remained consistent in the sensitivity analysis.

The rates of GTR were comparable between SOKHA and EEA (69.7% and 77.9%, respectively), with no statistically significant difference (RR = 0.92, 95% CI = 0.70–1.21, P = 0.55) and a heterogeneity of 48% (Fig. 5). Sensitivity analysis did not significantly affect the results.

CSF leak
Forest plot showing the risk ratio (RR) in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak rates in the supraorbital keyhole approach (SOKHA) vs endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) groups. RRs were calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method to combine summary statistics, and data were pooled using a random-effects model.
All six studies were included in the analysis. The risk of CSF leak was higher in the EEA group (18.8%, 18/96) than in the SOKHA group (1.5%, 1/67). However, the analysis was not precise enough to demonstrate a statistically significant difference between the two groups (RR = 0.34, 95% CI = 0.10–1.11, P = 0.07), with heterogeneity of 0% (Fig. 6). However, changing the analysis model to a fixed-effects model revealed a statistically significantly lower risk of CSF leak for SOKHA (RR = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.09–0.94, P = 0.04).

There was no statistically significant difference in the mean postoperative hospital stay between SOKHA and EEA (mean difference = −0.24, 95% CI = −2.58 to 2.11, P = 0.84), as depicted in Fig. 7. Discussion
Patients with larger SSM, those demonstrating lateral extension, and those with tumors extending beyond the internal carotid artery (ICA) were more frequently treated with the SOKHA compared to the EEA. Despite the SOKHA-treated cohort having a larger mean tumor size and a higher proportion of vascular encasement, the comparable risk profiles for major complications between EEA and SOKHA suggest that the latter may be a safer alternative, particularly in cases with greater tumor volume extending laterally to the ICA.
The EEA was associated with a higher likelihood of visual improvement in cases with preoperative visual deficits. While this correlation is of significant clinical interest in SSM, causality has not yet been established. Notably, previous studies have indicated that patients with smaller tumor sizes are more likely to experience favorable visual outcomes. Based on our findings, we can infer that patients with visual deficits in the included studies treated with EEA were more likely to experience visual improvement compared to those treated with SOKHA. However, whether this improvement is due to the surgical approach itself or the fact that smaller tumor sizes were treated with EEA remains unclear. Literature supports that the EEA offers better direct visualization and accessibility, facilitating less invasive manipulation around the optic chiasm, which may contribute to better postoperative visual outcomes.
Regarding tumor resection extent, both SOKHA and EEA demonstrated comparable rates of GTR, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two approaches. In meningioma surgery, achieving GTR is crucial for minimizing the likelihood of tumor recurrence and prolonging disease-free survival and retreatment-free survival. Our observation that both approaches have comparable GTR rates suggests that they are both viable options, offering flexibility in surgical planning for SSM resection. Indeed, the anatomical localization of the tumor does not appear to significantly affect GTR rates when the appropriate surgical technique is implemented. Modern minimally invasive techniques for PSM and TSM resection show effective and similar outcomes, suggesting that the various approaches can yield comparable GTR rates. This indicates that the choice of approach can be tailored to each patient’s individualized needs and align with surgeon preferences without compromising tumor resection completeness.
One of the critical EEA-associated complications is the higher incidence of CSF leaks, as the proximity of the endonasal route to important dural and osseous structures increases the risk of CSF leaks. Although our results were not sufficiently precise to demonstrate a statistically significant higher risk of CSF leak with EEA, a discernible trend was observed with 18 out of 96 patients treated with EEA experiencing postoperative CSF leak as compared to only 1 out of 67 patients treated with SOKHA. This emphasizes the need for larger, well-powered controlled studies to address this question. Nevertheless, using vascularized nasoseptal flaps and meticulous dural closure can enhance the success rates of EEA while limiting associated CSF leak risks. In this context, the importance of postoperative care including lumbar drainage and careful patient monitoring to improve overall surgical safety, ensure better patient outcomes, and mitigate the risk of CSF leak should not be neglected.

Implications
The choice between the SOKHA and EEA surgical approaches for SSMs should be tailored to the specific characteristics of each case. For small-volume tumors with minimal vascular encasement, the EEA approach can be utilized with minimal concern for major complications. Conversely, for larger tumors with significant vascular encasement, the SOKHA approach offers comparable outcomes. However, this review cannot conclusively determine whether these approaches are interchangeable in other scenarios. To address this question, a well-powered study with matched cases is required, particularly comparing EEA and SOKHA in managing small-volume tumors with minimal vascular encasement. Future research should prioritize such comparisons, focusing on the relative efficacy and safety of these techniques. Additionally, robust studies are necessary to clearly assess the risk of CSF leaks associated with EEA compared to SOKHA.

Limitations
The retrospective design of the included studies increases the risk of bias, confounding factors, and inconsistencies in data collection and reporting, reducing the reliability of findings. With only six retrospective studies meeting the inclusion criteria, the statistical power of the analysis is limited as this small sample size may not adequately represent the broader population of patients with SSM. Although accounting for study heterogeneity using statistical methods such as the random-effects model, residual heterogeneity due to differences in patient populations, tumor characteristics, surgical techniques, and follow-up durations can still impact the comparability of the results.
Another concern is the potential for publication bias, despite the assessment indicating no significant bias. Studies with beneficial (i.e. statistically significant and/or positive) outcomes are more likely to be published, skewing the overall findings of this meta-analysis. Additionally, variations in follow-up periods among the included studies could have affected the assessment of long-term outcomes, as shorter follow-up periods may fail to capture late complications or SMM recurrence. The included studies did not uniformly report surgeon skill and expertise, which could also affect the outcomes.
The analysis did not include covariates such as the exact size and location of the tumors, nor did it account for the potential impact of adjunctive therapies such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy and optic canal invasion. The former can influence the success and complications of the different surgical approaches, while the latter can significantly affect intraoperative and postoperative outcomes.

Conclusion
Both the SOKHA and EEA for the resection of SSM seem to yield comparable GTR rates and major complication profiles. Although EEA demonstrates statistically significant advantages in visual improvement rates, it also presents a higher risk of cerebrospinal (CSF) fluid leak, highlighting the need for careful patient selection and advanced surgical techniques implementation from experienced surgeons to mitigate this risk.
The heterogeneity in tumor size and vascular encasement between the two groups suggests that SOKHA may be preferred for larger tumors with significant lateral extension, while EEA may be preferable in smaller, midline tumors due to its enhanced visualization and accessibility. The serious overall risk of bias across all six included studies and the lack of uniform long-term data highlights the need for further, larger-scale and prospective studies to validate -or perhaps reject- these findings and refine SSM management surgical strategies while standardizing outcome measures and incorporating comprehensive follow-up to optimize long-term efficacy and safety.
