Diagnosis, evaluation, and management of patients with non-muscle invasive bladder cancer
Abstract
Introduction
Non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) accounts for over 75% of bladder cancer cases worldwide and is associated with high recurrence rates and significant surveillance costs. Advances in diagnostic modalities, risk stratification, and bladder-preserving therapies have transformed management strategies.
Areas Covered
This narrative review synthesizes evidence from 70 key publications identified through a comprehensive search of PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar (2005–2025). Topics include clinical presentation, diagnostic innovations such as enhanced cystoscopy and urinary biomarkers, contemporary risk stratification models, and evolving treatment paradigms including intravesical therapy, immunotherapy, and gene therapy.
Expert Opinion/Commentary
NMIBC management is shifting toward precision-based, multimodal approaches that integrate molecular biomarkers, immunotherapy, and novel drug delivery systems. While early-phase trials show promise, large-scale studies and real-world data are essential to validate these strategies. Personalized surveillance using circulating and urinary tumor DNA may reduce procedural burden and improve outcomes, marking a paradigm shift toward adaptive, patient-centered care.
ARTICLE HIGHLIGHTS
Over three-quarters of bladder cancers present as non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), a disease characterized by frequent recurrences and substantial long-term surveillance costs.
Advances in diagnostic tools, particularly blue light cystoscopy and evolving urinary biomarker assays, enhance detection accuracy and lower recurrence rates compared with conventional white-light cystoscopy.
Updated risk classification systems (AUA/SUO, EAU, and AI-driven models such as PROGRxN-BCa) are improving estimation of recurrence and progression risk, enabling more individualized care.
Intravesical treatment remains central to NMIBC management, with BCG continuing as the primary therapy for high-risk disease; during BCG shortages, sequential chemotherapy and novel heat-based delivery systems serve as alternatives.
Emerging bladder-preserving therapies, including checkpoint inhibitors, gene-based therapeutics, and controlled-release intravesical platforms, demonstrate encouraging activity in patients with BCG-unresponsive disease.
Novel molecular surveillance approaches (ctDNA, utDNA, and urine-derived biomarkers) are poised to shift follow-up toward adaptive, biomarker-guided protocols, potentially reducing procedural burden and improving patient-centered outcomes.

Introduction
Bladder cancer (BCa) is the 12th most common cancer worldwide, with an estimated 573,000 new cases and 213,000 related deaths reported in 2020, highlighting its considerable burden on public health. Globally, by 2040, the annual number of new BCa cases and deaths will increase to 991,000 (72.8% increase from 2020) and 397,000 (86.6% increase from 2020). BCa is more common in males than females, with a ratio of approximately 5:1.
The disease is characterized by marked heterogeneity in both clinical behavior and molecular profiles, with more than 75% of these cancers being staged as non-muscle invasive at initial presentation. Intravesical therapy with transurethral resection of bladder tumors (TURBT) is the standard initial treatment for non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), but it is associated with a high probability of recurrence. Despite adequate therapy, approximately 30-50% of patients will eventually experience disease recurrence within one year, necessitating repeat TURBT, which drives high treatment costs and morbidity in the elderly patient population. Moreover, NMIBC incurs significant costs for surveillance and treatment, with costs exceeding $ 200,000 over five years for high-risk NMIBC patients progressing after Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) treatment.
In recent years, attention has shifted toward bladder-sparing therapeutic strategies incorporating immune checkpoint inhibitors, viral vector–based gene therapies, targeted agents, and sophisticated intravesical drug-delivery technologies. Novel platforms such as TAR-200 and UGN-102 provide sustained intravesical exposure to therapeutic agents while limiting systemic toxicity. Ongoing clinical trials aim to clarify the benefits of these approaches in both intermediate-risk and high-risk NMIBC populations, and there is growing interest in active surveillance for select low-grade cases to limit overtreatment.
This present review aims to provide a review of NMIBC and focus on recently completed landmark clinical trials that can potentially change treatment paradigms.

Methods
A comprehensive literature search was undertaken on PubMed, MEDLINE, Google Scholar, Embase, and Scopus to prepare this narrative review. Publications from peer-reviewed journals written in English from 2005 to 2025 were reviewed. The search strategy included the following terms: non-muscle invasive bladder cancer, urothelial cancer, bladder neoplasms, diagnosis, risk stratification, management, surveillance, and guidelines. A total of 1,804 English-language articles were identified and reviewed; 70 articles were considered relevant to the aims and the findings and have been outlined in this review. Etiology and genetics
Bladder carcinogenesis is influenced by various environmental, behavioral, and hereditary factors. Tobacco use remains the predominant risk factor; although cessation lowers risk over time, former smokers continue to show elevated incidence compared with never-smokers. Additional contributors include occupational exposure to carcinogenic chemicals such as aromatic amines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and arsenic. Certain chemotherapeutic agents, such as cyclophosphamide, also increase bladder cancer risk. Hereditary syndromes—most notably Lynch syndrome—elevate susceptibility to urothelial cancers of both the upper urinary tract and bladder. Prior pelvic radiation therapy similarly raises long-term risk. Non-urothelial variants, including squamous cell carcinoma, can arise secondary to chronic irritation or infection, such as schistosomiasis or complications from long-term indwelling catheters or foreign bodies.
Polymorphisms in carcinogen-detoxifying genes such as GSTM1 and NAT2 have been implicated in increased susceptibility to bladder cancer in certain patient populations. Among the most frequent genetic alterations in NMIBC is chromosome 9 deletion, which includes loss of heterozygosity (LOH) at 9p, homozygous deletion of CDKN2A, and reduced expression of p16, all of which have been associated with recurrence-free survival. Carcinoma in situ (CIS) often harbors mutations in tumor suppressor genes such as TP53, RB1, and PTEN, whereas oncogenic drivers including FGFR3, PIK3CA, and RAS are commonly observed in other types of NMIBC and contribute to tumor development and progression. In the context of germline predisposition, universal genetic testing for urologic cancers has revealed that approximately 14% of bladder cancer patients carry pathogenic germline variants. Furthermore, 4.3% of patients with bladder cancer report a first-degree relative with the disease, and up to 50% of urothelial cancer patients have a family history of cancer. In a study by Oh et al. among 128 individuals who underwent genetic testing following a diagnosis of urothelial cancer, 26.6% were found to have pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, most commonly involving Lynch syndrome-associated genes and DNA repair genes. Current NCCN guidelines recommend genetic testing for patients with urothelial cancer who present at a younger age or have a personal or family history of Lynch syndrome-associated malignancies.

Clinical features and diagnosis
The most common presentation is that of painless hematuria, gross or microscopic. Urinary frequency due to irritation and/or reduced bladder capacity may also be a presenting feature, particularly for CIS. Upper tract obstruction or pain, or, uncommonly, urinary tract infections are also possible. In a systematic review of 44 studies and 2,29,701 cases, the pooled incidence rate for bladder cancer was 17% (95% CI 14–20%) for visible hematuria and 3.3% (95% CI 2.45–4.3%) for microscopic hematuria. Of 134,713 cases with a hospital diagnosis of hematuria, the detection rate of invasive bladder cancer and noninvasive bladder cancer within 3 months was 1.9 and 0.8%, respectively.
Initial assessment of such patients typically involves imaging of the upper urinary tract via CT, MRI, or renal ultrasound, followed by cystoscopic evaluation. Cystoscopy remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, demonstrating sensitivities between 87 and 100%, specificities from 64 to 100%, positive predictive values of 79–98%, and negative predictive values approaching 98–100%. Standard evaluation for hematuria involves in-office white light cystoscopy (WLC), which is also central to NMIBC surveillance. WLC is well tolerated under local anesthesia and avoids the risks associated with operative procedures; however, it is limited in detecting small or flat lesions, resulting in residual disease in up to 45% of patients. To overcome WLC’s sensitivity limitations, alternative enhanced imaging platforms have been incorporated into clinical practice. Narrow-band imaging (NBI), for instance, improves mucosal visualization. A meta-analysis of seven prospective studies including 1,040 NMIBC patients found that NBI detected 17% more lesions than WLC alone.
Among enhanced imaging modalities, blue-light cystoscopy (BLC) offers the greatest improvement in tumor detection. BLC uses hexaminolevulinate, a dye preferentially taken up by malignant cells that fluoresce red under blue illumination. Compared with WLC, BLC significantly increases detection of high-grade tumors and reduces short-term recurrence by 14–27%. Sensitivity is markedly higher with BLC (96 vs. 73% for WLC). A review by Cahill et al. further confirmed BLC’s superior diagnostic performance across multiple trials. A meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials involving 2,258 patients demonstrated longer times to first recurrence (delayed by ∼7.39 weeks, p < 0.0001) and improved recurrence-free survival at both 1 year (HR 0.69, p < 0.00001) and 2 years (HR 0.65, p = 0.0004). No significant difference in progression to muscle-invasive disease was observed (OR 0.85, p = 0.39).
Urine cytology, first established in 1945 by Papanicolaou and Marshall, is the most longstanding noninvasive diagnostic tool for bladder cancer. While several interpretive systems have been described, global standardization was achieved with the 2016 introduction of The Paris System, which clarified criteria for high-grade disease detection. Numerous adjunct cytologic and molecular assays, such as UroVysion, ImmunoCyt/uCyt+, BTA-stat, NMP22, SCD-A7, URO17, CellDetect, UroMark, and UroSEEK, have been developed to improve diagnostic performance.
Urinary biomarkers offer several benefits, including the potential to detect malignancies missed by cytology, reduction in cystoscopy frequency, decreased interobserver variability, and convenient longitudinal monitoring through noninvasive sample collection. These tests detect proteins, genetic mutations, epigenetic alterations, and extracellular vesicles that reflect underlying tumor biology and may help predict recurrence risk.
Cxbladder® Triage Plus, a multimodal assay incorporating five mRNA targets and ddPCR detection of FGFR3 and TERT mutations, demonstrated high accuracy in hematuria patients. When compared with cystoscopy, the test showed a predicted sensitivity of 93.6%, specificity of 90.8%, PPV of 46.5%, NPV of 99.4%, and a test-negative rate of 84.1% at a threshold score of 0.15. Raising the threshold to 0.54 increased PPV to 74.6%.
Heard et al. evaluated the combination of the Hematuria Cancer Risk Score (HCRS) and the Oncuria-Detect urinary biomarker, demonstrating sensitivity of 87.3% and specificity of 80.6%, with an AUC of 0.909 and NPV of 0.973. For patients with microhematuria, sensitivity remained high (82.4%) with an NPV of 98.7%. EarlyTect BCD, another urine DNA-based test, achieved a sensitivity of 81% and a specificity of 91.5%.
Before TURBT, cross-sectional imaging such as CT or MRI is recommended to delineate lesion characteristics and assess possible invasion depth. Evaluation of the upper urinary tract may involve CT/MR urography, renal ultrasound, CT without contrast with retrograde pyelography, or ureteroscopy. TURBT with bimanual examination under anesthesia remains essential for both diagnosis and treatment. Muscle sampling is required to determine the presence of invasion. Among patients with low-grade NMIBC, long-term follow-up data show that 40.9% experience recurrence or new urothelial tumors within five years.

Staging and grading
Urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma is the most common histologic subtype of bladder cancer, and the majority (75%) of bladder cancers are non-muscle invasive. Other pathologies include squamous cell neoplasms, glandular neoplasms, urachal and diverticular neoplasms, sarcomatoid variants, small cell/neuroendocrine differentiation, and plasmacytoid variants.
Bladder cancer staging follows the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) framework. Clinical staging incorporates pathology from the initial TURBT, findings from physical examination (including bimanual exam under anesthesia), and imaging studies. Pathology reports should clearly document the presence or absence of both lamina propria and muscularis propria, along with any depth of invasion if present. Assessment for lymphovascular invasion (LVI) is also important, as LVI is associated with poorer outcomes.
Tumor grade is an important prognostic factor for determining risk of recurrence and progression in bladder cancer. The World Health Organization Classification System of Urothelial Cancer 2022 is now the most widely accepted and utilized system in the United States. Significant effort has gone into developing tools for risk stratification and prognostication in non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). The most widely cited system is the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) risk calculator, derived from seven trials involving 2,596 NMIBC patients. This scoring system uses clinical and pathologic variables to estimate recurrence and progression probabilities at one and five years. Key recurrence predictors include prior recurrence rate, number of tumors, and tumor size, while progression risk is primarily influenced by T-stage, presence of carcinoma in situ (CIS), and tumor grade.
Another notable model is the CUETO (Spanish Urological Club for Oncological Treatment) calculator, which similarly integrates clinical factors for risk prediction. Despite their utility, these models have limitations. Both EORTC and CUETO lack applicability to contemporary practice because their development cohorts rarely received BCG maintenance or underwent re-staging transurethral resection. Additionally, the EORTC calculator relies on the 1973 WHO grading system, whereas the 2016 WHO classification is now standard, limiting its relevance in the US. These shortcomings underscore the need for modernized risk tools that reflect current treatment paradigms.
The AUA/SUO Guideline proposes a simplified consensus-based system with three categories—low, intermediate, and high risk—to guide counseling and management. Unique to this system is the incorporation of BCG response, where patients with persistent or recurrent disease at six months post-BCG are reclassified as high risk, reflecting more aggressive biology. While these groupings provide a practical framework, they require validation in large, contemporary cohorts to confirm predictive performance. Low-risk includes solitary, low-grade Ta tumors ≤3 cm. Intermediate-risk encompasses recurrent low-grade Ta within one year, multifocal low-grade Ta, solitary low-grade Ta >3 cm, and high-grade Ta ≤3 cm. High-risk features include any high-grade T1 tumor, carcinoma in situ (CIS), any recurrent high-grade Ta, high-grade Ta >3 cm or multifocal, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), variant histology, high-grade prostatic urethral involvement, and any BCG failure in a high-grade patient. These categories guide surveillance intensity and treatment decisions, with high-risk patients requiring the most aggressive management.
A study by Ritch et al validated the AUA/SUO risk stratification system for non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) in a contemporary cohort of 398 patients treated between 2001 and 2017 (median follow-up: 37 months). Five-year progression-free survival (PFS) was 93, 74, and 54%, while recurrence-free survival (RFS) was 43, 33, and 23% for low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively. High-risk patients had significantly higher recurrence (HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.11–3.71) and progression risk (HR 36, 95% CI 8.16–159) compared to low-risk patients. Time-dependent AUCs at 1 year were 0.60 for recurrence and 0.68 for progression. Sub-analysis of solitary high-grade Ta ≤3 cm tumors showed outcomes between intermediate and high-risk groups (5-year PFS: 69 vs. 79% and 54%; RFS: 32 vs. 36% and 20%). Overall, the AUA/SUO system effectively stratifies NMIBC patients and should guide counseling and therapy, though refinement for intermediate-risk classification is warranted.
The European Association of Urology (EAU), also in their guidelines, propose a risk-stratification model based on clinical factors. The updated EAU risk groups stratify NMIBC progression risk but tend to overestimate progression in BCG-treated patients In a cohort of 529 NMIBC patients treated with BCG, Lobo et al. found that observed progression rates were substantially lower than those predicted by the 2021 EAU risk calculator. For very-high-risk patients receiving adequate BCG, 1-year progression was 4.0% versus 16.0% predicted, and 5-year progression was 14.9 vs. 40.0%. Similarly, in the high-risk group, five-year progression was 5.3% compared to 9.6% predicted. Overall, the model’s discriminative ability declined in this BCG-treated cohort, with a c-index of 0.63 vs. 0.80 reported by Sylvester et al..
Traditional NMIBC risk calculators, such as the EAU model, often fail to capture heterogeneity within intermediate-risk patients, leading to challenges in management. In the largest international NMIBC cohort to date (n = 12,659), PROGRxN-BCa demonstrated superior discrimination for progression risk (c-index 0.79 vs. 0.71 for EAU, p < 0.001) and improved sub-stratification. Among intermediate-risk patients, the AI model delineated three distinct tertiles with estimated five-year progression risks of 2, 7, and 15%; the latter approximating high-risk disease. This granular stratification enables clinicians to tailor surveillance and intravesical therapy intensity more precisely, potentially reducing overtreatment in low-risk subsets while identifying patients who warrant aggressive intervention. Importantly, performance remained robust across the WHO 1973 and 2004/2022 grading systems, BCG treatment status, and guideline adherence, supporting its potential integration into global NMIBC management algorithms.

Treatment
The cornerstone of NMIBC management is complete transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT), which provides both diagnosis and initial treatment. Following TURBT, immediate intravesical chemotherapy within 24 h significantly reduces recurrence risk. Randomized trials have demonstrated that a single postoperative dose of gemcitabine or mitomycin C lowers recurrence by approximately 12–39%. Gemcitabine is preferred due to its favorable toxicity profile and lower drug cost. This approach is particularly beneficial for low-risk patients, where additional intravesical therapy may not be required.
Risk stratification informs subsequent therapy:
Low risk: Management typically involves TURBT and single-dose intravesical chemotherapy, followed by surveillance.
Intermediate risk: Recommended treatment includes TURBT, immediate single-dose intravesical chemotherapy, and a 6-week induction course of intravesical therapy (BCG or chemotherapy), with optional one-year maintenance for BCG.
High risk: Preferred treatment is BCG induction plus maintenance for up to three years. Early radical cystectomy should be considered for very-high-risk patients.
BCG remains the gold standard for high-risk NMIBC. Multiple trials confirm its superiority over chemotherapy in reducing recurrence and progression. However, global BCG shortages and intolerance necessitate alternative strategies, including dose splitting and substitution with intravesical chemotherapy. Recombinant BCG in the form of VPM1002BC is safe, and demonstrated a recurrence-free survival at 60 weeks was observed at 49.3%. Combination intravesical chemotherapy remains relevant, particularly during BCG shortages. Sequential gemcitabine and docetaxel achieved 1-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) of 85% and 2-year RFS of 81%. The ongoing BRIDGE trial will provide comparative data against BCG in treatment-naïve patients. Other strategies, such as cabazitaxel/gemcitabine/cisplatin combinations and hyperthermic intravesical chemotherapy (HIVEC), have shown encouraging early-phase results, with HIVEC achieving two-year RFS rates of 86.5 vs. 71.8% for BCG in high-risk NMIBC.
BCG-unresponsive NMIBC refers to persistent or recurrent high-grade disease within 12 months of adequate BCG therapy (≥5 of 6 induction doses and ≥2 maintenance doses). These patients face a high risk of progression, and radical cystectomy remains the standard of care. Given the morbidity and quality-of-life implications of cystectomy, recent research has focused on bladder-preserving strategies, including immunotherapy, gene therapy, and innovative drug delivery systems.
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed systemic cancer therapy and are now being evaluated in NMIBC. Pembrolizumab, a PD-1 inhibitor, was the first FDA-approved systemic agent for BCG-unresponsive NMIBC based on KEYNOTE-057, which reported a complete response (CR) rate of 41% at 3 months and a median duration of 16.2 months in patients with CIS. Among responders, 46% maintained CR for ≥12 months. Cohort B of KEYNOTE-057, which included papillary-only disease, demonstrated a 12-month disease-free survival (DFS) of 43.5%. Grade 3–4 adverse events (AEs) occurred in 13%, primarily hyponatremia and arthralgia. Ongoing trials such as KEYNOTE-676 (pembrolizumab + BCG) aim to improve outcomes through combination therapy. The results from the ALBAN study have demonstrated no significant lowering of recurrence in group with atezolizumab with BCG, compared to BCG alone, but it has a higher incidence of adverse effects.
Durvalumab and sasanlimab have been investigated in phase III trials (POTOMAC, CREST) for BCG-naïve and BCG-unresponsive cohorts. At a median follow-up of 60.7 months, there was a 39% reduction in the risk of recurrence of high-risk disease or death by any cause with durvalumab plus BCG induction and maintenance versus the comparison group (HR 0.68, p = 0.015). The CREST trial observed that there was meaningful prolongation of event-free survival (HR 0.68, p = 0.0095) in patients receiving sasanlimab and BCG, as compared to BCG alone for induction and maintenance.
Intravesical immunomodulators represent another major advance in the high-risk NMIBC population. Nogapendekin alfa-inbakicept (N-803), an IL-15 superagonist, enhances natural killer and CD8+ T-cell activity and was FDA-approved in April 2024 for BCG-unresponsive NMIBC. In QUILT 3.032, N-803 combined with BCG achieved a 12-month DFS of 58.2% in high-grade Ta/T1 papillary NMIBC. At 36 months, cystectomy-free survival was 81.8% among responders. No Grade 4–5 adverse events occurred, and Grade 3 adverse events were noted in 3%.
Gene therapy and viral vectors have shown promising results. Nadofaragene firadenovec, an adenoviral vector delivering interferon alfa-2b, demonstrated a 3-month complete response of 53.4% in CIS patients and durable responses in 45.5% at 12 months. Five-year follow-up revealed high-grade recurrence-free survival rates of 13% (CIS) and 57% (papillary disease), with cystectomy-free survival of 49%. Cretostimogene grenadenorepvec (CG0070), an oncolytic adenovirus, achieved a CR rate of 82.9% at any time and 57.1% at 12 months in combination with pembrolizumab in CORE-001.
Innovative drug delivery systems address limitations of conventional intravesical therapy. TAR-200, an intravesical osmotic pump releasing gemcitabine, achieved CR rates of 82.4% in BCG-unresponsive NMIBC in SunRISe-1, with a 1-year duration of response of 70.2%. Treatment-related AEs were mostly grade 1–2, and grade 3 or more AEs occurred in only 5.8%. UGN-102, a reverse thermal gel containing mitomycin, demonstrated a 3-month CR of 80% and 12-month durability of 82% in ENVISION, positioning it as a potential non-surgical option for low-grade intermediate-risk NMIBC.
Despite these advances, limitations persist. Most published studies are early-phase, single-arm trials, complicating cross-comparisons and evidence-based sequencing. Long-term durability, cost-effectiveness, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) remain underexplored, with only 6 of 36 trials incorporating PRO assessments. Furthermore, heterogeneity in NMIBC biology underscores the need for predictive biomarkers and molecular stratification to guide personalized therapy. Urinary and plasma-based assays for minimal residual disease may enable earlier intervention and tailored treatment strategies. Follow-up and surveillance
Surveillance schedules vary by risk group:
Low risk: Cystoscopy at 3–4 months, then annually.
Intermediate risk: Cystoscopy every 3–6 months for 2 years, then annually.
High risk: Cystoscopy every 3 months for 2 years, then every 6 months; urine cytology and periodic upper tract imaging.
Traditional surveillance schedules for NMIBC are based on clinical risk categories, but they do not account for individual biological risk or treatment response. This often results in uniform, procedure-heavy protocols that may lead to unnecessary cystoscopy procedures for some patients and delayed detection for others. Emerging molecular tools—particularly circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in plasma and urinary tumor DNA (utDNA) assays—offer a paradigm shift toward dynamic, personalized surveillance. These biomarkers enable real-time assessment of minimal residual disease (MRD) and early recurrence, often preceding visible lesions on cystoscopy. Patients with persistently negative ctDNA or utDNA could safely undergo less frequent invasive monitoring, while those with positive assays might benefit from intensified follow-up or early therapeutic intervention. Such an approach aligns with precision oncology principles, optimizing patient experience and resource utilization. Prospective trials are actively evaluating the integration of MRD biomarkers into clinical decision-making. The IMvigor011 and MODERN studies use ctDNA status post-cystectomy to guide adjuvant immunotherapy, demonstrating that ctDNA positivity strongly predicts recurrence and can inform escalation or de-escalation strategies. Similarly, longitudinal utDNA monitoring with assays such as utLIFE-UC and uCAPP-Seq has shown promise in predicting response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy and identifying candidates for bladder-sparing approaches. Emerging urine-based molecular tests are also being evaluated for surveillance in NMIBC. For instance, CxBladder Monitor, a multigene urine biomarker assay, demonstrated high sensitivity (0.93) and negative predictive value (0.97) for detecting recurrent urothelial carcinoma in a multicenter study. This test aims to reduce unnecessary cystoscopies by accurately identifying low-risk patients who may safely defer invasive procedures. The VERNAL study by Macchi et al. evaluated the mRNA-based urine test, Xpert Monitor BC, in the surveillance of NMIBC. In a prospective follow-up of 80 high-risk and very high-risk cases, the Xpert Monitor BC demonstrated a negative predictive value of 95.3%, sensitivity of 90.5%, and specificity of 74.5%. Uromonitor-V2 is a urine-based assay for detection of hotspot mutations in three genes (TERT, FGFR3, and KRAS). Sieverink et al. evaluated this assay for surveillance in patients with NMIBC and reported a sensitivity of 93.1%, a specificity of 85.4%, and a negative predictive value of 95.3%.
Several prospective trials are underway to validate biomarker-driven surveillance strategies. The Urine-based Molecular Testing vs Cystoscopy for Surveillance of NMIBC trial and the Longitudinal Bladder Cancer Study for Tumor Recurrence (LOBSTER) are exploring whether urinary genomic assays can replace or complement cystoscopy in routine follow-up. An upcoming study will build on LOBSTER to further refine adaptive surveillance algorithms.
As these technologies mature, biomarker-driven surveillance could replace rigid, one-size-fits-all schedules with adaptive algorithms that tailor intensity to molecular risk. This evolution not only enhances oncologic safety but also reduces procedural burden, improves cost-effectiveness, and incorporates patient-centered care into NMIBC management.

Future directions
Future research should prioritize multi-arm, adaptive trial designs to evaluate comparative effectiveness and optimize sequencing. Combinatorial regimens integrating ICIs, gene therapy, and sustained-release intravesical systems may offer synergistic benefits. However, these innovations must be balanced against toxicity, cost, and accessibility considerations. As the therapeutic armamentarium expands, shared decision-making and clinical trial participation will be critical to advancing care for NMIBC patients.

Conclusion
In summary, NMIBC management is shifting toward bladder-preserving approaches driven by immunotherapy, gene therapy, and novel drug delivery platforms. While early results are promising, rigorous phase III trials and real-world evidence are essential to validate these strategies and define their role in clinical practice. Personalized, multimodal regimens that prioritize oncologic control and quality of life represent the future of NMIBC care.
