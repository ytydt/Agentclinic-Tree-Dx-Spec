Non-congenital pediatric brain disorders have several etiologies, including genetic, metabolic, and toxic. These disorders often present with distinctive imaging signs that reflect specific underlying biochemical and structural abnormalities and provide critical diagnostic insights, especially in the early stages when clinical features are nonspecific. This pictorial essay highlights key neuroradiological signs such as the cortical ribbon sign in hyperammonemia, the batwing appearance in glutaric aciduria type 1, tigroid and leopard-skin patterns in leukodystrophies, and characteristic findings in the brainstem and basal ganglia, including the putaminal eye, face of the giant panda, and piglet sign. Familiarity with these signs enables radiologists to recognize these etiologies promptly, which in turn facilitates early diagnosis, guides appropriate metabolic or genetic testing, and enables timely therapeutic intervention.

INTRODUCTION
Non-congenital pediatric brain disorders are a heterogeneous group of inherited or acquired conditions that affect central nervous system development and function. MRI plays a pivotal role in their evaluation, often revealing characteristic patterns, or ‘signs,’ that reflect the underlying pathophysiology. Recognizing these hallmark signs is essential for narrowing the differential diagnosis, especially in pediatric populations where early diagnosis can enable targeted interventions and genetic counseling. This pictorial essay presents key MRI signs of these disorders, classified based on their anatomical locations (Table 1).

BRIEF STATEMENTS

CORTICAL RIBBON SIGN IN HYPERAMMONEMIA
The “cortical ribbon sign” is defined as gyriform or curvilinear restricted diffusion involving the cerebral cortex and subcortical white matter. It is a non-specific finding associated with numerous etiologies: ischemic causes such as hypoxic-ischemic injury and post-ictal states; metabolic causes such as hypoglycemia and hyperammonemia; infectious causes such as cerebritis and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease; and genetic syndromes such as mitochondrial myopathy, encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS). In acute hyperammonemic encephalopathy, MRI commonly reveals bilateral cortical diffusion restriction involving the insular and cingulate gyri, often extending to the frontal and parietal cortices (Fig. 1). Hyperammonemia may be seen with urea cycle disorders, organic acidurias, and acute liver failure in the pediatric population.

BATWING APPEARANCE OR OPEN OPERCULA SIGN IN GLUTARIC ACIDURIA TYPE 1
The batwing appearance, or open opercula sign, refers to the characteristic widening of the Sylvian fissures due to underoperculization with enlarged anterior temporal subarachnoid spaces (Fig. 2). This finding is a hallmark of glutaric aciduria type 1, a treatable neurometabolic disorder caused by a deficiency of glutaryl-coenzyme A dehydrogenase, the enzyme involved in the catabolism of lysine, hydroxylysine, and tryptophan. Additional characteristic MRI findings include cytotoxic edema of the basal ganglia (particularly the lentiform nuclei) in the acute phase with subsequent atrophy.

TIGROID PATTERN AND LEOPARD SKIN PATTERN IN METACHROMATIC LEUKODYSTROPHY
“Tigroid and leopard skin patterns” are seen in metachromatic leukodystrophy (MLD), which is a lysosomal storage disorder due to a deficiency in the arylsulfatase A enzyme. This deficiency results in accumulation of galactosylceramide in white matter of the central and peripheral nervous systems, leading to their symmetric demyelination. MRI findings include symmetric, confluent T2 hyperintensity in the periventricular white matter, centrum semiovale, and corpus callosum, often with frontal predominance. In the later course of the disease, atrophy and involvement of the cerebellar white matter are also seen. On T2-weighted images, linear hypointense structures radiating within the demyelinated white matter create the “tigroid pattern” (Fig. 3A), while punctate hypointense foci result in the “leopard-skin pattern” (Fig. 3B). These patterns are due to preserved perivenular myelin.

EAR OF LYNX SIGN IN HEREDITARY SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA
The “ears of the lynx” sign is a distinctive MRI feature of autosomal recessive hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) with thin corpus callosum, particularly in cases associated with SPG11 and, less commonly, SPG15 gene mutations. On axial T2-weighted and fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images, it consists of symmetrical hyperintensities in the forceps minor at the genu of the corpus callosum that resemble the tufted ears of a lynx (Fig. 4). This sign has high diagnostic value; FLAIR imaging demonstrates sensitivity ranging from 79%–97% and specificity of 91%–100%, making it a reliable clue for genetic HSP subtypes even without a family history or a markedly thin corpus callosum. Its presence strongly suggests an underlying genetic etiology, particularly SPG11, and can direct genetic testing for patients presenting with early cognitive impairment or spastic paraparesis.

PERIVENTRICULAR RIM SIGN IN ALEXANDER DISEASE
The “periventricular rim” sign, seen in juvenile-onset Alexander disease, is a T2-hypointense and T1-hyperintense signal that surrounds the lateral ventricles and may show postcontrast enhancement (Fig. 5). Alexander disease is a leukodystrophy caused by a mutation in the glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) gene. This mutation results in the accumulation of abnormal GFAP protein in astrocytes as intracellular inclusions (Rosenthal fibers), which cause severe damage to the neuronal white matter. The “ventricular garland sign,” seen in late-onset Alexander disease, is a garland-like pattern of T2-hypointense and T1-hyperintense linear foci along the inner surface of the lateral ventricles. Both signs are hypothesized to represent blood vessels with dense perivascular Rosenthal fiber accumulation.

BRIGHT TREE APPEARANCE IN ACUTE LEUKOENCEPHALOPATHY WITH RESTRICTED DIFFUSION
Acute leukoencephalopathy with restricted diffusion (ALERD) is a pediatric encephalopathy syndrome primarily seen in East Asia that is triggered by viral or bacterial infections, toxins, or metabolic stress. A hallmark imaging feature of ALERD, especially in its diffuse form, is the “bright tree appearance.” This refers to the high signal intensity on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) within the widespread subcortical white matter, resembling a branching tree (Fig. 6). These areas show a corresponding low signal intensity on apparent diffusion coefficient maps, confirming true restricted diffusion. Conventional sequences (T1, T2, FLAIR) may show only subtle or delayed changes, highlighting the importance of including DWI in MRI protocols for suspected cases. ALERD has two radiological subtypes (diffuse and central-sparing). The diffuse type is more severe, characterized by widespread white matter involvement, rapid progression to coma, and a poorer prognosis. In contrast, the central-sparing type is milder, characterized by sparing of the perirolandic areas, a biphasic clinical course, and relatively better outcomes.

EYE OF THE TIGER SIGN IN PANTOTHENATE KINASE-ASSOCIATED NEURODEGENERATION
The “eye-of-the-tiger sign” is a classic MRI feature of pantothenate kinase-associated neurodegeneration, the most common form of neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation. On axial T2-weighted images, a central hyperintense region is seen in the anteromedial globus pallidus, surrounded by a hypointense rim caused by iron deposition (Fig. 7). In early childhood, only the hyperintense center may be visible, while the hypointense rim appears later as iron accumulates with age. This hyperintense center can also present initially as a linear streak along the medial globus pallidus before evolving into the typical rounded appearance.

PUTAMINAL EYE IN 3-METHYLGLUTACONIC ACIDURIA WITH DEAFNESS AND LEIGH-LIKE ENCEPHALOPATHY
3-methylglutaconic aciduria with deafness and Leigh-like encephalopathy (MEGDEL syndrome) is caused by mutations in the SERAC1 gene, which is involved in mitochondrial function and intracellular cholesterol trafficking. Clinically, affected children present with deafness, dystonia, developmental delay or regression, and 3-methylglutaconic aciduria. A hallmark radiological feature is the “putaminal eye” sign, seen during the second stage of the disease’s evolution. This sign consists of a spared region in the middorsal putamen. On T2-weighted MRI, this area appears well-defined and signal-preserved, while the surrounding putamen shows swelling with abnormal T2 hyperintensity and T1 hypointensity (Fig. 8). This spared region becomes progressively smaller and eventually disappears as the disease advances. The “putaminal eye” was consistently observed in all patients aged 1–4 years and has not been reported in other metabolic or mitochondrial disorders, making it pathognomonic for MEGDEL syndrome.

DOUBLE-DOUGHNUT SIGN IN ACUTE NECROTIZING ENCEPHALOPATHY
The “double-doughnut” sign refers to a distinctive MRI appearance comprising symmetrical thalamic T2/FLAIR hyperintensities with central blooming on gradient echo sequences—consistent with hemorrhagic foci—and restricted diffusion on DWI (Fig. 9). This imaging pattern reflects cytotoxic edema and microhemorrhages secondary to vascular permeability and direct neuronal injury. Consequently, a wide spectrum of conditions, such as viral infections (dengue, Japanese encephalitis, herpes simplex, rabies, West Nile virus) and genetic or metabolic disorders, can produce this appearance, making clinical-laboratory correlation essential. Although not pathognomonic, this sign is highly suggestive of dengue encephalitis, particularly in patients with acute febrile illness, neurological deficits, and thrombocytopenia.

DOUBLE PANDA SIGN, LENTIFORM FORK, AND SPLIT THALAMUS SIGN IN WILSON’S DISEASE
The “double panda sign” in Wilson’s disease is observed on T2/FLAIR axial images of the midbrain. This sign consists of two components: the “face of the giant panda” and the “face of the miniature panda.” The “face of the giant panda” sign is characterized by a hyperintense signal in the tegmentum, with the hypointense red nuclei and substantia nigra forming the panda’s eyes and ears, respectively, and the hypointense superior colliculi forming the chin (Fig. 10), a finding highly suggestive of Wilson’s disease. The “face of the miniature panda” sign is characterized by a hyperintense signal in the dorsal pons with sparing of the central tegmental tracts (Fig. 10).
In contrast, the lentiform fork sign is a non-specific finding seen in various conditions that cause metabolic acidosis, such as uremic encephalopathy, Wilson disease, Leigh disease, and Kearns-Sayre syndrome. It results from abnormal signal involving the external and internal capsules and medullary laminae, which divides the lentiform nucleus into the putamen and the internal and external globus pallidus.
The split thalamus sign, also known as the “onion sign,” is seen on T2/FLAIR images as a hyperintense internal medullary lamina that clearly separates the medial and lateral thalamic nuclei (Fig. 11).

THALAMIC L SIGN IN PARTIAL PROLONGED HYPOXIC ISCHEMIC INJURY
The thalamic L-shaped (Fig. 12) sign is due to the involvement of the thalamic reticular nucleus (abutting the posterior limb of the internal capsule), the pulvinar nucleus, and the lateral geniculate nucleus. This involvement manifests as a hyperintense signal on axial T2/FLAIR sequences. The sign indicates a partial, prolonged hypoxic-ischemic injury in neonates but can be more pronounced in those with a combined hypoglycemic/hypoxic-ischemic injury.

OWL EYE OR PIGLET SIGN IN OSMOTIC DEMYELINATION SYNDROME
The “piglet sign” is a distinct MRI finding described in early-stage central pontine myelinolysis, a subtype of osmotic demyelination syndrome. It appears as a symmetric T2-hyperintense lesion in the central pons at the level of the middle cerebellar peduncles. This lesion spares the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts, creating a configuration that resembles a piglet’s face (Fig. 13): the spared tracts form the “snout,” the internal carotid arteries represent the “eyes,” the fourth ventricle the “mouth,” and the temporal lobes the “ears.” This sign is most conspicuous during the first week after symptom onset. As the disease progresses, it may evolve into the more classic “trident sign,” which is characterized by a hyperintense signal in the transverse pontine fibers with sparing of the corticospinal tracts.

TIGROID PATTERN IN AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE SPASTIC ATAXIA OF CHARLEVOIX-SAGUENAY
In autosomal recessive spastic ataxia of Charlevoix-Saguenay (ARSACS), brain MRI reveals a characteristic “tigroid pattern” in the pons on axial T2/FLAIR images. This pattern appears as horizontal linear hypointensities involving the pons (Fig. 14), which correspond to abnormally thickened transverse pontocerebellar fibers. This finding, along with superior vermian atrophy and bulky pons, is a key imaging clue for the diagnosis of ARSACS, especially in the presence of the classic clinical triad—spasticity, cerebellar ataxia, and peripheral neuropathy.

SANDWICH SIGN IN KRABBE DISEASE AND GIANT AXONAL NEUROPATHY
The “sandwich sign” on MRI refers to the layered appearance of the dentate nucleus, seen in the infantile form of Krabbe disease and in giant axonal neuropathy (GAN). This layered appearance results from a central T2-hypointense dentate nucleus “sandwiched” between a medially T2-hyperintense dentate hilus and peripherally T2-hyperintense deep cerebellar white matter (Figs. 15, 16). This pattern reflects demyelination with associated gliosis.
Although the “sandwich sign” appears similar in both Krabbe disease and GAN, their pathophysiologies differ. Clinical and other imaging features help differentiate these two conditions.
Krabbe disease is a lysosomal storage disorder caused by galactocerebrosidase enzyme deficiency, which results in progressive demyelination of the central nervous system and variable involvement of the peripheral nervous system. This sign is considered distinctive for early-onset forms and may appear as early as the first month of life. Other MRI features in early infantile Krabbe include signal changes in the deep and periventricular white matter, posterior limb of internal capsule, corpus callosum, brainstem corticospinal tracts, and deep cerebellar white matter; T2 hypointensity of the thalami; and thickening of optic nerves and chiasma. Variable cerebral, basal ganglia, and thalamic atrophy may also be seen.
GAN results from mutations in the gene encoding gigaxonin, which is involved in degrading protein substrates, resulting in aggregates of abnormal filaments in giant axons. Classic clinical features include early-onset polyneuropathy, cerebellar signs, and characteristic kinky or woolly hair. Other imaging findings include white matter demyelination that may involve periventricular and deep white matter in fronto-parietal, parieto-occipital lobes, corticospinal tracts, brainstem, and cerebellum.

CONCLUSION
In pediatric neuroradiology, imaging signs aid in recognizing complex non-congenital pathologies, thereby facilitating a timely and accurate diagnosis.
