Assessing neurocognitive outcomes in PTSD: a multilevel meta-analytical approach
ABSTRACT
Background: Evidence supporting the association between posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and cognitive impairment is accumulating. However, less is known about which factors influence this association.
Objective: The aims of this meta-analysis were to (1) elucidate the association between PTSD and a broad spectrum of cognitive impairment, including the risk of developing neurocognitive disorder (NCD) later in life, using a multilevel meta-analytic approach, and (2) identify potential moderating factors of this association by examining the effects of age (20–39, 40–59, 60+), study design (cross-sectional or longitudinal), study population (war-exposed populations/veterans or the general population), neurocognitive outcome assessed (i.e. a diagnosis of NCD or type of cognitive domain as classified according to A Compendium of Neuropsychological tests), gender (≥50% women or <50% women), study quality (high vs low), type of PTSD measure (self-report or clinical diagnosis), as well as the presence of comorbidities such as traumatic brain injury (TBI), depression, and substance use (all coded as either present or absent).
Method: Peer-reviewed studies on this topic were extracted from PubMed and Web of Science with predetermined keywords and criteria. In total, 53 articles met the criteria. Hedge‘s g effect sizes were calculated for each study and a three-level random effect meta-analysis conducted.
Results: After accounting for publication bias, the results suggested a significant association between PTSD and cognitive impairment, g = 0.13 (95% CI: 0.10–0.17), indicating a small effect. This association was consistent across all examined moderators, including various neurocognitive outcomes, age, gender, study design, study population, study quality, type of PTSD measure, and comorbidities such as depression, substance use, and TBI.
Conclusions: These findings collectively suggest that PTSD is associated with both cognitive impairment and NCD. This emphasizes the need for early intervention (including prevention strategies) of PTSD, alongside monitoring cognitive function in affected individuals.
International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) registration number: CRD42021219189, date of registration: 02.01.2021.
HIGHLIGHTS
The current meta-analysis was the first to comprehensively investigate the association between posttraumatic disorder (PTSD) and a broad spectrum of cognitive impairment, including the risk of developing neurocognitive disorder (NCD), using a less biased multilevel meta-analytical approach.
The results showed a significant association between PTSD and cognitive impairment, across all neurocognitive outcomes, age groups, genders, study designs, study populations, study qualities, types of PTSD measures, and the presence of comorbidities such as depression, traumatic brain injury or substance use, highlighting the broad effect of PTSD on cognitive function.
These findings emphasize the need for early intervention (including prevention strategies) of PTSD, alongside monitoring cognitive function in affected individuals.
Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a mental disorder that occurs in a subset of individuals exposed to traumatic events where the individual’s life or well-being or someone else’s is threatened (American Psychiatric Association,). PTSD is characterized by a severe reaction to trauma exposure, which impairs daily functioning (Iribarren et al.,). The diagnostic criteria for PTSD include a constellation of intrusion symptoms, persistent avoidance, marked changes in arousal and reactivity, and negative alterations in mood and cognition. The negative alterations in cognitive function, inherent in the diagnostic criteria, include memory and concentration problems (American Psychiatric Association,). Previous meta-analyses have shown that individuals with PTSD have a broad cognitive impairment that extends beyond these diagnostic criteria, such as impaired attention, processing speed, working memory and executive functioning (Qureshi et al.,; Schuitevoerder et al.,; Scott et al.,). These findings are concerning given that cognitive impairment may reduce the treatment response in psychosocial PTSD treatment since many treatments (e.g. cognitive behavioral therapy) require learning and acquisition of new skills (Falconer et al.,; Samuelson,; Wild & Gur,).
PTSD symptoms are thought to arise from stress-induced changes in the brain (Bremner,). Neuroanatomical correlates of PTSD include the limbic system (i.e. the hippocampus, the amygdala and the cingulate cortex) and the prefrontal cortex (Liberzon & Sripada,). In response to threats or emotionally arousing stimuli, PTSD is characterized by hyperactivation of the amygdala, i.e. ‘the brain's threat alarm’ can become overactivated. Concurrently, there is a hypoactivation of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, impairing its ability to properly inhibit the amygdala, i.e. ‘malfunctioning of the threat alarm's brake’ (Liberzon & Sripada,; Patel et al.,). This can lead to prolonged elevated levels of the stress hormone cortisol, which could potentially damage brain regions (e.g. hippocampus and prefrontal cortex) critical for cognitive function (i.e. memory, processing speed and executive function) (Arnsten,; McEwen et al.,). Supporting this notion, hippocampal atrophy (i.e. shrinkage of the hippocampus, a brain area essential for memory) has been observed among individuals with PTSD (Chao et al.,; Sapolsky,).
Recurrent stress, such as that experienced in PTSD, has been found to increase the risk of developing neurocognitive disorders (NCD) later in life. In line with this, PTSD has been identified as a risk factor for developing NCD (Greenberg et al.,; Günak et al.,). NCD is defined as cognitive impairment with substantial impairment in one or more cognitive domains which disrupt daily life (American Psychiatric Association,) and includes conditions formerly referred to as dementia in the DSM 4th edition text revision (DSM-IVTR) (American Psychiatric Association,; Escher et al.,; Nilaweera et al.,; American Psychiatric Association,). Due to longer life expectancy and increased exposure to cardiovascular risk factors (e.g. diabetes, midlife hypertension and obesity, physical inactivity, and smoking), NCD rates are expected to triple by 2050, representing one of the greatest public health challenges of the century (Livingston et al.,; Prince et al.,). Considering that risk factors for NCD can be altered throughout life, and that approximately a third of NCD cases could have been prevented or delayed (Livingston et al.,; Salthouse,), it is vital to investigate modifiable factors in the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment. This could potentially help reducing the impact of NCD on individuals and communities.
The current study aims to elucidate the relationship between PTSD and cognitive impairment. Previous meta-analyses explored the link between PTSD and cognitive impairment either without including an NCD diagnosis (Qureshi et al.,; Schuitevoerder et al.,; Scott et al.,) or focused solely on individuals with an established NCD diagnosis (Günak et al.,). Furthermore, previous research (Günak et al.,) has not examined the role of important moderators, such as depression and substance use. Expanding on the previous work, this study provides a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term effects of PTSD on cognitive function, including pre-clinical (cognitive impairment) to clinical (NCD) stages, and examines a broder range of clinically important moderators – namely age, study design, study population, neurocognitive outcomes, type of PTSD measurement, gender, study quality, and comorbidities such as traumatic brain injury (TBI), substance use and depression – that may influence the relationship between PTSD and cognitive impairment. Furthermore, the current study is the first to employ a multilevel design with maximum likelihood estimation to examine the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment, which reduces bias more effectively compared to traditional meta-analytic approaches (Cheung,). Unlike conventional approaches that might select or average effect sizes from each study, our approach accounts for heterogeneity and complex relationships among multiple effect sizes within studies (Cheung,).

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration
The protocol for the present study was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, registration number CRD42021219189). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systemic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement (Moher et al.,) and the Cochrane Handbook for Systemic Reviews of Interventions reporting items for meta-analyses (Higgins & Green,) were used to design, execute and report the findings.

Study eligibility
Inclusion criteria were that the studies (1) examined the association between a PTSD diagnosis and cognitive function in human adults aged 18 and older; (2) used validated and/or well-described methods to determine clinically impairing levels of PTSD (e.g. diagnosis or clinical cutoff on well-established measure); (3) employed neuropsychological assessment to measure at least two different cognitive domains; (4) used a clinical diagnosis of NCD (e.g. from medical records, clinical interviews and neuropsychological assessment); (5) included a comparison group consisting of individuals without a PTSD diagnosis or clinical levels of PTSD symptoms; (6) were published in a peer-reviewed journal in English; and (7) provided sufficient data to calculate effect sizes.
Both prospective and retrospective studies were included. When two manuscripts presented findings from overlapping datasets, the one of most relevance to our research question or the one with the greater number of subjects was included. For studies in which specialized subgroups were examined (e.g. individuals diagnosed with HIV (Rubin et al.,) or schizophrenia (Fan et al.,)), analyses comparing groups most accurately addressing the research question (i.e. comparison of participants with and without PTSD in terms of cognitive impairment) were included in the meta-analysis. Case studies and studies that only examined self-reported cognitive function (without neuropsychological assessment) or trauma (i.e. studies focusing on PTSD symptoms without a clinical cutoff) were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy
In a comprehensive search initially conducted in December 2019 and updated in April 2024, all research articles on the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment in English conducted on humans indexed in PubMed/Medline and Web of Science meeting the aforementioned inclusion criteria were identified. No publication date limitations were set. The search was performed by investigators with research experience in the field of PTSD and cognitive impairment (S.G.A., H.L.L.). Titles, abstracts and articles were reviewed to identify relevant manuscripts. The search terms included combinations of: PTSD, posttraumatic stress disorder, cognitive function, cognitive impairment, cognitive decline, cognitive dysfunction, neurocognitive disorder, Lewy Body dementia, vascular dementia, frontotemporal dementia and Alzheimer's disease. The detailed search terms and filters are provided in the Supplementary material S1. Reference lists of the included studies and published reviews of the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment were also searched for relevant studies to increase the comprehensiveness of the search.

Data extraction
Data for this meta-analysis were extracted from each study independently (S.G.A., H.L.L.) according to protocol. Data extraction variables included bibliographic information, study design, demographic information on participants (i.e. age, gender, ethnicity, education, study population), PTSD status, cognitive impairment, primary findings, statistical information needed to calculate effect sizes, and covariates in the analyses. Any conflicts regarding data extraction were reviewed and resolved by consensus with two additional reviewers (T.H., H.L.H.J.). Data from fully adjusted models were used to calculate effect sizes.
The Cochrane Handbook for Systemic Reviews of Interventions reporting items for meta-analyses (Higgins & Green,) and Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (Owens et al.,) guidelines were used to assess study quality. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for case-control, cohort and cross-sectional design was coded (by S.G.A., H.L.L, H.L.H.J) for all studies to assess the risk of bias within studies (Wells et al.,). Adequate reproducibility was found when replicating the NOS assessments (S.G.A., H.L.H.J), with 76.7% agreement among the raters.
Since either high or low scores can be indicative of cognitive impairment, depending on the neuropsychological test, all analyses were adjusted such that positive effect sizes consistently indicate greater cognitive impairment for the PTSD group compared to controls.

Moderators
To gain a deeper understanding of how various factors may influence the relationship between PTSD and cognitive impairment, moderator analyses were conducted. In these analyses, moderator variables were treated as categorical.

Age
Given the increased risk for cognitive impairment with age (Morovic et al.,), we classified the studies into distinct chronological age groups based on the mean age of their samples: early adulthood (20–39-years-old), middle adulthood (40–59-years-old), and old age (60 years and older) (Lachman,).

Gender
Since women may be likelier to develop both PTSD and NCD (Christiansen & Berke,; Günak et al.,), gender was included as a moderator and coded based on the proportion of women in each study, ≥ 50% or < 50% women. One reason for why women might be likelier to develop PTSD is that they are likelier than men to experience sexual assault (Tolin & Foa,), which is strongly associated with PTSD (Resnick et al.,).

To capture both short-term and long-term effects of PTSD on cognitive function, including the development of NCD, the included studies were coded based on their study design as either longitudinal or cross-sectional. Longitudinal studies can provide insights into changes in cognitive function over time, i.e. whether cognitive function stays stable, worsens or improves over time (see Table 1 for the follow-up duration of each longitudinal study). Meanwhile, cross-sectional studies offer a snapshot of cognitive function at a single point in time. Examining study design as a moderator helps determine if the duration and timing of data collection influences the strength or direction of the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment.

Study population
The study population was categorized into war-exposed individuals/veterans and the general population. Most research in this field focuses on veterans, who have higher prevalence rates of PTSD compared to many other subgroups. Therefore, it is important to determine whether the effects of PTSD on cognitive function are comparable in the general public (which includes different types of trauma exposures and clinical profiles), or if they are specific to characteristics associated with veterans (Scott et al.,).

Traumatic brain injury
TBI (also called concussion), including head injuries, is common among veterans and can also be a consequence of traffic-related accidents, falls, sports injuries, etc. Since TBI is a risk factor for developing both cognitive impairment and NCD (Barman et al.,; Shively et al.,), it was included as a moderator in this meta-analysis (coded as present or absent).

Depression
Depression (coded as present or absent) is a common comorbidity of PTSD and given its association with cognitive impairment and NCD (Cohn-Schwartz et al.,; Kuring et al.,), it was included as a moderator.

Substance use
Since substance use (coded as present or absent) is common among individuals with PTSD, as well as individually associated with cognitive impairment (Hua et al.,; Kutash et al.,), it was included as a moderator.

Study quality
To assess the impact of study quality on the association between PTSD and cognitive function, we conducted a moderator analysis, utilizing the NOS to rate the quality of the included studies. Initially, only studies with NOS scores of 7 or higher were considered high quality. Due to the limited number (11%) of studies meeting this criterion, we adjusted the threshold to include studies with scores of 6 or higher, thereby encompassing 28% of the total studies.

Neurocognitive outcomes
Informed by previous research on how PTSD can affect various cognitive domains and the risk of developing NCD (Günak et al.,; Qureshi et al.,; Schuitevoerder et al.,; Scott et al.,), neurocognitive outcomes were included as a moderator in the current study. PTSD may influence these neurocognitive outcomes differently, leading to varying degrees of cognitive impairment.
The neurocognitive outcomes in the present study included a diagnosis of NCD (i.e. either a clinical diagnosis of major NCD or mild cognitive impairment, see the supplementary material for more details), as well as the cognitive domains assessed in the retrieved studies. Neuropsychological tests were classified into cognitive domains based on the classification system of the Compendium of Neuropsychological Tests (Sherman & Spreen,): general cognitive function, which included broad-scale measures of cognitive functioning (e.g. Mini Mental State Examination, Montreal Cognitive Assessment); attention (e.g. Trail Making Test); working memory (e.g. WAIS-Digit Span); processing speed (e.g. Digit Symbol); memory (e.g. Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test); executive function (e.g. Controlled Oral Word Association Test, Stroop test); visuospatial perception (e.g. Judgement of Line Orientation, Block Design). Cognitive domains assessed by neuropsychological tests, which were underrepresented in the analyses, were added to the general cognitive function domain (i.e. language tests, motor function and time perception). See Supplementary Table S1 for the complete classification of the neuropsychological tests.

Statistical analysis
Effect sizes were calculated for each reported analysis in the eligible studies. Given that Cohen’s d tends to overestimate effect sizes in small samples, Hedge’s g correction formula was used to decrease this bias (Borenstein et al.,; Hedges,). Data were converted into Hedge's g effect sizes using the esc package in R (Lüdecke,). As is customary, effect sizes of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 were considered small, medium and large, respectively (Cohen,).
All retrieved studies used multiple neuropsychological tests to assess various cognitive domains. To include these multiple findings within each study, we conducted a three-level meta-analysis using the maximum likelihood method to estimate heterogeneity and account for dependency between the effect sizes. This statistical approach is preferred over traditional meta-analytic procedures, such as averaging effect sizes across studies or selecting one effect size per study, as it is less biased (Cheung,).
In the three-level meta-analytic model, we assumed that individual effect sizes (random effect at level 2) were nested within studies (random effect at level 3). Heterogeneity was calculated using the I2 statistic, a measure of the variance attributed to differences between the studies (0% = none, 25% = low, 50% = moderate, and 75% = high heterogeneity) (Thorlund et al.,). The fixed effects model analyses revealed high inter-study heterogeneity (I2 = 86.42%). Subsequently, a random effects model was used to account for this heterogeneity. Confidence intervals (CI; 95%) and p-values (significance threshold set at α = .05) were calculated. The metafor package in R (Viechtbauer,) was used to perform the three-level random effect meta-analysis (the rma.mv function).
The Egger’s test was utilized to assess publication bias (Egger et al.,), and Duval and Tweedie’s trim and fill analysis was employed to estimate the adjusted effect size (Duval & Tweedie,). Additionally, Rosenthal's fail-safe N was calculated to estimate the number of missing studies with non-significant results needed to nullify the findings of the current meta-analysis (Rosenthal,), ensuring the robustness of the results. The fail-safe N threshold was set to 5*k + 10, where k represents the number of effect sizes (k = 377) included in the current study (Rosenthal,; Shi et al.,).

Fifty-three studies were included in the meta-analyses with a pooled sample size of 5,709,547 participants across all the studies and an overall of 376 analyses examining the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment. The three-level model provided a significantly better fit than a two-level model, as indicated by lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values, and a significant likelihood ratio test (χ2 =  108.66, p < .0001). In the three-level model, the pooled effect size across the studies was g = 0.30, p < .0001, 95% CI (0.23–0.37), indicating a small to medium effect (Table 2). The finding suggests greater cognitive impairment among individuals with PTSD compared to controls.

Moderator analyses
All coefficients for the moderator analyses are displayed in Table 2. None of the moderators (i.e. study design, study population, age group, gender, neurocognitive outcome, depression, TBI, type of PTSD assessment, or substance use) significantly moderated the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment. However, significant effects were found within all subgroups, with effect sizes of small to medium magnitudes (see Table 2).

Publication bias
The Egger's test, which was used to assess publication bias in the main analysis for the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment, was significant, indicating a publication bias (t[374] = 3.08, p = .002). Therefore, Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure was employed to estimate the actual effect size. The initial pooled effect across the studies was g = 0.30, but after accounting for publication bias, it was g = 0.13, p < .0001, 95% CI (0.10–0.17), indicating a small effect size. The robustness of the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment was supported by Rosenthal's fail-safe N, which indicated that 9,531,366 null findings would be needed to nullify the effect, suggesting negligible risk of bias (Rosenthal,; Shi et al.,).

Discussion
This is the first meta-analysis, to our knowledge, to comprehensively investigate the association between PTSD and a broad spectrum of cognitive impairment, including the risk of developing NCD later in life. Additionally, this is the first meta-analysis to explore this topic employing a multilevel meta-analytic approach, which is less susceptible to bias than traditional meta-analytic methods (Cheung,). The objectives of the current meta-analysis were firstly, to elucidate the relationship between PTSD and cognitive impairment, and secondly, to identify moderating factors for cognitive impairment among individuals with PTSD.
In line with previous findings (Qureshi et al.,; Schuitevoerder et al.,; Scott et al.,), we found that PTSD was associated with a higher risk of cognitive impairment. This finding remained robust regardless of the moderators explored, i.e. individuals with PTSD were at an increased risk of cognitive impairment compared to controls across all neurocognitive outcomes, ages, genders, study designs, study populations, study qualities, types of PTSD measurements, and presence of comorbidities such as depression, substance use, and TBI. This means we did not achieve our aim of identifying moderating factors for cognitive impairment among individuals with PTSD. However, we were able to elucidate the relationship between PTSD and cognitive function further, demonstrating a consistent link to cognitive impairment that persisted across all explored moderators. The findings suggest that PTSD is associated with a higher risk of developing NCD later in life, which is consistent with a previous meta-analysis on the topic (Günak et al.,). There is also a possibility that individuals with pre-existing neurocognitive vulnerabilities – such as limited cognitive ability, low educational levels, and hippocampal atrophy – may be likelier to develop PTSD. These factors, which also predispose individuals to NCD, might heighten the risk of subsequent cognitive impairment (Elias et al.,; Günak et al.,).
Consistently, earlier meta-analyses have also found that PTSD is associated with a higher risk of impairments in specific cognitive domains such as attention, processing speed, working memory and executive functioning (Qureshi et al.,; Schuitevoerder et al.,; Scott et al.,). The inclusion of NCD in our study (not explored in previous studies (Qureshi et al.,; Schuitevoerder et al.,; Scott et al.,)) may have contributed to the smaller effect sizes observed in our meta-analysis compared to those reported by e.g. Scott and colleagues, whose meta-analysis is the most similar to ours in terms of study diversity and total number of included studies (Scott et al.,). This inclusion could potentially have diluted the specific neurocognitive effects of PTSD, as cognitive impairment may arise from both conditions. Another reason for the smaller effect sizes in our study compared to theirs might be differences in search criteria. Scott et al. searched for cognitive domain-specific keywords (e.g. memory, attention) and traumatic stress in addition to PTSD. Meanwhile, we excluded studies examining traumatic stress and did not search for cognitive domain-specific keywords. Our keywords were more targeted toward PTSD and more broadly defined cognitive function, cognitive impairment and NCD.
Consistent with the findings of Scott et al., neither age, type of PTSD measurement, depression, TBI, or substance use, significantly moderated the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment. The observation that PTSD was associated with cognitive impairment in both veterans and among the general population was also in line with previous results (Günak et al.,; Scott et al.,). Regarding gender differences, our findings diverge from those of Scott et al., who found that studies with a larger portion of men exhibited greater cognitive impairment. Conversely, Günak et al. found an increased risk of developing NCD among women with PTSD. The inclusion of both cognitive impairment and NCD in our study may have contributed to the absence of significant gender differences in our results.
While previous meta-analyses (that we know of) have not examined study design as a potential moderator of the relationship between PTSD and cognitive impairment, our observation that this association remains robust regardless of study design, provides new insights. This indicates that individuals with PTSD consistently exhibit cognitive impairments, regardless of whether a study collects data at a single timepoint or over a long period of time. This observation highlights the importance of early intervention and ongoing support for individuals with PTSD to mitigate potential development of cognitive impairment and NCD over time. To minimize patient burden, the cognitive function of individuals with PTSD could be monitored via self-report, since studies have found that self-reported memory complaints may predict NCD in later-life (Mitchell et al.,; Rönnlund et al.,). However, to our knowledge, this has not been studied yet among individuals with PTSD. Clinically, these findings underscore the importance of addressing the risk of cognitive impairment in individuals with PTSD. To mitigate this risk, it is essential to implement regular monitoring of cognitive function as part of standard care for PTSD patients. Additionally, targeted interventions should focus on promoting healthy sleep hygiene and encouraging engagement in regular physical and social activities (Livingston et al.,; Miller,). These approaches address common challenges faced by individuals with PTSD, such as disrupted sleep and social isolation, which are also recognized risk factors for cognitive impairment (Beidel et al.,; Han et al.,). By integrating these strategies into clinical practice, healthcare providers can better support the long-term cognitive and mental health of individuals with PTSD. Our findings also highlight the importance of future research focusing on identifying modifiable risk factors for cognitive impairment and NCD among individuals with PTSD, as well as potential treatment targets.
The strengths of the current study lie in its use of the three-level meta-analytic approach, and its inclusion of a large number of analyses and a broad range of studies examining a wide spectrum of cognitive impairment. Additionally, we only included studies that employed validated methods to clinically diagnose PTSD or established cutoffs shown to be indicative of a probable diagnosis. Furthermore, the current research encompasses all age groups and maintains a balanced representation of veterans and the general population in its analyses. This inclusive approach enhances the generalizability of the findings to the broader population compared to some previous meta-analyses (Qureshi et al.,; Schuitevoerder et al.,). When meta-analyses in this field consist primarily of veterans, they tend to overrepresent men (with comorbidities individually increasing the risk of cognitive impairment and NCD, such as TBI) and represent a different type of trauma occurring under special circumstances, which could potentially affect the study results (Scott et al.,).
Limitations of this study include that studies assessing cognitive function may exclude participants with TBI, potentially leading to a selection bias that could affect the generalizability of our findings regarding the moderating role of TBI. Furthermore, we could not control for anxiety, a common comorbidity of PTSD that may also elevate the risk of cognitive impairment, since only two of the included studies controlled for anxiety. One meta-analysis found an increased risk of NCD among individuals with depression and anxiety but not among individuals with PTSD (Kuring et al.,). In the current study, we found that even when controlling for depression, PTSD was associated with cognitive impairment. This contrasts the previous study (Kuring et al.,), which only included four studies on the association between PTSD and NCD, possibly limiting its ability to fully capture the impact of PTSD on cognitive function due to its narrow scope and small sample size. Moving forward, it is important that future studies control for both anxiety and depression and explore their potential roles in the association between PTSD and cognitive function. This approach would provide a clearer understanding of the mechanisms at play, which could inform therapeutic targets, and enhance the generalizability and applicability of the findings. Another limitation is that only two studies controlled for pre-morbid intelligence and one for attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, which Scott et al. found affected their effect sizes. Additionally, we did not control for antipsychotic use. One study (Roughead et al.,) found that veterans who were administered antipsychotics had a higher NCD diagnosis rate irrespective of PTSD status, but this could not be explored further due to lack of information on medication status in the studies.
Moreover, our findings exhibited considerable heterogeneity, the origins of which remain unclear. This heterogeneity persisted despite conducting sensitivity analyses, an issue also noted in previous meta-analyses in the field (Günak et al.,; Scott et al.,). To address this, the development of consensus guidelines on key methodological aspects – such as diagnostic criteria, control of confounders, and the inclusion of large, non-biased samples – is recommended to enhance the quality and consistency of findings across studies. As previously mentioned, only 11% of the included studies in this meta-analysis were categorized as having a low risk of bias (NOS score ≥ 7) (Lo et al.,). Additionally, the presence of a publication bias in this meta-analysis underscores the importance of pre-registering studies and publishing all results, including those with negative or null findings, to provide a more balanced view of the evidence.
Lastly, like many neuropsychological studies, ours contends with the limitation that neuropsychological tests often assess multiple cognitive domains simultaneously, complicating precise categorization into single domains. For example, impairments in executive function can adversely affect performance across various other domains (Harvey,). To apply a structured approach to classifying the wide array of tests, we used the Compendium of Neuropsychological Tests (Sherman & Spreen,) as a guide.
Our findings indicate that future meta-analyses on the association between PTSD and cognitive impairment might consider including NCD diagnoses, and a diverse range of studies in terms of populations, age groups and types of trauma. Such inclusions could expand upon our findings and provide deeper insights into the long-term effects of PTSD on cognitive function. The field would benefit from recruiting more women to enhance gender diversity and implementing performance validity tests to ensure that neuropsychological assessments accurately reflects participants’ true cognitive function, rather than their effort (Tierney et al.,; Wisdom et al.,). Future meta-analyses should stratify the results by gender, and account for the potential confounding variables mentioned above. It is also important to match the control group to the participants with PTSD on age, given the increased risk of cognitive impairment with age (Morovic et al.,). Additionally, they could include the age at which participants were exposed to the traumatic experience as well as their subsequent PTSD duration since trauma could have more profound and lasting effects on the developing brain (Nilaweera et al.,; Scott et al.,). Furthermore, future longitudinal research should explore whether self-reported cognitive complaints can predict NCD later in life among individuals with PTSD. Lastly, conducting longitudinal intervention studies is essential to determine whether the risk of cognitive impairment or NCD later in life can be mitigated among individuals with PTSD.

Conclusions
The results indicate that individuals with PTSD are at an increased risk of cognitive impairment, including the development of NCD later in life, compared to controls. This association remained robust regardless of the explored moderators, as it was consistently observed across all neurocognitive outcomes, age groups, genders, study designs, study populations, study qualities, types of PTSD measurements, and the presence of comorbidities such as TBI, depression and substance use.
