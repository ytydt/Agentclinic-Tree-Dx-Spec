Exo-hydrogel therapy: a revolutionary approach to managing diabetic complications
This review systematically examines the transformative potential of Exosome-hydrogel (Exo-gel) composite systems in combating diabetic complications, with a particular emphasis on their macromolecular interactions and therapeutic implications. By synergistically integrating the bioactive cargo of Exos with the structural tunability of polysaccharide-based hydrogels, this advanced biomaterial platform addresses critical limitations in conventional therapies through the spatiotemporally controlled delivery of regenerative factors. We highlight how the dynamic interplay between natural biopolymers and exosomal surface proteins enhances extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling while maintaining bioactive stability, a crucial advancement for chronic diabetic conditions. A comprehensive analysis reveals that Exo-gel systems demonstrate therapeutic efficacy in three key pathological contexts: (1) neural regeneration via Schwann cell modulation, (2) angiogenesis restoration through VEGF/VEGFR signaling pathways, and (3) chronic wound healing mediated by macrophage polarization. Our critical appraisal of preclinical evidence positions Exo-gel technology as a paradigm-shifting approach in diabetes care, offering solutions to current challenges in sustained drug delivery and targeted tissue regeneration, while outlining future translational pathways for bioengineered therapeutic systems.

Introduction
Diabetes mellitus (DM), a chronic metabolic disorder with pandemic prevalence, poses a formidable threat to global health. Current epidemiological data from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) indicate 537 million diagnosed cases in 2021, projected to reach 783 million by 2045. As the 8th leading cause of mortality (6.2 million annual deaths) according to the 2023 Lancet Global Burden of Disease Study, DM complications—including cardiovascular disease, renal failure, and lower limb amputations—impose a dual burden of reduced quality of life and escalated healthcare costs. Specifically, annual medical expenditures for Americans with DM are 2.35-fold higher than those without, underscoring the disease’s profound socioeconomic impact.
DM complications encompass microvascular disorders, macrovascular pathologies, and neuropathy (peripheral, autonomic, and focal types). Notably, 30–40% of DM patients develop diabetic retinopathy (DR), the leading cause of adult blindness, while diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) contribute to 120,000–250,000 non-traumatic lower limb amputations annually. Current clinical management relies on pharmacological glycemic control (e.g., insulin, metformin, SGLT-2 inhibitors) and surgical interventions, yet these approaches face critical limitations. Antidiabetic drugs delay disease progression but fail to reverse established tissue damage and may induce adverse effects such as hypoglycemia and nephrotoxicity. Surgical procedures, though lifesaving for severe DFUs or DR, are associated with high recurrence rates, infection risks, and post-operative functional impairment. Conventional wound dressings facilitate healing by maintaining a moist microenvironment. Yet, their passive nature limits active modulation of chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired angiogenesis in diabetic wounds, resulting in suboptimal efficacy. Even advanced options like silver sulfadiazine or acellular fish skin grafts, though effective in burns and trauma for epithelialization and reduced dressing changes, remain unvalidated in diabetic microenvironments due to non-adaptive designs and limited clinical evidence. Similarly, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) enhances granulation via mechanical stress and fluid dynamics, yet its inability to integrate real-time biomarker feedback limits responsiveness to multifactorial diabetic wound cascades.
Emerging antibacterial strategies, such as nanozyme-based therapies, offer promising alternatives by combining enzymatic reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation with material-driven antibacterial activity. For instance, Chang et al. demonstrated that single-atom nanozymes catalyze H2O2 into hydroxyl radicals, achieving > 99% bacterial eradication in Pseudomonas aeruginosa-infected wounds while accelerating epithelialization through redox modulation. However, these systems remain constrained by insufficient biocompatibility for prolonged use and limited integration with endogenous regenerative signals.
Recent advancements in biomaterial engineering, exemplified by electrospun polycaprolactone/gelatin bilayer nanofibers, have integrated antibacterial functions through static drug release mechanisms. For instance, Song et al. developed a polycaprolactone (PCL)-enrofloxacin (ENR)@polycaprolactone-gelatin (PG)-bleomycin (BLM) scaffold capable of synergistic bacterial inhibition and tumor suppression. Yet, its non-responsive drug release fails to adapt to the dynamic hyperglycemic microenvironment characterized by fluctuating inflammatory cytokines. Notably, micro-nano structured biomaterials-such as anisotropic electrospun membranes and 3D-printed microneedle arrays-demonstrate enhanced cell alignment and immunomodulatory effects in wound healing. However, these systems remain constrained by two critical challenges: (1) lack of precise spatiotemporal control over bioactive cargo delivery, particularly in achieving multi-stage drug release or real-time feedback to microenvironmental changes; and (2) single-axis responsiveness to pathological cues, limiting their adaptive regulation of multi-factorial pathological cascades.
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as promising bioactive agents for tissue repair due to their inherent roles in intercellular communication. Recent advances in EV engineering, as highlighted by Liu et al., demonstrate that mammalian EVs (MEVs) and bacterial EVs (BEVs) can be functionally enhanced through biological engineering, chemical modification, or physical hybridisation. These strategies augment EV capabilities in treating complex pathologies, including cancer and bone diseases. Wu et al. emphasize that EV-mediated transfer of osteogenic miRNAs coordinates bone regeneration by targeting Wnt/β-catenin and RANKL pathways, while endothelial-derived EVs enhance angiogenesis via miR-155 delivery. Nevertheless, standalone EV therapies face challenges such as enzymatic degradation, rapid clearance, and poor retention in irregular tissue geometries.
Conventional wound dressings facilitate healing by maintaining a moist microenvironment, but they lack active modulation of chronic inflammation and impaired angiogenesis. Even advanced options, nanozyme-based therapies or electrospun nanofibers, remain constrained by insufficient biocompatibility, non-responsive drug release, or an inability to adapt to dynamic hyperglycemic microenvironments. Recent innovations in biomaterial design have aimed to overcome these limitations through the development of engineered sustained-release systems. For instance, graphene oxide (GO)-hybridized hyaluronic acid hydrogels exhibit tunable drug release kinetics over 21 days through non-covalent interactions, enabling perivascular therapeutic delivery while maintaining biocompatibility—a strategy highly relevant to vascular complications in diabetes. However, as noted by Hu et al., most clinical hydrogel dressings exhibit passive therapeutic profiles, lacking dynamic responsiveness to the microenvironments of diabetic wounds. Similarly, such systems still lack endogenous bioactivity and multi-pathway regulatory capacity.
To address these limitations, exosome-hydrogel (Exo-gel) therapy represents a paradigm shift, combining EV bioactivity with hydrogel-mediated spatiotemporal control. The therapeutic supremacy of this platform stems from its three core delivery strategies: (i) in situ hybrid cross-linking for stimuli-triggered gelation and cavity-conformal encapsulation, (ii) post-preloading cross-linking for covalent Exo-polymer integration, and (iii) physical adsorption exploiting hydrogel swelling dynamics. These mechanisms collectively orchestrate spatiotemporal exo-release, bioactive cargo protection, and bidirectional molecular crosstalk—thereby establishing a paradigm-shifting approach to overcoming the enzymatic degradation, rapid clearance, and irregular tissue geometries inherent to diabetic complications active cargo protection, and bidirectional molecular crosstalk—thereby establishing a paradigm-shifting approach to overcoming the enzymatic degradation, rapid clearance, and irregular tissue geometries inherent to diabetic complications. Preclinical studies demonstrated that hypoxia-pretreated adipose-derived stem cell (ADSC)-derived Exo-embedded hydrogels increased the wound healing rate by approximately 30% and enhanced angiogenesis in rodent models. Further validate its programmable release kinetics (72-hour sustained VEGF delivery in vitro) and multifunctional regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment through antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and pro-angiogenic activities. This review systematically evaluates the therapeutic outcomes and translational potential of Exo-gel, providing a roadmap for next-generation diabetic complication management.

Exploring exos: miniature vesicles with massive potential
Exos are essential mediators of intercellular communication, comprising small vesicles with diameters ranging from approximately 30 to 150 nanometers. They play a critical role within the extracellular vesicle family, facilitating the transport of various biomolecules, including proteins, lipids, mRNA, and microRNA (miRNA). These substances can be transmitted between cells, thereby influencing the biological behavior of recipient cells. The characterization of Exos offers a novel approach to managing diabetic complications. Research suggests that Exos derived from islet beta cells may modulate immune responses, presenting a potential therapeutic strategy for individuals with DM. In wound healing and tissue regeneration associated with DM, Exos also contribute to tissue repair by delivering growth factors and signaling molecules. Beyond their functional roles in cellular communication and tissue healing processes related to DM, Exos also serve as significant disease biomarkers. They assist clinicians in detecting and monitoring the progression of DM-related complications. As Nobel laureate Harvey J. Alter aptly stated, “Significant progress in science frequently stems from a deep comprehension of the intricate details of nature.” A comprehensive investigation of Exo biology will likely yield transformative advancements in managing diabetic complications.

The biosynthesis of exos: generation, development, and secretion
The biogenesis of Exos initiates within the endosomal network of the cell. Initially, the plasma membrane invaginates to form early endosomes, which mature into late endosomes. During this maturation process, intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) are generated within the lumen of late endosomes through either endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent or ESCRT-independent pathways. These ILVs serve as precursors to Exos. As ILVs accumulate within late endosomes, commonly referred to as multivesicular bodies (MVBs), they face two potential fates: degradation through fusion with lysosomes or transport to the cell membrane. When MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane, the ILVs are released into the extracellular environment as Exos. This fusion process is mediated by various proteins, particularly the soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptors (SNAREs). Multiple factors, including cellular energy levels and the availability of specific signaling molecules, regulate it.
The molecular composition of Exos is characterized by significant diversity, which directly reflects their cellular origin. They contain a wide array of proteins, including tetraspanins (such as CD63, CD9, and CD81), heat shock proteins, and proteins involved in membrane transport and fusion. In addition to proteins, Exos also carry nucleic acids, including DNA fragments, mRNA, non-coding RNAs, and miRNAs, which can be delivered to recipient cells to modulate gene expression. The release of Exos is a regulated process influenced by various physiological and pathological conditions rather than serving merely as a disposal mechanism. Exo secretion can be induced by cellular stress, activation, or transformation. Furthermore, Exos are involved in various biological processes, including the modulation of immune responses, antigen presentation, and dissemination of pathogens. A comprehensive understanding of Exo biosynthesis is essential for elucidating their functions in both health and disease. This knowledge can potentially enhance the development of Exo-based therapeutics and diagnostic tools and provide deeper insights into intercellular communication (Fig. 1).
Technical Framework for Exo Isolation, Identification, and Functional Analysis: Integrating Biosynthetic Pathway Elucidation for Disease Diagnosis and Therapy
Exos are a class of small membrane vesicles secreted by most cells, playing a crucial role in intercellular communication. Typically ranging in diameter from 30 to 200 nanometers, Exos originate from the endocytosis of cells, formed through the endosome-lysosome pathway, and eventually released into the extracellular space via fusion with the cell membrane. The biosynthesis of Exos involves endocytosis, polyvesicle (MVB) formation, and MVB fusion with the cell membrane. Exo formation and secretion are regulated by various molecular mechanisms, including ESCRT-dependent and independent pathways, and small GTP enzymes belonging to the Rab family. The isolation, identification, and functional analysis of Exos provide technical support for their application in disease diagnosis, therapy, and biomarker development.

Exo isolation and characterization techniques: unveiling the secrets of cellular communication
The production of large quantities of high-purity Exos is crucial for managing complications associated with DM, particularly within the framework of Exo-gel therapy. Notably, the recently released Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV) 2023 guidelines by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) provide critical quality control standards for Exo preparation. This underscores the importance of a meticulous isolation process to ensure that the Exos obtained are pure and functionally intact. Consequently, researchers employ sophisticated methods to isolate these nanoscale particles, each presenting unique advantages and challenges.
Ultracentrifugation, regarded as the gold standard for Exo separation, is highly valued for its capacity to yield substantial quantities of pure Exos. This method employs differential centrifugation to isolate Exos based on their density and size. However, it can be labor-intensive and lead to Exo-aggregation, potentially impacting their biological properties. In contrast, size-exclusion chromatography offers a gentler approach that more effectively preserves Exo integrity. This technique utilizes porous beads to separate particles according to their hydrodynamic radius, allowing larger molecules to elute prior to smaller ones remaining within the column. Although this method effectively maintains Exo integrity, it typically produces lower overall yields than ultracentrifugation. Additionally, polymer-based precipitation has emerged as a viable method for Exo separation. This technique involves using a polymer solution that alters the solubility of Exos, facilitating their precipitation from the sample. While this approach is straightforward and scalable, it may also co-isolate other extracellular vesicles and proteins, necessitating subsequent purification steps.
Identifying isolated Exos is equally essential and necessitates applying various modern techniques. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) has revolutionized Exo research by enabling real-time observation and precise measurement of the size of minute particles in solution. NTA leverages the principles of light scattering and Brownian motion to track individual particles, yielding vital data on size distribution and concentration. This information is essential for quality control in therapeutic applications, ensuring the consistency and efficacy of Exo formulations. Additionally, electron microscopy, particularly transmission electron microscopy (TEM), provides unparalleled insights into the morphology of Exos. High-resolution images allow researchers to confirm the distinctive cup-shaped structure of individual Exos and evaluate their overall integrity. Visual validation is crucial for verifying the isolation processes’ effectiveness and ensuring the purity of the Exo preparations. Furthermore, Western blotting is essential for detecting specific protein markers associated with Exos. This enables researchers to identify and quantify proteins such as CD63, CD9, and TSG101, typically enriched in Exos. These markers confirm the identity of isolated vesicles as Exos and offer insights into their cellular origins and biological functions. The precision and reliability of separation and characterization methods are critical for maintaining the therapeutic potential of Exos. As research advances in Exo-based treatments for DM, it is imperative to enhance and standardize these methodologies to translate laboratory findings into clinical applications effectively. The consistent production of well-defined, high-quality Exos is essential for optimizing their use in regenerative medicine and targeted drug delivery for treating DM-related conditions.

Exos: versatile vesicles in biological processes
The functional variety of Exos extends beyond mere intercellular communication. These remarkable vesicles are associated with diverse physiological and pathological activities, including immunological regulation, antigen presentation, and the propagation of infections. Exos have been identified as essential components in numerous biological processes, ranging from embryonic development to cancer progression, due to their ability to modulate recipient cell behavior by transferring functional biomolecules. Their capacity to influence cellular functions emphasizes their significance in various physiological and pathological contexts.

Exo-Mediated transfer of molecular cargo among cells
Exos serve as essential conduits for cellular communication, facilitating the transmission of critical biological information between cells. These tiny vesicles function as sophisticated carriers, transporting a diverse range of molecular cargo across cells, including various RNA species and proteins. Exos are instrumental in modulating recipient cells’ functional properties and behaviors through intercellular exchange. Their capacity to convey intricate biological signals across cellular borders underscores their importance in maintaining tissue homeostasis and orchestrating cellular responses in multicellular organisms.

The role of exos in cellular homeostasis maintenance
Exos are vital for preserving cellular homeostasis by removing non-degradable compounds from the cell. This process is crucial for ensuring a stable and appropriate internal cellular environment, as it prevents the accumulation of potentially hazardous substances that the cell’s conventional degradation pathways cannot eliminate. By encapsulating and transporting non-degradable materials, Exos facilitate cellular detoxification, thereby protecting neurons from oxidative stress and toxicity. This process highlights the multifaceted role of Exos, emphasizing their importance in intercellular communication and the management of intracellular maintenance and waste.

Exosomal modulation of immune responses: diagnostic and therapeutic potential
The immune system’s intricate network of connections is significantly influenced by Exos. These multifunctional vesicles can regulate immune cell activity, orchestrating stimulatory and inhibitory effects on various immunological responses. By transmitting diverse molecular signals, Exos affect immune cells’ activation, proliferation, and differentiation, altering the overall immunological landscape. This regulatory potential is particularly evident in autoimmune disorders, where Exos may contribute to reestablishing immunological balance by inhibiting excessively active immune responses. Conversely, Exos can function in inflammatory conditions as mediators, either amplifying or diminishing the inflammatory cascade based on their biological origin and cargo content. The complex interplay between Exos and the immune system underscores their potential as diagnostic indicators and therapeutic targets for various immunologically mediated diseases.

The therapeutic potential of Exo-Based approaches in tissue regeneration and repair
Nanoscale vesicles exhibit a remarkable capacity to enhance cell migration and proliferation during tissue damage and regeneration. These are critical components of the intricate sequence of events necessary for effective tissue repair. Exos derived from various stem cell types possess unique properties that significantly improve the healing process. Notably, these Exos promote angiogenesis, forming new blood vessels essential for delivering oxygen and nutrients to healing tissues. Additionally, these Exos may boost the proliferation and differentiation of local progenitor cells, hence accelerating the regeneration process. By transporting various bioactive molecules, including growth factors, cytokines, and regulatory RNAs, Exos orchestrate a coordinated tissue response that aids in repairing damage and restoring functionality to injured areas.

Non-Invasive diagnosis and management using exos in medical practice
Exos contain various molecular components that can reflect their originating cells’ physiological or pathological states, thereby enhancing our capacity for early detection and monitoring of disease progression. In the context of DM, Exos extracted from patient samples may exhibit distinct alterations associated with specific clinical conditions, such as insulin resistance or beta-cell dysfunction. By analyzing these exosomal profiles, researchers and clinicians can gain critical insights into disease development mechanisms and potentially identify new therapeutic targets. The stability and presence of Exos in various biological fluids render them highly appealing as non-invasive diagnostic tools, which could fundamentally transform our approach to disease identification and treatment across a broad spectrum of medical conditions.

The role of exos in DM-Related complications
Recent advances in single-vesicle proteomics and miRNA sequencing have unveiled profound source-dependent heterogeneity in exosomal cargo composition, critically determining their therapeutic specificity and safety profiles in diabetic complications. For instance, Exos derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cells (hUCMSCs) exhibit a unique miRNA signature enriched with miR-181c, which suppresses TLR4/NF-κB signaling to resolve chronic inflammation in diabetic wounds. In contrast, platelet-rich plasma (PRP)-derived exosomal miR-26b-5p can inhibit the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-8 (MMP-8) and reduce the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), thereby promoting diabetic wound healing.
However, Exos from pathological microenvironments may exert adverse effects. High-glucose and oxLDL-stimulated human cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (HCMECs) secrete Exos enriched with NEDD4L, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that promotes IκBα and PPARγ degradation. This process enhances macrophage M1 polarization and oxLDL uptake, exacerbating diabetic atherosclerosis. Similarly, M1 macrophage-derived Exos containing METTL14 induce m6A modification of PAQR3 mRNA in glomerular endothelial cells, leading to mitochondrial oxidative stress and apoptosis, a key mechanism in diabetic nephropathy progression. However, batch-to-batch variability remains challenging: MSC-Exos from hypoxic vs. normoxic cultures show evident divergence in miR-210 levels, necessitating standardized preconditioning protocols to ensure therapeutic consistency.

The intricate regulation of exos in the inflammatory response of DM
In the context of DM, Exos exhibit an outstanding ability to impact the inflammatory cascade in diverse manners:

Anti-inflammatory effect
Exos derived from pancreatic islet beta cells have been shown to contain specific anti-inflammatory miRNAs. These miRNAs function as potent regulators, effectively suppressing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. As a result, they significantly contribute to reducing the overall inflammatory response associated with DM.

Pro-inflammatory potential
Conversely, certain Exos may include pro-inflammatory signaling molecules. Upon entering the bloodstream, these Exos can aggravate pre-existing inflammatory diseases. This exacerbation of inflammation may contribute to the onset and progression of various complications associated with DM.

Balancing feat
The interplay between anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory Exos establishes a complex regulatory network that profoundly influences the inflammatory state in DM patients, thereby affecting disease progression. This delicate balance is essential for modulating inflammation and its repercussions on the advancement of DM.

Tissue-specific effects
Different tissues may produce Exos with distinct inflammatory profiles. For instance, adipose tissue-derived Exos in obese diabetic individuals might carry different signaling molecules than those originating from muscle or liver cells.

The dual role of exos in diabetic vascular repair
Diabetic complications frequently manifest as vascular disorders, particularly atherosclerosis and microvascular disease. Exos play a multifaceted dual role in healing vascular injuries. Exos hold considerable promise in facilitating vascular repair and regeneration, as effective delivery systems for various growth factors and cytokines are essential in the intricate vascular repair process. By transporting these critical molecules to damaged areas, Exos efficiently promote the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells, thereby aiding in restoring vascular integrity and function. However, the complex nature of Exos may also pose potential disadvantages in harboring and transmitting pro-apoptotic signals-molecular messengers capable of triggering programmed cell death. These signals may induce cellular dysfunction upon interaction with vascular endothelial cells, potentially leading to a cascade of adverse outcomes. This aspect of exosomal activity could exacerbate vascular damage, complicating the complex terrain of diabetic vascular problems.
The delicate equilibrium between Exos’ conflicting activities in vascular healing underscores the intricacy of their participation in diabetic complications. Comprehending and altering this equilibrium could open new opportunities for therapeutic strategies aimed at treating DM-related vascular disorders.

Exploring the complex role of exos in the pathophysiology of DN
DN presents a multifaceted spectrum of symptoms, including diminished sensory awareness, persistent discomfort, and extensive nerve impairment. Recent research has increasingly focused on the complex function of Exos in the etiology, and prospective neuropathy therapy has emerged as a central theme, primarily due to their varied cargo of miRNAs and proteins. Comprehensive studies have demonstrated the potential therapeutic benefits of specific Exos in alleviating neuropathic symptoms. A study report indicates that miR-20b-3p is concentrated in human plasma Exos, and both can relieve symptoms of diabetic neuropathy (DN). Another study discovered that Exos originating from mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) alleviate peripheral neuropathy by inhibiting pro-inflammatory genes. Nonetheless, researchers also revealed that Exos produced from Schwann cells cultivated in high-glucose media promoted the progression of DN by diminishing epidermal nerve fibers. The dual nature of exosomal activity highlights the intricacy of their function in neuropathic disorders.

Exo-Mediated signaling in diabetic nephropathy: implications for kidney health
Diabetic nephropathy stands as one of the most prevalent and concerning microvascular complications afflicting patients with DM. Prior research indicates that in diabetic nephropathy, the level of urine Exo miRNA molecules, such as miR-145-5p, miR-320c, miR-133b-3p, miR-342-3p, and miR-30a-5, are elevated. Concurrently, the concentrations of exosomal proteins, such as Xaa-Pro dipeptidase and Wilm’s tumor 1 (WT1), have also increased, indicating their potential as novel biomarkers for diagnosing diabetic nephropathy. However, an increasing body of compelling evidence suggests that Exos derived from stem cells can significantly enhance renal function, suppress inflammation and fibrosis, and impede the progression of DM-induced chronic renal injury, demonstrating both effectiveness and safety for treating renal diseases. Therefore, the role of Exos in renal diseases presents a complex and multifaceted mechanism.

Impact of exos in energy and metabolic regulation
Exos affect the body’s energy equilibrium and metabolic homeostasis by transporting bioactive substances that modulate glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism. Exos derived from adipose tissue or the liver can modulate lipid metabolism in macrophages, reducing lipid levels. Simultaneously, Exos transport bioactive compounds that can modulate insulin signaling pathways, influence insulin sensitivity, and contribute to insulin resistance associated with DM complications. Notably, Exos derived from M2 macrophages enhance insulin sensitivity through the action of miR-690 and amplify the effects of insulin on adipocytes and hepatocytes. This mechanism suggests a potential therapeutic avenue for addressing DM and its related complications. Conversely, some Exos are carriers for signaling molecules that promote lipid accumulation. This pro-lipogenic activity contrasts with the lipolytic effects of insulin resistance-related disorders.

Exos exhibit extensive and diverse therapeutic potential in addressing the multifaceted challenges of diabetic complications. Their applications encompass various therapeutic domains, including the sophisticated modulation of inflammatory pathways and immune responses, the enhanc
ement and acceleration of vascular tissue repair and regeneration processes, the comprehensive management of DM-related neuropathic conditions, targeted intervention in diabetic nephropathy and associated kidney disorders, effective delay and mitigation of various peripheral complications associated with DM, as well as significant promotion of wound healing through multiple cellular and molecular mechanisms. These versatile extracellular vesicles demonstrate remarkable capabilities in facilitating tissue repair and regeneration, rendering them particularly valuable in developing advanced therapeutic strategies for managing the complex spectrum of diabetic complications.

Exosomal delivery of therapeutic molecules for improving myocardial function in diabetic patients with cardiomyopathy
Exos and their miRNAs provide significant promise for treating diabetic cardiomyopathy. They operate as drug-delivery vehicles and therapeutic agents, potentially enhancing heart function and clinical outcomes by regulating specific miRNAs. The biological molecules used in gene therapy exhibit instability inside the body, are readily degraded by nucleases, and encounter challenges in targeting particular cells; hence, selecting an efficient gene delivery vector is essential. Compared to conventional viral vectors, Exos exhibit superior membrane stability, targeting capability, and high efficacy as drug delivery vehicles. In addition, the significance of Exos produced from stem cells in myocardial cell repair and the preservation of heart function and structure is gaining recognition. Exos derived from embryonic stem cells may transport substances like miR-294 to neighboring target cells, ameliorating myocardial necrosis in ischemic cardiomyopathy, promoting new microangiogenesis, stimulating cardiomyocyte proliferation, and facilitating myocardial repair after myocardial infarction.

Harnessing exos for innovative diabetic nephropathy therapies
Exos may alleviate the symptoms of diabetic nephropathy through various mechanisms. Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell (AMSC)-derived Exos (AMSC-Exos) may mitigate podocyte damage in diabetic nephropathy rat models by upregulating miR-486 expression, blocking Smad1 to activate the mTOR pathway, and enhancing autophagy. The Exos from bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) may improve the condition of renal tubular epithelial cells and podocytes exposed to high glucose levels, promoting cell survival and reducing apoptosis by inhibiting the TLR4 and NF-κB pathways and downregulating VEGF production. Further research indicates that Exos from human urine stem cells diminished urine volume and microalbuminuria excretion in type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) nephropathy rats. These Exos also inhibit apoptosis in podocytes and tubular epithelial cells by downregulating caspase-3 overexpression and enhancing glomerular endothelial cell proliferation. Collectively, these findings suggest that Exos hold significant therapeutic potential for the management of diabetic nephropathy.

Exo-Based approaches for precision management of DR
DR is a leading cause of blindness, with current therapies primarily addressing the advanced stages of the disease through a singular therapeutic approach. Exos released by stem cells exhibit a broad disease-modulating effect comparable to stem cells. These Exos can regulate intercellular signaling, alleviate oxidative stress, suppress inflammatory responses, inhibit angiogenesis, reduce apoptosis, and protect brain tissue. Initial clinical studies exploring Exo therapies for DR are in progress. Trial NCT 06198543 aims to investigate exosomal alterations linked to DR to examine its therapeutic potential. Additional studies, including NCT 06188013 and NCT 03264976, analyze the potential of Exos as indicators for DR while further exploring their molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets. Notwithstanding the positive perspective, clinical applications encounter obstacles, including standardized manufacturing, refined administration techniques, and thorough long-term safety and effectiveness assessment.

Exo-mediated therapeutic strategies for DN
The application of Exos in treating DN is contingent upon the type and severity of the injury, with emerging studies elucidating precise molecular mechanisms and experimental validation. For instance, plasma-derived Exos isolated from healthy Sprague-Dawley rats significantly improved neurological function in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced DN models, as demonstrated by reduced mechanical allodynia, enhanced thermal sensitivity, and accelerated sciatic nerve conduction velocity. Histological analyses revealed that plasma-Exo treatment promoted remyelination, increased intraepidermal nerve fiber density, and restored neuromuscular junction reinnervation. Mechanistically, miR-20b-3p-a highly enriched miRNA in plasma Exos-was shown to directly target the 3’-untranslated region (UTR) of Stat3 mRNA via complementary seed sequences, suppressing its translation. This inhibitory effect led to a reduction in the overall phosphorylation level of Stat3 (total p-Stat3 was detected by Western blot; however, the specific Stat3 phosphorylation site was not explicitly mentioned), thereby down-regulating the downstream autophagy-related markers, including the LC3-II/I ratio and Atg10 expression. These alterations were validated in the RSC96 Schwann cell line under high-glucose conditions and further corroborated in vivo within the sciatic nerve tissue of diabetic rats. In addition, MSC-Exo therapy has been found to increase the density of intraepidermal nerve fibers (IENFs) and axon diameter, concurrently increasing myelin thickness in diabetic mice. Mechanistically, miRNA profiling of MSC-Exos identified miR-17, miR-23a, and miR-125b as key regulators targeting the TLR4/NF-κB pathway. These findings, validated through TaqMan miRNA arrays and Ingenuity Pathway Analysis, highlight the precision of MSC-Exos in attenuating neuroinflammation and restoring axonal integrity, providing a molecularly grounded rationale for their therapeutic potential in DN.

Advancements in Exo-Based therapies for DFU
Exos in managing DFU provide benefits such as low immunogenicity, diminutive size, extended half-life, robust penetrating capability, and superior biocompatibility relative to conventional therapeutic approaches. Research indicates that the localized administration of Exos can elevate the expression of angiogenic factors, including Angiopoietin 1 (ANG1), Fetal Liver Kinase 1 (FILK1), and VEGF, while concurrently diminishing the levels of endogenous angiogenesis inhibitors such as Vasohibin 1 (VASH1) and Thrombospondin 1 (TSP1), thereby augmenting endothelial cell function and angiogenic capacity, ultimately expediting the healing of diabetic wounds. Moreover, Exos may facilitate the physiological processes of peripheral nerve, muscle, and bone regeneration in the lower extremities. Exos from ADSCs block alkaline ceramidase via miR-125b-5p, enhance the proliferation and migration of myoblasts in diabetic mice, and are crucial for the healing of ischemic lower limb muscles in DM.

Challenges of exo therapy for diabetic complications
Despite their extensive potential applications, the implementation of Exo treatment faces numerous technological and ethical challenges. The therapeutic efficacy of Exos can be influenced by several variables, such as their origin, production techniques, and delivery strategies, which introduce ambiguity in clinical use. Although Exos are regarded as having little immunogenicity and toxicity, their long-term safety and adverse effects in clinical applications need more investigation and assessment.

Technical challenges: prioritization and mechanistic insights
Current isolation methods fail to resolve exosomal subpopulations derived from diabetic tissues, leading to inconsistent therapeutic outcomes. For instance, Exos derived from hyperglycemia-primed mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) exhibit elevated levels of miR-155, which paradoxically promote β-cell apoptosis via NF-κB activation in preclinical T1DM. This heterogeneity stems from diabetic microenvironments altering parent cell secretion patterns, necessitating single-vesicle analysis technologies to characterize the molecular complexity.
Despite surface functionalization with CD47 (“don’t eat me” signals) improving circulation half-life by 3-fold in murine models, batch-to-batch variability in tetraspanin profiles remains a critical reproducibility issue, as evidenced by 35% divergence in angiogenic potential between MSC-Exo batches in diabetic wound studies. Bioreactor systems standardize Exos production by real-time monitoring and dynamic adjustment of critical process parameters (e.g., glucose, pH, dissolved oxygen, temperature), thereby reducing inter-batch variability.
Systemic administration results in < 5% Exo accumulation in target tissues due to rapid hepatic clearance and ligand-receptor mismatch between preclinical models and human microenvironments. While retinal endothelial cell-targeted Exos enhance DR treatment efficacy in rodents, clinical translation is limited by interspecies differences in receptor glycosylation patterns. Humanized 3D organoid screens are being explored to validate tissue-specific ligands for diabetic wound targeting.

Safety considerations: addressing cancer risk and immunogenicity
Emerging evidence suggests that Exo derived from specific cell sources may inadvertently transfer oncogenic cargo, such as tumor-promoting miRNAs, oncoproteins, or mutated DNA, which could theoretically activate proliferative or anti-apoptotic pathways in recipient cells. Chronic hyperglycemia and oxidative stress in DM may further alter exosomal content, potentially exacerbating genomic instability or fostering a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. For instance, Exos from hyperglycemia-primed MSCs exhibit elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and MMPs, which could promote tissue remodeling conducive to tumor progression. In comparison, no direct clinical evidence links MSC-Exos to cancer in diabetic patients; preclinical studies highlight the need for cautious evaluation of exosomal cargo and long-term safety.
Innovative strategies are being explored to mitigate these risks. First, stringent donor cell selection and preconditioning can normalize exosomal profiles, reducing pro-tumorigenic factors. Second, engineered Exos with CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of oncogenic miRNAs or incorporation of tumor-suppressive molecules offer precise control over cargo composition. Third, surface modifications using targeting ligands enhance tissue specificity, minimizing off-target effects on healthy or precancerous cells. Additionally, advanced quality control measures, including proteomic and genomic profiling of Exos batches, are critical to ensure clinical-grade safety.
Looking ahead, integrating “smart” Exos responsive to microenvironmental cues could enable context-dependent cargo release, further reducing unintended interactions. Combining multi-omics analyses with longitudinal safety studies in diabetic models will elucidate Exo-cancer interplay, guiding the design of next-generation therapies. By prioritizing mechanistic clarity and engineering precision, Exo-gel therapies can achieve transformative benefits for diabetic complications while circumventing oncogenic risks.

Roadmap for clinical translation
To bridge the gap between bench and bedside, the following milestones are imperative:

Phase 1
Establish GMP-compliant Exo manufacturing protocols with < 5% batch variability, validated via multi-omics profiling.

Phase 2
Conduct longitudinal safety studies in humanized diabetic models to assess oncogenic risks over 24 months.

Phase 3
Initiate multicenter trials evaluating Exo-gel composites in refractory diabetic complications. By integrating precision engineering and rigorous safety frameworks, Exo-gel therapies may overcome current barriers, ushering in a new era of personalized diabetic care.

Physicochemical properties of hydrogels for biomedical applications
Hydrogels are three-dimensional cross-linked polymer networks with highly hydrating properties, capable of absorbing large amounts of water or biological liquids. They are widely used in medicine, drug delivery, and tissue engineering. Hydrogels are usually classified based on their crosslinking and physicochemical properties.

Water content
The water content of hydrogels is categorized as “primary bound water” and “secondary bound water.” Primary-bound water arises from the interaction between water molecules and the polar and hydrophilic groups in the dry gel matrix. In contrast, secondary-bound water is formed through the interaction of hydrophobic groups located on the surface of the gel. Ultimately, the hydrogel reaches a stable water absorption capacity, with any additional water absorbed subsequently classified as “free water” or “bulk water,” which occupies the spaces between the gel network chains or inside bigger pores, macropores, or voids.

Gelation and rheology
Thermoresponsive hydrogels exhibit a transition from a solution to a gel in response to temperature fluctuations. This phenomenon alters the thermoresponsive polymer’s equilibrium between hydrophilic and hydrophobic groups. Gelation is crucial for maintaining cellular integrity and facilitating pharmaceutical delivery. The gelation parameters- such as temperature, duration, and density- must meet the specific requirements of the intended applications.

Porosity and pore size
Hydrogels are characterized by numerous pores, which may result from phase separation occurring during the synthesis process or smaller holes inherent in the network. The characteristics of the pores, including average pore size, pore size distribution, and pore connectivity, are critical parameters for the hydrogel matrix. Although quantifying these pore properties can be challenging, they are essential for the overall efficacy of the hydrogel matrix.

Biocompatibility
Hydrogels’ biocompatibility is critical for their use in tissue engineering and cellular scaffolds. This pertains to the hydrogel’s capacity to remain inside the host organism without adversely affecting adjacent cells, as such disruption could interfere with their physiological functions.

Mechanical properties
Hydrogels’ mechanical characteristics primarily depend on the inherent stiffness of the polymer chains, the nature of the crosslinker, the density of crosslinking, and water absorption, which is influenced by the hydrophilic/hydrophobic equilibrium. In tissue engineering applications, hydrogels are required to establish and maintain an optimal environment for tissue growth. Consequently, their mechanical qualities significantly influence cell adhesion and gene expression.
Regulated Biodegradability: In tissue engineering applications, the regulated biodegradability of hydrogels is a crucial consideration. The degradation kinetics of hydrogels are critically influenced by their crosslinking strategies. Chemical crosslinking stabilizes the network through covalent bonds (e.g., amide bonds), significantly delaying degradation. For instance, (3-Dimethylaminopropyl)−3- ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC)- N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS)-crosslinked collagen films exhibit a 2000% increase in tensile modulus due to covalent conjugation between carboxyl and amine groups, with degradation primarily relying on enzymatic hydrolysis, making them suitable for scenarios requiring prolonged mechanical support. In contrast, physically cross-linked systems (e.g., temperature/pH-responsive hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic interactions) depend on dynamic non-covalent interactions, leading to rapid degradation under environmental stimuli. As demonstrated in STINGel—a peptide hydrogel—electrostatic interactions between lysine termini and thiophosphate groups decelerate drug release by 8-fold compared to collagen gels, highlighting the role of physical cross-linking in sustained delivery. These distinctions dictate therapeutic design: covalent networks are ideal for long-term release (e.g., chronic tissue regeneration), while physical networks enable on-demand release triggered by microenvironmental cues (e.g., pH changes in diabetic wounds).

Surface properties
The hydrogel scaffold’s surface is the first interface with adjacent cells and tissues and the locus for regulating cell adherence and proliferation. Hydrogel scaffolds characterized by substantial and accessible surface areas are beneficial for accommodating the requisite number of cells to replace or restore tissue or organ function.

Vascularization
Developing appropriate scaffolds to facilitate neovascularization continues to pose a significant problem. High porosity and optimal pore size must be considered to develop vascularized scaffolds. Alginate-based hydrogel scaffolds have shown efficacy in facilitating the restoration of vascularized bones.

Innovation in hydrogel technology for diabetic complication management
Hydrogels have emerged as a transformative biomaterial platform for addressing diabetic complications, with unique properties that position them as ideal carriers for Exo-based therapies. Critically, hydrogels offer distinct advantages for therapeutic delivery: their protective 3D microenvironments shield labile cargo from rapid degradation; their tunable porosity and crosslinking density enable precise control over cargo release kinetics; their injectability permits minimally invasive deployment that conforms to irregular tissue geometries; their bioadhesive surfaces promote localized retention at target sites; and their high water content combined with ECM-like architecture supports bioactive molecule stability while facilitating cell-matrix interactions. These intrinsic carrier capabilities make hydrogels exceptionally well-suited for delivering fragile nanovesicles, whose therapeutic efficacy hinges on sustained bioavailability and spatiotemporal precision.
This synergy is powerfully demonstrated in the context of diabetic wound management. Hydrogels establish a moist, protective barrier that not only accelerates healing by enhancing keratinocyte proliferation and angiogenesis but also optimizes Exo delivery by maintaining a stabilized reservoir of bioactive cargo at the wound bed. Their capacity to absorb excess exudates reduces the risk of infection, while their modular design accommodates the seamless integration of growth factors, pharmaceuticals, or Exos themselves, enabling personalized regenerative strategies. In addition, hydrogels have emerged as a novel drug delivery system for managing DR, a primary cause of visual loss in diabetic patients. Their capacity to regulate the administration of pharmaceuticals within the eye signifies a notable progression in ophthalmic therapies. Hydrogels facilitate the continuous and consistent release of therapeutic substances over prolonged periods, thereby improving treatment effectiveness and reducing the frequency of medication administration. This improvement in medication administration may lead to enhanced patient outcomes.
Furthermore, the intrinsic transparency of hydrogels offers a significant benefit in ocular applications. Unlike several alternative drug delivery technologies, hydrogels do not obstruct or compromise vision, making them an optimal selection for ocular therapies. This attribute guarantees patients therapeutic benefits without sacrificing visual acuity during treatment. In the context of DN, a problem often linked to significant pain and sensory impairment, hydrogels offer unique therapeutic opportunities. They mimic the properties of the ECM, fostering an environment conducive to the regeneration and repair of nerve cells. This biomimetic method shows potential for restoring nerve function and mitigating the devastating effects of neuropathy. Moreover, the adaptability of hydrogels facilitates the integration of neurotrophic substances or anti-inflammatory medications, enabling a multifaceted strategy for managing neuropathy by targeting both the fundamental nerve injury and the related inflammatory responses. As researchers and healthcare professionals work to enhance the structure and functionality of hydrogels, the potential applications for diabetic complications treatment are increasingly broadening (Fig. 3) (Table 2). This evolution solidly positions Exo-gel as a cornerstone of next-generation therapies for diabetic complications, merging the regenerative potential of Exos with the precision delivery capabilities of hydrogels.

Construction and optimization of Exo-gel composites
The synergistic efficacy of Exo-gel systems arises from the complementary interplay between Exo biology and hydrogel physicochemical properties. This section delineates the rational design principles, material optimization strategies, and mechanistic foundations underlying Exo-gel composites.

Rational design of Exo-gel materials
Exo-gel composites are engineered to address two critical challenges in diabetic complication management: (1) preserving Exos bioactivity during encapsulation and (2) achieving spatiotemporal control over therapeutic cargo release. Hydrogels composed of natural polymers (e.g., alginate, chitosan) or synthetic derivatives [e.g., Gelatin Methacryloyl (GelMA), polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA)] provide tunable porosity, mechanical strength, and biodegradability. These properties enable precise modulation of Exo release kinetics through stimuli-responsive crosslinking (e.g., pH, temperature, enzymatic triggers). For instance, Schiff base linkages in chitosan-based hydrogels facilitate pH-sensitive Exos release in the acidic wound microenvironment of diabetic ulcers, enhancing localized bioavailability.

Synergistic mechanisms in bioactivity and delivery
The therapeutic superiority of Exo-gel systems arises from their ability to orchestrate dynamic molecular crosstalk between exosomal membrane proteins and natural biopolymers, which synergistically regulate ECM remodeling while preserving bioactive stability through three hierarchical mechanisms. The first strategy, in situ hybrid cross-linking, enables direct injection of Exo-polymer mixtures into target tissues, where external stimuli (light, ion exchange, or pH/temperature changes) trigger rapid gelation into a three-dimensional network that encapsulates Exos with > 85% efficiency while conforming to irregular tissue cavities. In the second approach, post-preloading cross-linking, Exos are covalently conjugated to functionalized polymers prior to hydrogel network formation using agents like PEGDA or genipin, ensuring stable integration within the matrix. The third method, physical adsorption, exploits the hydrogel’s water absorption and swelling properties by immersing dehydrated cross-linked hydrogels into Exo solutions, where Exos load into porous structures via physisorption; however, precise pore size control is critical to balance loading efficiency and prevent leakage. Collectively, these mechanisms enable spatiotemporal regulation of Exo release kinetics, tissue-targeted delivery, and functional cargo activity, establishing Exo-gel systems as innovative platforms for managing diabetic complications.

Bioactive cargo protection
Hydrogel matrices act as bioactive reservoirs that protect exosomal cargo (e.g., miRNAs, proteins) from enzymatic degradation and rapid systemic clearance, thereby extending their bioactivity and enabling spatiotemporally controlled release. For instance, 28% Pluronic F-127 (PF-127) hydrogels exhibit temperature-dependent sol-gel transitions (initial gelation at 18.1 °C, gelation time ~ 43 s at 37 °C), forming a porous network that preserves Exo integrity over 21 days by modulating hydrogel degradation kinetics and shielding Exos from protease-rich microenvironments. This protection is critical for maintaining the bioactivity of exosomal SCNN1B, a key regulator of osteogenic differentiation, which is delivered to AMSCs to activate the PERK/ATF4 signaling axis, a pathway essential for resolving endoplasmic reticulum stress and promoting tissue repair.

Dual-Function signaling
The dual-function signaling of Exo-gel systems transcends passive delivery by establishing dynamic bidirectional crosstalk between hydrogel matrices and exosomal cargo, amplifying therapeutic outcomes through microenvironment-responsive modulation. For instance, hyaluronic acid-polylysine hydrogels engineered with oxidative stress-responsive Schiff base bonds actively degrade in ROS-rich infarcted myocardium, triggering on-demand release of MSC-derived Exos while simultaneously scavenging ROS.

Synergistic approach of Exo-gel therapy in treating diabetic complications
Given the specific benefits of hydrogels and the unique properties of Exos, researchers are diligently exploring several innovative applications of Exo-gel therapy for diabetic complications. These novel approaches capitalize on the synergistic advantages of hydrogels’ biocompatibility and controlled release capabilities in conjunction with Exos’ regenerative and immunomodulatory features. Integrating these two potent components establishes an advanced therapeutic framework that simultaneously addresses multiple aspects of DM-related issues. Hydrogels, characterized by their three-dimensional network structure and elevated water content, create an optimal milieu for Exo-regulated release. This controlled release mechanism ensures continuous and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to afflicted regions, possibly improving treatment effectiveness while minimizing systemic adverse effects. Moreover, the adjustable characteristics of hydrogels allow researchers to refine the release kinetics of Exos, customizing the therapy for diabetic complications. Conversely, Exos contribute their unique cargo of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. These hold significant promises in regulating the immune response, enhancing tissue regeneration, and enabling intercellular communication. Exos may mitigate many pathological processes associated with DM consequences, including chronic inflammation, poor wound healing, and vascular dysfunction.

Combating delayed healing in diabetic wounds
Diabetic wound healing is a multifaceted process that involves the coordinated interplay of several cellular and molecular activities, such as cell migration, proliferation, and ECM deposition. Multiple factors often impede the healing process of diabetic wounds, leading to prolonged healing times. The microenvironment of diabetic wounds is characterized by chronic inflammation, hyperglycemia, impaired immune function, microbial colonization, biofilm formation, hindered angiogenesis, protracted tissue repair, delayed fibroblast activity and collagen synthesis, and compromised keratinocyte proliferation and migration (Fig. 4).
Exo-gel Mediated Diabetic Wound Repair in Hyperglycemic Microenvironments
Exo-gels facilitate skin healing through a diverse range of mechanisms, encompassing the promotion of cell migration and proliferation, augmentation of cell survival, stimulation of angiogenesis and oxygen release, mitigation of oxidative stress, facilitation of collagen remodeling and neovascularization, acceleration of wound healing processes, and prevention against infection. These properties render exo-gels a promising material for treating DM and infected wounds.

Adipose cell-derived exos
Researchers have developed several treatment strategies utilizing Exo-gel therapy to address these challenges. Wang et al. introduced a peptide-based hydrogel named FHE, composed of poly-ε-L-lysine (EPL), PF-127, and oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA). This hydrogel exhibits inherent antibacterial properties and facilitates sustained pH-responsive release of stimuli-responsive AMSC-Exos, along with temperature responsiveness. The incorporation of Exos is designed to enhance angiogenesis, a critical factor in tissue regeneration during diabetic wound healing. The loading of Exos occurs owing to the electrostatic contact between the negative charge of Exos and EPL. In contrast, the cleavage of Schiff base bonds triggers their release in a mildly acidic environment. In vitro studies indicate that the prolonged pH-responsive Exo release characteristics of FHE@Exos hydrogel enhance the tube-forming capacity, proliferation, and migration of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). In vivo results revealed that FHE@Exos hydrogel achieved 88.67% wound closure at day 14 and promoted 4-fold more dermal appendage regeneration than Exos alone, confirming its superior efficacy in accelerating complete wound healing and skin restoration.
A separate research team created an innovative Exo-gel composite material called Q5Exo. This system can efficiently encapsulate Exos from ADSCs inside a protein-based hydrogel matrix, creating a distinctive platform for these therapeutic vesicles’ regulated release and distribution. When applied topically to diabetic mice, a model characterized by impaired wound healing, Q5Exo demonstrated a significant reduction in the duration necessary for complete wound closure. This substantial increase in healing rates emphasizes the efficacy of Q5Exo as a powerful agent for treating diabetic wounds. It highlights the broader implications of protein-based Exo-gel therapy in regenerative medicine. The study utilized Exos obtained from ADSCs to create a novel wound dressing named OxOBand, designed to improve the healing of diabetic wounds. OxOBand comprises antioxidant polyurethane (PUAO), facilitating continuous oxygen release and incorporating Exos. Research indicates that OxOBand may facilitate the migration of human keratinocytes and fibroblasts while also increasing the survival rate of human neuroblasts under high-glucose conditions. In the diabetic wound model, OxOBand has the potential to accelerate wound healing, enhance collagen deposition, expedite re-epithelialization, increase neovascularization, and mitigate oxidative stress.
Research has also indicated that hypoxia-pretreated ADSC-derived Exos (ADSC-HExo) incorporated in GelMA hydrogel (GelMA-HExo) may significantly enhance wound healing in diabetic mice. The research investigated the regulatory role of circ-Snhg 11 in enhancing diabetic wound healing via Exo transport. Modified ADSC-Exos containing circ-Snhg 11 enhanced the migration, proliferation, and vascular regeneration capabilities of vascular endothelial cells (ECs). The alternative research team incorporated the ADSC-derived Exos into alginate-based hydrogels, using them as bioactive scaffolds at wound sites in animal models. Experimental data, both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrate that this bioactive wound dressing technique significantly enhances wound closure, collagen formation, and vascularization. The research presents a cell-free therapeutic approach using Exo-gels, offering a novel viewpoint on wound healing. Additionally, several scholars also believe that AMSC-Exos have similar anti-inflammatory and tissue repair functions as stem cells. Therefore, they prepared Exo/Gel dressings by physically mixing ADSC-Exos with chitosan hydrogel. This can slowly release Exos and show the characteristics of promoting cell migration and angiogenesis in vivo and in vitro experiments.

HUVEC-derived exos
In addition, a composite hydrogel system composed of ADM-Fe3+@PA-Exos/GelMA was developed by integrating Exos derived from HUVECs with acellular dermal matrix (ADM), protocatechualdehyde (PA), and trivalent iron ions (Fe3+), followed by the incorporation of photocurable GelMA. The study demonstrated that this Exo-gel system can significantly enhance the healing process of diabetic wounds through various mechanisms, including antioxidative properties, antibacterial effects, and promotion of cell growth. Another study also utilizes Exos produced from HUVECs in conjunction with a unique form of host-guest macromer (HGM) and quaternary ammonium chitosan hydrogel. Their findings revealed the creation of hydrogel-loaded hypoxic Exos (Gel-H-Exos), which demonstrates antimicrobial capabilities, superior biocompatibility, and the capacity to sustainably release Exos, thereby enhancing the healing process of diabetic wounds. In an independent study using HUVEC-derived Exos, researchers used methacrylate GelMA and PEGDA as matrix materials. They included the β-cyclodextrin derivative β-CD-AOI₂ to encapsulate tazarotene. The findings indicated that administering Exos and tazarotene to the wound site via a microneedle patch may enhance collagen deposition, epithelium regeneration, and angiogenesis.

Mesenchymal stem cell-derived exos
Recent discoveries indicate that MSC-Exos may promote the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype. In this context, researchers have created a multifunctional hydrogel composed of gallic acid (GA)-conjugated chitosan (Chi-GA) and partly OHA for the sustained release of Exos produced from hypoxic bone marrow MSCs (hyBMSC-Exos). This study revealed that hydrogel improves the stability of hyBMSC-Exos, allowing a sustained and progressive release at the wound site, hence enhancing Exo absorption efficiency by target cells. Accordingly, a novel composite hydrogel dressing has been developed utilizing the properties of MSC-derived Exos for managing diabetic wounds. This hydrogel consists of chitosan, chitosan nanoparticles (CS-NPs), bioactive glass (BG), and titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanoparticles. Research demonstrates that hydrogel has excellent cell compatibility, promotes the adhesion and proliferation of endothelial cells, and possesses anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and antibacterial properties. In a mouse model of full-thickness skin damage, the composite hydrogel dressing expedited wound healing, promoted angiogenesis, enhanced collagen deposition, and produced anti-inflammatory markers. Additionally, based on the characteristics of MSC-derived Exos, a sprayable alginate hydrogel (SA) dressing consisting of oxygen-productive calcium peroxide (CP) microspheres and Exos was prepared for the treatment of chronic wound nonhealing in diabetic patients. The results showed that the dressing could continuously release oxygen for up to seven days, reducing the expression of hypoxic factors in fibroblasts. In vivo experiments, this CP/EXO/SA dressing significantly accelerated full-layer wound healing, including improved wound healing efficiency, rapid re-epithelialization, good collagen deposition, abundant angiogenesis, and shortened inflammatory periods.
Researchers have suggested using MSC-derived Exos packed in adjustable poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) hydrogel needle tips, together with removable 3 M medical tape as a supporting substrate, for the management of diabetic ulcers. The encapsulation of MSC-derived Exos in endograft microneedle tips efficiently enhances the functionality of fibroblasts, vascular endothelial cells, and macrophages, consequently enhancing the wound microenvironment and expediting the wound healing process. This work demonstrates the effective use of these microneedles in treating full-thickness skin lesions, underscoring their clinical significance and promise in biomedical domains such as DM complication management and tissue engineering.
The alternative research team successfully developed a combination of Exos obtained from hUCMSCs and PF-127 heat-sensitive hydrogels for treating chronic diabetic ulcers. Both in vivo and in vitro studies demonstrated that the administration of Exo-gel complexes accelerated wound healing rates, enhanced epithelial regeneration, and facilitated improved repair of skin appendages. The results suggest that incorporating Exos into biological scaffolds facilitates the prolonged release of Exos and other biomolecules, enhances early angiogenesis in diabetic wounds, and accelerates wound healing and skin remodeling. The alternative research team has investigated hUCMSC-derived Exos in conjunction with co-assembling silk-fibroin ε-poly-l-Lysine nanoparticles (NSF-EPL) and subsequently integrating NSF-EPL with polydeoxyribonucleotide (PDRN). Due to the alteration of EPL, PDRN, and Exo, composite nanoparticles demonstrated synergistic antibacterial properties, anti-inflammatory effects, and angiogenesis, which may substantially enhance wound healing. Furthermore, some scholars have applied Exos from human placenta-derived MSC in combination with hydrogel and found that the complex has rejuvenated the structure and function of the wound site by enhancing angiogenesis and suppressing cell death.

M2 macrophage-derived exos
Research has indicated M2 macrophage-derived Exos (M2Exo) may promote the polarization of M1 macrophages to M2, thereby accelerating wound healing and improving the healing process by increasing collagen deposition, angiogenesis, and re-epithelialization. In response to these findings, several researchers integrated M2Exo with OHA and amalgamated it with polyethylene glycol fibroin protein to create Exo-gel. Preclinical investigations have shown that Exo-gel therapy modulates cytokine levels in the wound microenvironment, enhances interactions and migrations between fibroblasts and keratinocytes, and promotes organized collagen deposition. A novel in situ injectable HA@MnO2/FGF-2/Exos hydrogel has been introduced to improve diabetic wound healing. This hydrogel, administered via a simple local injection, establishes a protective barrier over the site, facilitating fast hemostasis and enduring antibacterial protection. The MnO2/ε-PL nanosheet catalyzes the excess H2O2 generated in the wound, turning it to O2, thus mitigating the detrimental effects of H2O2 while supplying O2 for wound healing. Furthermore, the secretion of M2-derived Exos and the FGF-2 growth factor promote angiogenesis and epithelialization, respectively. The in vivo and in vitro findings indicate enhanced healing of diabetic wounds using the HA@MnO2/FGF-2/Exos hydrogel.
Recent advances by scholars have focused on improvements based on M2-based Exo-gels; an ion-cross-linked dissipative network was incorporated and synthesized using gold nanorods (AuNRs) as a particular cross-linker. This ion network is cross-linked in situ with a long-chain polymer to create a distinct network structure, bestowing anti-swelling and photothermal properties to the hydrogel. The integrated hydrogel system efficiently reduced reactive oxygen species, suppressed inflammation, enhanced angiogenesis, and activated photothermal antibacterial activity by near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. In vivo findings validated that M2-Exos produced from the hydrogels were crucial in wound closure. Moreover, the combined action of AuNRs and NIR photothermal effects eliminated bacterial infections in the wound region. This integrated hydrogel technology presents a promising approach for accelerating the healing of chronic diabetic wounds and facilitating tissue regeneration.

Activated neutrophil-derived exos
Neutrophils represent one of the human body’s most prevalent categories of leukocytes. They serve as the primary defense mechanism against invading pathogens through their capability to phagocytose these pathogens. Research indicates that activated neutrophil-derived Exo mimics (aPMNEM) exhibit significant antibacterial efficacy attributed to their content, which includes myeloperoxidase, elastase, antimicrobial peptides, and lysozyme. VEGF may induce angiogenesis via many signaling pathways, including the PI3K–AKT and MAPK–ERK pathways. However, the therapeutic application of VEGF is constrained by its susceptibility to degradation, limited targeting capability, and tendency to disperse throughout the body. Delivery by Exos may enhance therapeutic stability and augment drug accumulation in target cells, boosting effectiveness and decreasing the necessary dosage. Furthermore, the ECM consists of diverse proteins and polysaccharides that form a distinctive three-dimensional architecture and surface topology. Beyond providing structural support to tissues, the ECM plays a crucial role in recruiting and regulating cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. Following digestion, the ECM can be processed into a thermosensitive material known as ECM hydrogel, which requires no intricate cross-linking procedure and exhibits excellent biocompatibility with minimal cytotoxicity. A research team has created a novel hybrid hydrogel, VEGF-aPMNEM-ECM, which enhances the healing of chronic diabetic wounds by prolonging the retention of Exos at the wound site. The findings of this study indicated that the hydrogel successfully enhances the healing of chronic diabetic wounds and serves as a therapeutic platform using cytokines for the treatment of various ailments.

PRP-derived exos
PRP is derived from autologous whole blood and is characterized by elevated concentrations of platelets and a variety of growth factors. PRP-derived Exos have a greater concentration of growth factors than PRP, including bFGF, VEGF, PDGF, and TGFβ. Guo et al. demonstrated that PRP-Exos significantly improved the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells and fibroblasts compared to PRP for chronic wound healing. Consequently, the researchers developed a fiber-reinforced gelatin (GEL)/β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) hydrogel incorporating PRP-Exos. The research revealed that GEL/β-CD hydrogel offers a biocompatible milieu and active components conducive to cell adhesion, proliferation, and skin tissue regeneration. Conversely, PRP-Exos inside the therapeutic hydrogel increased autophagy flux by 1.8-fold (via upregulating LC3 expression to levels comparable to normal conditions) and reduced apoptosis rates by 50% in HUVECs and skin fibroblasts. This dual activation of autophagy and inhibition of apoptosis promotes angiogenesis, collagen synthesis, and re-epithelialization.
A research study was conducted to develop and evaluate double-crosslinked hydrogels to improve diabetic wound healing. The incorporation of silk protein (SP) into PRP, PRP-Exos, and MSC-Exos enhanced their mechanical properties and extended the release of bioactive compounds, including VEGF and TGFβ1. This enhancement initiated a series of beneficial biological processes, augmenting their tissue regeneration capacity in a diabetic wound model. The double-crosslinked hydrogels diminished NETosis and oxidative stress while promoting M2 polarization, granulation tissue matrix formation, neoangiogenesis, sebaceous glands, and hair follicle regeneration. Additionally, these hydrogels improved collagen deposition, facilitating re-epithelialization and scar-free healing. Although further clinical studies are necessary to validate the efficacy of these double-crosslinked hydrogels in human subjects, the researchers propose that these hydrogels are promising bioactive dressings for treating and managing chronic wounds, including infected diabetic wounds.

Effective management of diabetic bone regeneration
Diabetic individuals encounter an increased risk of fractures and experience delayed healing of these fractures. This underscores the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies aimed at accelerating bone healing in patients with metabolic complications due to DM. Current research suggests moderate endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress can promote osteogenesis; however, excessive and aberrant ER stress might result in osteogenic disorders. Therefore, the observed bone loss and impaired repair mechanisms in diabetic patients may be closely associated with dysfunction of the ER. Recent findings indicate that the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-derived Exos is intricately linked to the modulation of ER stress. However, pure Exos’ efficacy is limited; certain scholars developed a loading Sephin1 (Sep), a secure and specific small molecule that facilitates protein folding and supports ER proteostasis. This approach aims to functionalize MSC-derived Exos to regulate ER homeostasis and restore MSC function in a high glucose environment. The intricate mechanisms by which Exos maintain ER homeostasis and support osteogenesis have been elucidated. Furthermore, a self-healing bioadhesive hydrogel has been employed to encapsulate these modified Exos, allowing for sustained release. This strategy could regulate ER homeostasis, enhance MSC activity, and promote bone regeneration in a diabetic femoral lesion model.
Research is also exploring the efficacy of Exos derived from dental apical papilla stem cells (SCAP-Exo) in facilitating bone defect repair in diabetic individuals. The research showed that SCAP-Exo significantly enhanced angiogenesis and osteogenesis in normal and diabetic environments. To facilitate the controlled release of SCAP-Exos, researchers devised a dynamic bioresponsive polyethylene glycol (PEG)/DNA hybrid hydrogel technology. This system may react to pathogenic signals in the diabetic milieu, such as MMP-9, hence initiating the release of SCAP-Exos. In a diabetic rat mandibular defect model, local administration of SCAP-Exo-loaded hydrogel restored bone volume fraction to 58.4 ± 7.4% at 6 weeks, comparable to non-diabetic controls (59.1 ± 9.8%) and significantly higher than diabetic controls (16.5 ± 6.3%). Additionally, miRNA sequencing revealed that the dual role of SCAP-Exo may be associated with the elevated expression of miR-126-5p and miR-150-5p. This work offers insights into creating effective bioresponsive Exo delivery systems to enhance bone repair in diabetic patients (Fig. 5).
Exo-Laden Hydrogels for Diabetic Bone Regeneration: Synergistic Bioactive Cues, Osteogenic Reprogramming, and Tissue Engineering Applications
Exosomal hydrogels have emerged as promising therapeutic agents for bone regeneration in diabetes mellitus (DM) and have found extensive applications in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Enhanced healing of DFUs
Diabetic patients often experience DFUs in a prolonged high blood sugar environment, leading to increased local osmotic pressure that hinders tissue fluid from penetrating the wound and facilitating repair. Furthermore, the location of these wounds on the foot subjects them to frequent movement, which can result in re-tearing and subsequent secondary damage. Therefore, an ideal hydrogel composite scaffold for diabetic wound repair should possess several key characteristics: excellent biocompatibility, antibacterial properties, appropriate mechanical strength, and the capability for sustained release. Therefore, scholars constructed a composite scaffold of ECM, quaternized chitosan, gelatin, and Exos derived from ADSCs, employing 3D printing technology. These scaffolds have favorable mechanical properties and biocompatibility and demonstrate effective antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, thereby mitigating the risks of bacterial infection in wounds. In addition, these composite scaffolds hold promise as Exo-loaded hydrogel dressings, laying the groundwork for future investigations into the repair of DFUs.

Functional recovery and pain relief in DN
The prolonged hyperglycemic condition associated with DM can lead to DN, characterized by discomfort and potential motor impairment. Researchers are investigating methods to facilitate nerve healing and alleviate pain efficiently. One research has developed an innovative therapeutic approach integrating electroconductive hydrogels (ECHs) with Exos derived from BMSCs-Exos to create ECH-Exos hydrogel. This hydrogel is a soft, tissue-adhesive, electrically conductive, and biocompatible polymer that increased axonal density by 2.1-fold compared to untreated controls and improved myelinated axon diameter by 1.5-fold (from 1.2 ± 0.3 μm to 1.8 ± 0.4 μm) through activation of the MEK/ERK pathway (p-ERK levels increased by 68%). Functional recovery was demonstrated by a 43.6% improvement in Sciatic Function Index (SFI: from − 75.99 ± 1.95 to −42.72 ± 1.95) at 4 weeks post-injury. Furthermore, it enhances motor function rehabilitation and alleviates inflammatory pain in diabetic rats. Compared to ECH or Exos treatment alone, the combination of ECH-Exos significantly promotes axonal regeneration and myelination, thereby aiding nerve regeneration, alleviating inflammatory pain following DN, and improving motor function recovery and quality of life for DM patients (Fig. 6).

Therapeutic potential for DR
Diabetic patients exhibit varying levels of corneal lesions that diminish corneal transparency and significantly affect the course of therapy and quality of life for DR patients. Advances in stem cell technology and tissue engineering have opened new avenues for improving DR treatment and facilitating corneal restoration. The effectiveness of corneal regeneration and healing hinges on using optimal seed cells. Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived MSCs (iPSC-MSCs) are advantageous due to their abundance, high proliferation potential, and lack of teratoma formation during prolonged culture. However, upon in vivo transplantation, these stem cells are unstable, have a low survival rate, and often need to be injected repeatedly. Recent findings indicate that stem cells exert a more direct paracrine effect in vivo, with the Exos and microvesicles they create being essential factors in altering how host cells and tissues function. Exos can release miRNAs into the extracellular environment, which lowers the production of proteins that target the mRNAs encoded. MSC-derived Exos have shown promise in mitigating inflammation and eye damage in ocular disorders, demonstrating efficacy comparable to transplanted MSCs. Nonetheless, traditional MSC transplantation frequently falls short of adequate mechanical support, mainly when corneal injury involves the corneal stroma. Therefore, appropriate corneal scaffold materials are essential for constructing effective tissue-engineered transplants.
As a result, some researchers have introduced a novel therapeutic approach based on thermosensitive hydrogels and Exos. Studies have demonstrated that Exos derived from iPSC-MSCs efficiently aid in restoring injured corneal stroma and epithelium, lessen scarring, and expedite the healing process when administered alongside thermosensitive hydrogels. The thermosensitive chitosan-based hydrogel (CHI hydrogel) employed in this study continuously releases iPSC-MSC Exos while simultaneously suppressing the mRNA expression of the three predominant collagens in the corneal stroma (type I collagen α1, type V collagen α1, and type V collagen α2), thereby promoting corneal epithelium and stroma repair, and lessen scar formation. Furthermore, miR-432-5p, present in the Exos released by iPSC-MSCs, inhibits the expression of translocation-associated membrane protein 2 (TRAM2), preventing the formation of ECM. The findings suggest that miR-432-5p may stop ECM deposition by directly targeting TRAM2, and Exos containing miRNA released by iPSC-MSCs can encourage the regeneration of corneal epithelium and stroma. Consequently, the use of thermosensitive CHI hydrogel based on Exos is a potential cutting-edge technology for the therapeutic management of several corneal disorders (Table 3).

Engineering strategies: CRISPR-based screening and AI-guided hydrogel design
The convergence of emerging biotechnologies holds transformative potential to overcome limitations in Exo-gel therapy. By integrating the cutting-edge biotechnologies, targeted delivery, standardized manufacturing, and functional customization can be systematically addressed.

CRISPR-mediated cargo optimization
The CRISPR-Cas system, renowned for its precision in nucleic acid detection, can be repurposed to engineer parent cells for Exo customization or screening of exosomal cargo. Recent advances in CRISPR/Cas systems, such as the hairpin-enhanced CRISPR/Cas13a circuit reported by Xie et al., demonstrate precise RNA detection within exosomal cargo. This system enables identification of breast cancer-associated miRNAs (e.g., miR-21 and miR-375) while effectively excluding non-target miRNAs (e.g., miR-155) through its high specificity. The logic-based detection system achieved a coefficient variation of less than 5% across five independent tests for miRNA detection, demonstrating high reproducibility that could support the evaluation of batch-to-batch consistency in Exo manufacturing.

AI-Driven hydrogel design for Spatiotemporal control
Artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms, intense learning models, are revolutionizing hydrogel formulation by predicting structure-function relationships. As demonstrated by Liu et al., AI-driven hydrogel design using generative adversarial networks (GANs) enables inverse engineering of fiber distributions in hydrogel composites to achieve controlled drug release through modulation of stress concentration zones. This approach innovatively constructs datasets linking mechanical fields (e.g., stress/displacement) to fiber distributions, offering tailored design strategies for complex physiological requirements, such as the dynamic mechanical microenvironment of diabetic wounds. Additionally, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) can process real-time electrical signal waveforms from the Biomimetic Multifunctional Hydrogel Electronic Skin to automatically extract features for material type and surface roughness identification, achieving accuracies of 95.00% and 97.20%, respectively. AI algorithms can facilitate data-driven support for designing stimulus-responsive hydrogels by integrating patient-specific data, such as blood glucose fluctuation patterns and fibrosis progression characteristics, with predicted pathophysiological changes. This AI-enabled delivery system can trigger Exo release at predefined pH levels or enzyme activity thresholds, thus enabling more precise spatiotemporal control over drug or Exo release.
Looking ahead, multimodal engineering strategies hold promise for precision management of diabetic complications yet require a balanced integration of technological innovation and ethical governance. Future studies should utilize organ-on-chip platforms to validate the in vivo safety of these strategies, accelerating clinical translation. Additionally, open-source AI platforms for hydrogel design could reduce commercial bias but require robust data anonymization to protect patient privacy under General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) standards. Collaborative efforts between biotechnologists and ethicists are critical to balance innovation with societal responsibility.

Discussion
Compared to conventional therapies for diabetic complications, Exo-gel systems demonstrate multidimensional advantages across efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness parameters. Regarding therapeutic efficacy, traditional growth factor therapies (e.g., recombinant PDGF-BB) require frequent administration due to short half-life and fail to address the multifactorial pathological microenvironment characterized by persistent inflammation and impaired angiogenesis. In contrast, advanced Exo-gel platforms like chitosan-based thermo-sensitive hydrogels (CHI hydrogel) loaded with iPSC-MSC-derived Exos achieve sustained release of exosomal cargo over 5 days, enabled by dynamic boronate ester bonds responsive to hyperglycemic environments. Regarding biosafety, conventional silver-based dressings exhibit dose-dependent cytotoxicity (IC50: 3.8 µg/mL) and impair epithelialization through reactive oxygen species generation. The newly developed biodegradable Exo-engineered hydrogels demonstrate superior biocompatibility, maintaining 98.7 ± 1.2% viability in human peritoneal mesothelial cells (HPMCs) after 48 h exposure and showing no detectable inflammatory cytokine release. This safety profile stems from chemical-free fabrication using enzymatic crosslinking and physiological temperature gelation. Moreover, Exo hydrogel can promote wound healing by multi-dimensional regulation of immune response. Cost-effectiveness analysis reveals that although initial Exo isolation costs remain substantial (~$800/1012 particles), the total 6-week treatment expenditure decreases by 58.3% compared to standard care through three mechanisms: (1) reduced administration frequency, (2) decreased resource utilization, and (3) prevention of secondary complications. Long-term economic modeling predicts additional cost savings from improved healing quality and reduced recurrence risk.
While Exo-gel composites demonstrate promising therapeutic effects in short-term studies, their long-term stability and bioavailability remain critical challenges for clinical translation. The degradation kinetics of hydrogels directly govern the sustained release of Exos: covalently cross-linked PEGDA hydrogels require 6–8 weeks for complete degradation in vivo, whereas thermosensitive PF-127 hydrogels maintain gel state for only 24 h at 37 °C, posing risks of Exo burst release. To enhance stability, recent studies employ dual-network hydrogel designs, such as gelatin-OHA composites, which synergize dynamic covalent bonds (Schiff base) and hydrogen bonding to prolong Exo release over 21 days, with in vivo experiments showing 2.3-fold higher VEGF expression in diabetic wounds compared to controls. Furthermore, hydrogel porosity must match Exo size to prevent leakage; nanopores may restrict Exo diffusion but hinder cellular infiltration. Future designs should balance degradation rates, pore architecture, and bioactivity preservation—for instance, incorporating enzyme-responsive peptides for microenvironment-triggered release.
The long-term immunogenicity of Exo-gel composites warrants rigorous evaluation. Allogeneic Exos carrying MHC class I molecules may activate CD8+ T cells, leading to graft rejection, whereas autologous Exos exhibit lower immunogenicity but face scalability challenges. Hydrogel materials can provoke inflammatory responses: chitosan hydrogels elevate IL-6 and TNF-α levels 28 days post-implantation, while synthetic polyacrylamide correlates with sustained α-SMA expression, a fibrosis marker. Mitigation strategies include surface PEGylation or CD47 overexpression to reduce macrophage phagocytosis, extending circulation half-life to 48 h. Chronic toxicity studies reveal that residual hydrogel monomers (e.g., acrylamide) accumulate in kidneys, reducing glomerular filtration rate by 15%−20%, necessitating ultra-purification to limit monomer residues to < 0.1 ppm. Thus, standardized manufacturing (e.g., GMP-grade Exos) and real-time immune monitoring are imperative for clinical safety.

Future perspectives
Three strategic priorities demand immediate attention to propel Exo-gel systems toward clinical translation. First, standardized Exo characterization must align with MISEV2023 guidelines and ISO standards, integrating multi-modal quality control assays such as NTA with dynamic light scattering for size distribution profiling, and high-resolution flow cytometry for single-vesicle surface marker quantification. Microfluidic platforms should adopt inertial focusing and acoustic sorting to achieve cGMP-compliant production of Exo-gel complexes with batch-to-batch consistency in cargo loading efficiency. Second, next-generation hydrogels must integrate microenvironment-responsive functionalities to overcome passive diffusion limitations. Advanced designs employing dynamic bonding networks can achieve sustained Exo delivery, while surface engineering strategies minimize immunogenicity and enhance biocompatibility for targeted therapeutic applications. Third, comprehensive safety evaluations require 12-month longitudinal studies in large-animal diabetic models with single-cell spatial transcriptomics to map T-cell clonal expansion in lymphoid organs and mass spectrometry imaging to quantify residual hydrogel monomers in the liver/kidney. Hierarchical validation using patient-derived organoid (PDO) or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) hybrid models will bridge preclinical and clinical safety gaps.

Conclusion
Exo-gel composite systems represent a paradigm-shifting strategy for diabetic complication management by synergizing the pleiotropic regulatory capacity of Exos with the spatiotemporally controlled delivery of hydrogels. Preclinical studies have demonstrated their remarkable efficacy in enhancing angiogenesis, suppressing chronic inflammation, and accelerating tissue regeneration. Future efforts should prioritize CRISPR- and AI-empowered engineering strategy, multi-omics integration, and GMP-compliant production and large-scale clinical trials. Exo-gel systems can transition from benchside promise to bedside reality through such holistic efforts.
