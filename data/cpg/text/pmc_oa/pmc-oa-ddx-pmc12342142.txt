A community-based approach to ethical decision-making in artificial intelligence for health care
Abstract
Objectives
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is transforming healthcare by improving diagnostics, treatment recommendations, and resource allocation. However, its implementation also raises ethical concerns, particularly regarding biases in AI algorithms trained on inequitable data, which may reinforce health disparities. This article introduces the AI COmmunity-based Ethical Dialogue and DEcision-making (CODE) framework to embed ethical deliberation into AI development, focusing on Electronic Health Records (EHRs).
Materials and Methods
We propose the AI CODE framework as a structured approach to addressing ethical challenges in AI-driven healthcare and ensuring its implementation supports health equity.
Results
The framework outlines 5 steps to advance health equity: (1) Contextual diversity and priority: Ensuring inclusive datasets and that AI reflects the community needs; (2) Sharing ethical propositions: Structured discussions on privacy, bias, and fairness; (3) Dialogic decision-making: Collaboratively with stakeholders to develop AI solutions; (4) Integrating ethical solutions: Applying solutions into AI design to enhance fairness; and (5) Evaluating effectiveness: Continuously monitoring AI to address emerging biases.
Discussion
We examine the framework’s role in mitigating AI biases through structured community engagement and its relevance within evolving healthcare policies. While the framework promotes ethical AI integration in healthcare, it also faces challenges in implementation.
Conclusion
The framework provides practical guidance to ensure AI systems are ethical, community-driven, and aligned with health equity goals.

Introduction
Artificial intelligence is integral to healthcare, offering transformative capabilities in diagnostics, treatment recommendations, and resource allocation. However, alongside its promise, AI also presents significant risks of creating new or reinforcing existing inequalities, particularly for marginalized populations, such as gender, racial and ethnic minorities, individuals with disabilities, and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds. AI systems trained on historical and contemporary data risk amplifying biases embedded in these datasets, and exacerbating disparities in healthcare access and outcomes. For example, algorithmic bias has contributed to Black patients receiving less access to opioid misuse treatment and being excluded from care management programs due to AI predictions prioritizing healthcare costs over illness severity. Given these challenges, AI scholars and researchers advocate for developing ethical frameworks integrating AI, ethics, and human rights into healthcare systems.
To address these concerns, we introduce the AI COmmunity-based ethical Dialogue and DEcision-making (CODE) framework, which incorporates ethical deliberation into AI design and implementation. This framework aims to mitigate bias in Electronic Health Records (EHRs), a critical component of clinician decision support (CDS) in the healthcare system.

Challenges of AI in healthcare
Ensuring that AI systems in healthcare contribute to equitable outcomes requires several critical challenges, particularly when integrating EHR data. Key concerns include: (1) algorithmic bias; (2) transparency and accountability; and (3) bias in EHR Data.

Algorithmic bias
AI models trained on EHRs often inherit biases embedded within the data, reinforcing systemic inequities. These biases are linked to longstanding disparities in healthcare access, treatment patterns, and diagnostic practices. As a result, when biases are embedded into AI-driven CDS tools, such biases can lead to unequal treatment recommendations, further disadvantaging marginalized patients. Building on this, research has shown that AI-driven risk assessment tools often underestimated the health risks of Black patients, leading to lower prioritization for care management programs, even when their conditions are equally or more severe than those of White patients. Integrated into clinical practices and perpetuated in EHR data, AI bias can result in diagnostic algorithms that underperform for marginalized patients, thereby exacerbating health disparities. Another study on pediatric obesity prediction highlights how AI models may inadvertently amplify disparities, as predictive features can disproportionately favor or disadvantage specific populations based on historical healthcare patterns. Addressing biases requires targeted audits, bias mitigation techniques, and transparent AI model designs. Collaboration and shared accountability between AI developers, healthcare organizations, and policymakers are essential to ensure equitable patient recommendations.

Transparency and accountability
Ensuring transparency and accountability are critical to fostering trust in AI-driven healthcare decisions. AI models must be interpretable, allowing clinicians and patients to understand how decisions are made. They must also be explainable; without clear explanations, AI-driven recommendations may be viewed as untrustworthy, potentially reducing adoption and limiting their effectiveness in clinical decision-making. Tools such as SHapley Additive exPlanations and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations help provide insights into AI interpretability for decision-making. Regular AI audits and transparency reports can enhance accountability and ensure that their decision-making processes align with equitable care.

Bias in EHR data
EHRs are essential for AI training, but often contain incomplete, biased, or unrepresentative data. As a result, AI models trained on these datasets can perpetuate healthcare disparities. For instance, conditions that disproportionately affect certain racial or socioeconomic groups may be underdiagnosed or misclassified, leading AI systems to overlook or inadequately treat these populations. For example, a study on machine learning models found that predictions for Black children and Medicaid enrollees were skewed due to overrepresentation of conditions like asthma and allergic rhinitis. Similarly, predictive models for perinatal mood and anxiety disorders demonstrated how biases in training data led to disparities in diagnosis rates, reinforcing gaps in mental health treatment. However, data reweighing helped mitigate bias while maintaining model performance, significantly improving fairness metrics.
To reduce these risks, AI systems must incorporate bias-detection mechanisms, ongoing monitoring, and diverse datasets that reflect the full spectrum of patient populations. Additionally, healthcare organizations must critically assess how biases in clinical data influence AI predictions, ensuring that models do not reinforce existing disparities in care delivery. Addressing these challenges: algorithmic bias, transparency and accountability, and biased EHR data—is necessary to ensure AI in healthcare advances equity rather than exacerbates disparities. Without these safeguards, AI-driven tools will continue to mirror and magnify existing inequities.

The AI CODE framework
The AI CODE framework is founded on ethical dialogue and ethical deliberation. Ethical dialogue fosters co-learning by integrating diverse perspectives and ensuring all voices are heard without requiring consensus. It engages stakeholders: AI designers, clinicians, patients, community members, ethicists, and policymakers to share insights on AI use in healthcare. Ethical deliberation builds and enables collective decision-making where participants propose and evaluate solutions that reflect their values. Participants in the deliberation process have an equal status, ensuring inclusive decision-making. AI CODE is rooted in a pragmatist vision of ethical deliberation, emphasizing problem recognition, solution generation, and value-driven decision-making. This supports collecting stakeholder action scenarios as potential ethical solutions and evaluating the decision based on the selection of scenarios.

Empirical evidence and theoretical foundations
The AI CODE framework is grounded in evidence demonstrating the importance of community engagement in addressing AI biases and aligning HER systems with the needs of marginalized populations. Obermeyer et al. highlighted how racial bias in healthcare algorithms disadvantaged Black patients by equating lower healthcare utilization with lower healthcare needs. Algorithmic bias reinforces systemic inequities by relying on cost to predict Black patients’ risk and determine their enrollment in a care management program, even when they are sicker than their White counterparts. Integrating diverse datasets and community values into AI design can help prevent or reduce such biases from becoming embedded in healthcare systems. Additionally, studies by Hendricks-Sturrup et al. and Chin et al. demonstrate that multi-level, community-based approaches like AI CODE improve patient trust and reduce algorithmic bias. To address these issues, stakeholders collaborated with experts, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, and the National Institute for Minority Health and Health Disparities to mitigate bias in algorithms. This evidence reinforces the importance of co-designing AI systems with those most affected by inequities to ensure the final products are equitable, responsive, and aligned with societal values.

Applying the AI CODE framework to EHRs is crucial, as bias and inequity often arise from incomplete or unrepresentative data. Contextual Diversity and Priority: A diverse stakeholder group identifies systemic barriers affecting underserved populations. Incorporating health data from marginalized groups ensures that AI-driven risk stratification is inclusive. Addressing data gaps reduces the risk of perpetuating disparities in AI-driven tools. These discussions also help AI systems to reflect broader community priorities and support equity-centered decision-making.
Sharing Ethical Propositions: Structured discussions identify ethical concerns and inform AI design. Methods include focus groups, data exploration workshops, and sharing of lived experiences. Stakeholders identify risks, such as biased AI predictions based on incomplete patient data and propose safeguards. These discussions ensure that potential harms are proactively addressed before implementation.
Dialogic Decision-Making: Stakeholders collaboratively evaluate proposed ethical solutions based on feasibility and impact. EHR applications may incorporate socioeconomic factors, such as access to healthy food or housing stability into treatment plans. Integrating these considerations makes AI-driven recommendations more practical, equitable, and aligned with real-world needs.
Integrating Ethical Solutions into AI: Stakeholders review updated solutions to confirm alignment with community needs, particularly those of vulnerable populations. For instance, an AI tool predicting follow-up care for patients with chronic conditions might incorporate transportation barriers, recommending telehealth visits or closer clinic locations. This ensures the AI-driven recommendations are accessible for patients.
Evaluating the Process and AI’s Effectiveness: Continuous evaluation ensures AI systems remain responsive to patient and community needs. To ensure AI systems promote equitable outcomes, evaluation should include fairness metrics, real-world clinical performance assessments, and stakeholder feedback. For example, AI tools predicting risks should be monitored to assess performance across demographic groups. Audits can identify biases, disparities in treatment recommendations, and shifts in model accuracy. These approaches support necessary system refinements to align AI-driven decisions with health equity goals.

Discussion

Legal frameworks and policy relevance
The AI CODE framework aligns with ongoing efforts to address the ethical challenges of AI in healthcare, even amid shifting federal priorities. While previous administrations introduced AI safety and bias mitigation policies, recent changes have left the regulatory landscape uncertain at the federal and state levels. For instance, California’s AI safety bill (Senate Bill 1047) was vetoed over concerns about its alignment with federal AI regulations. In contrast, Colorado remains one of the few states actively addressing AI bias. The Colorado AI Act (Senate Bill 24-205) requires developers to assess and mitigate algorithmic discrimination in high-risk systems. Without clear federal regulation, the AI CODE framework offers a structured, community-driven approach to embedding ethical deliberation into AI development, ensuring bias and accountability are addressed.

Resource Constraints: Smaller healthcare organizations may lack the financial or technical capacity to implement the framework fully. Challenges include limited funding for community-driven deliberations, insufficient resources for stakeholder engagement, or staff training. Additionally, the number of complex cases to be handled and the lengthy decision-making process involved in resolving ethical issues in AI can make it difficult to integrate moral values into AI systems at scale. To address this, phased implementation strategies, such as pilot projects, can demonstrate the framework’s value before wider adoption. Seeking grants or external funding for community-led AI initiatives can help alleviate resource limitations.
Stakeholder Resistance: Some stakeholders may hesitate to engage in ethical deliberation due to unfamiliarity with AI ethics or mistrust in the process. Building trust through early and transparent engagement and foundational AI ethics training can help participants feel more confident in their involvement. Selecting real-world examples can further assist participants in connecting, demonstrating the value of ethical AI integration, and boosting confidence in contributing to the framework.
Limited Access to Diverse Datasets: Ensuring AI systems reflect marginalized communities requires diverse and representative datasets. However, data gaps persist especially in multi-tiered health systems like the U.S. Partnerships with community organizations and data-sharing agreements can help build more inclusive datasets, ensuring AI models capture a diverse population spectrum. Additionally, the AI CODE framework can facilitate data exchange between developers and implementers, mitigating patient harm while respecting their values.
Difficulty Measuring Long-Term Impacts: Ethical concerns in AI systems may only emerge after extended use, particularly as biases and disparities emerge over time. Long-term monitoring and evaluation processes are critical to addressing these evolving risks. However, continuous AI monitoring is costly. Although this monitoring is expensive, it should be noted that integrating AI with EHRs can improve efficiency, enhance patient outcomes, and reduce some of the financial burdens. Establishing audits and stakeholder feedback mechanisms ensures AI systems align with ethical standards and equity goals.

Conclusion
The AI CODE framework provides a structured, community-focused approach to ensuring that AI systems in healthcare are ethical, equitable, and responsive to the needs of diverse populations. Ethical AI ultimately represents both a technical challenge and a societal responsibility. By integrating ethical considerations into AI development, the framework aids in reducing biases, enhancing transparency, and aligning AI-driven decisions with real patient experiences. As AI continues to shape healthcare, it is vital to ensure it does so in ways that promote equity instead of reinforcing disparities. The AI CODE framework empowers stakeholders to develop AI systems prioritizing health equity.
