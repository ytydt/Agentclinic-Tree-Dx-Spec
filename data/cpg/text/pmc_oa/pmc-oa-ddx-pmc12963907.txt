Non-syndromic hyper-IgE in children: A practical approach☆
Hyper-IgE, generally defined as serum IgE levels exceeding 2000 IU/mL, presents a common yet complex diagnostic challenge in pediatric practice. While elevated serum IgE are frequently observed in atopic conditions such as food allergy or atopic eczema, or parasitic infections, they may also signal underlying monogenic immunological diseases, specifically inborn errors of immunity (IEI) categorized under hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES). Distinguishing between common atopic diseases and HIES is essential, especially in children with early-onset, severe, or treatment-resistant presentations. This review focuses on non-syndromic causes of hyper-IgE in children, aiming to provide a practical, structured framework for clinicians. A broad array of conditions, including allergic diseases, infections, inflammatory disorders, malignancies, drug reactions, and environmental exposures, can result in elevated IgE levels. Given this wide differential, a systematic approach that incorporates detailed clinical history, physical examination, and targeted investigations is critical to guide diagnostic reasoning. To aid clinical decision-making, the authors propose a stepwise diagnostic algorithm that prioritizes common causes while also alerting clinicians to red flags suggestive of IEI or other rare conditions. This approach facilitates timely referral for immunologic or genetic evaluation when appropriate and minimizes unnecessary testing. Increased awareness of the diverse etiologies of hyper-IgE can improve diagnostic accuracy, enhance early intervention, and reduce morbidity. Future research should aim to refine diagnostic strategies, validate clinical algorithms, and develop standardized guidelines. Moreover, long-term data regarding characterization and subsequent follow-up of children with an isolated increase in serum IgE levels is fundamental to understanding the clinical and immunological trajectories of these patients.

Immunoglobulin E (IgE), first described as “reagins” by Kimishige and Teruko Ishizaka in 1966, makes a fundamental contribution to the host immune system, especially against parasitic infections, and in the pathogenesis of allergic diseases, playing a key role in type I hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis. Serum IgE typically ranges from approximately 2 to 214 IU/mL in adults, which is significantly lower than IgG by a factor of 10,000 to 50,000. In children, IgE levels that are elevated by more than 2 standard deviations above the mean according to the specific age range are considered high (Table 1). Moreover, independently of age, serum IgE levels exceeding 2000 IU/mL are classified as remarkably high or ‘hyper-IgE’. The evaluation of a patient with hyper-IgE still represents a significant clinical challenge, especially in children. Indeed, markedly elevated IgE levels may be seen in children with apparently no other conditions than atopic diseases, such as food allergy and atopic dermatitis. However, hyper-IgE can be the hallmark of inborn errors of immunity (IEI), identifying the clinical conditions called “hyper-IgE syndromes (HIES)”. These conditions will be briefly discussed herein but have been recently reviewed by the Immunology Task Force of the Italian Society of Pediatric Allergy and Immunology (SIAIP) in Castagnoli et al. The current review aims to discuss non-syndromic conditions associated with increased serum IgE levels and propose a diagnostic algorithm to assist pediatricians in the differential diagnosis of children with increased serum IgE levels. A detailed review of available literature was conducted using the PubMed database, relying on a critical assessment in the absence of standardized methods or statistical analyses. Key terms included “IgE” OR “Immunoglobulin E” AND “hyper-IgE in children” OR “pediatric hyper-IgE” OR “hyper-IgE syndromes” OR “non-syndromic hyper-IgE”. Our analysis included observational studies, randomized controlled studies, reviews, meta-analyses, and evidence-based guidelines.

Molecular mechanisms of IgE production and regulation
IgE is a tetramer composed of 2 light (L) chains (κ or λ) and 2 ε-heavy (H), connected by intrachain disulfide bonds. The variable sequences (VH and VL) at the N-terminal regions of these chains form antigen-specific binding sites. Meanwhile, the C-terminal regions of the heavy chains comprise 4 Cε domains (Cε 1–4), with Cε 2–4 forming the Fc domains responsible for isotype-specific functions, such as receptor binding (FcεRI and FcεRII). Two forms of IgE exist: a membrane-bound B-cell receptor expressed on B cells following class switching, and a serum form secreted by plasma cells. With a serum half-life of 2–3 days, IgE has the shortest lifespan among immunoglobulins, especially compared to IgG's 23 days. This brief half-life may limit IgE cross-reactivity, thus minimizing the risk of anaphylactic reactions. Following infection or immunisation, class-switch recombination (CSR) in naïve B cells leads to the production of IgE antibodies. CSR occurs via 2 main pathways: direct switching from IgM to IgE or sequential switching through an IgG1 intermediate. The Iε promoter, which regulates CSR, contains binding sites for various transcription factors, including STAT6, NFkB, Pax5, and AP-1. Upon antigen exposure or parasitic infection, epithelial and innate immune cells release cytokines, including IL-4, which drives CSR by inducing germline transcription and activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID). The interaction between CD40L on activated T helper (TH) cells and CD40 on B cells further stimulates the NF-κB pathway. NF-kB and STAT6 binding sites cooperate to enhance transcription. The essential role of STAT6 is highlighted by observations in STAT6-deficient mice, which exhibit similar defects to IL-4-deficient mice, such as impaired Th2 differentiation and class switching. Moreover, mice lacking the p50 subunit of NF-kB show reduced IgE production and ε-germline transcription, underscoring NF-kB's importance. IL-13, like IL-4, promotes IgE synthesis by activating STAT6 and appears to enhance IgE production from follicular TH (TFH) cells, a TH subset expressing CD40L. In contrast, IL-21 inhibits IgE synthesis by suppressing ε-germline transcription via STAT3 signalling. IL-21 receptor (IL-21R)-deficient mice exhibit elevated IgE levels compared to wild-type counterparts. The co-expression of IL-4, IL-13, and IL-21 by TFH cells suggests a finely tuned regulatory mechanism controlling IgE production. Beyond IgE, TFH cells play a crucial role in the production of IgG4. While IgE is primarily associated with acute allergic responses, IgG4 has a regulatory role in chronic immune reactions and is linked to IgG4-related diseases, characterized by persistent inflammation and plasma cell infiltration. The interplay between IgG4 and IgE production, mediated by TFH cells, highlights the immune system's balance, with implications for autoimmune and allergic disorders.

When evaluating a child with serum hyper-IgE, it is crucial to recognize common features and potential red flags indicative of HIES and other IEI presenting with atopic phenotypes. Table 2 summarizes the common features of HIES, and Table 3 lists the main red flags to consider. IEI can manifest early in life with severe atopic disease, high serum IgE, and eosinophilia, often unresponsive to conventional therapies. The presence of additional clinical features, such as recurrent infections and autoimmunity, or a family history suggestive of IEI, should prompt further investigation. Of note, elevated serum IgE, traditionally considered a hallmark of HIES, is also observed in various other IEI, including Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome, Immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked, and Omenn syndrome. Advances in genetic testing over the past decade have significantly improved the diagnosis and characterization of these disorders, enabling targeted therapeutic approaches. Failure to recognize an underlying IEI may result in misdiagnosis, delayed treatment, and increased morbidity. Therefore, comprehensive immunological evaluation and genetic testing are critical in cases where atopic diseases are severe, atypical, or associated with other immunological abnormalities. A diagnostic algorithm to assist clinicians in identifying HIES and other IEI with atopic phenotypes, as well as the single conditions that comprise the umbrella term “hyper-IgE syndromes,” has been recently described in Castagnoli et al. Moreover, an expert consensus on diagnosis and management of these conditions is now available.

Infectious diseases
One of the most common conditions to consider when elevated IgE serum levels are found is an infection; in particular, parasitic infections are the most common infectious conditions associated with elevated serum IgE levels.

Parasitic infection
As IgE's primary immunological function is to protect the organism against parasites, 1 of the first conditions to investigate is parasitic diseases. Strongyloides, Toxocara, Trichuris, Ascaris, Echinococcus, hookworms, Filaria, and Schistosoma are common parasites known to increase serum IgE levels, frequently associated with eosinophilia.
Strongyloidiasis is a soil-transmitted helminthiasis endemic to the subtropics and tropics, whereas in temperate regions, poor sanitation facilitates the transmission through fecal contamination. Humans are generally infected transcutaneously by filariform larvae that naturally occur in dogs, primates, and humans. Acute infection can present with irritation at the site where larvae penetrate the skin, sometimes accompanied by localized swelling or hives, and gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pain, or anorexia). Infection may become chronic, clinically asymptomatic with elevated serum IgE levels and/or peripheral eosinophilia, or symptomatic with gastrointestinal manifestations (diarrhea and intermittent vomiting or constipation), dermatologic manifestations (recurrent urticaria), and/or unusual manifestations (e.g., arthritis, nephrotic syndrome, focal hepatic lesions). The diagnosis is based on stool analysis, which involves the detection of larvae. Antibody detection with immunoassays (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays [ELISAs]) is of limited utility. Coproantigen detection assays and molecular diagnostics (e.g., PCR) are other possible diagnostic tests, although used less frequently.
Human toxocariasis is one of the most common soil-transmitted zoonoses, resulting from infestation by Toxocara ascarid larvae endemic in rural areas, particularly in the tropics. Several conditions increase the risk of infestations, including poor personal hygiene, geophagia (the practice of eating soil), and the consumption of contaminated raw vegetables or raw meat from paratenic hosts (such as rabbits, chickens, or lambs). Human infection is caused by the ingestion of eggs from contaminated soil (geophagia, pica), raw vegetables, or larvae from undercooked poultry giblets. The diagnosis is based on ELISA and Western Blot using Toxocara antigens. The disease may manifest with the following conditions: (1) Visceral larva migrans: typical in children between 2 and 7 years old, who usually present with abdominal pain, reduced appetite, agitation, fever, respiratory symptoms such as coughing and wheezing, hepatomegaly and marked eosinophilia, leukocytosis, and hyper-gammaglobulinemia. (2) Ocular larva migrans: typically unilateral in children, which may be subclinical or present with visual loss, with acute/subacute onset. Funduscopy and biomicroscopic examination often reveal endophthalmitis, uveitis, retinal granulomatous lesions, papillitis, or inflammatory masses (snowbanks) in the peripheral vitreous. (3) Other syndromes may present with systemic and respiratory symptoms such as fever, anorexia, cephalalgia, abdominal pain, gastrointestinal distress, lethargy, neurobehavioral disturbances, pharyngitis, lower respiratory tract infections, limb aches, cervical lymph node enlargement, and hepatomegaly. (4) Neurological toxocariasis.
Trichuris trichiura could be asymptomatic but in case of severe infection, it causes trichuris dysentery syndrome characterized by chronic, mucoid, bloody diarrhea, iron-deficiency anemia, wasting, and stunting. The diagnosis is established via stool examination for eggs.
Ascaris lumbricoides is acquired by consuming embryonated eggs from materials contaminated with feces. The infection is widespread in tropical and subtropical zones, particularly in areas where poverty, inadequate sanitation infrastructure, and limited sewage treatment are common. Children younger than 5 years old are most affected. The intestinal ascariasis is mostly asymptomatic, but nausea, anorexia, recurrent abdominal pain, discomfort and distension and intermittent diarrhea may be present. Potential complications include Löeffler's syndrome, bowel obstruction, biliary colic, recurrent bacterial cholangitis, cholecystitis (including acalculous forms), obstructive jaundice, cholelithiasis, pancreatitis, and nutritional deficiencies. Diagnosis is confirmed by identifying characteristic eggs in stool samples through direct microscopy or after applying concentration methods.
Echinococcus (also known as “hydatid disease” or “hydatidosis”) is a foodborne parasitic disease with a high prevalence in Spain, southern Italy, Sardinia, Africa, Australia, and South America. The disease occurs frequently asymptomatic, with common incidental findings of hydatid cysts during screening abdominal ultrasounds or necropsies. Symptoms may vary significantly depending on the cystic location (mostly in the liver - 55–75% - and lung - 10–30%), size, and condition, with symptoms of increasing pressure in the cyst area accompanied by elevated blood eosinophils. Diagnosis is established by ultrasonography and chest X-ray. Serologic tests (including ELISA and Western Blot) are used for diagnosis confirmation.
Hookworms (Ancylostoma duodenale, Ancylostoma ceylanicum, and Necator americanus) commonly infect humans through percutaneous penetration of larvae, or via percutaneous or oral (and even trans-colonial) transmission of the ancylostomatids. The infection is commonly asymptomatic. Still, it may cause iron deficiency anemia, especially in subjects who suffer from poor nutrition or have low (e.g., women of reproductive age and children) iron stores. Migrating larvae may cause vesicular, tortuous and intensely pruritic lesions. Eosinophilic pneumonia, characterized by cough, respiratory distress, and hemoptysis, represents a possible diagnosis. Perioral infection may induce Wakana syndrome, characterized by pharyngeal irritation, nausea and vomiting, as well as cough and dyspnea. Intestinal infection can cause abdominal pain, tenderness or discomfort, weakness, fatigue, anemia, generalized edema and joint pain. Useful diagnostic tools are chest X-ray, bronchoscopy, microscopic analysis of sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage, or gastric aspirate, and stool microscopy in case of intestinal infection.
Filaria infection could manifest as follows: (1) Acute onset: fever with chills, lymphadenopathy, hematuria, chyluria, epididymoorchitis and funiculitis. (2) Chronic disease: hydrocele, lymphoedema, and elephantiasis. (3) Occult disease: tropical pulmonary eosinophilia, mono/polyarthritis, tenosynovitis, glomerulonephropathy, retroperitoneal lymphangitis, endomyocardial fibrosis, recurrent scleritis, iridocyclitis, macular edema, urticaria, and bronchial asthma-mimicking symptoms. Antibody-antigen tests, Doppler ultrasound, and lymphoscintigraphy are used to establish a diagnosis.
Schistosoma infection typically occurs following direct exposure to freshwater contaminated with the parasite, commonly during activities like swimming or washing. Once inside the body, schistosome eggs can become lodged in organs such as the liver and bladder, triggering granulomatous inflammation, periportal fibrosis, and proliferation of bile ducts. Acute schistosomiasis, also referred to as Katayama syndrome, is characterized by the sudden onset of symptoms such as fever, fatigue, general malaise, muscle aches, urticarial rash, wheezing, headache, abdominal pain, elevated eosinophil counts, and potentially hepatosplenomegaly. Chronic schistosomiasis may present in various forms. (1) In intestinal schistosomiasis symptoms include recurrent abdominal pain, persistent diarrhea, and rectal bleeding. In some cases, hepatosplenic involvement may develop, with features such as periportal fibrosis, discomfort in the upper abdomen, a firm and nodular liver, splenomegaly, and signs of portal hypertension. (2) Pulmonary schistosomiasis can lead to pulmonary hypertension. (3) Urogenital schistosomiasis may present with hematuria, increased frequency and urgency of urination, dysuria, and suprapubic pain. Advanced disease may result in fibrotic urinary tract obstruction, causing hydroureter and hydronephrosis. In women, complications may include pelvic pain, stress incontinence, infertility, and an increased risk of miscarriage. In men, symptoms may involve low sperm count, blood in semen, testicular inflammation, and prostatitis. Additional manifestations may include swimmer's itch (a pruritic rash), low-grade fever, anemia, and malnutrition, which can adversely affect child growth and development. Ectopic deposition of eggs, though rare, can lead to cerebral schistosomiasis, manifesting as headache, vomiting, visual disturbances, high-grade fever, altered mental status, or focal seizures such as Jacksonian epilepsy. Moreover, spinal involvement can present as acute transverse myelitis or subacute myeloradiculopathy. Diagnosis is confirmed by microscopic identification of eggs in urine or stool, or by detecting them in tissue biopsies (e.g., rectum, liver, intestine, or bladder). Serologic testing for schistosomal antigens serves as a standard screening approach. Lab findings may include eosinophilia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia—often due to splenic sequestration. Abdominal ultrasound is a helpful imaging tool for assessing hepatosplenic disease.

Non-parasitic infections
HIV infection. Serum IgE levels could be high in children with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-1 infection, related to allergic reactions and adverse reactions to drugs. The reason for that is beyond an imbalance between a “T(H)1-like” and a “T(H)2-like” cytokine profile. Although atopic symptoms (sinusitis, eczema, and wheezing) are common in HIV-infected children, increased IgE is not associated with atopic symptoms in children. Particularly, specific IgE against HIV antigens and IgE elevation can be seen in children with or without aggravation of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome, as this does not correlate with the degree of immunodeficiency.
Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Serum IgE levels may be elevated in atopic patients with M. tuberculosis infection, with levels higher than those in non-atopic patients. IgE concentrations decrease after successful treatment of tuberculosis, and serum IgE levels do not influence the therapeutic outcome. This could be explained by the probable development of an intrinsic propensity to produce IgE, likely due to higher parasitic burdens and/or genetic predisposition in specific populations.
Cytomegalovirus (CMV). CMV infection could elevate serum IgE levels. Hyper-IgE could be found more frequently during CMV primary infection than in patients with recurrent CMV infection.
Epstein-Barr virus. IgE serum levels increase during the acute infection and return to baseline within weeks to months. In the context of polyclonal Ig production, this is a sign of the intense immune response during the acute phase of infectious mononucleosis.
Leprosy. Mycobacterium leprae may cause an inconsistent elevation in serum IgE levels, with no significant difference in the mean serum IgE levels between patients with lepromatous and tuberculoid leprosy.
Candidiasis. During Candida infections (especially in recurrent vaginal candidiasis), an elevation of serum IgE may be observed as an increase of IgE antibodies against Candida epitopes.

Atopic diseases
As mentioned above, an increase in serum IgE is often observed in atopic diseases.
Atopic dermatitis (AD). Even if more detailed subendotypes have been recently described, AD is commonly classified into 2 major subtypes based on serum IgE level: extrinsic (elevated IgE) and intrinsic (normal IgE). Intrinsic AD is associated with a later onset, is often more severe and is not typically related to barrier dysfunction. Extrinsic AD is more frequent (80% of AD cases) and is associated with protein allergies and early onset. It is linked to impaired skin barrier function, characterized by higher transepidermal water loss (TEWL), and possibly associated with filaggrin (FLG) gene mutations. While both subtypes exhibit TH2 signalling, intrinsic AD also displays stronger TH1 and TH17 activation, characterised by higher levels of IFN-γ–producing TH1 cells in the blood. Elevated total IgE levels are found in most patients with extrinsic atopic dermatitis, with some individuals potentially having very high levels (higher than 2000 IU/mL), especially when AD is associated with multiple food allergies and higher AD severity. Determinants of disease severity among patients with atopic dermatitis and in eczema exacerbation in children hospitalized for atopic dermatitis. Elevated serum total IgE levels are associated with eczema exacerbation in children hospitalized for atopic dermatitis.
Food allergy (FA). The relationship between food allergy and high IgE levels has been extensively studied. In 2001, Sampson et al. demonstrated a direct correlation between the outcome of oral food challenge and food-specific IgE levels. Specifically, the higher the food-specific IgE level, the greater the risk of reaction to the OFC. Indeed, total serum IgE levels at baseline, along with the downward trend of food-specific IgE levels over time, are helpful in the prognosis of natural tolerance acquisition. Anyway, the correlation between serum total IgE levels and specific IgE is not linear. Moreover, the presence of AD as a comorbidity represents a factor that can't be underestimated. Specifically, almost one-third of children with moderate-severe AD have IgE-mediated hypersensitivity to food proteins. An evaluation for food allergy should be performed in these patients. Starting from the relationship between FA and high serum IgE levels, anti-IgE monoclonal antibody omalizumab is considered as monotherapy and as an adjunct to OIT in patients with IgE-mediated FA.
Allergic asthma. Children with asthma usually have higher IgE levels than individuals without asthma. Moreover, high levels of IgE in non-atopic patients increased the risk of developing asthma. Interestingly, lung function correlates with serum IgE, with total IgE levels correlating with more severe airflow limitation and with asthma severity. Airway hyperresponsiveness, typically measured by bronchoprovocation challenges with methacholine, correlated strongly with serum IgE levels.
Allergic rhinitis. Patients with allergic rhinitis commonly show an elevation in serum total IgE levels, with higher values in those patients with concomitant allergic asthma and, with regard to adolescents, in both current and past cigarette smokers. In a recent study, serum total IgE were evaluated in 584 patients with mild intermittent, mild persistent, moderate to severe intermittent and moderate to severe persistent AR. The serum total IgE level was not significantly different among the groups, ranging from 296.9 to 452.5 KU/L and never exceeding 1500 KU/L. Of note, for both allergic asthma and allergic rhinitis, it is unusual to have very high serum levels of IgE (especially higher than 2000 IU/mL).
Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA). ABPA is a hypersensitivity lung disorder triggered by an immune reaction to Aspergillus fumigatus (AF). Contributing risk factors include prolonged azithromycin use, bacterial colonization of the airways, high-dose inhaled corticosteroids, genetic predispositions such as CFTR mutations (as seen in cystic fibrosis), and atopic conditions—particularly severe asthma and eosinophilia. Elevated total serum IgE and specific IgE antibodies to Aspergillus fumigatus are typical findings. An increased absolute eosinophil count is also common. Diagnosis is established using a scoring system that integrates radiologic evidence (typically via high-resolution chest CT) and immunologic markers. Spirometry may reveal airflow obstruction; however, normal spirometry does not rule out a diagnosis of ABPA.
Allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS). AFRS is a chronic, Th2-mediated form of rhinosinusitis, characterized by nasal polyps, distinctive CT imaging, eosinophilic mucus, and the presence of fungal elements in surgical samples—without evidence of tissue invasion. The Bent and Kuhn criteria are used for diagnosis and include major criteria (nasal polyposis, detection of fungi on staining, eosinophilic mucin without tissue invasion, characteristic CT scan findings with soft tissue density variations, and type I hypersensitivity to fungal antigens) and minor criteria (abundant eosinophilic infiltration, positive fungal staining, and absence of conditions such as diabetes or immunodeficiency). Elevated total IgE and the presence of specific IgE antibodies targeting fungal allergens are commonly seen.

Inflammatory diseases
Besides infections and atopic diseases, inflammatory conditions may be associated with increased IgE levels.
Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA or Churg–Strauss syndrome). EGPA is a small-vessel vasculitis mainly involving the upper and lower airways and classically present with severe asthma, systemic and tissue eosinophilia, and extrapulmonary manifestations (including sinusitis and nasal polyps; skin involvement with subcutaneous nodules, palpable purpura and/or non-specified cutaneous symptoms; gastrointestinal symptoms as abdominal pain, colitis, hepatitis cytolysis, and/or gastritis; cardiac involvement with pericarditis, myocarditis, arterial hypertension, arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy and/or heart failure; neurological manifestations as mononeuritis, polyneuropathy and/or central nervous symptoms; proteinuria, arthralgias and/or myalgias). General symptoms (fatigue, weight loss, and fever) are possible. Laboratory examinations typically reveal eosinophilia, normal or elevated C-reactive protein levels, increased IgE serum levels, and the detection of ANCA (usually pANCA/anti-MPO antibodies). Diagnosis is challenging in children and several clinical score has been used in the different studies reported to date.
Kawasaki disease (KD). KD is an acute self-limiting systemic vasculitis typically affecting the pediatric population. The diagnosis is made when clinical criteria are met. Within the several non-specific laboratory findings (raised acute phase inflammatory markers - erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein -, neutrophil leukocytosis, pyuria, raised platelet count 10 days after the onset of the illness, and possible raised transaminases or bilirubin), also IgE (and IL-5) levels appear to be significantly higher.
Kimura disease is a benign rare chronic inflammatory disorder of unknown etiology that involves deep subcutaneous tissue and lymph nodes of the head and neck region with regional lymphadenopathy and/or salivary gland enlargement, mainly without systemic symptoms. Laboratory findings are elevated serum immunoglobulin E levels and peripheral blood eosinophilia. The disease is uncommon in pediatric age. Diagnostic gold standard is histopathological examination of involved regions samples.

Neoplasms
Less frequently, increased serum IgE levels are associated with neoplastic diseases.
Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (nHL). HL may occur with associated increased total IgE levels (even markedly increased), although it is a rare event that is more common in nodular sclerosing and Stage II Hodgkin's disease. Notably, if an elevation in serum IgE levels is accompanied by no underlying allergic diathesis or other diseases associated with IgE elevation, HL should be included in the differential diagnosis. Narrowing additional investigations, such as a complete blood cell count, LDH, and chest X-ray, could help in the diagnosis. Increased production of IgE could be due to a general disturbance in the regulation of humoral immune response. Within the nHL clinical spectrum, T-cell lymphoma could present with hyper-IgE as a consequence of monoclonal expansion of a subpopulation of IgE specific helper T cells. In particular, in Sézary syndrome (SS) hyper-IgE is frequent, with or without atopic disease. It is due to the presence of malignant circulating T memory cells with skin homing potential with the ability to produce a variety of Th2 soluble factors, including IL-4 and IL-5.
IgE myeloma: IgE myeloma is 0.01% of all plasmocytomas and present with a more malignant clinical course.

Drugs
Many drugs (antibiotics, NSAIDs, biologics, chemotherapy) may cause allergic reactions through any mechanism of hypersensitivity. Antibiotics, in particular β-lactams, are the most common cause of immediate reactions mediated by IgE antibodies, leading to mast cell and basophil degranulation with symptoms ranging from urticaria to anaphylaxis. Serum IgE could rise due to the presence of specific IgE against the antibiotic involved in the reaction. However, in a study in which patients developed an immediate reaction after penicillin treatment and in vitro assay to serum IgE antibodies to benzylpenicilloyl and amoxicilloyl were performed, low levels of specific sIgE were mostly found. IgE-mediated allergy is investigated by skin testing and drug challenge.

Miscellaneous

Tobacco smoke
It is well known that tobacco smoke determines various immunological effects, both proinflammatory and immunosuppressive. Within the proinflammatory effects, increased Th2-regulated allergic disease is of particular interest. Tobacco smoke contains endotoxin (lipopolysaccharides, LPS), a powerful inflammatory agent, which could contribute to elevated serum IgE levels. In addition, early exposure to tobacco smoke may increase the risk of IgE sensitization to indoor inhalants and food allergens.

Nephrotic syndrome (NS)
IgE may play a role in the development of kidney diseases related to hypersensitivity reactions. High serum IgE levels could be found in patient with NS. In children, IgE rise may reflect disease activity and be related to poor responses to steroids and to poor disease prognosis and frequent relapses. In NS, T-cell dysfunction and disruptions in cytokine signalling contribute to alterations in glomerular permeability, damaging the filtration barrier and ultimately leading to proteinuria. Serum IL-4 levels are not increased in patients with NS, regardless of whether they are steroid-sensitive or resistant. Serum soluble CD23 (sCD23) levels are significantly higher than normal controls and correlate with total IgE levels. In individuals with IgA nephropathy, serum levels of sCD23 have been shown to correlate positively with IgE concentrations.

Bone Marrow Transplantation (BMT) and Graft versus Host Disease (GvHD)
Total serum IgE levels may increase in a high proportion of patients after BMT, both in those who develop GvHD and those who do not. The absolute elevation is higher in patients with subsequent GvHD. Notably, the rise in IgE does not correlate with an increase in eosinophil granulocytes. In most patients, there is no concurrent elevation in serum levels of IgG, IgA, or IgM. The association of IgE elevation and GVHD is not constant. In fact, both recipients of syngeneic, allogenic and autologous grafts could show a rise in total IgE levels. Augmented IgE synthesis may be a natural consequence of immune reconstitution in the presence of potentially reaginic agents (e.g., antibiotics and infectious agents). This rise in IgE level is commonly temporary.

Bullous pemphigoid (BP)
BP is an autoimmune blistering disease characterized by autoantibodies targeting cellular adhesion molecules (IgG and/or IgE autoantibodies against BP180 and/or BP230). Clinically it is characterized by tense blisters following an urticarial or erythematous phase. It is uncommon in children, affecting mostly elderly individuals. Elevated levels of circulating IgE, with associated eosinophilia, reflect the linear deposition of IgE at the basement membrane of the skin. These findings are associated with increased serum levels of Th2 biomarkers. Both IgE autoantibodies and a Th2 immune response may play a pathogenetic role in BP, as also demonstrated by reports of clinical benefits after omalizumab and dupilumab treatment.

Increased serum IgE levels in children can be indicative of various underlying conditions, ranging from atopic diseases and parasitic infections to more complex immune dysregulation disorders, including HIES. Given the diverse range of potential etiologies, a structured approach to diagnosis is crucial. The proposed diagnostic algorithm emphasizes a stepwise evaluation that starts with a detailed clinical history and physical examination, alongside initial laboratory investigations. Early consideration of red flags and hallmark features of HIES, as outlined in Table 2, Table 3, is essential in differentiating primary immune disorders from common atopic diseases. First-line diagnostic tests should aim to evaluate common causes such as parasitic infections and atopic conditions (e.g., atopic dermatitis and food allergy). When no clear cause is found, more specific second-line investigations, including serologic tests for non-parasitic infections and assessments for inflammatory conditions (e.g., EGPA), should be performed. In cases where malignancy or rare inflammatory disorders are suspected, advanced diagnostic modalities, such as lymph node biopsy or comprehensive imaging studies (e.g., CT-PET), may be warranted. The algorithm presented in Fig. 1, Fig. 2 aims to streamline the diagnostic process, reduce unnecessary testing, and enable the timely identification of underlying conditions. When a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation fails to identify an underlying cause, longitudinal follow-up of children presenting with isolated elevation of serum IgE levels is crucial for detecting emerging clinical features or laboratory abnormalities that could ultimately lead to a definitive diagnosis.

Conclusions
Non-syndromic hyper-IgE in children presents a significant diagnostic challenge due to the broad spectrum of potential underlying causes. This review emphasizes the importance of a comprehensive and systematic approach to evaluation, considering both common conditions and rare immune dysregulation syndromes. The proposed algorithm provides a practical framework for clinicians, facilitating differential diagnosis. Early identification and appropriate management of underlying conditions associated with hyper-IgE are vital to reducing morbidity and improving long-term outcomes. Advances in genetic testing and immunological research continue to enhance our understanding of HIES, paving the way for more targeted therapeutic interventions. Future studies should focus on refining diagnostic criteria and developing evidence-based guidelines to further aid clinicians in managing this complex clinical presentation. Moreover, long-term data regarding characterization and subsequent follow-up of children with an isolated increase in serum IgE levels is fundamental to understanding the clinical and immunological evolutionary trajectories of this condition.
