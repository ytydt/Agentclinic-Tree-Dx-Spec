Decreasing Proprotein Convertase 5 Causes Hirschsprung’s Disease by Disrupting Neural Crest Cell Migration
Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR) is a disease where the migration of vagal neural crest cells (NCCs) is impaired during enteric nervous system (ENS) development, resulting in symptoms such as abdominal distention, constipation, and growth failure, which are life-threatening for the affected children. However, only a few genes have been identified for HSCR thus far. In this study, we performed a genome-wide association study followed by integrated multi-omics analysis and identified proprotein convertase 5 (PCSK5) as a new HSCR-susceptibility gene.

We conducted an HSCR genome-wide association study in the southern Chinese population consisting of 4107 samples (623 patients with HSCR and 3484 controls) (Supplementary Figure 1A). Besides the well-established HSCR-associated loci, including RET and NRG1, we additionally identified susceptibility loci surpassing genome-wide significance near LINC01752, IDO2, and SEMA3A. We also observed risk loci with a slightly lower association significance between 5 × 10-7 and 5 × 10-8 near or within the LOC441601, MIR548AD, and PCSK5 region (Figure 1A and Supplementary Figure 1B). Next, using an independent cohort containing 1337 cases and 1041 controls, we successfully replicated the associations for the lead SNP rs6040150 (upstream of JAG1) and rs1339253 (linked with the PCSK5 lead SNP rs6560480, LD r2 = 0.97) (Figure 1B–C). Interestingly, PCSK5 was explicitly expressed in small intestines and colons, indicating that PCSK5 could be a functionally guided associated gene with HSCR (Supplementary Figure 1C).
We found that the HSCR risk allele rs1339253-C was significantly correlated with lower expression of PCSK5 in cultured fibroblasts (Supplementary Figure 1D). We further validated its regulatory potential using Luciferase assay in the SK-N-SH cells, a commonly used cell line for HSCR studies. Results showed that the rs1339253-C significantly weakened the transcription activity (Figure 1D). Next, we found fetal intestines revealed specifically strong enhancer signals surrounding rs1339253 compared with signals from other non-intestinal tissues (Figure 1E). These results suggest that the rs1339253 risk allele may increase HSCR susceptibility by down-regulating PCSK5 expression.

Then, using spatial RNA sequencing data of colons from 12 post-conceptual weeks fetus, we found a notable number of PCSK5-expressing cells at the myenteric plexus marked by RET (Supplementary Figure 2A). Moreover, we collected the HSCR ganglionic and aganglionic colons and showed that PCSK5 was expressed in neurons, with decreased production in aganglionic colons (Figure 2A and Supplementary Figure 2B), suggesting that insufficient PCSK5 expression is related to the neuronal dysfunction.
To investigate the role of PCSK5 in ENS development, we first checked the in vitro gene expression at a single-cell level in NCCs derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). One cluster (hs_NCC) showed higher expression of PCSK5 as well as NCC markers, suggesting an essential role of PCSK5 in NCCs (Supplementary Figure 2C–D). Then, we checked the in vivo gene expression using enteric cells isolated from E13.5 mouse embryonic guts, a critical stage for early ENS development in mice. We found cluster E13.5_c5 showed strong expression of migratory genes, and its biological function was significantly enriched in “regulation of cell migration,” indicating these cells may be at the wavefront of NCC invasion. Interestingly, Pcsk5 was highly co-expressed with the migratory genes in this cluster, indicating its function in NCC migration (Supplementary Figure 2E–G). In iPSCs-derived NCCs from patients with HSCR, we validated the decreased expression of PCSK5 and migratory genes (Supplementary Figure 2H). Additionally, we silenced PCSK5 in iPSCs-induced NCCs and detected lower expression of migration-related genes except for ACTA2 (Figure 2B and Supplementary Figure 3A–E).
Next, we used both in vitro and in vivo experiments to validate the effect of PCSK5 reduction on NCC migration. In vitro, the wound healing assay showed compromised migration capability with PCSK5 knockdown at 8 hours and significant impairment in wound closure at 24 hours (Figure 2C). The transwell experiment showed that remarkably fewer cells migrated through the membrane in the PCSK5 silencing group (Figure 2D). In vivo, we generated a pcsk5b-morpholino model in zebrafish embryos. pcsk5a and pcsk5b are 2 zebrafish genes orthologous to human PCSK5. pcsk5b showed much stronger expression than pcsk5a in both whole zebrafish and intestines, as well as in the neural crest cells of zebrafish embryos at 18 hours post-fertilization (hpf) (Supplementary Figure 3F–I). We observed impaired migration of NCCs marked by sox10 at the end of the pcsk5b morphants’ intestines at 72 hpf (29/40; 72.5%) (Figure 2E) and fewer enteric neurons in the intestines at 120 hpf (Supplementary Figure 3J).
To unpin the underlying mechanism, we checked the transcriptomic alterations after silencing PCSK5 in human iPSC-induced NCCs. We observed that the genes involved in Wnt signaling were down-regulated along with the PCSK5 silencing, including WNT11 and Wnt receptor FZD7, which are essential for NCC migration (Supplementary Figure 3K and Figure 2F). This is consistent with the observation that Wnt-Frizzled signaling, a key functional signaling for the wavefront enteric NCCs, is disrupted in colons of patients with HSCR. Thus, our results suggested that PCSK5 reduction regulated cell migration through the Wnt signaling pathway.
In summary, we identified PCSK5 as a new HSCR risk gene. Decreasing PCSK5 expression was observed in the aganglionic colons. Lacking PCSK5 caused impaired NCC migration. Our study showed that PCSK5 plays an essential role for ENS development through Wnt signaling.
