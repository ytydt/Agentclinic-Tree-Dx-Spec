Evaluation of Artificial Intelligence Conversational Models in Providing Information on Dental Implants: A Comparative Analysis of ChatGPT, Gemini and MedGebra
ABSTRACT
Aims and Objectives
This cross‐sectional study aims to compare the accuracy, precision and clarity of responses to dental implant questions from three artificial intelligence (AI) models: ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT‐4), Gemini (Google) and MedGebra.
Materials and Methods
56 questions from an EAO patient guide were used and responses from each AI system were evaluated independently by two oral surgery specialists using a four‐point Likert scale for correctness, precision and clarity.
Results
ChatGPT consistently scored highest especially in clarity with perfect marks from both evaluators. Gemini ranked lower in accuracy and precision but exhibited similar clarity. MedGebra received the lowest ratings on all criteria. Though consistency generally remained excellent, the inter‐rater reliability study revealed some variance in agreement levels between two examiners.
Conclusion
ChatGPT and Gemini provided more accurate and informative responses on dental implants questions compared to MedGebra. According to the findings of the present study AI chatbots have potential for enhancing patient education on dental implants but professional oversight is crucial for ensuring accuracy and reliability. The study highlights the promising and potential benefits of large language models (LLMs) in dental patient education.

Introduction
Dental implants play a crucial role in aiding the rehabilitation of edentulism, enhancing smiles, and improving functionality. The global implant market is expanding worldwide, driven by advancements in technology, increased public awareness, and a growing demand for effective solutions. Recent reports from Sweden indicate a significant rise in implantusage from 2.1% to 4.7% over the last decade. As more individuals opt for dental implants, there is a growing need for comprehensive information to help them make informed decisions about their oral health. artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots have become important allies and emerged as valuable tools in providing personalised and easily accessible support for patient education.
AI has transformed healthcare by providing valuable insights to patients through large language models like ChatGPT about implant placement procedures, possible complications and post‐surgery care. AI chatbots have the potential to reduce patient anxiety, but concerns remain about how accurate, consistent and well‐perceived their responses are. Studies show that AI‐generated information can occasionally be incomplete, lack proper references, or too complex, affecting its reliability as a learning resource.
While AI chatbots are increasingly being used in dentistry, there is still limited data on their efficacy and effectiveness in conveying information about dental implants. Recent studies address general dental issues rather than delving into the details of implantology. Additionally, offering clear and consistent responses is crucial for helping patients comprehend their oral care. It's important to evaluate the accuracy and reliability of AI chatbots when it comes to information about dental implants. By examining the differences and similarities, we can gain insights into their strengths and weaknesses, which can influence their integration in clinical practice. This is the first study aims to evaluate AI tools in dental implantology focusing on accuracy, reliability and clarity of AI chatbot responses regarding dental implants. The results will inform enhancements in AI technology and determine the necessity of human involvement in delivering quality patient education.

Materials and Methods
This study employed a comparative evaluation design to assess the performance of three conversational AI models—ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT‐4), Gemini (Google), and MedGebra—in providing accurate, clear, and well‐supported responses to patient inquiries about dental implants. The assessment was based on 56 standardised questions derived from the European Association for Osseointegration (EAO) comprehensive patient guide on dental implants. To ensure objectivity, reliability, and reproducibility, the responses generated by each AI model were independently evaluated by two experienced dentists, specialised in oral surgery. Each response was scored on three key criteria: accuracy, precision and clarity, using a four‐point Likert scale. This methodological framework was chosen to provide a quantitative, structured and standardised comparative analysis of AI tools, ensuring statistical processing of results and facilitating the reproducibility of this study in future studies.

AI Models Evaluated
The study included three conversational AI models: ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT‐4), a widely used natural language processing model with an extensive knowledge base, including medical and dental topics; Gemini (Google, latest version), a conversational AI model developed by Google, capable of analysing both general and specialised medical inquiries; and MedGebra, a specialised AI tool designed exclusively for medical applications, with a specific focus on patient education in dentistry and implantology.

Standardised Question Set
The set of 56 questions used in this study was obtained from the EAO and encompassed a comprehensive array of topics related to dental implants. The questions addressed general information regarding dental implants, materials and biocompatibility, treatment protocols and prosthetic alternatives, long‐term efficacy and potential complications, the influence of systemic diseases such as diabetes and osteoporosis on implants, peri‐implant diseases, and maintenance strategies, as well as lifestyle factors such as smoking and oral hygiene that impact implant success. Adopting a standardised question set ensured uniformity in assessing various AI models, thereby mitigating potential biases that could arise from selecting disparate questions for each tool. This methodology preserved the objectivity of the comparisons and the validity of the study's findings. The questions are in the Appendix.

Evaluation Criteria
The quality of AI‐generated responses was systematically evaluated based on three predefined criteria: accuracy, precision and clarity, each scored on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = poor, 4 = excellent). Accuracy assessed the factual correctness of the information provided, with a score of 4 assigned to responses that were completely accurate and aligned with evidence‐based guidelines and current literature in dental implantology. A score of 3 reflected mostly correct responses but included minor inaccuracies; a score of 2 indicated partially correct answers with some incorrect or misleading content, while a score of 1 was assigned to responses containing major factual errors or irrelevant information.
Precision referred to the extent to which the response provided relevant, complete information with minimal ambiguity. A response was rated 4 if it directly addressed all aspects of the question without including extraneous details. Responses that were mostly relevant but included minor omissions or off‐topic content received a score of 3, while those with significant omissions or excessively irrelevant information were rated 2. Responses that failed to address the question or were entirely off‐topic received a score of 1.
Clarity measured the ease with which a layperson (i.e., a typical dental patient) could understand the response without requiring prior clinical knowledge or professional interpretation. A response was rated 4 if it was completely clear, well‐structured, and easy to comprehend. A score of 3 was given for mostly understandable responses but included some technical language or ambiguous phrasing. A rating of 2 reflected partially clear responses but contained confusing elements, while a score of 1 was assigned to responses that were largely incomprehensible or poorly articulated.

Data Collection Process
The data collection was meticulously designed to ensure consistency, objectivity, and minimal bias in evaluating responses. The 56 standardised questions were recorded in a structured database before being submitted to the AI models. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the following operational procedures were followed during the input of AI queries. Each of the 56 standardised questions from the EAO patient guide was entered into each AI model—ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT‐4, version as of March 2024), Gemini (Google, version as of April 2024), and MedGebra (web interface version as of May 2024)—under standardised conditions.
All models were accessed via their official web‐based user interfaces, using a desktop browser in private/incognito mode to minimise influence from past interactions or cached memory. For each model, a new and separate session was initiated for each question, meaning that no conversational memory influenced subsequent responses. This approach was chosen to avoid biases, particularly for models like ChatGPT that have memory or session‐continuity features.
The questions were submitted using the exact words provided in the EAO guide (e.g., “What is a dental implant?”). No supplementary instructions, context, or patient‐directed modifiers (such as “explain this to a patient” or “use lay language”) were added to preserve uniformity and eliminate bias introduced by prompt elaboration. The goal was to evaluate the default response quality of each AI model when presented with standardised, unmodified patient questions.
All outputs were generated in English. The responses were recorded without alteration or editorial correction and then randomised for blind scoring by the evaluators. The full prompt‐response data set is available on request for verification and replication purposes.
A blind evaluation process was implemented to enhance objectivity. The recorded responses were randomly mixed, and the two independent evaluators were unaware of which AI model had generated each response. This step was crucial in preventing bias from the evaluators and ensuring that responses were judged solely on content rather than preconceived notions about the AI system's performance.
The scoring process followed a structured three‐step assessment based on predefined criteria: accuracy, precision and clarity. Each evaluator independently assigned a score to every response using a four‐point Likert scale. The overall score for each response was calculated by summing the three individual scores, with a maximum possible score of 12 points per response. This quantitative approach facilitated a comprehensive comparison of AI model performance across multiple assessment dimensions. To ensure inter‐rater reliability and maintain consistency among different reviewers, each response was rated independently by two experienced evaluators. Furthermore, to assess intra‐rater reliability, the first examiner re‐evaluated 50% of the reactions in duplicate later, allowing for the measurement of scoring stability within the same rater. These procedures improved the objectivity and reproducibility of the evaluation process.

Statistical Analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the performance of each AI tool (ChatGPT, Gemini, and MedGebra) in terms of accuracy, precision, and clarity based on ratings provided by the two observers. Mean values and standard deviations were calculated for each parameter and model. The Cohen's Kappa test was used to assess inter‐rater and intra‐rater reliability. The Shapiro‐Wilk test was applied to assess whether the data met parametric assumptions. Since the data were not normally distributed, non‐parametric tests were selected for group comparisons. The Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to determine whether there were statistically significant differences among the three AI models across the evaluated parameters. This test is a non‐parametric alternative to one‐way ANOVA and is appropriate for comparing more than two independent groups on ordinal or non‐normally distributed data. When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated significant differences, pairwise post hoc comparisons were conducted to identify specific differences between the models. All statistical tests had a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

Results

Reliability Analysis of Examiner Ratings
The inter‐rater reliability analysis (Table 1) demonstrated variable levels of agreement among evaluators when assessing the performance of different AI tools. Specifically, Gemini achieved the highest inter‐rater agreement for accuracy (κ = 0.568), falling within the “moderate agreement” range. ChatGPT showed fair agreement in accuracy (κ = 0.254) but performed better in clarity (κ = 0.426) and precision (κ = 0.419), both reaching the threshold for moderate agreement. MedGebra consistently presented lower kappa values across all domains, with the lowest observed in clarity (κ = 0.257), indicating fair to minimal agreement between raters for this model. Overall, the average kappa value across all tools and parameters was κ = 0.457, indicating moderate inter‐rater agreement.

Discussion
This study examined how three conversational AI models—ChatGPT (GPT‐4), Gemini (Google), and MedGebra—performed in providing information about dental implants. The evaluation was based on a consistent set of 56 questions taken from the EAO patient guide. The results indicated that ChatGPT consistently outperformed the other models in all areas we assessed, such as accuracy, precision and clarity. Gemini demonstrated strong performance, particularly in clarity, though it was somewhat less consistent regarding accuracy and precision. MedGebra, although marketed as a specialised medical tool, fell short compared to the other models in every category we evaluated.
The findings showcase how general‐purpose large language models, such as ChatGPT and Gemini, can play a valuable role in educating patients about dental implantology. ChatGPT's impressive clarity demonstrates its ability to convey complex clinical information in a manner that patients easily understand. The results align with earlier studies indicating that ChatGPT can produce health information that is coherent and easy to read. However, the differences in the accuracy and precision of responses underscore the importance of involving professionals when integrating AI tools into patient education processes. Although MedGebra is specifically designed for medical contexts, its lower scores in various areas may suggest challenges in its language training, infrequent updates to the model, or a need for better communication strategies with patients. This resonates with the concerns expressed by Jacobs et al. and Dursun and Bilici Geçer, who emphasise that specialised AI tools may struggle to meet the communication standards required in clinical environments unless they are regularly evaluated or fine‐tuned with actual patient feedback data.
This study reveals a moderate level of agreement among raters (average κ = 0.457), highlighting the need to develop clearer response structures and ensure consistency in AI‐generated content. While the consistency of evaluations from the same rater was relatively high (κ = 0.561), the variations among different raters indicate that AI‐generated outputs can still be interpreted in various ways. This is especially crucial in medical communication, as any ambiguity could lead to misunderstandings for patients. The moderate level of inter‐rater agreement observed (κ = 0.457) likely reflects inherent subjectivity in interpreting language‐based responses, particularly when applying Likert‐type scales to constructs such as clarity and precision. Even with an objective scoring rubric, evaluators may differ in how they weigh the completeness or interpretability of a given response. Additionally, dental implant terminology can vary in perceived technicality; a phrase that one evaluator deems patient‐friendly may be seen as ambiguous or overly clinical by another. These findings highlight the need for more clearly defined scoring anchors and possibly evaluator training when conducting similar future studies.
The study is the first to evaluate the accuracy and consistency of AI chatbots in addressing inquiries related to implantology. It presents a novel approach by emphasising patient education, distinguishing it from the existing literature on AI in dentistry, which primarily concentrated on diagnostic functions. The use of a careful designed set of questions by professionals, along with a blinded assessment procedure, significantly enhances the credibility of the results. The findings presented here could have a significant impact how we approach clinical practice. As AI becomes increasingly integrated into patient interactions, including online consultations, triage systems, and preoperative counselling, selecting the appropriate model is essential. ChatGPT, known for its impressive clarity and accuracy, could serve as a valuable tool for dental professionals. It is crucial to establish validation protocols when utilising AI tools, and clinicians should always verify the recommendations to avoid any misinformation.
A few limitations should be reported. The cross‐sectional analysis evaluated AI models simultaneously; however, it's essential to note that these systems are frequently updated, which may impact their performance in future versions. Additionally, the study also only considered responses in English, so future research should explore the multilingual capabilities of AI models and their effectiveness with diverse patient populations. Furthermore, although efforts were made to keep evaluators unbiased, the subjective nature of scoring could have introduced some subtle bias. The utilisation of a completely structured questionnaire from the EAO patient guide is an additional significant limitation of this investigation. Although this standardised approach guarantees methodological reliability and comparability, it fails to accurately represent the informal or fragmentary nature of real‐world patient interactions. In clinical practice, patients frequently pose questions in their unique manner or pose compound questions that lack a distinct structure. These characteristics may present a challenge to the language models' capacity to interpret their objectives and respond appropriately. A further limitation of this study is the absence of a pre‐study calibration session for the two evaluators. Although both were experienced clinicians with expertise in oral surgery and familiar with dental education, they scored the AI responses independently without prior consensus alignment. This may have contributed to the moderate inter‐rater agreement (κ = 0.457) observed across models and scoring domains. Given the subjective nature of evaluating language‐based outputs—particularly for constructs like clarity—future studies should consider incorporating structured training or calibration procedures to enhance scoring reliability.
Future research should expand to include a broader range of AI platforms, investigate how performance changes over time, and assess the quality of citations along with the transparency of sources. Research involving actual patients could illuminate how easy it is to use, comprehend and trust AI‐generated responses, enhancing our understanding of AI's role in contemporary dental care.

Conclusions
This study demonstrated that ChatGPT and Gemini provide more accurate, precise, and transparent educational content regarding dental implants compared to MedGebra, with ChatGPT emerging as the most reliable tool overall. The findings highlight the promising role of conversational AI models in enhancing patient education in implant dentistry. However, professional oversight remains essential to ensure clinical accuracy and reliability of AI‐generated information. As AI technologies continue to evolve, ongoing evaluation and validation are critical for their safe integration into dental practice. Future research should focus on expanding comparative analyses across multiple languages and patient populations and assessing AI‐generated content in real‐world clinical settings.
