Research advancements and evaluation of multifactor‐induced murine models for gastric cancer
Abstract
As one of the most prevalent gastrointestinal malignancies in humans, gastric cancer (GC) is often detected at an advanced stage, resulting in a poor prognosis and ranking it the fifth leading cause of cancer‐related deaths. Due to their high genomic correlation with humans, mice are ideal in vivo models for investigating GC‐related pathogenesis and therapeutic interventions. This review provides an overview of different GC models, including genetically engineered, transplantation‐based models, and chemically or biologically induced models, and discusses the recent advancements for each type, highlighting their unique contributions to the field. In addition, it summarizes the strengths, limitations, and typical applications of these models and offers a critical assessment of their applicability in research while acknowledging their current limitations in fully mirroring human GC progression. Furthermore, we analyze how each model accurately recapitulates the complexities of human GC and evaluate their potential for clinical translation. This review provides a reference for model selection in future GC research.

INTRODUCTION
Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common cancer and leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide, with an estimated 1 million new cases annually. Although the incidence and lethality of GC have decreased over the past two decades, clinicians anticipate that its incidence will continue to increase due to an aging population. Risk factors for GC include Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori)infection, cigarette smoking, and dietary habits, such as a high salt diet, intake of processed meats, and low intake of fresh fruits and vegetables. Among them, H. pylori infection is considered the most common and predominant incidence factor.
GC typically results from precancerous lesions in the gastric mucosa, marked by abnormalities such as inflammation, ulceration, or intestinal metaplasia. Cancer cells gradually infiltrate the muscular and plasma membrane layers of the stomach, spreading through the bloodstream or lymphatic system to neighboring tissues. This progression can result in gastrointestinal bleeding, anemia, emaciation, and other serious and malignant conditions in patients. Most patients are diagnosed at later stages, and despite surgical intervention and chemotherapy, the prognosis remains poor, rendering early detection and timely treatment extremely important. However, current research efforts on GC are limited, thus underscoring the need for a more profound understanding of its pathological mechanisms and molecular changes and discovering effective therapeutic approaches to advance the field.
Mice exhibit similarity to humans in terms of protein‐coding genes, rendering them a widely favored choice for in vivo experimentation across various diseases due to their ease of breeding and high reproductive rates. The commonly employed GC mouse models can be broadly classified into genetically engineered mouse models (GEMM), transplantation models, and chemically and biologically induced models, each distinguished by unique inducing factors (Figure 1). Their distinct construction methodologies vary in their ability to simulate different facets of the GC process. However, because each model typically mimics only a subset of the multifaceted factors involved in gastric carcinogenesis, they inherently possess limitations. Thus, selecting an appropriate animal model for GC research is important.
Introduction of animal models of GC.
In this review, we critically evaluate the advantages and limitations, particularly in simulating the occurrence and progression of human GC, of commonly used mouse models in GC research and assess their applicability in various studies. This review aims to guide researchers in selecting appropriate models to enhance the effectiveness of GC‐related fundamental research and drug development.

GENETICALLY ENGINEERED MOUSE MODELS
When genes in mice are artificially modified, they are referred to as GEMMs. Among them, transgenic mice are those in which exogenous genomic sequences have been introduced, whereas those with lost or altered endogenous genes are referred to as knockout (knockdown) or knock‐in mice. The criteria for the successful development of GEMMs primarily include successful transformation and expression of the target gene, similarity of tumor incidence and histological characteristics to human diseases, and genetic stability. Traditional GEMMs have altered expressions of target genes in all cells of their body, potentially resulting in survival difficulties of the initial embryo. Researchers have recently utilized technologies such as the Cre‐lox recombination system and electroporation to control the expression of target genes at specific times or in organs and cells. These mice are referred to as conditional transgenic or knockout/knock‐in mice. However, after genetic modification, the development of precancerous lesions is prolonged in these mice. Therefore, GEMMs are often combined with chemical or biological factors to induce and accelerate precancerous lesions and GC progression. Moreover, GEMMs encompass a wide array of genes implicated in gastric carcinogenesis and progression. Based on the edited genes, GC GEMMs can be categorized into those targeting GC‐related hormones and those targeting proto‐oncogenes or oncogenes.

GC‐related hormone GEMMs
The most commonly used GEMMs for GC‐related hormones are insulin–gastrin (INS–GAS) transgenic mice on the FVB/N (Friend Virus B NIH) genetic background. Gastrin is produced by G cells in the gastric pyloric and duodenal mucosa and promotes the secretion of gastric acid, gastric juice, and pepsin, which are elevated during gastric carcinogenesis. Serum amidated gastrin levels in INS–GAS mice can be increased twice that of normal levels by expressing a human gastrin mini‐gene spliced into the insulin promoter. Researchers have observed an increase in apical cell content and enhanced gastric acid secretion in the early stages of INS–GAS mice, resulting in the development of gastric metaplasia, dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, and GC with vascular invasion at ~20 months, with H. pylori infection accelerating this process. INS–GAS mice are a widely accepted model for constructing GC in vivo platforms to investigate the relationship between microbiota and GC. Therefore, these models are often used in combination with H. pylori infection and can be used in multiple GC research fields, such as pathomechanisms and preventive and curative measures.
Advantages: INS–GAS mice are easily obtainable and have been extensively used for GC modeling over an extended period, fostering a comprehensive understanding of their unique traits and optimal modeling environments. Moreover, GEMMs often preserve the immune system of mice, facilitating the exploration of the intricate relationships between tumors and their microenvironment.
Limitations: Cancer progression is slow in these models, particularly in the absence of cofactor infection. Moreover, alterations in hormone levels after gene editing may introduce bias, thereby influencing study outcomes.

Proto‐oncogenic or oncogenic GEMMs
Proto‐oncogenic or oncogenic GEMMs are valuable models for investigating the effects of target genes on GC development. For example, K‐Ras transgenic, Tff1−/−, and p27‐deficient mice are precise and controllable models for investigating the detailed mechanisms of GC development. A model using the K19 promoter to overexpress K‐Ras (V12D) was first used to demonstrate that as a proto‐oncogene, K‐Ras could induce a pro‐inflammatory response and cause the early onset of gastric dysplasia. In addition to performing systemic gene editing during mouse embryonic development, researchers have developed new technologies to advance the development of GEMMs. For instance, researchers recently introduced the Claudin18‐IRES‐CreERT2 (Cldn18‐IRES‐CreERT2) driver, which selectively induces conditional mutations in the gastric epithelium of the pyloric and gastric body regions. Additionally, a GEMM model utilizing tissue electroporation was established to directly deliver genetic elements to the stomach, enabling invasive procedures and facilitating metastasis to other organs. Using proto‐oncogene or oncogene GEMMs, researchers have gained a better understanding of the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying GC. Simultaneously, cutting‐edge research, such as immunotherapy, selects this type of model due to its ability to spontaneously develop GC and its intact immune system.
Advantages: GEMMs enable precise genetic modifications to elucidate gene roles in GC, facilitating mechanism research and targeted drug development. New GEMM technologies can be used to precisely modify genes at specific times and in certain tissues, thus avoiding lethality during embryonic stages.
Limitations: The occurrence and development of GC result from a combination of genetic and external factors. The modification of individual genes limits the in‐depth study of the complex processes involved in GC. In contrast to commercially available INS–GAS mice, the process of developing GEMMs is cumbersome and expensive.

TRANSPLANTATION MODELS
Naturally occurring tumors in animals are inherently different from those found in humans. By implanting human‐derived cell lines or tissue fragments into animal models, researchers have discovered that diverse GC metastasis models can be developed using both orthotopic and ectopic transplantation approaches. These transplantation models are fundamentally categorized into two groups: cell line–derived xenograft (CDX) and patient‐derived xenograft (PDX) models. Of these, the CDX model is more prevalent and frequently utilizes human‐derived cell lines such as MGC803, BGC823, and SGC7901. Because other models rarely exhibit spontaneous tumor metastasis, transplantation models are commonly used for examining peritoneal metastasis.

CDX models
CDX models are typically developed using cell lines that have undergone rigorous artificial purification and culture over multiple generations. The common transplantation methods include subcutaneous and orthotopic transplantation and intraperitoneal injection. In ectopic transplantation models, subcutaneous tissue is the most commonly used site for transplantation. The standard approach involves selecting nude mice aged 4–6 weeks and administering a suspension containing a certain quantity of cells, with tumor formation typically manifesting within 1–2 weeks. As another method of ectopic transplantation, intraperitoneal injection is commonly used to establish models of peritoneal metastasis in GC. In one study, the cell line was first injected into immunodeficient mice via the abdominal cavity. The researchers reported a decrease in body weight (>1.5 g) between days 0 and 7 post‐tumor implantation, confirming the establishment of the peritoneally disseminated mouse model. In contrast, orthotopic transplantation requires the direct implantation of GC cell lines into the gastric wall of mice via incision and injection.
Proficiency in transplantation techniques significantly affects the success rate of CDX models. In orthotopic transplantation, the fragile nature of the gastric wall of mouse poses several challenges. For example, a very deep implantation risks tumor cells from infiltrating the gastric lumen, whereas an insufficient depth may result in their entry into the abdominal cavity, inadvertently inducing metastasis. The orthotopic transplantation model can also serve as a model for the peritoneal metastasis of GC because it more effectively simulates the natural process of peritoneal metastasis compared to intraperitoneal injection. The development of CDX models has revolutionized how researchers observe and evaluate tumor progression, which encompasses certain metrics such as tumor volume, growth rate, metastasis, and invasiveness.
Advantages: Conventional CDX models have a streamlined construction process marked by swift cycle times, a high success rate in modeling, and cost effectiveness. Its primary advantage is its capability to monitor tumors in vivo in real time, rendering it an invaluable tool for predicting drug efficacy. Furthermore, the plethora of transplantation techniques and the establishment of diverse GC cell lines empower researchers to precisely tailor models to their specific research needs.
Limitations: Because transplanted cell lines are derived from human sources, using immunodeficient mice is imperative to prevent rejection, thereby hindering the exploration of the intricate relationship between the immune system and tumors. Additionally, CDX models are adversely affected by a lack of tumor heterogeneity, and tumors originating from external sources significantly differ from naturally occurring spontaneous tumors, rendering them suboptimal for accurately simulating human tumor conditions.

PDX models
Developing a PDX model is significantly more intricate than that for the CDX model. In this process, tissue samples obtained from patients are meticulously divided into pieces (~2 mm3) and then implanted beneath the skin of immunodeficient mice. Once the tumor reaches 1–2 cm3, the tumor‐bearing mice are killed, and the tumor tissue is transplanted into a new generation of mice (F1). The model is deemed successful if the tumor tissue can be successfully passed on for three or more consecutive generations in mice, rendering it suitable for further research. The PDX model is one of the most suitable preclinical models for GC research because it retains a significant level of similarity to primary tumors in terms of histopathological characteristics, gene expression patterns, and drug responsiveness. Numerous studies have highlighted the profound connection between PDX models and parental tumors. Using advanced techniques, such as targeted next‐generation sequencing, in situ hybridization, and immunohistochemical analyses, researchers have conducted a comprehensive genomic and molecular profiling of PDX models derived from 50 patients. These investigations revealed that most PDX models mirror the histopathological and molecular traits of their respective primary tumors with fidelity. Consequently, PDX models significantly enhance individualized treatment strategies and facilitate the validation of key GC drug targets, including EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor), Met (mesenchymal‐epithelial transition factor), and GRK3 (G protein‐coupled receptor kinase 3), through targeted drug testing. The PDX model can also serve as an effective platform for exploring the peritoneal metastasis of GC using tumor samples obtained from peritoneal metastases or ascites. The PDX model shows great promise for future GC research, and researchers are actively developing new technologies to advance its application. However, traditional PDX models using immunodeficient mice lack an immune system, thus limiting their ability to mimic tumor–immune interactions, tumor progression, and patient survival outcomes.
To address these challenges, humanized mouse models have been developed by implanting human cells from various sources, such as peripheral blood mononuclear cells, human hematopoietic stem cells, or human embryonic stem cells, into immunocompromised mice. Furthermore, using gene knock‐in techniques, researchers have directly integrated genes for human cytokines or growth factors into the mouse genome, effectively overcoming the limitations of mouse‐derived cytokines and chemokines that fail to maintain immune cell function in humanized PDX models. These approaches enable researchers who use humanized PDX models to simulate human immune responses in evaluating new immunotherapies. These models hold significant promise for advancing GC research by accurately simulating tumor–immune interactions and providing insights into how tumors evade immune surveillance and respond to different immunotherapies. Currently, efforts to optimize the production of humanized mice are ongoing, aiming to improve the success rate and reliability of PDX models. Key methods include enhancing the sources of immune cells, increasing the diversity of the human immune repertoire, and applying high‐throughput technologies. In addition to humanized mice, other innovative technologies are advancing the development of PDX models. PDX biobanks will help identify and validate novel “druggable” targets and optimize therapeutic strategies. Moreover, techniques using CRISPR‐Cas9 for editing PDX models have been developed, enhancing their utility as genetically programmable models of human cancer and broadening their application to other explant models.
Advantages: Compared to CDX models, PDX models more accurately preserve the histopathological and molecular features of original tumors, rendering them superior for investigating targeted therapies and chemotherapeutics. In addition, they maintain genetic diversity and intratumoral heterogeneity, enabling the comprehensive analysis of variations within primary tumors and metastases. Humanized PDX models incorporate human immune components, enabling the evaluation of tumor–immune interactions and effectiveness of immunotherapies.
Limitations: Establishing and maintaining PDX models can be time consuming and expensive, and using patient‐derived tumor tissue often lacks reproducibility due to ethical and legal challenges. Additionally, as the number of passages increases, the original tumor microenvironment is gradually replaced by mouse stroma, thereby affecting the stability and authenticity of the model over time. The inherent heterogeneity of tumors may also result in PDX models that do not adequately represent the range of characteristics found in different patients. Furthermore, humanized PDX models fail to replicate the human immune system and may exhibit tissue incompatibility and deficiencies in gut microbiota, severely limiting their ability to accurately simulate tumor progression and respond to treatments.

CHEMICALLY INDUCED GC MODELS
In 1966, methylnitrosourea (MNU; N‐methyl‐N‐nitroso‐N‐nitroguanidine) was recognized as a potent chemical inducer of GC in rats, and the carcinogenic activity of this class of compounds stems from their nitroso group. Recent developments in GC mouse models have resulted in the development of various chemical inducers. Among them, N‐methyl‐N‐nitro‐N‐nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) and MNU are two commonly employed agents. MNNG is more prevalent in GC induction studies in rats, whereas MNU is more frequently used in mouse models. These compounds exert their carcinogenic effects primarily through damaging DNA, inhibiting DNA repair mechanisms, and suppressing apoptosis.
MNU is typically administered via drinking water, and its carcinogenic potency is determined by its concentration (most commonly 120 ppm) rather than the total dosage. Research has demonstrated that exposing mice to 120 ppm MNU for 5 weeks can enhance GC development across diverse genetic backgrounds. By comparing five mouse strains under identical MNU‐inducing conditions, researchers have determined that BALB mice, characterized by rapid tumor progression, are the optimal strain for MNU‐induced GC models. These mice commonly exhibit adenocarcinomas originating from gastric epithelial cells. Compared with MNNG‐induced GC rat models, MNU‐induced GC mouse models offer a closer approximation of human GC pathology. Consequently, these models have served as invaluable in vivo research platforms for uncovering numerous signaling molecules and pathways associated with GC development, encompassing Wnt/β‐catenin, p53, and NF‐κB. Similar to numerous chemically induced models, the MNU model requires a prolonged induction period and is frequently employed in precancerous GC lesion studies.
The role of MNU extends beyond its ability to induce spontaneous GC models. It also serves as an accelerator of the carcinogenic effects of H. pylori (as discussed in Section 5.3) and a catalyst for tumor occurrence in GC GEMMs. For example, Apc (Min/+) mice can spontaneously develop GC by 20 weeks of age; however, when combined with MNU induction, the onset of tumors can be advanced to 15 weeks of age, achieving a 100% incidence rate by 25 weeks of age. Furthermore, the combination of MNU with the two different carcinogenic pathways in Apc (Min/+) mice may result in more aggressive tumor development. Other models combining chemical factors with GEMMs have also confirmed the catalytic carcinogenic effect of MNU. Thus, MNU‐treated GEMMs are important models for analyzing the multistage carcinogenic mechanisms of GC.
Advantages: Chemical inducers offer versatility and compatibility with both wild type and genetically modified mice, effectively mimicking various stages of GC development and pathology within controlled experimental settings. Their simplicity and cost effectiveness have facilitated their widespread adoption in large‐scale disease research.
Limitations: Chemical inducers inherently possess carcinogenic and toxic characteristics, potentially attributing to unintended tumor types or damaging nontarget tissues, thereby diminishing experimental specificity and complicating result interpretations. Furthermore, the GC induction rate in these models is susceptible to multiple factors, including reagent dosage, environmental conditions, and model‐specific variables, which add layers of complexity. This lengthy induction cycle poses challenges in monitoring the progression of precancerous changes, and once initiated, tracking their evolution in real time becomes difficult.

BIOLOGICALLY INDUCED GC MODELS
Biological factor‐induced GC models predominantly consist of animal models in which H. pylori serves as a pivotal inducing agent. Since its initial isolation from inflamed human gastric tissue in 1982, H. pylori has been acknowledged as a potent carcinogen involved in GC development. H. pylori is a gram‐negative, spiral bacterium that extensively colonizes the surface of the human gastric mucosa. Due to the inherent resistance of mice to H. pylori, two surrogate strains are commonly employed: Helicobacter felis (H. felis), derived from the gastric mucosa of cats, and H. pylori Sydney Strain 1 (H. pylori SS1), which was isolated from an Australian patient with gastric ulcers and subsequently propagated in vitro. Both strains can successfully colonize mice for extended periods, eliciting varying degrees of inflammatory responses and pathological alterations. In addition to observing visible tumors, the success of H. pylori–induced GC or precancerous lesion models can be evaluated using histological assessments via hematoxylin and eosin and immunohistochemical staining and polymerase chain reaction amplification to detect the colonization of H. pylori. The H. pylori–induced GC model is an essential tool for evaluating drugs and investigating mechanisms related to H. pylori–associated GC and provides valuable insights for investigating precancerous lesions of GC due to its ability to simulate the complete process of spontaneous GC development.

Advantages: These models can simulate the GC process caused by H. pylori infection in humans, thus demonstrating high biological similarity. Moreover, the controllability of the model enables researchers to more accurately assess the development of GC and effectiveness of preventive strategies under varying environmental conditions by adjusting the dietary structure, antibiotic exposure, and microbial communities in mice, providing feasible guidance and recommendations for clinical practice.
Limitations: Mice exhibit resistance to the types of H. pylori that infect humans, rendering clinical infection simulations incomplete. The most commonly used H. pylori strains lack important virulence factors present during human infections. Typically, H. pylori infections require a prolonged duration, and mice with different genetic backgrounds exhibit varying sensitivity to H. pylori, with only a few strains able to progress to the metaplastic stage and result in cancer.

Mouse models of H. felis infection
H. felis, a spiral bacterium originating from feline stomachs, exhibits a strong genomic connection to human infections caused by H. pylori, sharing over 60% homology in their genetic makeup. This bacterium harbors numerous orthologous genes essential for gastric colonization, similar to those found in H. pylori. Particularly, H. felis has a wider host range than its human‐infecting counterpart. Germ‐free mice infected with H. felis serve as an inaugural murine model of bacterial gastritis. Researchers reported that within 2 weeks of infection, mice manifested mild inflammatory alterations restricted to the basal mucosa. Over the course of 4 weeks, changes were observed, including glandular hyperplasia, epithelial cell immaturity, and dedifferentiation. By the eighth week, the mice exhibited markers of chronic gastritis, thus mirroring the transition from acute to persistent chronic inflammation observed in human H. pylori infections.
Models involving H. felis infection often elicit more severe inflammatory responses than those employing other prevalent strains such as H. pylori SS1. Thus, they are frequently used to explore the interplay between inflammatory factors and the progression of gastric lesions. Upon administering five gavages to C57BL/6 mice at daily intervals, researchers observed inflammation, glandular atrophy, metaplasia, dysplasia, and adenocarcinoma in the stomachs of H. felis–infected mice. A recent study in BALB/c mice suggested that the colonization of H. felis may be influenced by the mural cells of the host. In particular, the local acidity of the stomach can modulate colonization success. Therefore, researchers often pretreat mice with acid‐suppressing medications to diminish stomach function prior to infection with H. pylori or H. felis, thereby enhancing the colonization efficiency of H. pylori.

Mouse models of H. pylori SS1 infection
In 1997, researchers isolated H. pylori strains that could adapt to and colonize mice by passing H. pylori through successive generations, naming them “H. pylori SS1”. These strains were referred to as “pre‐mouse” SS1 (PMSS1) until they were fully adapted to colonize mice. Compared to the better‐colonizing H. pylori SS1, H. pylori PMSS1 carries and expresses the cytotoxin‐associated genes A (CagA) and type IV secretion system (T4SS), which play key roles in human infections. However, the ability of H. pylori PMSS1 to inject CagA proteins into host cells decreases 1 month after colonization. This might be related to the genetic instability of Cag pathogenicity island (PAI) in this strain.
H. pylori SS1 colonizes the upper foveolae of the stomach, similar to that in humans infected with H. pylori. Moreover, H. pylori SS1 can colonize the mouse stomach long term, resulting in gastric lesions in several strains of mice. For example, in C57BL/6 mice, chronic gastritis develops by the sixth month of infection, with more severe inflammatory infiltration and atrophic gastritis by the eighth month. Through this type of modeling, researchers have gained insights into H. pylori–mediated gastric lesions. Such models have been used to investigate the role of various cytokines in GC and provide new research directions for future GC treatment.

Multifactor‐induced GC model
Gastric carcinogenesis induced only by biological factors in mice is relatively rare. Researchers more commonly use biological factors in combination with MNU or GEMMs, such as INS‐GAS mice, to accelerate the progression of GC.
The model combining MNU with H. pylori infection has significant advantages, particularly in terms of accelerating the time to GC formation and increasing the incidence rate. This model is particularly significant in understanding the impact of H. pylori infection on the gastric mucosa because it can significantly enhance the action of chemical carcinogens by increasing the levels of oxidative stress and pro‐inflammatory factors. A study demonstrated that the incidence rate of gastric adenocarcinoma in the MNU group was only 27%, whereas that in the MNU combined with the H. pylori infection group reached as high as 80% by the 50th week. Furthermore, isolated H. pylori infection results in chronic atrophic gastritis without triggering GC. Therefore, the GC induction model that combines H. pylori with MNU resembles the pathogenesis of human GC and holds significant application value in drug development and mechanistic investigations.
GEMMs are a rapidly developing research tool, and by combining them with H. pylori, researchers can develop various genetically modified mouse models. This combination offers a rich array of options for diverse research objectives. Among these models, the INS‐GAS mouse model provides important insights into the relationship between H. pylori and GC. The development of GC in uninfected INS‐GAS mice is a slow process, requiring ~18–20 months; however, the combination of hypergastrinemia and H. pylori infection leads to rapid atrophy and the onset of GC. More than half of the INS‐GAS mice infected with H. felis showed evidence of intramucosal and submucosal and intravascular (invasive) carcinomas by 6 months of age, rendering it widely utilized in various research contexts. Furthermore, H. pylori can be used in conjunction with other GEMMs. By combining it with mice that have different genetic modifications, researchers can conduct in‐depth studies on host genetic susceptibility, tumor–immune mechanisms, and the pathways involved in GC development.

DISCUSSION
Mouse models are important tools for investigating the development of GC. They provide observational and experimental conditions that are not available in the human environment and assist in the investigation of GC pathogenesis, exploration of new therapeutic strategies, and evaluation of therapeutic efficacy. In this review, we discussed GC mouse models, including GEMMs, transplantation models, and chemical and biological factor–induced models. Each model has distinct advantages and limitations, rendering it essential to select an appropriate model based on the specific research objectives and requirements.
GEMMs are widely used to investigate the intricate relationships between individual genes and GC. Advances in gene editing technology have enabled researchers to precisely manipulate specific genes within designated tissues at specific times. However, other models are imperative to emulate the multigenic basis of GC. Transplantation models, which are widely used in GC drug discovery and development, expedite the understanding of chemical or drug treatment effects through in vivo tumor detection. The CDX model is gradually evolving toward the PDX model, promoting advancements in personalized therapies. However, both GEMMs and transplantation models initiate tumors through intrinsic or artificial alterations, potentially overlooking the influence of extrinsic factors, such as diet and environment, on GC. To address this gap, models induced by chemical and biological factors have attracted widespread attention in precancerous lesion and GC research. As a biological factor commonly used in GC modeling, H. pylori demonstrates varying carcinogenic effects among different strains, with H. felis inducing a higher level of inflammation in mice. These models offer valuable insights into the role of external stimuli in GC development.
In current GC research, researchers frequently opt for a multifactorial combined induction model to expedite tumorigenesis, thus aligning more closely with the intricate processes observed during cancer development. Although laboratory accessibility and cost effectiveness are considered when selecting a GC model, three crucial aspects should be considered: the model‐inducing factors, associated gastric pathological alterations, and specific experimental objectives. The choice of model can significantly affect the ultimate experimental outcomes.
Future animal models of GC will advance toward a heightened resemblance to the progression of human GC. The rapid development of GEMMs has progressively enabled the precise control of gene mutations at well‐defined tissues at well‐defined times; however, they summarize the sporadic and random acquisition of multiple mutations in human cancers. Thus, viral gene delivery strategies and other techniques require further improvement to introduce multiple mutations at different times in a small number of cells. PDX models are currently the best models for retaining the original features of the tumor and developing humanized mice, and other supporting technologies will promote GC models to better mimic clinical patients. Furthermore, in tandem with advancements in cutting‐edge technologies, such as in vivo imaging, gene editing, and immunotherapy, GC animal models are evolving in novel directions, fostering an in‐depth understanding of the developmental mechanisms of the disease, exploring new therapeutic avenues, and offering patients more effective treatment options.
