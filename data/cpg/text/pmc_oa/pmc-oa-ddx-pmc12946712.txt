A Survey on Medical Competence Evaluation Benchmarks for Large Language Models
ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) show considerable potential to revolutionize healthcare through their performance across diverse clinical applications. Given the inherent constraints of LLMs and the critical nature of medical practice, a rigorous and systematic evaluation of their medical competence is imperative. This study presents a comprehensive review of the established methodologies and benchmarks for evaluating the medical competence of LLMs, encompassing a thorough analysis of current assessment practices across medical knowledge, clinical practice competence, and ethical–safety considerations. By integrating clinician competency assessment frameworks into LLMs evaluation, we propose a structured tri‐dimensional framework that systematically organizes existing evaluation approaches according to medical theoretical knowledge, clinical practice ability, and ethical–safety considerations. Furthermore, this research provides critical insights into future developmental trajectories while establishing foundational frameworks and standardization protocols for the integration of LLMs into medical practice.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have gained substantial prominence in the medical domain. These models have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in language generation and comprehension within natural language processing (NLP), exhibiting exceptional capability in handling diverse human instructions. Within healthcare contexts, LLMs have demonstrated substantial potential across numerous applications, including biomedical named entity recognition, clinical prediction, medical document generation, medical question and answer (Q&A), patient education, medical research, and clinical decision support.
Nevertheless, despite these promising applications, there remain critical limitations that hinder the effective and safe integration of LLMs into real‐world clinical scenarios. These limitations manifest as medical knowledge coverage bias, hallucination (i.e., generating responses with factual errors), bias, poor interpretability, and limited robustness, potentially leading to misinformation for patients and, consequently, adverse medical events. Additionally, LLMs are vulnerable to privacy breaches when processing patient data and generating outputs, as well as a range of moral and ethical challenges. Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate models before their real‐world clinical application, as such assessment determines whether the models meet deployment standards and guides their appropriate use in clinical decision‐making and patient communication. Moreover, medical competence evaluation reveals how models encode, generalize, and apply medical knowledge, thereby helping us understand the degree to which their reasoning aligns with that of human clinicians.
Prior research has revealed significant inconsistencies and fragmentation in the frameworks currently used to evaluate LLMs in clinical settings. Existing benchmarks lack unified standards and fail to provide comprehensive assessments of medical competence, ethical considerations, and task‐specific accuracy, thereby falling short of truly reflecting the medical competence of LLMs. Moreover, the evaluation landscape remains largely unsystematic, resulting in diverse and sometimes incompatible evaluation methodologies.
Given the complexity, sensitivity, and high stakes associated with medical applications, there is an urgent need for standardized and comprehensive benchmarks to accelerate the development and deployment of LLMs in the medical domain. Therefore, this paper summarizes existing evaluation methods; identifies critical limitations and gaps in prior work; introduces an analytical framework related to medical safety, competence, and ethics; and proposes a synthesis assessment benchmark aimed at fostering the application of LLMs in clinical environments.

Methods

Search Strategy and Inclusion Criteria
To clarify the current landscape of benchmarks evaluating the medical capabilities of LLMs, we conducted a comprehensive literature search in PubMed and Web of Science on April 28, 2025, covering studies published from 2018 through 2025. We selected 2018 as the starting point for our literature search, as it marks the introduction of Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT), which brought transformer‐based large‐scale pre‐trained language models into mainstream NLP research. BERT laid the foundation for later medical models such as BioBERT and ClinicalBERT, as well as autoregressive LLMs like GPT‐3 and GPT‐4. Since this review focuses on evaluating transformer‐based LLMs in medical contexts, earlier studies are not directly relevant in terms of model capabilities or evaluation paradigms. Specifically, we used a query string: (“Large Language Model” OR “LLM” OR “GPT” OR “Generative AI”) AND (“Health” OR “Medical” OR “Clinical” OR “Medicine”) AND (“Evaluate” OR “Assess” OR “Test”) OR “benchmark”. This query was applied to the Title/Abstract fields in PubMed and the Topic field (i.e., Topic Search) in Web of Science, ensuring the search targeted relevant titles and abstracts.
Studies were included if they evaluated LLMs in medical or clinical tasks (e.g., diagnosis, decision support, and clinical text processing). Excluded studies were those not related to clinical or medical benchmark evaluation of LLMs (e.g., studies solely about medical education uses or NLP were excluded) (Figure 1).

In addition, starting from the 26 benchmark‐evaluation papers we initially identified, we traced their reference lists and incorporated additional studies relevant to our topic. These sources are analyzed in subsequent sections that focus on medical theoretical knowledge, clinical practice competence, and medical safety and ethics.

Results
All titles and abstracts returned by the search were screened for relevance against the inclusion criteria. We identified a total of 131 records through the database searches (53 from PubMed and 78 from Web of Science). Ultimately, 26 benchmarks met all criteria and were included in this review (Figure 1).

Drawing from the medical licensing examination frameworks of both China and the United States, we propose a three‐dimensional assessment model encompassing medical theoretical knowledge, clinical practice competency, and medical safety and ethics considerations. This tripartite framework aligns with contemporary medical education standards and professional certification requirements across major healthcare systems. This comprehensive approach ensures a holistic evaluation of LLMs' capabilities while maintaining alignment with established professional standards in healthcare delivery. The medical theoretical knowledge dimension evaluates a comprehensive understanding of fundamental medical sciences and clinical medicine, with benchmarks like MultiMedQA and the Japanese National Dentist Examination (JNDE) assessing alignment with established medical exams. Clinical practice competency focuses on diagnostic reasoning, treatment planning, and patient management capabilities in clinical scenarios, where benchmarks such as Mayo Clinic Symptom Checker and AMEGA evaluate LLMs against standardized clinical protocols and evidence‐based guidelines. The medical safety and ethics dimension addresses critical aspects of hallucination, bias, interpretations, and ethics.
Many of the datasets underlying current evaluation benchmarks draw on real‐world medical competence examinations and clinical guidelines. However, most of these benchmarks have not undergone multiple rounds of expert review and consensus building, making their assessments relatively subjective. Moreover, although these benchmarks represent substantial progress in evaluating LLMs for medical applications, they remain largely confined to research settings and have seen limited deployment in routine healthcare practice.

Current Status of Medical Competence
In accordance with the established assessment framework, researchers conducted a medical competence assessment of the current mainstream or fine‐tuned LLMs. The findings indicated that LLMs exhibited a certain level of medical competence (Table 1).

Basic Medical Knowledge Performance
LLMs have demonstrated proficiency in certain aspects of both basic and specialized medical disciplines. For instance, GPT‐4 exhibited an 80% accuracy rate in infective endocarditis knowledge assessment. Instruction‐fine‐tuned models such as Med‐PaLMs are more closely aligned with human physicians in their ability to answer medical questions. In a panel of long‐form answers aligned with scientific consensus, Med‐PaLM achieved an accuracy of 92.6%, comparable to the clinician‐generated answers (92.9%).

Clinical Practice Competency
GPT‐4 scored the highest on the AMEGA benchmark, with 41.9 out of 50. LLMs demonstrate information‐processing capabilities and can improve diagnostic accuracy when provided with additional clinical information. However, their diagnostic accuracy remains inferior to that of physicians.

Comprehensive Assessment
In the MedS‐Bench assessment benchmark, MMedIns‐Llama 3 exhibited superior performance in the clinical tasks of NLP in medicine, diagnosis, treatment planning, and prediction of clinical outcomes. However, a study indicates that LLMs' level of clinical practice is not yet at a level that can be considered competent for clinicians.

Medical Safety and Ethics
Research has shown that current medical LLMs are inadequate in addressing safety concerns. Additionally, there is a discernible prevalence of professional gender stereotyping, power, and social class bias.

Discussion
Based on the benchmarks we retrieved and the additional literature we incorporated, we synthesized and categorized the common approaches currently used to evaluate the medical capabilities of LLMs.

Medical Theoretical Knowledge
We argue that the level of LLMs' medical theoretical knowledge forms the foundation for evaluating their medical competence, which differs from NLP tasks such as named entity recognition and relationship extraction. Assessments of LLMs' theoretical knowledge can be categorized into three main types: first, self‐administered disciplinary assessment questions. Assessment questions, typically with a small sample size (n < 1000), are constructed by researchers based on different sources, including medical literature, books, guidelines from professional societies/institutions, the Internet, and medical professional forums. The accuracy and completeness of the LLMs' responses are then manually evaluated by clinicians or experts in relevant fields.
Secondly, the content is derived from the questions posed in the medical licensing examinations or domain‐specific medical examinations across various countries. For instance, one study reported that ChatGPT passed the US Medical Licensing Examination. Additionally, medical licensing examination questions from 17 countries and regions, including the United Kingdom, China, and Israel, have been used to assess the theoretical knowledge mastery of LLMs. Gupta et al. conducted an evaluation using specialty medical questions from the ACR Diagnostic In‐Training Examination.
The third category comprises self‐constructed, comprehensive assessment benchmarks, specifically those containing at least 1000 items and categorized datasets. For instance, the MultiMedQA, proposed by the Google team to assess LLMs' performance in answering medical questions, draws upon a number of medical Q&A datasets, including MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, MMLU, HealthSearchQA, LiveQA, and MedicationQA. Unlike other assessments, this one goes beyond evaluating the accuracy of responses in the form of multiple‐choice questions. The responses are then manually evaluated to ascertain their alignment with scientific and clinical consensus, the potential for harm, reading comprehension, recall of pertinent clinical knowledge, and the ability to apply knowledge through sound reasoning. The Chinese Medical Benchmark (CMB), proposed by a research team from The Chinese University of Hong Kong (Shenzhen), is a medical benchmark designed specifically for the Chinese medical environment, encompassing the knowledge systems of both Chinese and Western medicine. The benchmark is divided into two subsets: CMB‐Exam and CMB‐Clin. The former comprises medical examination questions from Chinese doctors, nurses, medical technicians, and so forth, designed to assess the model's grasp of fundamental and clinical medical knowledge. The latter consists of open‐ended questions derived from clinical cases, intended to evaluate the model's diagnostic and reasoning capabilities in intricate clinical contexts.
Current assessment techniques and benchmarks primarily gauge the breadth of knowledge embodied in LLMs' medical competence. However, they do not capture the intricacy and sensitivity of authentic clinical scenarios, and therefore fall short of fully assessing clinical competence.

Clinical Practice Competence
The clinical practice competence of LLMs is primarily evaluated based on their capacity to integrate their professional knowledge, comprehensively collect and analyze clinical information, as well as to reason and make decisions in clinical scenarios. This competence can be broadly classified into two dimensions: clinical information processing and clinical reasoning and decision‐making abilities. The majority of studies conducted on this dimension are based on clinical vignettes or electronic medical record (EHR) simulated clinical application scenarios. These studies aim to assess the diagnostic accuracy and correctness of treatment recommendations of LLMs, as well as to explore the factors affecting the diagnostic accuracy.

Clinical Information Processing
The assessment of clinical information processing ability is concerned with the capacity of LLMs to engage in activities such as history taking, the selection of appropriate physical and ancillary tests, and preliminary analysis of the results of these tests. In the initial stages of assessment, researchers typically provide LLMs with comprehensive medical history data, enabling the model to generate diagnoses and evaluate the outputs. However, this approach does not comprehensively evaluate the model's ability to take a detailed medical history. To address this issue and comprehensively examine LLMs' ability to process complete information, Rao et al. evaluated the utility of ChatGPT across the clinical workflow, using 36 standardized cases from the Merck Sharp & Dohme Clinical Manual. The study assessed how well the LLMs analyzed and utilized the clinical information to improve diagnostic accuracy performance by adding or subtracting patient medical information provided to ChatGPT.

Clinical Reasoning and Decision‐Making Abilities
The assessment was designed to evaluate the capacity to make an initial diagnosis, perform a differential diagnosis, and develop a rational treatment and management plan based on established clinical information. The Shanghai AI Lab has proposed the development of MedGPTEval, a Delphi‐optimized assessment system evaluating diagnostic performance across 14 disease categories using a Chinese dataset of 27 medical conversations and 7 case reports. Expert clinicians conduct comprehensive evaluations using 16 refined metrics spanning multiple competency domains‐including clinical performance, treatment planning, diagnostic reasoning, and contextual understanding. This expert‐driven evaluation framework uniquely emphasizes qualitative assessment of medical professional competence, social integration abilities, and reasoning robustness that automated metrics alone cannot capture. The NAVER digital healthcare team proposed EHRNoteQA, which created 962 question–answer pairs related to patient discharge based on discharge summaries from the MIMIC database. The research evaluated GPT‐4's performance in a real query process, assessed the correctness of LLMs' output results, and compared the consistency between LLMs' performance on EHRNoteQA and clinicians' manual assessments. Furthermore, the MIMIC‐CDM dataset evaluated the adherence of LLMs to treatment regimens in 2400 cases of four prevalent abdominal disorders, namely appendicitis, cholecystitis, diverticulitis, and pancreatitis. The results demonstrate that the diagnostic accuracy of LLMs remains significantly lower than that of physicians, indicating that substantial progress is still required before these models can be formally implemented in clinical practice.
The aforementioned studies delved into the intricacies of LLMs' medical competence by gathering authentic clinical scenarios and formulating comprehensive assessment criteria. They evaluated the multifaceted clinical proficiency of diverse LLMs. Nevertheless, these benchmarks are not without constraints. These limitations include a modest assessment dataset, a narrow disease scope, neglect of comprehensive clinical pathways, the necessity for time‐consuming manual assessments, and reliance on natural language to process assessment metrics.
In response to these limitations, several collaborative initiatives are currently underway to address these shortcomings. The researchers at Stanford developed a benchmarking framework, the Holistic Evaluation of Language Models, which enabled a comprehensive evaluation of model performance across a spectrum of real‐world medical scenarios, from documenting diagnostic reports to facilitating patient education.

Medical Safety and Ethics
In medical applications, LLMs must not only demonstrate high accuracy but also ensure that their output aligns with safety and ethical standards. Prior research has identified potential risks associated with LLMs, including accuracy, transparency, bias, and privacy protection. This paper provides an overview of the current state of research on assessing medical safety and ethics in terms of three key dimensions: bias, hallucination, and interpretability; morality and ethics.

Bias
LLMs are typically trained on a corpus of human‐generated text and reinforcement learning based on human feedback, a process that may introduce bias. This bias can result in inequities, such as unequal distribution of healthcare resources and gender discrimination when dealing with marginalized or underrepresented populations, thereby exacerbating the problem of health inequalities. LLMs are frequently trained using human‐generated text and reinforcement learning based on human feedback. The most common methods of bias assessment are as follows: (1) the gender and race information of vignettes is altered to ascertain whether the diagnosis and treatment plan differ between different genders and races; (2) gender‐ambiguous occupations (e.g., “doctors” and “nurses”) are used. Paired with gendered verbs (e.g., “he is late” and “she is late”) to assess the presence of gender bias in LLMs; and (3) LLMs were tasked with generating a substantial volume of virtual picture data or population data for comparison with the actual situation. To assess the propensity of LLMs to exhibit gender and ethnic bias, Ishika et al. proposed a specific Hindi dataset, HinStereo‐10043, for manual evaluation. This dataset was designed to ascertain whether LLMs make inferences about occupational population distributions based on gender stereotypes. To this end, sentences containing gender‐ambiguous occupational descriptions were constructed. In their study, Travis employed the data from the NEJM Healer's 19 clinical education cases for outpatient and emergency decision‐making scenarios. They altered the gender and race of each patient to test for differences in LLMs' recommendations for different variables in diagnosis and treatment.

Hallucination and Interpretability
Inadequate and opaque training data and model mechanisms can result in LLMs outputting information that appears realistic but is factually incorrect and highly misleading (hallucinations), as well as outputs that are poorly interpretable. In the medical field, this phenomenon presents a significant challenge. Chen et al. trained the MediHallDetector hallucination‐detection model, whose outputs exhibit greater consistency than GPT across different hallucination texts. However, the model performance was not compared with that of clinical experts, and the reliability was inadequate. In contrast, Mahmud et al. evaluated the correlation between accuracy and confidence of 12 LLMs in answering clinical questions based on 1965 multiple‐choice questions covering five medical domains. This study provides methodological references for assessing the interpretability of models.

Morality and Ethics
Ethical questions in medicine are inherently complex and multifaceted, encompassing patient‐privacy protection, the autonomy of clinical decision‐making, and accountability for a model's outputs. These concerns become particularly acute when LLMs are used to support diagnostic decisions involving uncertain outcomes or ambiguous presentations. In an evaluation of 376 unique real‐world clinical cases, 20.1% of the model outputs were deemed inappropriate. If deployment may potentially result in direct patient harm or provide clinicians with misleading yet seemingly authoritative justifications for their clinical decisions. Compounding these risks, the use of LLMs in healthcare currently lacks a unified legal framework and clear regulatory accountability, making it difficult to assign responsibility when harm occurs. In response to these challenges, existing safety and ethics assessment frameworks, such as SafetyBench, proposed by Tsinghua University, which encompasses 11,435 multiple‐choice questions spanning seven categories of safety issues, provide comprehensive benchmarks for evaluating the safety of LLMs in both Chinese and English environments. Similarly, MedSafetyBench, developed on the basis of the American Medical Association's Principles of Medical Ethics, is designed to assess and enhance the safety of LLMs in healthcare.

Comprehensive Evaluation Benchmarks
In order to comprehensively evaluate the clinical application capabilities of LLMs, the Shanghai AI Lab has proposed the development of two new benchmarks: MedBench and MedS‐Bench. These benchmarks cover multiple high‐level clinical tasks and clinical datasets and evaluate the performance of LLMs in 43 clinical specialties based on three key dimensions: medical language processing, medical competency, and healthcare safety and ethics. The assessment framework employs a comprehensive evaluation methodology to assess the competence of LLMs through a range of metrics, including accuracy, BLEU, and ROUGE, in both zero‐ and few‐sample settings. It also provides a standardized and fully automated assessment platform that supports a dynamic assessment mechanism, which is designed to prevent model shortcut learning and answer memorization. The assessment benchmark encompasses a vast array of subjects and establishes a more comprehensive and scientific assessment mechanism.
However, the assessment does not encompass more NLP tasks (such as interactive clinical dialogs or long‐form clinical narrative generation), and they have not achieved an evaluation that dynamically simulates a complete, authentic clinical workflow. Furthermore, while automatic metrics such as ROUGE are widely used for evaluation, they have limitations in the medical domain. ROUGE primarily measures n‐gram overlap with reference answers, which does not adequately reflect the accuracy or clinical relevance of the content. As a result, these metrics cannot gauge critical aspects of performance such as logical coherence, appropriate tone and empathy, strict adherence to clinical guidelines, or understanding of nuanced contextual information.

Limitations of Current Evaluation
Existing assessment benchmarks demonstrate significant limitations in their capacity to comprehensively evaluate the medical competence and potential risks associated with LLMs. These benchmarks are less capable of addressing multiple dimensions of medical competence, including medical theoretical knowledge, clinical practice competence, medical safety and ethics, and robustness. As medical knowledge is constantly updated and LLM competence continues to improve, the assessment benchmarks used to evaluate their medical competence must also adapt.
Most current assessment benchmarks predominantly employ static evaluation, failing to accommodate the dynamic capabilities and evolutionary nature of LLMs. Additionally, the public availability of assessment datasets and ground truth information introduces potential vulnerabilities to evaluation integrity, as models may be inadvertently or deliberately exposed to these data during training processes. Such exposure significantly compromises the validity and reliability of evaluation outcomes.
In summary, prior benchmarks exhibit limitations including narrow task scenarios, single‐dimensional evaluation metrics, and static evaluation datasets. There is an urgent need to construct a comprehensive, integrated, and dynamic evaluation framework that adapts to clinical scenarios.

Future of Evaluation Benchmarks for LLMs
Current evaluation benchmarks for medical LLMs often rely on static datasets and simplified tasks, which do not fully capture the intricacies of real clinical environments. To truly assess and improve LLMs' medical competence, future benchmarks must evolve to reflect real‐world clinical workflows and patient interactions. Based on this, we propose a new evaluation benchmark (Box 1) that considers both the competency assessment process of clinicians and the limitations of LLMs.

To address the limitations of current evaluation benchmarks, we propose a novel benchmark framework (Box 1) that comprehensively incorporates three evaluation dimensions: Theoretical Medical Knowledge, Clinical Practice Competency, and Medical Safety and Ethics. This framework establishes an integrated, interdisciplinary item bank by aggregating multi‐source information from real medical records and standardized clinical examinations across medical specialties. The evaluation methodology combines automated performance metrics with expert‐mediated structured assessments, enabling a thorough evaluation of the medical competence of LLMs.
This benchmark assesses LLMs in three sequential dimensions. First, medical theoretical knowledge is evaluated with physician licensing examination questions drawn from multiple countries. Second, medical practice competence is tested with real‐world clinical cases. Finally, ethics and safety performance are measured against existing ethical evaluation benchmarks. To prevent data sparsity and cheating, a large, diversified item bank is maintained, and random sampling is employed; each test item is dynamically rotated to safeguard confidentiality and ensure fairness.

Conclusions
Because hallucinations in current LLMs remain unresolved, relying unconditionally on their automated outputs poses major risks in clinical settings. Even when a model performs well, its adoption in routine workflows should be accompanied by continuous monitoring and periodic re‐evaluation. Moreover, every LLM output must be reviewed by qualified human experts before release to guarantee safety and usability, thereby reducing medical disputes and adverse events.
Collaboration between AI researchers and medical professionals will be essential to refine these evolving benchmark criteria. We suggest establishing interdisciplinary partnerships or committees that involve clinicians, medical ethicists, and AI developers from the outset of benchmark design. Such collaborations can ensure that evaluation scenarios are realistic and clinically relevant and that metrics used to score model performance correspond to meaningful patient outcomes and safety standards.
Finally, to overcome the limitations of static evaluation, researchers should explore more dynamic and longitudinal assessment methods. Traditional Q&A or classification tests fail to capture how a model might perform over sustained interactions or adapt to new information. Future research should develop evaluation environments that mirror the evolving nature of clinical encounters—such as simulation platforms where LLMs engage in multi‐turn dialogs with virtual patients or providers or continuous evaluation pipelines that monitor a model's performance on new data over time. Such comprehensive evaluation frameworks facilitate the effective deployment of models across diverse global healthcare systems, addressing the heterogeneous requirements of contemporary medical practice.
