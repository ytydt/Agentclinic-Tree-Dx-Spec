Evaluation of malignancy risk in patients with SSc by autoantibody profile: a systematic review with meta-analysis
Abstract
Objective
Increased risk of cancer has been reported in SSc patients; however, the magnitude of this risk seems to vary with SSc-specific autoantibody profile. We aimed to review cancer risk in SSc patients by autoantibody subtypes.
Methods
We systematically reviewed literature related to cancer in SSc patients from January 1980 to December 2023 using PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Library. We performed a meta-analysis comparing cancer risk in SSc patients with ACA, anti-RNAP III antibody, and anti-Scl70 antibody. The search was conducted on 1 December 2023 and updated on 30 December 2023. Eligible studies were required to (1) include participants ≥18 years who fulfilled the 2013 ACR/EULAR, 1980 ACR modified, or LeRoy and Medsger classification criteria, (2) report cancer incidence with SSc-specific autoantibody, and (3) analyse the relationship between SSc-specific autoantibodies and cancer.
Results
Twenty-two eligible studies were included and underwent meta-analysis. Anti-RNAP III positivity was associated with a significantly increased cancer risk in SSc patients (pooled relative risk (RR) 1.48; 95% CI 1.16–1.80), compared with anti-RNAP III-negative patients. While the presence of ACA was associated with a lower cancer risk (pooled RR 0.78; 95% CI 0.60–0.96) and may serve as an indicator for solely SSc, specifically limited systemic scleroderma.
Conclusion
Our meta-analysis showed that anti-RNAP III-positive SSc patients had a 48% higher risk of developing cancer compared with anti-RNAP III-negative SSc patients. Therefore, physicians should be aware of this elevated risk. Rigorous screening for malignancy by age-appropriate and relevant family history is recommended.

Introduction
SSc, also known as scleroderma, is a multisystem connective tissue disease that affects the skin and internal organs. The pathogenesis of SSc remains poorly understood. Several hypotheses have been proposed, such as immune dysregulation, vascular endothelial apoptosis, and fibroblast over-activation, which lead to sclerosis of the skin and internal organs. SSc is classified into two subtypes, based on the localization of skin sclerosis. Limited SSc is characterized by skin sclerosis distal to the elbows and knees, whereas skin sclerosis in diffuse SSc is proximal to the elbows and knees. SSc primarily affects females. The prevalence of SSc is 0.3–2.8 cases per 100 000 individuals annually. SSc-specific autoantibodies play a crucial role in the diagnosis of SSc as they are one of the criteria for classification of SSc used in the ACR/EULAR collaborative initiative. Moreover, SSc-specific autoantibodies serve as prognostic markers in SSc patients, i.e. (1) ACA is associated with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), (2) anti-topoisomerase-1 antibody (anti-Scl70) is linked to digital ulcerations, interstitiallung disease (ILD), and skin fibrosis, and (3) anti-RNA polymerase III antibody (anti-RNAP III) is associated with renal crisis, skin fibrosis, and paraneoplasia.
Increasingly, studies have reported differences in risk of malignancy by SSc-specific autoantibody subtype. SSc patients with anti-Scl70 had a significantly increased risk of developing lung cancer, with OR 2.61 (95% CI 1.00–6.79). An increased risk of cervical cancer in SSc patients with anti-Scl70 has also been reported (OR 1.87; 95% CI 1.01–3.47). Lazzaroni et al. reported a 2.17-fold increased risk of developing cancer in patients with positive anti-RNAP III, particularly solid tumours (OR 2.52; 95% CI 1.21–5.27) and breast cancer (OR 3.65; 95% CI 1.14–11.7). Moreover, the occurrence of cancer within 2 years after SSc onset is considerably higher in individuals with positive anti-RNAP III compared with those with negative anti-RNAP III (OR 5.08; 95% CI 1.60–16.1). However, some studies reported no statistically significant correlation between the presence of anti-RNAP-III and malignancy. Data for positivity of ACA and cancer risk were heterogeneous. There was a study that reported a statistically significantly higher risk of lung cancer and stomach cancer, whereas some studies found no correlation (OR 0.66; 95% CI 0.45–0.97). A study by Lu et al. showed a higher prevalence of ACA positivity in SSc patients with breast cancer compared with anti-RNAP III and anti-Scl70 positivity. To date, the results are mixed and inconclusive. Therefore, we performed systematic review and meta-analysis aiming to examine malignancy risk in SSc patients by SSc-specific autoantibody profile. The review objectives, as registered in PROSPERO, were: (1) Do patients with SSc have a higher risk of malignancy than the general population? (2) Do patients with SSc who have anti-RNAP III have a greater risk of malignancy than those who do not? (3) Do patients with SSc who have anti-Scl70 have a greater risk of malignancy than those who do not? (4) Do patients with SSc who have ACA have a greater risk of malignancy than those who do not? and (5) Does the duration between diagnosis for SSc and cancer correlate?

Materials and methods
The systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, with adaptations for observational studies to assess cancer risk stratified by SSc-specific autoantibody profiles. The review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023470571) and is available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42023470571. The study was reviewed and approved by the Srinakharinwirot Ethics Committee. The committee specifically approved this study under reference number SWUEC/E/M-026/2566E.

Information sources and search strategy
Comprehensive searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials Database, using the terms ‘Systemic sclerosis’ AND ‘Malignancy’ OR ‘Cancer’ OR ‘Neoplasm’ OR ‘Carcinoma’ OR ‘Lymphoma’ OR ‘Adenocarcinoma’ OR ‘Tumor’ (Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology online). We retrieved the articles that were published from 1 January 1980 to the search date. The search was performed on 1 December 2023 and was updated on 30 December 2023. Additionally, the reference lists of all retrieval studies were reviewed and manually searched.

Eligibility criteria and selection process
To be included in the analysis, studies were required to (1) study participants of 18 years old and above who fulfilled the 2013 ACR/EULAR criteria, the 1980 ACR modified criteria, and/or the LeRoy and Medsger classification criteria; (2) report cancer incidence in SSc patients with SSc-specific autoantibody; and (3) analyse the relationship between SSc-specific autoantibodies and cancer. No restriction on sex, race, language, or country of study. Two reviewers (K.T. and W.S.) independently screened titles and abstracts. Potentially relevant articles were advanced to full-text review. Any discrepancies at full-text screening were resolved by discussion between the two reviewers and when necessary, a senior investigator (C.L.).

Data collection and data items
Two reviewers independently extracted data from full-text articles. In case of incomplete data of interest in the full-text article, we directly contacted the corresponding authors through email. Relevant information included the year of study, study duration, study design, baseline characteristics of SSc patients (i.e. mean age, sex, race, type of SSc, age of SSc diagnosis, internal organ involvement), follow-up duration for SSc and cancer, number and type of cancers, positivity of anti-Scl70, anti-RNAP III, and ACA, variables adjusted for in the regression model, and any of the standardized incidence ratio (SIR), odds ratio (OR), relative risk (RR), or hazard ratio (HZ) with 95% CI when presented.

Study risk of bias assessment
The relevant full-text articles were assessed for quality of methodology using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies—of Exposures (ROBINS-E) tool, version 20 June 2023, which was designed for nonrandomized studies in meta-analysis. Low risk of bias is defined as low risk in all domains, except for Domain 1 (confounding), where some concerns about residual confounding were permissible. Some concerns are defined as at least one domain rated as having some concerns, but no domains rated as high or very high risk. High risk of bias is defined as at least one domain rated as high risk, or multiple domains with some concerns, leading to an overall judgement of high risk. Very high risk of bias is defined as at least one domain rated as very high risk, or multiple domains rated as high risk, resulting in an overall judgement of very high risk.

Effect measures and synthesis methods
Results from individual studies and syntheses were organized into structured summary tables showing key characteristics and outcome data. For quantitative syntheses, we used forest plots to visually present the effect estimates and their CIs. The meta-analyses were performed only when data from ≥4 studies were included. The measure of interest was the RR, estimated by the OR in case–control or cross-sectional studies and by SIR in cohort studies with 95% CI. The outcomes of each study were combined using the inverse variance method. Statistical heterogeneity within studies was tested using the chi-square-based Cochrane Q test. If P > 0.1 and I2 < 50%, an absence of heterogeneity across studies was identified, and then the fixed-effects model was used. Otherwise, the random-effects model was used. If P < 0.1 or I2 > 50%, then the heterogeneity was considered statistically significant. To explore potential sources of study heterogeneity, meta-regression with prespecified variables, including sex and age, was conducted. The statistical analysis was performed using STATA version 17 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA).
In this analysis, the outcomes of interest were RR of malignancy in (1) SSc patients with anti-Scl70 vs. without anti-Scl70, (2) SSc patients with anti-RNAP III vs. without anti-RNAP III, and (3) SSc patients with ACA vs. without ACA, in relation to both overall and type-specific cancer incidence. To determine which studies were eligible for each synthesis, we reviewed and tabulated key characteristics such as autoantibody profile, cancer outcome reporting, and measurement methods. Studies with comparable characteristics were included in the same synthesis.

Reporting bias assessment
We assessed the possibility of publication bias using funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s test.

Results

Search results and characteristics of included studies
A total of 4809 articles were systematically identified. After removing duplicates, 3070 articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Eighty-eight full-text articles were evaluated for eligibility (Fig. 1). Twenty-two studies were included in systematic review and meta-analysis: 17 cohort studies, 3 case–control studies, and 2 cross-sectional studies. A total of 3431 SSc patients were included. The follow-up duration of study ranged from approximately 6 months to 40 years. The characteristics of the studies are summarized in Table 1.

Risk of bias in studies
Nine studies were judged to have low risk of bias. Two studies were rated as having some concerns. Nine studies were considered as high risk of bias. Two studies were assessed as having a very high risk of bias (detailed in Supplementary Fig. S1, available at Rheumatology online).

Overall cancer incidence and SSc-specific autoantibodies
Seventeen studies that reported an association between SSc-specific autoantibodies and overall cancer incidence were included in the meta-analysis. The risk of cancer in SSc patients with different autoantibody status revealed the following results. A significantly higher risk of cancer in SSc patients was associated with anti-RNAP III positivity with the pooled RR 1.48 (95% CI 1.16–1.80), compared with SSc patients who had negative anti-RNAP III (Fig. 2A). Contrasting results were found in SSc patients with positive ACA; the pooled RR was 0.78 (95% CI 0.60–0.96) (Fig. 2B). The presence of ACA in SSc patients seemed to lower the risk of cancer. In terms of anti-Scl70, the analysis showed a non-statistically significant association between cancer risk and the positivity of anti-Scl70 in SSc patients (pooled RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.75–1.03) (Fig. 2C).

Type-specific cancer incidence and SSc-specific autoantibodies
Types of malignancies, with the number of cases, were delineated in Table 1. The most commonly reported cancers were breast cancer and lung cancer. There were four studies that reported an association between the incidences of these two cancers and ACA and anti-Scl70 with sufficient data to perform meta-analyses. Looking at lung cancer, SSc patients with positive anti-Scl70 (pooled RR 1.70; 95% CI 0.46–2.94) seemed to have a higher risk of developing malignancy than SSc patients with positive ACA (pooled RR 0.17; 95% CI −0.05 to 0.38) (Fig. 3A and B). With wider 95% CIs, the results required great caution with interpretation. The presence of both anti-Scl70 and ACA was not associated with an increased risk of breast cancer, with pooled RR 0.72 (95% CI 0.28–1.16) and 0.97 (95% CI 0.57–1.36), respectively (Fig. 4A and B).

Publication bias
The funnel plot examining cancer risk in SSc patients with SSc-specific autoantibodies (anti-RNAP III, ACA, and anti-Scl70) showed no obvious asymmetry. Consistently, Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias for anti-RNAP III (P = 0.155), ACA (P = 0.219), and anti-Scl70 (P = 0.156) (Supplementary Fig. S2A–C, available at Rheumatology online). Additionally, the funnel plots of lung and breast cancer risk in SSc patients with ACA and anti-Scl70 showed no discernible asymmetry (Supplementary Figs S3A and B and S4A and B, available at Rheumatology online).

Discussion
Our meta-analysis revealed a significantly increased incidence of cancer in SSc patients with anti-RNAP III-positive compared with those with anti-RNAP III-negative, with a pooled RR of 1.48 (95% CI 1.16–1.80). This finding is consistent with several previous studies supporting a strong association between anti-RNAP III positivity and malignancy in SSc.
According to Moinzadeh et al., SSc patients with anti-RNAP III (14.2%) had a significantly higher cancer rate than those with anti-Scl70 (6.3%) and ACA (6.8%) (P < 0.0001 and P < 0.001, respectively). Saigusa et al. also reported a considerably greater incidence of cancer (31.8%) among anti-RNAP III-positive SSc patients compared with those with anti-Scl70 (2.4%), with a P-value <0.0005. A study by Lazzaroni et al. in 158 SSc patients with anti-RNAP III+ showed 2.17-fold increased risk of malignancy (95% CI 1.15–4.08, P = 0.015), with a particularly elevated risk for breast cancer (RR 3.65; 95% CI 1.14–11.7; P = 0.03) and solid tumours (RR 2.52; 95% CI 1.21–5.27; P = 0.012). Recently, Santos et al. studied the association between cancer prevalence and SSc-specific autoantibodies in a cohort of SSc patients with a mean age of 65.9 years, two-thirds of whom were female. Their findings showed that SSc patients who tested positive for anti-RNAP III were significantly more likely to develop cancer than anti-RNAP III-negative patients (OR 10.9; 95% CI 1.08–109.3, P = 0.04), with lung cancer, gastrointestinal cancer, and breast cancer being the most frequently reported malignancies.
In terms of anti-RNAP III and lung cancer risk, Moinzadeh et al. reported a higher percentage of positive anti-RNAP III in SSc patients with lung cancer compared with SSc patients without cancer (25% vs. 12.2%; RR 2.37; 95% CI −0.90 to 5.63). In contrast, Sakr et al. found no association between anti-RNAP III and lung cancer cases (calculated RR 0.36; 95% CI −0.99 to 1.72). Regarding risk of breast cancer and the presence of anti-RNAP III, a large cohort of 4986 SSc patients found a higher frequency of breast cancers in anti-RNAP III-positive patients compared with anti-RNAP III-negative patients (P = 0.03). Similarly, a retrospective study in a large UK cohort showed an increased percentage of positive anti-RNAP III SSc patients with breast cancer (RR 2.82; 95% CI 1.28–4.36). Furthermore, the study by Igusa et al. revealed a temporal relationship between malignancy diagnosis and scleroderma onset within 3 years. The SIR of cancer in SSc patients with anti-RNAP III was 2.84 (95% CI 1.89–4.10). Possible mechanisms underlying the close association of anti-RNAP III with malignancy in SSc patients were proposed, as anti-RNAP III may initiate an antitumour immune response that may cross-react against specific host tissue, resulting in SSc development. Joseph et al. also found missense mutations in the polymerase III polypeptide A (POLR3A) gene, which encoded RNA polymerase III subunit (RPC1) protein in six out of eight SSc patients with anti-RNAP III antibody, whereas none of the SSc patients with anti-Scl70 or ACA had this mutation. Interestingly, antibodies from all six patients with the mutated POLR3A gene recognized both mutated and wild-type (non-mutated) RNA polymerase III. The immune system may then recognize mutated RNA polymerase III as foreign and cause damage to healthy tissue in SSc. This could be a consequence of the immune system’s efforts to eradicate cancer cells.
Our meta-analysis of 13 studies showed no significant association between the presence of anti-Scl70 in SSc patients and an increased incidence of cancer (pooled RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.75–1.03). Despite this overall finding, individual studies have reported mixed results. A study in 2022 by Carbonell et al., involving 1930 Spanish SSc patients, found that the presence of anti-Scl70 was significantly higher in SSc patients with cancer than in SSc patients without cancer (13.4% vs. 7.0%, P = 0.024). Another study in a Spanish population revealed that patients with cancer were 2.55 times more likely to be anti-Scl70-positive compared with those without cancer (OR 2.55; 95% CI 1.03–6.3, P = 0.04). Conversely, other studies reported no significant correlation between the presence of anti-Scl70 and overall cancer (OR 0.73, P = 0.44), breast cancer (RR 0.97; 95% CI 0.25–3.72, P = 0.97), or lung cancer. These conflicting results highlight the need for further research to clarify the potential relationship between anti-Scl70 and cancer risk in SSc patients.
Looking at ACA, our analysis suggested that SSc patients with positive ACA seemed to have a significantly lower risk of cancer compared with those without ACA (RR 0.78; 95% CI 0.60–0.96). This result aligned with the report by Carbonell et al. that the presence of ACA in SSc patients decreased cancer risk significantly (OR 0.66; 95% CI 0.45–0.97, P = 0.036). Recently, the study of 562 SSc patients with positive ACA in the Canadian Scleroderma Research Group registry from 2004 to 2019 showed that the risk of cancer within 2 years was decreased among ACA-positive patients (OR 0.46; 95% CI 0.12–1.36). Some authors reported contrary results. Derk et al. found no significant difference in cancer risk between SSc patients with ACA and those without (OR 0.66, P = 0.23). Higuchi et al. reported that positive ACA is a significant risk factor for cancer in a relatively small Japanese SSc cohort; however, this might not represent the common SSc epidemiology due to its extremely low ACA prevalence.
Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, moderate heterogeneity was observed in the meta-analysis assessing the association between the presence of ACA and cancer risk (I2 = 57.25%, P = 0.01). However, due to the limited number of studies, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses. Potential sources of heterogeneity may include differences in cancer types, age, and race. Second, we only looked at the three most common SSc-specific autoantibodies; other autoantibodies, such as anti-Ku, anti-PM-Scl, were not included. Third, we did not report the time to occurrence of malignancy in the SSc cohort by specific autoantibody profiles, due to the lack of access to individual patient data. Fourth, the included studies did not incorporate appropriately matched control groups, and RR was assessed only among patients with different autoantibodies, without reference to the expected incidence of cancer in the general population. This limits our ability to estimate absolute cancer risk. Lastly, although we systematically reviewed the literature to address all objectives registered in PROSPERO, we were unable to perform a meta-analysis on the risk of malignancy in patients with SSc compared with the general population. This was due to significant heterogeneity among the included studies—such as differences in cancer types, race, and geographic location—as well as a limited number of studies (fewer than 10) per cancer type. Furthermore, we could not conduct a meta-analysis to assess whether the duration between SSc diagnosis and cancer onset was correlated, as most studies did not report individual patient-level data. An individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis may be more appropriate for exploring this association and warrants further investigation.

Conclusion
In conclusion, our meta-analysis confirmed a significantly higher risk of cancer in SSc patients who were positive for anti-RNAP III compared with those who were negative. While the presence of ACA was associated with a lower risk of cancer and may serve as an indicator for solely SSc, specifically limited SSc. Physicians should consider this increased cancer risk in the presence of anti-RNAP III when evaluating and managing patients. Rigorous screening for malignancy by age-appropriate and relevant family history is recommended in this population.
