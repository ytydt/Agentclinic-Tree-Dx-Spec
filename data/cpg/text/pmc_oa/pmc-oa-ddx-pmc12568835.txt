Body Muscle-to-Fat Ratio (BMFR) as a Promising Anthropometric Index for Use in the Evaluation of Insulin Resistance and Metabolic Disease Risk: a Narrative Review
Purpose of Review
Insulin resistance is a key component of the metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and related cardiovascular complications. However, despite its clinical relevance, a simple and reliable surrogate marker of insulin resistance is lacking. The body muscle-to-fat ratio (BMFR) is a novel anthropometric index, and particularly in treatment-naïve patients with T2DM, BMFR correlates more closely with insulin sensitivity, assessed using the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp, than conventional indices, such as the Matsuda Index and the Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index. In this review, we critically evaluate the current evidence regarding the BMFR as an index of insulin resistance and metabolic disease risk, highlight its strengths and limitations versus conventional measures, and discuss future directions.
Recent Findings
Several recent studies have demonstrated the importance of body composition metrics for the evaluation of metabolic health, including those by Guan et al. (Adv Nutr), Oliver et al. (J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle), and Vieira et al. (Rev Endocr Metab Disord). The findings have resulted in a growing recognition of the need for reliable, accessible markers of muscle quality and metabolic risk.
Summary
BMFR offers advantages over the fat-to-muscle ratio and conventional anthropometric indices as an intuitive and clinically relevant marker of insulin resistance. It represents a promising method of screening for obesity-related complications, evaluating treatment responses, and guiding personalized care, including in patients with high-risk phenotypes, such as sarcopenic obesity. With further validation, BMFR could become a standard tool for the evaluation of metabolic health and risk in clinical practice.

Introduction
Obesity and metabolic health are increasingly recognized to be closely linked in modern clinical practice. This relationship has gained particular attention because of the rising global prevalence of obesity, a key driver of metabolic dysfunction, and a major contributor to the increasing prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MetS), which includes type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) as a defining component. Individuals with MetS are at a higher risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, with hazard ratios of approximately 1.5 to 2.5 obtained in epidemiological studies. This high risk is largely attributable to insulin resistance, a core metabolic abnormality in the pathogenesis of MetS that contributes to the development of T2DM and the progression of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
To monitor and prevent disease progression, simple and reliable anthropometric indices that reflect insulin resistance are increasingly needed in clinical practice. Among the proposed candidates, the body muscle-to-fat ratio (BMFR) has emerged as a promising and readily interpretable surrogate marker. Moreover, because of the widespread availability of body composition analyzers, particularly those based on bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), it is now feasible to measure muscle and fat mass noninvasively and cost-effectively, facilitating the practical use of indices such as BMFR in real-world clinical settings. In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that these indices may provide superior insights into metabolic health than conventional anthropometric measures, although no comprehensive review has evaluated their clinical significance in relation to insulin resistance, especially regarding potential differences in the performance of BMFR vs. body fat-to-muscle ratio (FMR).
The clinical advantages of BMFR have been demonstrated in our previous studies of single-center datasets. However, these did not provide a comprehensive analysis of the evidence as a whole or compare the utility of BMFR with other anthropometric indices. Therefore, in the present review, we aimed to critically appraise the utility of BMFR as an index of insulin resistance and metabolic disease risk by considering the content of recent high-impact reviews by Guan et al.. and Oliver et al.. Furthermore, we aimed to discuss BMFR within the broader framework of load–capacity indices (LCIs), to provide a novel perspective regarding its clinical relevance and limitations.
Given the rapid advances in body composition measurement technologies and the increasing importance of the optimal management of obesity and its complications, we aimed to clarify the clinical utility of body composition-based indices, particularly BMFR, for the assessment of insulin resistance and to support the personalized management of metabolic disorders.
In accordance with this objective, we first discuss the limitations of the use of conventional anthropometric indices as body composition measures and markers of metabolic risk, and emphasize the clinical significance of evaluating skeletal muscle. Next, we review the utility of muscle and fat-based indices and discuss their associations with various metabolic diseases. We then focus on the validity of BMFR as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, evaluated using hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp studies. Subsequently, we compare BMFR with FMR, especially with respect to their relative clinical applicability. Finally, we outline future directions with respect to the use of BMFR in clinical and research settings. We conclude by summarizing the key findings and discussing their implications for personalized metabolic care.

LimitatIons of the use of Conventional Anthropometric Indices As Body Composition Measures and Metabolic Risk Markers and the Clinical Significance of Skeletal Muscle Evaluation
Conventional anthropometric indices such as body mass index (BMI) and body fat percentage remain widely used in both clinical and research settings; however, they have the fundamental limitation that they fail to capture the qualitative aspects of body composition. For example, individuals with high muscle mass, such as athletes or manual laborers, may be misclassified as having obesity on the basis of their BMIs, and obesity may go undetected in those with low muscle mass and high fat mass, including individuals with “normal-weight obesity” or “sarcopenic obesity”. Therefore, for individuals with similar BMIs, differences in fat distribution and muscle mass can result in very different health risks.
Similarly, waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) are widely used as surrogate markers of visceral adiposity; however, several limitations of their use have been noted. WC is not corrected for height, which can compromise its accuracy when comparing the adiposity of individuals with different body frames. Moreover, WHR often closely correlates with WC or BMI; therefore, it may not offer any advantage over these simpler measures. In South Asian populations, for instance, obesity often presents with a high level of visceral adiposity, despite a relatively low BMI. Riaz et al. emphasized that BMI-based definitions frequently fail to capture the metabolic risk of individuals of specific ethnicities, and therefore advocated the use of more context-sensitive indices. BMFR, which directly reflects the balance between muscle and fat mass, may serve as an ethnicity-independent and physiologically relevant surrogate for abnormal metabolism. In addition, waist and hip circumference measurements are susceptible to inter-observer variability and posture-related inconsistencies, which can compromise measurement reproducibility. The anatomical landmarks used for these assessments are often defined ambiguously, particularly in individuals with obesity or abdominal deformities, which further limits the clinical applicability of WHR in routine settings. Recent studies have shown that the waist-to-height ratio has a closer association with cardiovascular mortality than either WC or WHR, which suggests that indices reflecting visceral adiposity in a height-standardized manner may be more useful for risk assessment.
Importantly, although conventional anthropometric indices such as BMI, body fat percentage, WC, WHR, and waist-to-height ratio are convenient means of assessing obesity and fat distribution, they do not necessarily reflect the degree of insulin resistance. Specifically, these indices do not account for skeletal muscle mass, which plays a crucial role in insulin-stimulated glucose disposal. Skeletal muscle is the primary site of glucose uptake, and both the quantity and quality of muscle tissue have a profound effect on insulin responsiveness. By contrast, adipose tissue, especially when present in excess, secretes proinflammatory adipokines and free fatty acids that contribute to insulin resistance through mechanisms such as chronic low-grade inflammation and ectopic lipid accumulation. Therefore, from a physiological perspective, skeletal muscle and fat mass have opposing influences on insulin sensitivity, and thus the evaluation of the balance between muscle and fat mass provides insight into the underlying metabolic state of an individual. Because they do not account for this balance, conventional indices can miss differences in insulin resistance and disease risk. Specifically, they may underestimate risk in individuals with low muscle mass, such as those with sarcopenic obesity or frailty (who may be classified as being of normal weight, but have insulin resistance), or overestimate risk in muscular individuals such as athletes (who may be classified as having overweight or obesity, despite having normal insulin sensitivity). Thus, conventional measures lack the ability to differentiate between metabolically healthy and unhealthy phenotypes within the same anthropometric categories, which limits their utility as markers of insulin resistance.
As knowledge of the physiological mechanisms underlying insulin resistance and metabolic disease has grown, several ratio-based indices have been proposed to better reflect the balance between muscle and fat mass. These indices offer a more nuanced and physiologically grounded approach to the evaluation of metabolic health and the stratification of cardiometabolic risk. Oliver et al.. stated that the fat-free mass index, although widely used, conflates skeletal muscle with non-muscle components such as organ and connective tissue. They argued that the association between fat-free mass index and insulin resistance may be driven by the negative effects of adiposity, rather than the positive effects of muscle mass. By contrast, ratios such as muscle-to-fat mass offer more metabolically meaningful representations. The muscle-to-fat ratio referred to by these authors is obtained by dividing the fat-free mass index (FFMI) by the fat mass index (FMI), a theoretical construct based on height-adjusted values, whereas the BMFR is calculated from directly measured whole-body muscle and fat mass obtained using BIA. This concept forms the basis for muscle and fat-based indices, such as BMFR and FMR, which aim to combine these two variables as a single, readily interpretable measure. In the next section, we explore the clinical relevance of muscle and fat-based indices, focusing on their associations with various metabolic diseases.

Clinical Relevance of the Muscle-to-fat Ratio
In this review, the term “muscle and fat-based indices” refers to indices describing the balance between muscle and fat mass, which are collectively conceptualized as load–capacity indices (LCIs), and “body muscle-to-fat ratio (BMFR)” refers to the index defined as muscle mass divided by fat mass. Unless otherwise specified, BMFR is calculated using the percentages obtained using BIA; i.e., the muscle percentage (%) divided by the fat percentage (%). As described in the previous section, skeletal muscle plays a central role in insulin sensitivity, overall metabolic regulation, and metabolic health. As BIA-based body composition analyzers have become widely accessible in recent years, the quantification of skeletal muscle and fat mass has become a noninvasive, rapid, and convenient process. Accordingly, indices that incorporate muscle mass, which is important for insulin sensitivity, and fat mass, which contributes to insulin resistance, have been proposed, namely the BMFR and FMR. These indices are essentially reciprocal and theoretically equivalent; both reflect the balance between the muscle and fat composition of the body.
Although fat and muscle have long been studied in relation to metabolic diseases, the predictive ability of the multidimensional index LCI has only recently been recognized to be superior to any single body composition parameter. A prospective study using data from the UK Biobank showed that individuals with a poor LCI have a higher risk of developing T2DM. In addition, a large study of 66,829 individuals in Taiwan showed that a poor LCI is significantly associated with MetS, prediabetes, T2DM, and hypertension. However, the results demonstrated the clinical utility of using a muscle-and-fat-based index as a predictor of cardiometabolic risk. As outlined by Fearon et al., skeletal muscle loss is central to the definition and progression of cancer cachexia. Therefore, the early identification of sarcopenic phenotypes using quantitative muscle-and-fat-based indices, such as BMFR, may provide insight into both metabolic status and prognosis in broader clinical contexts. Additionally, other studies have demonstrated that LCI is a valid predictor or diagnostic marker of MetS. Numerous other studies have linked this ratio to lipid metabolism and cardiovascular disease. In a cross-sectional study performed in Korea, adults with a poor LCI had high coronary artery calcification scores, and this association remained significant even after adjustment for BMI and WC. Furthermore, LCI has been shown to be associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. In addition, a recent study showed that FMR is significantly associated with insulin resistance and cardiometabolic disorders in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus, suggesting the potential utility of this index for patients with various types of diabetes.
Beyond cardiometabolic disorders, several studies have shown the relevance of LCI to obesity-related conditions. For example, BMFR is considered to be a predictor of hepatic steatosis; it closely correlates with liver fat accumulation assessed using the liver-to-spleen area ratio on computed tomography, and is highly diagnostic for fatty liver disease. The ratio is also useful for predicting the risk of obstructive sleep apnea syndrome and significantly correlates with the respiratory disturbance index, particularly in patients with T2DM but no obesity, likely because muscle mass and lean body mass are related to airway patency during sleep. In terms of kidney function, a poor LCI is associated with a higher risk of poor estimated glomerular filtration rate in middle-aged men, which is partially mediated by hyperuricemia. This ratio has also been linked to the onset of chronic kidney disease, suggesting its potential as an early screening tool. Furthermore, a poor LCI was found to be associated with a higher risk of chronic kidney disease development in healthy adults in a large-scale study. From a neuropsychiatric perspective, studies of older Asian adults have suggested that similar indices may predict the risk of sarcopenia-related dementia. However, it should be noted that LCI has not always been shown to be superior to conventional indices. In a cohort of obese individuals, BMI, rather than FMR, was found to be significantly associated with the severity of MetS. Similarly, in men, WC outperforms FMR for the prediction of MetS, and the association between FMR and hypertension is weaker in men than in women. These findings suggest that the predictive value of BMFR and FMR may vary according to sex, population, and outcome, implying that they should be interpreted with caution.

BMFR as an Index of Insulin Resistance
As noted above, insulin resistance is a core pathophysiological feature of MetS and a major contributor to T2DM and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. In this section, we evaluate the clinical utility of BMFR as a noninvasive and quantifiable marker of insulin resistance. BMFR is not merely a novel body composition index, it is a clinically meaningful metric for use in patients with metabolic dysfunction. The gold-standard method for the quantification of insulin resistance is the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp technique. The M value (mg·m−2·min−1) derived from this procedure represents the rate of glucose disposal under insulin-stimulated conditions. Lower M values indicate worse insulin resistance, and the M value is therefore widely used as a marker of the severity of insulin resistance. By contrast, the M/I ratio (mg·m−2·min−1·µU−1·mL), which is calculated by dividing the M value by the corresponding plasma insulin concentration, serves as a more precise and standardized index of insulin sensitivity, because it accounts for individual variations in circulating insulin concentrations.
In our study of treatment-naïve patients with T2DM, we found a close positive correlation between BMFR and the M/I ratio on univariate analysis (B = 0.806, P < 0.0001), suggesting that BMFR may be a useful marker of insulin resistance. This correlation was closer than those of the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (B = − 0.511, P < 0.0001), the Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (B = 0.751, P < 0.0001), and the fasting insulin concentration (B = − 0.476, P = 0.0001). BMFR also was associated with a higher Nagelkerke R² (0.533) than weight (0.406), BMI (0.506), or WC (0.512), which indicates a better model fit. On receiver operating curve analysis, the optimal BMFR cutoff value for the prediction of insulin resistance was 2.40, which was associated with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.87, a sensitivity of 91%, and a specificity of 76% (P < 0.001), consistent with BMFR being a useful predictor of insulin sensitivity in untreated patients with T2DM. Moreover, healthy women have more adipose mass than men and only two-thirds the amount of skeletal muscle mass, which means that body composition-based parameters used to predict insulin resistance need to be interpreted differently in women and men. However, sex-specific analyses revealed a close correlation between BMFR and the M/I ratio in both men (B = 0.77, P < 0.001) and women (B = 0.83, P < 0.001). On receiver operating curve analysis, sex-based cutoff values of 2.75 for men (AUC = 0.83, sensitivity 75%, specificity 76%) and 1.65 for women (AUC = 0.87, sensitivity 84%, specificity 81%, P < 0.001) were identified. These thresholds correspond to an M value of approximately 5.7 mg/kg/min under standard hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp conditions, which is widely used to define the presence of insulin resistance. Overall, these findings support the clinical relevance of BMFR as a robust surrogate marker of insulin resistance. However, the utility of several other muscle and fat-based indices, and particularly FMR, have been investigated. In the next section, we compare the utility of BMFR, FMR, and other body composition metrics, focusing on their respective associations with insulin sensitivity and their interpretability in clinical settings.

Comparison of BMFR and FMR: Associations with the M/I Ratio, Clinical interpretability, and Practical Utility
For the evaluation of insulin resistance, it is important to confirm linear relationships between variables to optimize the predictive accuracy of indices and their responsiveness to therapeutic interventions. Specifically, if body composition indices demonstrate linear correlations with insulin sensitivity, they can be more readily used for clinical management and patient education. In our study of 118 treatment-naïve patients with T2DM, hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamps were performed to obtain the M/I ratio. Figure 1 shows scatter plots of the relationship of the M/I ratio with BMFR and FMR. BMFR showed a close positive linear correlation with M/I (B = 0.756, P < 0.001, R² = 0.572), and FMR also significantly correlated (B = − 0.601, P < 0.001, R² = 0.362), but demonstrated lower explanatory power. Analysis using the mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) also favored the use of BMFR (MAE = 3.19, RMSE = 4.14) over FMR (MAE = 3.97, RMSE = 5.05). Moreover, the absolute value of the regression coefficient for BMFR was higher than that for FMR (P = 0.027), supporting a closer association with M/I. Moreover, the goodness-of-fit for a reciprocal curve of fat percentage was better than that for a linear approximation, and the reciprocal of fat percentage and the muscle-to-body weight ratio both positively correlated with M/I. Thus, BMFR showed a more linear association with M/I than FMR.

In addition, FMR has conceptual limitations compared with BMFR. Although BMFR and FMR are mathematical reciprocals and both reflect the balance between muscle and fat mass, differences in their statistical and physiological characteristics result in distinct patterns of association with insulin resistance. FMR, which is calculated as fat mass divided by muscle mass, tends to produce disproportionately high values when muscle mass is low, leading to a wider and more skewed distribution. This may contribute to a weaker linear relationship with measures of insulin sensitivity. By contrast, BMFR, calculated as muscle mass divided by fat mass, is a more stable index, because variations in muscle mass generally occur within a narrower range than fat mass in most populations. Because BMFR reflects the functional insulin-sensitive component (muscle) per unit of tissue associated with insulin resistance (fat), and skeletal muscle plays a central role in glucose metabolism, BMFR may better reflect the abnormal metabolism associated with insulin resistance. This characteristic, along with its narrower numerical range, likely contributes to the more linear association of BMFR with M/I, and potentially renders it a more interpretable index for use in the clinic. For example, BMFR emphasizes muscle preservation or enhancement, and increases in BMFR reflect either muscle gain or fat loss, both of which are favorable and easy for patients to understand and target through lifestyle changes. Thus, BMFR is not only statistically robust but also readily interpretable in a clinical context. Nonetheless, previous studies have highlighted the utility of FMR as a potential index of insulin resistance and cardiometabolic risk. For example, Seo et al. found that FMR was significantly associated with the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance and MetS in a large Korean cohort (n = 13,032), demonstrating its value for the identification of metabolically unhealthy individuals across various BMI categories. Moreover, FMR remains widely used in epidemiological research, and associations have been demonstrated of FMR with metabolic or cardiovascular diseases and mortality. Therefore, FMR should be regarded as complementary to BMFR. Therefore, the choice of whether to use BMFR or FMR should depend on the study design and population characteristics, and FMR may be a preferable measure in certain scenarios. For example, in sarcopenic populations, in which a loss of muscle mass is the primary concern, FMR may serve as a sensitive indicator of low muscle mass, particularly for the early detection of sarcopenia. However, in patients with sarcopenic obesity, which is characterized by the coexistence of low muscle mass and high fat mass, both components should be assessed. In this instance, BMFR and FMR, which directly reflect the balance between muscle and fat, may offer a more practical and scalable screening tool. In addition, for individuals with severe obesity, in whom fat mass predominates, FMR may better reflect the absolute burden of adiposity. Therefore, the choice between BMFR and FMR should be guided by the study objectives and the characteristics of the population under investigation.
Overall, the evidence collected to date, including from our previously published dataset, suggests that BMFR has a more linear association with clamp-derived insulin sensitivity than FMR, but that FMR is valuable in a number of epidemiological settings, and therefore index selection should be context-specific. Although both indices provide valuable information, BMFR offers advantages in terms of its stability, interpretability, and predictive accuracy. Future Perspectives
Future research should focus on validating the use of BMFR in large-scale, prospective cohort studies, to assess its value as a predictor of disease onset, cardiovascular events, and mortality. Although longitudinal studies have yet to be conducted, the physiologically relevant basis of BMFR, which reflects both muscle and fat mass, raises the possibility that it may be useful to assess the response to interventions such as exercise, diet, and pharmacotherapy. This hypothesis-generating potential warrants its evaluation in interventional studies. Special attention should also be paid to subgroups such as older adults, women, and individuals with sarcopenia or obesity, for whom the optimal cutoff values may differ. In this context, osteosarcopenic obesity and sex-specific differences in body composition have recently been shown to substantially contribute to frailty and metabolic vulnerability. A 2024 review underscored the relevance of the interplay between fat and muscle in the susceptibility to frailty syndrome, and suggested that indices such as BMFR may be clinically useful in this context.
In addition, glucose-lowering agents with proven cardiometabolic benefits, such as sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, may induce favorable shifts in body composition. Evaluating how these therapies influence BMFR could help determine whether improvements in BMFR are linked to an improvement in prognosis. If so, BMFR may serve as a biomarker of the response to treatment and assist with therapeutic decision-making, which could facilitate a more personalized approach to care.
With the increasing availability of smart BIA-enabled devices, including consumer-grade scales and wearable technologies, BMFR could be integrated into remote health monitoring platforms. This development may allow patients and clinicians to track changes in BMFR over time and enable the early detection of metabolic deterioration and more personalized, real-time intervention strategies.
Recently, poor muscle quality (MQ) has gained attention as a hidden but clinically important condition in obesity. Vieira et al. emphasized that although MQ is closely associated with adverse metabolic outcomes, its assessment remains heterogeneous and is often impractical in routine clinical settings. In this context, BMFR, a simple ratio derived from BIA data, may represent a feasible surrogate for MQ, particularly for use in metabolic risk stratification. In the future, studies should explore whether BMFR represents a practical LCI that reflects impaired muscle function and fat overload.
Most of the current evidence regarding BMFR was obtained in populations with T2DM. To support its broader clinical adoption, future research should establish normal reference ranges and clarify the utility of BMFR for use with healthy individuals and other patient populations. Ultimately, after accumulating further evidence, BMFR could become a new standard index for metabolic assessment, complementing or even superseding conventional metrics, and may contribute substantially to the use of precision medicine in the management of metabolic disorders. Limitations
We must also acknowledge several methodological limitations of these indices. BMFR and FMR are typically derived from BIA data, which are subject to several sources of variability, including device-specific algorithms, hydration status, and ethnic differences in body composition. These factors may affect the reproducibility and cross-population comparability of the indices, and should be carefully considered when interpreting or generalizing findings associated with BIA-derived indices. In addition, much of the current evidence was collected through cross-sectional or observational studies, which cannot ascribe causality. Large-scale longitudinal studies and intervention trials are still lacking, and further research is needed to validate the prognostic utility of BMFR and FMR in diverse populations. Finally, although in this review we have attempted to comprehensively summarize the available evidence, the possibility of publication bias cannot be excluded, and negative or null findings may be underrepresented in the literature.

Conclusion
BMFR has emerged as a promising index for use in the evaluation of metabolic health, because it offers superior physiological interpretability and practical applicability to conventional indices such as BMI or body fat percentage. In multiple clinical contexts, BMFR demonstrates consistent associations with insulin resistance and other obesity-related conditions. As body composition analysis techniques advance and become more widely accessible, BMFR may become both a useful risk stratification tool and a marker for the monitoring of treatment responses. With further validation in diverse populations and integration into clinical workflows and digital health platforms, BMFR has the potential to become a new standard method of assessing metabolic risk, screening for obesity-related conditions, and guiding personalized interventions. The clinical utility of BMFR positions it as a promising metric for use in the routine care of patients with metabolic disease and precision medicine in diverse populations.
