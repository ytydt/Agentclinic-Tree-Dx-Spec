Research progress on immune tolerance mechanisms in liver metastatic tumors and the “Liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism therapeutic strategy” approach
Abstract
Liver metastases pose a serious challenge in the field of systemic cancer treatment, as this organ has a particular microenvironment that favours malignant cells disseminating to settle there. We outline major steps of liver immune tolerance in metastasis including pre-metastatic niche formation, immune evasion during circulation, establishment of an intrahepatic immune desert and metabolism, myeloid cell networks and gut microbiome-mediated coordinated tolerance. We then combine new combination and integrative therapies that are intended to break this tolerance; these include immunochemotherapy regimens, synergistic antiangiogenics and immunotherapies, dual immune checkpoint blockade and myeloid-cell reprogramming, combined locoregional and systemic therapies and new microenvironmental targeting. Each strategy is assessed with regard to its potential disruption of hepatic immune quiescence, improved clinical translation and durable antitumour activity. We suggest a proposed solution termed Liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism therapeutic strategy, which may target multiple metastatic bottlenecks due to similarities. This framework provides a basis for personalizing combination therapies and designing future clinical trials for treating liver metastases, with organ-specific considerations and will be the subject of a commentary.

Introduction
The liver is a common site for metastasis of many primary tumors. About 5.14 % of cancer patients present with liver metastasis at diagnosis. Most commonly, liver metastasis is noted in gastrointestinal tumors, ]. The process of metastasis is not that easy. It requires various coordination steps like adhering and getting out through the hepatic sinusoids. After that, there is adaptation to the pre-metastatic niche and subsequent colonization and outgrowth. Finally, metabolic escape takes place. The last step to exploiting the liver’s immune tolerance takes place. All these steps are interlinked to cause immune blockade and impaired drug penetration, thereby leading to inefficient drug treatment, ]. The immune systems fail to identify tumor cells which enable the mutant cells to multiply more. As a result, this has become an important goal in the treatment of liver‐metastatic cancers; that is, overcoming hepatic immune tolerance so that the immune system can recognize and destroy tumor cells. Recently, emerging therapies, including Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell (CAR-T) cell therapy, multitargeted agents, and modulation of the gut microbiome plus traditional modalities, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, have created a comprehensive “encirclement” therapeutic paradigm. This combination, which has been shown to significantly improve remission and survival rates in patients with hepatic metastases, has become the prevailing clinical approach. In this article, we investigate the immunological basis of liver metastasis and its potential impact on the treatment strategies. Building on these insights, we propose a liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism therapeutic framework that targets convergent immunological and microenvironmental vulnerabilities across distinct primary tumors metastasizing to the liver, while complementing rather than replacing established primary tumor-specific treatment paradigms. The selection and sequencing of immunotherapy-based combinations should remain anchored in the biological features, molecular subtypes, and guideline-recommended treatment backbones of each primary tumor, with liver-specific immune modulation serving as an integrative rather than standalone component. Finally, we systematically delineate how this novel approach targets the myriad of mechanisms and immune‐tolerant features of liver metastases. We provide a review that can help clinicians dealing with metastatic tumors of the liver both theoretically and practically.

The treatment of liver metastases depends on various factors, such as the histology of the primary tumor, tumor load, stage of disease, and the immune microenvironment of the liver. Chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy are among the treatment options, but the effectiveness of these treatment options is often limited by the liver’s special immunologic tolerance and complex microenvironment, ]. Oncological therapy is moving towards multicomponent, multimodal combinations and is the current paradigm. The main reason why this very approach is most effective is to counteract the major hurdles of immune escape and acquired resistance. At present, for metastatic tumors from different sources, individualized treatment strategies combining multidisciplinary treatment methods are generally used to improve efficacy and prolong patient survival. In the treatment of metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), patients with RAS/BRAF-mutated cancer should be treated with cytotoxic chemotherapy (FOLFOX or FOLFIRI) in combination with the anti-angiogenics antibody bevacizumab or immune checkpoint inhibitor nivolumab. For the specific cohort of patients harboring the BRAF V600E mutation, the therapeutic regimen is augmented with the targeted agent doublet of encorafenib and cetuximab. Mechanisms of immune tolerance in hepatic metastases

Formation of the pre-metastatic niche (PMN)
The development of hepatic metastases is not solely reliant on the intrinsic invasive and metastatic capabilities of tumor cells. It is also critically dependent on the pre-conditioning of the hepatic microenvironment by the primary tumor through the secretion of various soluble factors and extracellular vesicles (EVs). This process establishes a favorable milieu for subsequent metastatic colonization and serves as a crucial prelude to successful tumor cell dissemination, ]. Key effectors in this systemic crosstalk include tumor cell–derived cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), as well as specific microRNAs (miRNAs) encapsulated within tumor-derived EVs, which collectively converge to activate indigenous hepatic cell populations like hepatocytes, hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), and Kupffer cells (KCs). For instance, IL-6-induced secretion of serum amyloid A (SAA) from hepatocytes mobilizes myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), thereby augmenting hepatic susceptibility to metastatic seeding. TGF-β also instills HSCs with a pro-fibrotic phenotype, thereby forming a supportive matrix that promotes tumor cell engraftment and growth. The delivery of miRNAs through the EV causes further enhancement of the molecular reprogramming. It is mainly noted with miR-934 and miR-203a-3p, which could trigger the conversion of macrophages to an immunosuppressive M2 polarity. The process takes place through downmodulation of PTEN and activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. It solidifies the pro-metastatic niche, ,, (Figure 1). Importantly, the creation of this PMN is a conserved, pan-cancer process that creates a ‘soil’ in the liver irrespective of the histology of the primary tumor. As a result, PMN formation may be a promising tumor-agnostic strategy to prevent the formation of hepatic metastases.

Immunotolerant milieu and molecular mechanisms of hepatic metastases
The immune tolerant environment inside a hepatic metastatic lesion is a complex, self-sustaining state that relies on the liver’s tolerogenic nature. The intrahepatic changes happen indirectly due to the primary tumor’s signaling and the restructuring of the cellular and stromal networks inside the liver. Effector immune systems show a sustained hypo-responsiveness in this environment due to particular molecular programs. It can greatly influence the effectiveness and duration of liver system therapies. Five interconnected key mechanisms underpin the environment.

The cascade of immune tolerance along the “extravasation-lymph node-circulation” continuum
A primary tumor drives a cascade of immune tolerance first by establishing a tolerogenic base in regional and distant sites (e.g., PMN and tumor-draining lymph nodes [TDLNs]) through soluble factors and extravesicular soluble. The tumor cells that go into lymphatic and circulatory systems get marked with immune hypo-responsiveness by this process. When draining lymph nodes get colonized, tumor antigens will get presented suboptimally due to immature or inhibitory antigen presentation and the lack of co-stimulation. As a result, the peripheral T cells become hypo-responsive to these antigens as they become skewed towards a progenitor-exhausted lineage or are made dominant by regulatory cells. This inhibitory program then propagates throughout the organism by way of cell migration, soluble factors, and vesicular signaling to establish a global immunosuppression,,. During the circulatory phase, circulating tumor cells (CTCs) uses different strategies to stay disguised and survive. Techniques such as platelet cloaking appeared, as well as reversible chromatin and metabolic reprogramming. The CTCs inhibit multiple ligand-receptor pathways targeting different immune cells. For example, the PD-L1/PD-1 axis and the CD47/SIRPα axis. Then, they finish the extravasation as single cells or micro-clusters. When CTCs reach the hepatic sinusoids, they quickly change the local microenvironment to a more stable, less reversible state of immune hypo-responsiveness. Formation of the “immune desert”
Hepatic metastases first trap peripheral tumor-specific CD8+ T cells in the hepatic sinusoids to form an “immune desert”. Integrin-mediated adhesion is responsible for this. Activated T cells regulate LFA-1 and CD44 which bind to ICAM-1 and hyaluronic acid in the liver, respectively. These T cells meet with expanded population of tumor-induced monocyte-derived macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+), locally. These macrophages can present antigens to other immune cells. They also express a lot of FasL. This kills CD8+ T cells. The localized deletion of T cells leads to T cell loss throughout the system and failure of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. Systemic immune desert or isolate causes reduced peripheral lymphocyte counts, decreased T cell clonal diversity and abundance in extrahepatic lesions and impaired effector functions .
Single-cell analyses offer further evidence on this front. Hepatic metastases are associated with a reduced fraction of conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) and dampened antigen presentation pathways. TGF-β signaling inhibits cytotoxic NK cells and the suppression of both innate and adaptive immunity which further diminishes the infiltration and killing capacity of immune cells,,,,. At a metabolic level, tumor and macrophages utilize lipid uptake through CD36 to activate inhibitory macrophages and stabilize the immunosuppressive niche promote metastatic progression. At the same time, Treg cells expand in liver metastasis, thereby regulating systemic immune-response, and modifying the immunotherapeutic response, providing durable suppression, ]. Moreover, hepatic stellate cells are activated by a Netrin-1-retinoic acid-ELF3 feed-forward signaling loop, which causes them to release pro-fibrotic factors that increase matrix density and stiffness in collaboration with tumor cells. It establishes a physical blockage to the delivery of T cells and drugs, and enhances the environment with immunosuppressive mediators,,, These overlapping processes reinforced by soluble inhibitory signals and metabolic reprogramming renders hepatic metastatic lesion into an immunologically desertified lesion. This multi-factorial entity can potentially manifest a strong influence on systemic anti-tumour immunity (Figure 3).

Metabolism and immune tolerance in hepatic metastases
Tumor cells create an unfavorable metabolic environment by upregulating glycolysis and lactate export, creating local hypoxia and acidosis. The depletion of glucose inhibits TCR signaling and the expression of cytotoxic molecules in T cells, ]. While also impairing the functions of dendritic cells and natural killer cells,,. Amino acid depletion, such as through degradation of l-arginine via ARG1, also drives T cells hypo-responsiveness. Through increase in oxidized lipids uptake by CD36, lipid reprogramming causes oxidative stress and loss of effector function, while helping Tregs and MDSC recruitment and homeostasis, ,,,. These three metabolic pathways act synergistically to create an acidic, hypoxic, and nutrient-competing intrahepatic microenvironment that directly undermines therapeutic efficacy through the systemic therapeutic approaches, specifically within the liver (Figure 4). Accumulating evidence indicates that lactate functions as a pivotal metabolic hub that drives “immune exclusion” and mediates low sensitivity to ICIs in metastatic lesions. Lactate not only acidifies the intrahepatic microenvironment and inhibits CD8+ T cell effector functions but also epigenetically reinforces immunosuppression and M2-like macrophage polarization through histone lactylation. In the context of liver metastases, this intrahepatic metabolic suppression often occurs in parallel with the “immune desert” phenotype making the immune ecosystem highly sensitive to metabolic pressure .
Three pathways through which tumor metabolic reprogramming shapes an immunosuppressive intrahepatic microenvironment.

Macrophage-associated immune tolerance
Metabolic signals from tumors and microbes drive macrophages to acquire an inhibitory phenotype, which reinforces T cell exclusion and exhaustion, ]. In colorectal liver metastases (CRLM), intratumoral bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli) can enhance lactate flux, triggering RIG-I lactylation in macrophages. This, in turn, inhibits the transcription of NF-κB target genes, promotes M2-like polarization, and amplifies Treg signaling, thus forming a metabolic-innate-adaptive immune tolerance cascade. Furthermore, high expression of NLRP6 in macrophages within the liver metastatic microenvironment promotes the accumulation of monocyte-derived myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) in both the pre-metastatic niche and established metastatic sites via the upregulation of CCL2. This suppresses CD8+T cell infiltration and facilitates CRLM progression through the pro-proliferative and pro-migratory effects of M-MDSCs, constituting a tolerogenic pathway that runs parallel to and supports the macrophage network, ,,. Additionally, a specific population of SPP1+ macrophages in liver metastases can form a stable inhibitory interaction network with tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells. This network drives T cell dysfunction and is associated with the generation of a fibrotic stroma  suggesting that the “SPP1+ macrophage-exhausted T cell-fibrosis” axis represents a critical node in immune tolerance (Figure 5).

Gut microbiota-mediated regulation of the hepatic immune microenvironment
The immune status of the liver is profoundly regulated by the gut microbiome. Via the portal vein, the liver receives the majority of the intestine’s blood supply, leading to its continuous exposure to microbial components and their metabolites. A dysbiotic gut microbiota can impair intestinal barrier function, allowing an increased influx of bacterial products into the portal circulation. This perpetually activates Kupffer cells (KCs) and other resident immune cells in the liver, fostering a chronic, pro-tumorigenic inflammatory environment, ]. Studies in both colorectal cancer (CRC) patients and experimental models have demonstrated that intratumoral E. coli can disrupt the gut-vascular barrier (GVB) in a manner dependent on the virulence regulator VirF. This facilitates bacterial dissemination along the portal vein into the liver, where it induces a pre-metastatic niche. In clinical cohorts, elevated levels of PV-1, a marker of GVB impairment in the primary tumor, are significantly associated with distant recurrence, hepatic metastasis, and the detection of bacteria within CRC liver lesions, ].

Breaking hepatic immune tolerance to restore systemic anti-tumor immunity
Disruption of hepatic immune tolerance has implications that extend beyond local disease control and directly influences systemic anti-tumor immunity. The liver represents a dominant site of immune regulation, and liver metastases function as a major sink for tumor-reactive immune cells. Consequently, effective dismantling of intrahepatic immune suppression can relieve liver-mediated immune constraints at the organismal level.
Therapeutic strategies that remodel the hepatic immune microenvironment-including immunotherapy-based combinations, vascular normalization, induction of immunogenic cell death, and locoregional interventions-an restore antigen presentation and prevent ongoing deletion or dysfunction of tumor-reactive T cells within the liver. By interrupting these liver-centered inhibitory circuits, peripheral T-cell pools and clonal diversity can be preserved or re-expanded, enabling renewed immune surveillance at extrahepatic tumor sites .
Importantly, this liver-focused immune reprogramming provides a mechanistic basis for the observed enhancement of systemic treatment efficacy. Clinical improvements in extrahepatic responses and overall survival following effective control of liver metastases suggest that breaking hepatic immune tolerance converts the liver from a site of immune suppression into a platform that permits durable systemic immune activation, ]. Thus, targeting hepatic immune tolerance should be viewed as a prerequisite for achieving robust and long-lasting systemic anti-tumor immunity in patients with liver metastases.

Liver metastases function as an immune “sink” that sequesters and exhausts tumor-reactive T cells, and relieving this liver-centered suppression facilitates the restoration of systemic T-cell circulation and immune surveillance. Liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism strategies to dismantle hepatic immune tolerancee
The concept of “Liver-Metastasis-Oriented Shared-Mechanism Strategies to Dismantle Hepatic Immune Tolerance” is based on similarities in the underlying mechanisms of different disease conditions. The idea is to achieve systemic targeting at multiple common pathogenic links using a combination of multi-nodal therapies combining systemic and local and throughout the disease. The goal of therapy is to reconstruction the immunosuppressive ecosystem from ‘immune exclusion, functional anergy and transient response’ to ‘immune infiltration, robust activation and durable cytotoxicity.’ Transformation would augment intrahepatic therapeutic efficacy and clinical response rates and promote patient survival. Importantly, the deployment of liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism strategies should be guided by the dominant immunological and microenvironmental features of the liver metastatic niche (LMN) in individual patients. Based on the mechanisms discussed in Section 3, liver metastases can be pragmatically stratified according to the prevailing suppressive axis, such as myeloid suppression–dominant, fibrotic/metabolically adverse, or vascular abnormality/immune desert phenotypes (Table 3). This prototype-based stratification does not introduce new therapeutic entities but serves as a decision framework to prioritize and sequence the strategies detailed below.

Dismantling immune tolerance through poly-pharmacotherapy
The principle underlying the strategy of “Treating Different Diseases with the Same Method” is to combine immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) with non-specific drugs that can fundamentally remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Given the risks of cumulative toxicities and potential impairment of liver function associated with combination therapies, the management of patients with liver metastases should prioritize individualized dose adjustment, rigorous toxicity monitoring, and rational sequencing of therapeutic agents. For example, cytotoxic chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic therapy, or locoregional interventions may be administered upfront to achieve tumor debulking, vascular normalization, or antigen release, followed by immunotherapy to consolidate and sustain antitumor immune responses, rather than simple concurrent administration, thereby achieving an optimal balance between therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Synergizing icis with anti-angiogenic agents/tkis
When they are applied in a low to moderate (and within a therapeutic range) manner, the anti-VEGF/VEGFR agents (including multi-targeted TKIs) won’t stop the tumor only but will also partially normalize the tumor vasculature abnormalities and leaking. As a result, interstitial fluid pressure is lowered, while perfusion and oxygenation have been improved. This is associated with the upregulation of endothelial adhesion molecules and CXCL9 gradients and the downregulation of hypoxia and adenosine signaling pathways. As a result, the process promotes CD8 T cells’ trans-endothelial migration and intratumoral retention, thereby optimizing the therapeutic efficiency of immune checkpoint inhibitors,,,. According to clinical studies, in NSCLC patients with baseline hepatic metastases, immunotherapy plus anti-VEGF therapy plus platinum-based chemotherapy yielded a better median Overall Survival (OS) than anti-VEGF therapy plus chemotherapy, about 13.2 vs. 9.1 months (HR for death, about 0.68). This evidence suggests that “vascular-immune normalization” can partially counteract immune hypo-responsiveness in the context of liver metastases, translating into a significant survival benefit.

Synergizing icis with immunogenic cell death (ICD)-Inducing hemotherapy
Certain chemotherapeutic agents, such as oxaliplatin, are capable of inducing CD, thereby exposing a multitude of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). This process leads to calreticulin (CALR) translocation to the cell surface, extracellular release of ATP, and secretion of HMGB1, which collectively facilitate dendritic cell (DC) antigen uptake and maturation, and enhance cross-presentation and T-cell priming. Subsequently, PD-1/PD-L1 blockade releases T cell inhibition at the effector stage, creating a complementary mechanism that improves the anti-tumor response efficiency in immunologically hypo-responsive lesions,,,. This principle has been robustly validated in clinical trials. In metastatic colorectal cancer, the integration of a PD-L1 inhibitor into the FOLFOXIRI plus bevacizumab backbone led to a significant extension in Progression-Free Survival (PFS) (13.1 vs. 11.5 months, HR 0.69). Furthermore, a combination of a PD-1 inhibitor, bevacizumab, and CAPOX chemotherapy in patients with MSS and RAS-mutated tumors achieved an Overall Response Rate (ORR) of 84 % and a median PFS of 18.2 months, with observed signals of a “cold-to-hot” conversion in the microenvironment. This provides further evidence for the biological rationale and clinical translatability of the “ICD induction plus immune blockade” strategy. In primary liver cancer, these regimens primarily aim to alleviate immune exhaustion arising from chronic inflammatory and fibrotic liver disease, whereas in liver metastases they are mainly deployed to reprogram a tumor-induced hepatic immune tolerance that is externally imposed by metastatic seeding.

Dual immune checkpoint blockade plus anti-angiogenic therapy and chemotherapy
Dual blockade of PD-1 and CTLA-4 acts concurrently on the priming, clonal expansion, and effector phases of the T cell response, ]. Within a low-to-moderate dose range, anti-VEGF/VEGFR agents can transiently “normalize” the vasculature, thereby improving perfusion and oxygenation, reducing adenosine- and hypoxia-related inhibitory signals, decreasing the recruitment of Tregs and MDSCs, and enhancing drug delivery and T cell infiltration, ]. Concurrently, chemotherapy induces immunogenic cell death, which boosts antigen presentation and dendritic cell maturation, ]. These three modalities together exert a multi-pronged attack upon the “immune exclusion/exhaustion, metabolic suppression, and myeloid dominance, ” all common intrahepatic challenges, thereby improving the efficacy and durability of immunotherapy in the liver. This concept is supported by emerging clinical data. According to the results of the Phase III CheckMate-9LA trial (JTO 2023), nivolumab plus ipilimumab plus 2 cycles of chemotherapy statistically improved OS vs. chemotherapy alone in patients with liver metastases (10.2 vs. 8.2 months; HR 0.84, 95 % CI: 0.60–1.18). Nivolumab plus ipilimumab plus chemotherapy also statistically prolonged OS in the overall trial population (15.8 vs. 11.0 months; HR 0.74, 95 % CI: 0.62–0.87).

Myeloid reprogramming in combination with ICIs
The aim of this therapy is to act on the mechanistic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) by blocking recruitment and immunosuppressive functions of MDSCs and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) whilst making them pro-inflammatory. This mechanism efficiently transforms the intrahepatic inhibiting network that consists largely of TAMs/M-MDSCs into a T-cell-infiltrating and antigen presenting Type I interferon (IFN) signaling ecosystem, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and T-cell infiltration, ]. Subsequent integration with PD-1/PD-L1 (or CTLA-4) inhibitors then potentiates the immune response to immunotherapy, thus optimizing the efficiency of both the priming and effector phases, ], ]. More recent evidences indicate that activation or reprogramming of liver-resident macrophages (Kupffer cells) may reduce hepatic metastatic burden and restore sensitivity to PD-1 blockade. In hepatocellular carcinoma, myeloid-targeted strategies mainly counteract immune exhaustion within a chronically inflamed liver background, whereas in liver metastases they are designed to dismantle a myeloid-dominant, metastasis-induced immune desert characterized by monocyte-derived macrophages and M-MDSCs that actively delete tumor-reactive T cells.

Synergistic integration of iocoregional and systemic therapies
Locoregional therapies including hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy/perfusion (HAIC/HAIP), selective internal radiation therapy (Y-90 TARE), thermal ablation, and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) can induce an immunogenic cell death (ICD). This process is the release of danger signals ATP and HMGB1 and the surface exposure of Calreticulin (CALR), which promotes dendritic cell uptake and cross-presentation of antigens, ,,. Simultaneously, anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Receptor (VEGFR) blockade reduces aberrant angiogenesis, myeloid-derived suppressor cells infiltration, and boosts the chances of CD8+T-cell infiltration in the tumor. When these regional strategies come together with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which correct the functional exhaustion of effector T cells, there is a synergistic effect. This concerted effort changes the tumor immune microenvironment from a cold state to a hot state, thus raising conversion rates and durability of systemic therapy, ]. An experiment showed that second-line chemotherapy combined with Y-90 prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) as well as intra-hepatic PFS. While this benefit did not confer an overall survival (OS) benefit, it validated the complementarity between locoregional control and systemic treatment and provides a feasible integration point for the addition of immunotherapy or anti-angiogenic agents. A retrospective propensity score matching (PSM) study gave compelling evidence of benefit: in patients with curatively resectable or ablative liver metastases from breast cancer, the median OS for ablation plus systemic therapy vs. systemic therapy only was 65.5 vs. 40.4 months (HR: 0.48, p=0.003). In the subgroup with concurrent extrahepatic metastases, the OS was 46.4 months vs. 40.8 months (HR: 0.58, p=0.047). Even in the population deemed non-curatively ablative, the trend remained: 45.2 months vs. 29.0 months (HR: 0.67, p=0.084). Furthermore, a multi-center prospective study employing HAIC combined with intravenous 5-FU/LV and Cetuximab for colorectal liver metastases reported a median OS of 46.3 months (with intrahepatic PFS of 36.6 months, an overall response rate [ORR] of 88 %, and a secondary resection/ablation rate of 66 %). The toxicity profile was generally manageable. This finding further underscores the potential for enhanced outcomes on the dominant hepatic lesions and long-term survival when integrating regional with systemic treatments. While therapies such as TACE, TARE, or ablation constitute indispensable components of disease control along the natural history of hepatocellular carcinoma, in liver metastases these modalities also play an important role in modulating the immune microenvironment, aiming to enhance and prolong the efficacy of systemic therapies by reshaping the intrahepatic niche. The integration of locoregional and systemic therapies introduces additional challenges related to timing and safety. Locoregional interventions may function as immunogenic priming events, but inappropriate scheduling or overlapping toxicities can compromise hepatic reserve, underscoring the need for careful temporal coordination.

Novel therapeutics targeting the microenvironment
A progressive shift from invention to clinical application of revolutionary therapeutic technologies in recent years. Doctors use new and fresh waiting on agents. And by new, we mean never before seen agents. A defining feature of these new modalities is the variety of their mechanisms. Oncolytic viruses exemplify an effect of “oncolysis and immune activation”, bispecific antibodies are able to achieve a “dual blockade” and local treatments coupled with systemic treatments show “locoregional priming coupled with systemic responsiveness”. What unites these strategies and concepts is a multi-faceted attack on the tumor and its microenvironment. The philosophy takes on a similar view of “Liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism therapeutic strategy”. The philosophy predicts a future for liver metastasis treatment that will be a highly integrated and comprehensive therapeutic model, going beyond single-target therapies. Although these emerging modalities target common microenvironmental vulnerabilities within liver metastases, their clinical deployment must remain aligned with tumor-type–specific targets, indications, and prior lines of therapy defined by the primary cancer. Emerging modalities such as CAR-T, oncolytic viruses, and antibody–drug conjugates expand therapeutic possibilities but also raise concerns regarding off-target toxicity, drug–drug interactions, and patient selection. Their clinical deployment should therefore be guided by prior treatments, liver function status, and multidisciplinary evaluation.

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy
The creation of CAR-T cell therapies battling TME-specific antigens in the liver at least provides a breakthrough against the solid tumor’s high hurdle. A 2025 study claims CAR-T cells that target antigen Cadherin-17 (CDH17) can eradicate liver tumors of colorectal cancer without impacting normal liver cells. This antigen is highly expressed in liver metastases. This result unveils a completely new target for liver metastasis therapy. Additionally, because they secrete IL-15, “armored” CAR-T cells may also show excellent potential in a solid tumor’s immunosuppressive TME, ].

Oncolytic viruses (OVs)
OVs replicate better in malignant cells. They cause lysis in infected tumor cells. This results in the release of tumor-associated antigens together with DAMPs/PAMPs. These immunogenic effects significantly enhance dendritic cell cross-presentation. OVs also promote CD8 T lymphocyte infiltration and effector functions. Collectively, such mechanisms transform immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones and elicit robust systemic antitumor immunity, ,,,. In 2025, Phase I clinical data on VG161-an oncolytic virus for refractory hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) -revealed an objective response rate (ORR) of 17.7 % with a favorable safety profile. This outcome establishes a solid foundation for the therapeutic application of OVs in hepatic malignancies. Moreover, other investigational OVs, such as ONCR-177 carrying five immune-stimulatory genes, have demonstrated promising multi-mechanistic antitumor potential.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)
ADCs are heralded as “biological missiles” that leverage a specific antibody to deliver highly potent cytotoxic payloads directly to tumor cells expressing designated antigens as well as their surrounding microenvironment. Notably, a 2025 report in Nature Medicine highlighted the CEACAM5-targeted ADC Precemtabart tocentecan (Precem-TcT), which demonstrated preliminary antitumor activity (with a blinded ORR of approximately 8 %) and a manageable safety profile in a Phase I dose-escalation study involving patients with mCRC refractory to multiple prior therapies including irinotecan. With ongoing development of ADCs targeting molecules within the tumor microenvironment-such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and TAMs -these therapies hold promise as precision tools for selectively eradicating pro-tumorigenic components within LMN.

Bispecific and trispecific antibodies
These antibodies possess the capability to simultaneously bind two or three distinct targets, thereby achieving synergistic enhancement of therapeutic efficacy. Through multivalent binding within a single molecule, bispecific and trispecific antibodies orchestrate effector cell recruitment and concurrent blockade of multiple signaling pathways within the tumor microenvironment. This combinatorial mechanism amplifies cytotoxicity while mitigating immune escape, all underpinned by stringent target selectivity. A 2024 Phase I dose-escalation study demonstrated that the bispecific antibody ivonescimab targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) andVEGF achieved an ORR of 25.5 % and a disease control rate (DCR) of 63.8 % across various refractory solid tumors. At doses ≥3 mg/kg, peripheral blood receptor occupancy of PD-1 exceeded 80 %, and serum free VEGF levels decreased by 80 %–95 % within 24 h, indicating rapid dual-target suppression. This strategy, which concurrently intervenes in immunosuppression and angiogenesis through a single molecular entity, perfectly embodies the multi-target therapeutic paradigm of “Liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism therapeutic strategy”.
Despite their promising activity, these emerging therapeutic modalities may also encounter resistance mechanisms that are uniquely shaped by the hepatic microenvironment. In the liver, CAR-T cells can be sequestered or eliminated by Kupffer cells, exposed to persistent antigen stimulation leading to functional exhaustion, and physically restricted by fibrotic and metabolically adverse niches. The efficacy of antibody–drug conjugates may be limited by heterogeneous antigen expression, dense stromal barriers, and liver-associated drug clearance, while oncolytic viruses face rapid hepatic clearance and strong innate antiviral immunity that can attenuate viral persistence and immune priming, ]. Similarly, although bispecific and trispecific antibodies are designed to overcome single-pathway escape, their activity in liver metastases may still be constrained by baseline hepatic immunotolerance, myeloid dominance, and immune-desert phenotypes. Recognizing these liver-specific resistance mechanisms is essential for optimizing combination strategies and guiding future translational and clinical development.

Microbiome modulation
Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) represents a revolutionary approach to directly intervene in dysfunction of the gut-liver axis. Ample evidence increasingly supports that transplanting gut microbiota from healthy donors into immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-resistant cancer patients can reshape their intestinal microecology and restore sensitivity to immunotherapy. According to FMT, a first and broadly relevant sensitization strategy is modulating the gut microbiome to ‘sanitize’ immunoregulatory signals that enter the liver. The favorable microenvironmental condition created by this approach is favorable for all therapies aiming to activate hepatic antitumor immunity, especially ICIs.

Resistance considerations in the liver microenvironment
Despite their promising activity, emerging therapeutic modalities such as CAR-T cells, ADCs, oncolytic viruses, and bispecific or trispecific antibodies are also likely to encounter liver-specific resistance mechanisms. In the hepatic microenvironment, CAR-T cells may be rapidly sequestered or eliminated by Kupffer cells, exposed to persistent antigen stimulation leading to functional exhaustion, and physically restricted by a fibrotic and metabolically hostile niche, ], ]. For ADCs, heterogeneous antigen expression, impaired intratumoral penetration, and liver-associated drug clearance may limit effective payload delivery. Oncolytic viruses face additional barriers from innate antiviral immunity and rapid hepatic clearance, which can attenuate viral replication and immune priming, ]. Similarly, although bispecific and trispecific antibodies are designed to overcome single-pathway escape, their efficacy may still be constrained by the high baseline immunotolerance, myeloid dominance, and suppressive cytokine milieu characteristic of liver metastases. Awareness of these liver-specific resistance mechanisms is essential for the rational optimization and future clinical translation of these novel therapies.
Although certain metastatic processes, such as pre-metastatic niche formation, myeloid cell recruitment, and metabolic competition, are shared across metastases in different organs, the liver represents a uniquely tolerogenic environment due to its physiological role in immune homeostasis and continuous exposure to gut-derived antigens. Liver-specific features-including resident immune populations, sinusoidal architecture, metabolic zonation, and gut–liver axis signaling-create a microenvironment that is distinct from that of other metastatic sites and confers disproportionate systemic immunosuppressive effects. Therefore, while some mechanistic principles discussed here may be partially applicable to extrahepatic metastases, the proposed “one-pattern” therapeutic concept is specifically optimized for liver metastases rather than intended as a universally transferable strategy.

Practical challenges in multimodal combination strategies
While multimodal combination therapies provide a compelling framework for overcoming hepatic immune tolerance, their clinical implementation is associated with several practical challenges. Cumulative toxicities remain a major concern, particularly when immune checkpoint inhibitors are combined with chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic agents, or locoregional therapies in patients with limited hepatic reserve. Careful dose adjustment and close monitoring of liver function are therefore essential. In addition, the sequencing and timing of different modalities may critically influence both efficacy and safety. Increasing evidence supports a sequential strategy in which cytotoxic or locoregional therapies are applied first to achieve tumor debulking or immunogenic priming, followed by immunotherapy as consolidation or maintenance. Finally, potential drug–drug interactions, especially among targeted agents, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and immunotherapies, require multidisciplinary evaluation and individualized treatment planning. Addressing these challenges is crucial for translating biologically rational combination strategies into safe and effective clinical practice, ].

Outlook
It should be clarified that the discussion on preventing metastatic seeding by modulating the liver microenvironment is based on consistent observations across tumor-related clinical studies showing that measurable alterations of the hepatic microenvironment accompany both the development and treatment of liver metastases. These findings support the notion that microenvironmental reprogramming represents a prerequisite or enabling condition for metastatic colonization. Accordingly, the preventive implications proposed in this review are derived from mechanistic inference and clinical pattern recognition, rather than from trials explicitly designed to test metastasis prevention. At present, liver microenvironment modulation is clinically validated primarily as a therapeutic approach for established metastases, while its potential role in metastasis prevention remains a hypothesis-generating concept that warrants future investigation.
The principle of “Liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism therapeutic strategy” highlights the liver microenvironment as the central battlefield, from where we can distil consistently verifiable shared pathologies and actionable intervention leverage points in addition to the diverse primary tumors. As a result, treatment strategies move from stratification by tumor origin to the organizational strategies of “measurable intra-hepatic ecology”: first that the stratification is clear, and the entry points are being designed, and later that consolidation and maintenance will be done. Ultimately, a feedback-controlled early-warning system will be designed to prevent recurrence. This requires not just new drugs but an overall system upgrade involving conceptual framework, research design and endpoints configuration to produce measurable and reproducible improvement in managing liver metastasis.
To further enhance the translational impact of liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism strategies, a more structured roadmap is required. Future efforts should prioritize liver-specific preclinical models that better recapitulate hepatic immune tolerance, including orthotopic metastasis models and immune-competent organoid or ex vivo liver platforms. In parallel, integrated AI-driven diagnostic frameworks combining spatial pathology, radiomics, and multi-omics data may enable objective identification of dominant liver microenvironment prototypes, such as myeloid-dominant, fibrotic/metabolically adverse, or immune-desert phenotypes. These advances would support prospective clinical trial designs that stratify patients based on intrahepatic microenvironmental states rather than primary tumor origin alone, facilitating rational selection and sequencing of combination therapies.
To redefine liver metastasis is the first act of clinical practice of the future. It requires the identification of the dominant inhibitory features in the LMN in each patient; beyond classical histological classification and with the innovation of spatial omics and AI-based pathological analysis. Rather than reiterating specific technical approaches, this paradigm emphasizes that redefining liver metastasis requires identifying the dominant inhibitory features within the LMN of each patient, beyond classical histological classification. Tumors classified as “myeloid suppression-dominant” have a high number of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages. Tumors designated “fibrotic/metabolically adverse” are characterized by cancer-associated fibroblast activation and remodeling of the extracellular matrix. Tumors described as having a “vascular abnormality/immune desert” phenotype have disorganized vasculature and few effector T cells. This granular classification informs downstream treatment decisions. By adopting this approach, it enables matching optimal treatment strategies to patients bearing liver metastases with different tumor origins but similar microenvironmental prototypes, thus realizing the essence of “Liver-metastasis-oriented shared-mechanism therapeutic strategy” in an authentic manner.
Precision classification based on microenvironmental prototypes will elevate future therapies beyond empirical drug combinations to a biologically informed “therapeutic engineering” Central to this paradigm is the intentional deployment of each treatment modality tailored to accurately modulate LMN states, intervening at optimal windows to design temporally optimized, mechanistically complementary, and synergistic intervention sequences.
Based on the regulation of the LMN, therapeutic approaches are shifting focus from treating established macroscopic metastases to intercepting the metastatic cascade itself. This conceptual classification is intended to provide clinicians with an operational lens for selecting and sequencing liver-directed combination strategies, rather than redefining tumor entities. By dissecting key stages-from the pre-metastatic microenvironment, tumor cell-induced systemic immune tolerance in tumor-draining lymph nodes, to mechanisms allowing circulating tumor cells to evade immune surveillance in the bloodstream-we have opportunities to intervene prior to successful hepatic colonization by tumor cells.
