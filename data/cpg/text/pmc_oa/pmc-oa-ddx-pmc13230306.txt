History and evolution of the far-lateral approach in neurosurgery
The far-lateral approach (FLA) to the foramen magnum, lower clivus, and craniovertebral junction has become a cornerstone in accessing these regions. Historically, lesions arising at the ventral craniovertebral junction posed significant surgical challenges because of limited visualization, poor exposure or considerable brainstem retraction, and high complication rates with midline posterior and anterior transoral approaches. To overcome these limitations, early pioneers proposed the FLA, which extended the lateral suboccipital exposure and enabled controlled drilling of the occipital condyle, thereby offering a more direct, tangential route to anterior brainstem lesions. This approach has undergone technical refinements such as alternative skin incisions to improve closure, consistent resection of the posterior arch of C1, posterior fossa decompression with condyle and jugular tubercle drilling in select cases for expanded access, vertebral artery transposition, and endoscope-assisted visualization. These enhancements have improved exposure while minimizing brain retraction and preserving critical neurovascular structures. The FLA has also evolved into distinct anatomical variants—most notably the transcondylar, supracondylar, and paracondylar extensions—offering dynamic exposure tailored to the pathology and anatomical nuances of the case. Quantitative anatomical studies have further supported the concept that these variants function as modular exposure corridors that can be selected according to lesion location and direction of extension. Originally developed to access vertebral artery aneurysms, the FLA has since demonstrated utility across a spectrum of pathologies, including meningiomas, chordomas, schwannomas, and complex vascular malformations. Compared with traditional midline and transoral approaches, it offers superior access to ventrolateral lesions, with low rates of postoperative instability and complications in experienced hands. Today, the FLA is considered essential in skull base and spinal neurosurgery. Ongoing advancements, such as endoscopic augmentation, neuronavigation, and patient-specific preoperative modeling, continue to refine this technique. This review outlines the anatomical and technical evolution of the FLA and highlights landmark contributions that have shaped its role in contemporary neurosurgical practice.

Introduction
A far-lateral approach (FLA) to the skull base offers access to the craniovertebral junction (CVJ), including the lower clivus, foramen magnum, and upper cervical spine. The approach allows access to lesions that have historically been difficult to reach, with safe exposure of critical blood vessels, including the intradural vertebral artery (VA), the vertebrobasilar junction, and the proximal posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA). Access to lesions of the anteromedial or anterolateral brainstem can be achieved by continuing a lateral suboccipital approach more inferolaterally, drilling the foramen magnum posterolateral rim when necessary, and performing a hemilaminectomy at C1. The approach also enables access to ventral brainstem and upper cervical cord lesions using minimal brain and spinal cord retraction. Quantitative anatomical analyses have also reinforced the idea that these variants act as modular exposure corridors, allowing tailored selection based on lesion location and its direction of extension.
Artist’s illustration showing the exposure of the anterolateral brainstem, vertebral artery, basilar region, and the lower cranial nerves afforded by the far-lateral approach. © Department of Neurosurgery, University of Utah
The rationale for using a FLA is to enable a more anterior exposure at the region of the lower clivus and foramen magnum. This enables exposure of the anterolateral brainstem, VA, basilar region, and lower cranial nerves (Fig. 1). First developed for the management of VA aneurysms in the 1980 s, the FLA has become a workhorse for a variety of pathologies at the foramen magnum. Historically, it evolved, in part, to counter the limitations of the midline and anterior approaches. Iterative improvements in patient positioning, bone removal, and microsurgical technique have optimized its utility and mitigated patient morbidity. In this narrative review, we explore the historical context and technical evolution of the FLA from its original conceptualization to present-day use, exploring how and why major modifications were made. We review key developments and contributions within the field to demonstrate the evolution of the approach’s importance in addressing lesions of the CVJ.

Historical background

Early approaches to the CVJ
Before the introduction of the FLA, neurosurgeons used midline posterior or anterior approaches for lesions at the foramen magnum. For tumors such as foramen magnum meningiomas, the traditional dorsal (posterior) suboccipital craniectomy afforded only limited access to ventrally located lesions. To access anteriorly located disease, surgeons often retracted the brainstem or cerebellum significantly. More anterior lesions (i.e., ventral bony compression or clival tumors) necessitated the design of the transoral approach, which offered a direct approach to the CVJ.

Challenges with midline and anterior techniques
By the mid-twentieth century, surgeons observed high rates of morbidity with traditional approaches. Complete resection was frequently difficult, particularly with tumors that encased the VA or extended far laterally, and the necessary brain retraction or odontoid resection had considerable associated risks, including neural injury to the patient. Transoral approaches, although directly anterior, were largely limited to extradural decompression (e.g., odontoidectomy) because intradural pathology required access through the oropharynx leading to a high risk of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak and meningitis, difficulty removing lesions with lateral extension, and high rates of postoperative instability leading to fusion. Clearly, a new strategy was required for lesions ventrolateral to the brainstem—one that could spare neurological functionality and lower the likelihood of infection and instability.

The idea of approaching the CVJ from a side trajectory began to emerge in the 1970s. In 1972, Hammon and Kempe were among the earliest to describe a unilateral suboccipital approach to the vertebral and basilar artery aneurysm. This precursor to the FLA demonstrated that a posterolateral route could provide access to the vertebrobasilar junction without the drawbacks of transoral exposure, but the FLA was not formally described until the mid-1980s. The first detailed description of the FLA (lateral suboccipital) was published in 1986 by Heros (Fig. 2). Heros reported clipping aneurysms of the distal VAs and basilar trunk by extending the lateral suboccipital craniectomy more laterally to include drilling of the lateral rim of the foramen magnum. This crucial turning point demonstrated that lesions anterior to the brainstem could be accessed posteriorly without dangerous retraction.
At the same time, other surgeons were independently devising similar strategies. George and colleagues were among the earliest to formalize lateral exposure of the foramen magnum and VA, particularly through systematic extradural management of the V3 segment, which helped improve control of the periarterial venous plexus and laid the groundwork for safer VA mobilization in CVJ surgery. The introduction of extreme lateral approaches to intradural tumors began in the late 1980s when George in France and Sen and Sekhar in the United States published their approaches. In 1990, Sen and Sekhar described an “extreme lateral” approach for intradural tumors of the foramen magnum and upper cervical spine. They reported drilling part of the occipital condyle (OC) to access anteriorly located lesions, which was an important innovation to expand the operative corridor. Meanwhile, in Germany, Bertalanffy and Seeger were investigating a comparable dorsolateral transcondylar approach. In 1991, they shared their experience of a “dorsolateral suboccipital transcondylar approach to the lower clivus and anterior portion of the craniocervical junction,” which was essentially identical to the FLA. As a result, evaluations of the FLA to the CVJ began to surface in the literature from several centers in the early 1990s. A culmination of incremental improvements addressing shortcomings of previous approaches, the FLA allowed access from the side of the foramen magnum with removal of the obstructing bone (OC and jugular tubercle) to provide a more tangential view of the ventral brainstem without the need for further transoral exposure or excessive retraction.

Technical evolution
The FLA was refined over the subsequent decades. Surgeons gradually adjusted every component of the technique, including the skin incision, bone removal, patient positioning, and instrument usage, to enhance exposure and safety. In the next subsections, we discuss the important technical evolutionary steps and implications for surgical outcomes.

Incision modifications

Early incision techniques
The original FLAs often used relatively simple skin incisions. For instance, the 1991 description by Bertalanffy and Seeger employed a straight vertical paramedian incision approximately 2 cm medial to the mastoid, extending from just below the occipital bone to the upper cervical levels. This linear incision provided direct access to the suboccipital muscles and lateral occipital bone, but it could pose challenges for closure, especially after extensive deep dissection. Other early adopters, including Heros and Sen and Sekhar, favored variations of the standard hockey-stick or L-shaped incision common in lateral skull base surgery (starting behind the ear and curving down toward the neck).

More recent use of the FLA has emphasized the importance of tailoring the incision to the goals of surgery (Fig. 3). As experience with the far-lateral technique increased, surgeons became aware that the shape of the incision and technique for dissection of muscle could affect complications, including wound healing and CSF leakage. More complex incisions were gradually adopted to optimize exposure and facilitate layered closure over time. The use of a “reverse hockey-stick” incision became more common; this question mark–shaped opening started in the occipital region, sweeping behind the ear (around the mastoid), and then descending along the upper neck. This gentle course permits the reflection of a large musculocutaneous flap, providing extensive exposure and preserving an ample soft-tissue flap for closure.

The retroauricular C-shaped incision that curves from above the pinna, behind the mastoid, and down the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle is another variation. These curvilinear cuts reduce the tension on the wound compared with a straight incision and have been shown to lower the incidence of postoperative CSF leak by allowing a tighter closure of the deep muscle layers. In the 1990s, in a comment published with the description by Bertalanffy and Seeger, Sekhar and Sen went so far as to recommend a “ ∩ -shaped” incision on the grounds that it would allow for more favorable tissue layer interdigitation at closure. Most surgeons today opt for an incision that compromises between exposure and reconstruction, commonly a hockey-stick or C-shaped incision with a large muscle flap, rather than the small linear incision used in early cases. Over time, these changes to the dissection of the skin and suboccipital muscle have greatly improved surgical access and reduced wound-related complications. The more tailored incisions require muscle dissection to recognize the important bony and vascular anatomy in the region of the lateral cervical spine and suboccipital region (Fig. 4).

Craniectomy and craniotomy modifications

Expanding bony exposure
A key aspect in the evolution of the FLA is the tailored resection of bone to increase the size of the operative window. Surgeons initially performed a small suboccipital craniectomy to enlarge the foramen magnum on the operative side. For instance, the technique of Bertalanffy and Seeger included expanding the foramen magnum by 2–3 cm in the dorsolateral direction (not extending into the mastoid bone). This small expansion, along with C1 laminectomy, granted access to the lateral side of the dural sac. However, lesions located well anteriorly were still occluded by the residual OC and jugular tubercle.

Introduction of condylar resection
The most significant advancement in exposure was the addition of OC resection—a maneuver that differentiates the far-lateral (or “transcondylar”) approach from a conventional lateral suboccipital one. Pioneers such as Sekhar and Heros intuited that the posterolateral portion of the OC could be safely drilled to significantly enlarge the angle of attack to anterior foramen magnum lesions. In their 1990 report, Sen and Sekhar described the removal of approximately half of the OC to access intradural tumors located ventral to the brainstem. This approach, involving a condylar resection in conjunction with a segmental resection of the lateral C1 arch or lateral mass of C1, enables the surgeon to see around a corner around the brainstem. Surgeons also gradually determined the extent of OC resection needed depending on the underlying pathology; generally, resection of approximately one-third to one-half of the OC is enough for most lesions, preserving most of the joint for craniocervical stability. This selective philosophy was later reinforced by Bassiouni and colleagues, who demonstrated that a posterolateral retrocondylar approach could achieve effective resection of ventral foramen magnum meningiomas while preserving craniocervical stability in carefully selected patients. If the entire OC must be resected to reach the target (a rare occurrence), current practice is to perform an occipitocervical fusion at the same time to avoid instability. This combined transcondylar approach (far lateral with condylectomy) was a game changer that enabled safe resection of meningiomas, chordomas, and other lesions previously considered inaccessible or only partially resectable.
Clinical outcome series evaluating the far-lateral corridor for foramen magnum meningiomas have demonstrated that high rates of tumor resection can be achieved using different extents of condylar drilling. In early reports using the transcondylar variant, gross-total resection (GTR) was achieved in approximately 80–90% of cases, although this exposure required partial drilling of the OC to improve the angle of attack to ventral lesions. Subsequent studies demonstrated that similar resection rates could often be achieved using condyle-preserving retrocondylar approaches, with one series reporting 96% Simpson grade II resection with only 8% permanent surgical morbidity. More recent tailored strategies emphasize minimal or selective condylar drilling, reporting GTR or near-total resection in 16 of 20 patients while maintaining craniocervical stability. Together, these outcome data suggest that the key determinant of surgical success is not routine condylar removal but rather tailored exposure based on tumor location and ventral extension, allowing surgeons to balance operative access with preservation of craniovertebral stability.

Supracondylar extension and other less common variations
Once surgeons began to use the transcondylar approach more extensively, they were also able to identify bony obstacles and develop targeted solutions. One such bony structure is the jugular tubercle, a bony prominence of the occipital bone located above the OC that may obscure the upper clivus and ventrolateral brainstem. The exposure of the transcondylar approach can be further augmented by drilling the jugular tubercle (also referred to as supracondylar extension of the approach), which allows for more upward and anterior reach. A closely related corridor has also been described as the transcondylar fossa approach in the skull base literature. In practice, the supracondylar and transcondylar fossa corridors are often discussed together because both target bone superior to the OC and improve access toward the ventrolateral brainstem and lower clivus, although terminology varies somewhat across anatomical and surgical series. Likewise, dissection of the transverse process of C1, as well as the paracondylar extension of the lateral mass of C1, may facilitate mobilization of the VA as well as access to tumors extending towards the jugular foramen. The details of these modifications were provided in a systematic fashion by Rhoton in 2000, who noted the extensions were transcondylar, supracondylar, and paracondylar variations of the FLA. Each variation removes progressively more bone (namely, the OC, the bone over the OC, and the bone lateral to the OC, respectively) to expand the surgical corridor in a certain direction. The philosophy behind modern far-lateral craniotomy/craniectomy is to remove as much bone as necessary but as little as possible. This same principle has also been applied to preservation of the posterior arch of C1 in selected retrocondylar cases, further emphasizing that bone removal should be tailored rather than routine. Early surgeons were cautious about extensive bone removal, but as techniques for stabilization improved, the fear of instability has lessened. Today’s best practices emphasize aggressive drilling of obstructing bone (e.g., OC, jugular tubercle) for maximal exposure when needed, coupled with immediate stabilization (fusion) if the removal crosses a threshold (usually > 50% of the OC). This evolution of bone work—from a limited craniectomy to tailored transcondylar and transjugular extensions—has been critical in minimizing brain retraction and achieving more complete tumor resections.
Clinical outcome series suggest that the extent of condylar drilling should be individualized according to tumor location and the degree of ventral exposure required. Condyle-preserving retrocondylar approaches have demonstrated that effective tumor removal can often be achieved without routine condylar drilling. In a series of 12 foramen magnum meningiomas treated through a retrocondylar far-lateral exposure, GTR was achieved in 9 of 12 patients (75%), transient lower cranial nerve deficits occurred in 4 of 12 patients (33%), and no postoperative craniovertebral instability requiring fusion was observed. In another series of 25 patients treated via a posterolateral retrocondylar approach, Simpson grade II resection was achieved in 24 of 25 cases (96%), with permanent surgical morbidity reported in 2 of 25 patients (8%) and mortality in 1 of 25 patients (4%), while craniocervical stability was preserved during follow-up. By contrast, larger series including transcondylar or extreme-lateral variants, which involve partial drilling of the OC to improve the surgical trajectory to ventral lesions, report complete tumor removal in approximately 77% of cases, with permanent morbidity generally ranging from 0 to 17%, most commonly related to lower cranial nerve dysfunction. These studies also emphasize that condylar removal is typically limited to roughly one-third to one-half of the condyle to avoid postoperative craniocervical instability that could necessitate occipitocervical fusion and that larger skull base exposures may also increase the risk of postoperative CSF leakage reported in some far-lateral series. Taken together, these data suggest that while transcondylar drilling may improve exposure for ventral pathology, condyle-preserving or limited-drilling strategies can achieve high rates of tumor resection in selected cases while potentially reducing the risk of instability and skull-base complications.
The current literature does not provide direct head-to-head comparisons between retrocondylar, transcondylar, and supracondylar variants of the FLA. Most available data come from individual institutional series in which the choice of approach was dictated by tumor location and surgeon preference rather than by a comparative study design. Consequently, differences in reported rates of GTR, cranial nerve morbidity, CSF leakage, and postoperative instability must be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, these series collectively suggest that although condylar drilling may improve the angle of attack for ventral lesions, condyle-preserving strategies can achieve high rates of tumor resection in appropriately selected cases while potentially reducing the need for occipitocervical stabilization.

Positioning and retractor systems

Patient positioning innovations
Proper positioning is critical in far-lateral skull base surgery. In the early 1990 s, some surgeons performed the FLA with the patient seated. The sitting or semi-sitting position, a tradition from posterior fossa surgery, provides venous drainage and gravity-guided cerebellar fall-away. Bertalanffy and Seeger employed the sitting position with the patient’s head flexed and rotated contralaterally. Although there are benefits, the sitting position is also associated with risks, including air embolism and hemodynamic instability. Sekhar and Sen were among the early practitioners of the lateral park-bench position for FLAs. They described using a pure lateral decubitus position to eliminate the complications associated with sitting. With a slight forward tilt, gravity pulls the cerebellum away from the foramen magnum in the lateral position, minimizing the need for fixed retraction. Today, the modified park-bench position is one of the most frequently used positions. In this positioning, the patient is placed laterally with the head turned towards the floor; the head is flexed down and the ipsilateral shoulder is down, allowing for a balance of surgical exposure and hemodynamic stability. The positioning maximizes natural gravity retraction and minimizes risk (especially that of venous air embolism). Today the seated position is rarely used for FLAs.

Retractor systems and brain relaxation
As positioning evolved, there were simultaneous advances in creating and maintaining exposure. In the early years of far-lateral procedures, fixed retractors were frequently used to retract the cerebellum or tonsil to enhance the exposure. As more bone was removed and the need for retraction and retraction-mediated motion decreased, surgeons moved to soft, dynamic methods of retraction. Routine measures include the use of lumbar CSF drains to relax the intradural space, careful intraoperative monitoring of brainstem pressure, and intermittent retraction (instead of prolonged fixed retraction). Modern skull base surgery frequently uses techniques that forgo retractors where feasible by using suction or an ultrasonic aspirator as a gentle retractor, as opposed to fixed blades, alternating angles of approach. Flex retractors and frameless stereotactic navigational guidance (to choose the shortest path to the lesion) are other innovations that can reduce the amount of retraction required. Instead of the ‘retract to expose’ method used initially, the mindset has shifted to ‘expose, so that little retraction is needed.’ By removing obstructive bone (as described above) and using optimal patient positioning, the FLA today typically requires only minimal cerebellar retraction, a stark contrast to the substantial retraction needed in midline approaches of the past.

The VA and jugular tubercle

VA
The VA exits the C1 transverse foramen and courses posteriorly and laterally within the sulcus arteriosus on the superior surface of the posterior arch of C1, curving behind the lateral mass before turning anteromedially to pierce the dura and enter the foramen magnum. Inadequate handling of the VA can limit the exposure and also risk catastrophic injury. Historically, surgeons approached the VA with great caution. In early cases, the VA might be only partially exposed or not mobilized at all, meaning the working corridor to approach the tumor of interest remained narrow. Over time, anatomical studies and surgical experience delineated safe techniques to mobilize the VA and thereby widen the corridor. A standard maneuver now is to expose the VA extradurally from the transverse foramen of C1 up to its dural entry point at the foramen magnum. Small muscular and meningeal branches of the VA are coagulated and cut, and the dural ring is opened at the dura. These steps allow the surgeon to gently mobilize the VA medially or laterally as needed. By the 1990 s, this technique was well established. As described by Sen and Sekhar, it involves fully freeing the VA from C1 (and even from C2 in some cases) all the way to the vertebrobasilar junction, which permits manipulation of the artery or even trapping/reconstructing it if it is encased by tumor. In essence, the evolution of VA management has turned the artery from an obstacle into a structure that can be moved to expand the window. In difficult cases (such as those involving large tumors or aneurysms), temporary occlusion or bypass techniques are available, but these are seldom needed when mobilization is adequate. Preoperative imaging (e.g., computed tomography angiography or magnetic resonance angiography) now guides surgeons on the dominance and course of the VA, helping them to plan safe mobilization. This careful handling of the VA, developed over years of cadaveric dissection and clinical refinement, is one of the reasons the FLA allows for such extensive exposure around the brainstem with acceptable risk.
Clinical experience has demonstrated that systematic extradural exposure and mobilization of the V3 segment improves operative freedom and proximal vascular control during far-lateral surgery. The subperiosteal exposure of the V3 segment, which allows the VA to be mobilized while controlling the periarterial venous plexus, was formalized as a reproducible surgical technique for CVJ surgery. Subsequent anatomical and operative studies further emphasized that staged muscular dissection and early VA identification during the FLA improve surgical orientation and facilitate safer manipulation of the artery during tumor dissection. Together, these refinements transformed the VA from a limiting obstacle into a structure that can be strategically mobilized to expand the operative corridor.

Jugular tubercle
Previously, some early FLAs avoided drilling in the jugular tubercle anteriorly out of concern for the preservation of adjacent structures (e.g., the hypoglossal nerve in the hypoglossal canal at the jugular tubercle). Further experience showed that removing some of the jugular tubercle can significantly improve the visualization of the upper clivus and petroclival area. The techniques used included extradural drilling of the jugular tubercle before dural opening. This maneuver—which is part of the supracondylar extension mentioned earlier—proved to be particularly valuable when incorporating lesions that extend rostrally toward the jugular foramen or lower clivus. Surgeons learned to safely unroof the hypoglossal canal and flatten the jugular tubercle without damaging the nerves, by using anatomical landmarks or by using neuronavigation and drilling the bone piecemeal. Over the years, these techniques developed into a systematic method to remove bone. The detailed anatomical guidelines described by Rhoton clarified how much of the OC and jugular tubercle to remove. Currently, the FLA is often extended to include skeletonization of the VA and resection of obstructing bony anatomy such as the OC and jugular tubercle when necessary. This progression in handling bony and vascular anatomy has been a breakthrough; once considered an anatomic “no man’s land,” the area has become a well-trodden trail for skull base neurosurgeons that affords bony working space and confident access to the ventrolateral brainstem and upper cervical cord.

Microsurgical and endoscopic advances

Microsurgical advances
The FLA matured with modern microsurgery. In the 1980s and 1990s, the use of an operating microscope was already standard. High magnification and illumination allowed surgeons to work in the deep, narrow corridors around the brainstem and assisted in the delicate dissection of cranial nerves and vessels. Over the years, improvements in microscopes with infrared autofocus, tumor fluorescence, and heads-up displays have improved the surgeon’s abilities to perform these approaches. Microsurgical instruments themselves—from bayonet forceps to angled micro-scissors—have evolved to allow for working at extreme angles. Notably, the introduction of ultrasonic aspirators enabled safer debulking of large CVJ tumors and minimized traction on nearby neural structures.

Endoscope-assisted advances
To further improve visualization, endoscopic assistance has been incorporated into the FLA over the past few years. The endoscope gives angled views around corners where the microscope cannot see, such as the far contralateral aspect of the dural sac or behind bony edges that were not removed. Original studies on combined endoscopic-microscopic techniques date back to the 2010s. The use of cadaver models proved the feasibility of an endoscope-assisted far-lateral keyhole approach, in which adequate exposure of dorsal brainstem can be accomplished with a smaller craniectomy using 0° or 30° endoscopes. After microsurgical exposure is performed, the surgeon can use the endoscope to inspect blind spots for residual tumor or navigate further bone drilling without large incisions. Although pure endoscopic FLAs through small skin incisions have been described by some surgeons, these methods are not yet widespread. Endoscope-assisted microsurgery, in which the surgeon mostly operates under the microscope but occasionally uses an endoscope for visualization, is more typical. This combination takes advantage of the stability and depth perception of the microscope, as well as the panoramic, around-the-corner view of the endoscope.

Neurotechnology integration
In addition to the endoscope, other advances in technology have improved the FLA. Neuronavigation systems are currently used in clinical practice to optimize the skin incision and trajectory angle to the target lesion while removing minimal bone. Intraoperative neuromonitoring of the lower cranial nerves (IX-XII) has been performed to provide an extra safety aspect. Indocyanine green intraoperative angiography can confirm the patency of the VA and the adequacy of aneurysm clipping in real time. These microsurgical and technological advances were absent in the earliest far-lateral procedures but have become pervasive over the last 20 years, improving precision and outcomes. Today, the FLA is not only a technique but also a platform that integrates contemporary tools and does not shy away from evolution.

Evolving indications
Although the FLA was primarily designed for limited applications (e.g., vascular aberrations of the vertebrobasilar junction), the approach has been applied to a wide range of pathologies since its inception. In 1986, Heros discussed clipping aneurysms of the VA and basilar artery. Initial studies leveraged the approach’s potential to access the undersurface of the brainstem where these aneurysms live. Because the method worked, neurosurgeons saw its wider potential, and the approach has been used for various lesions at the foramen magnum and CVJ, both intradural and extradural, over the years.

Tumors
The most dramatic expansion has come in tumor treatment. By the 1990s, the FLA (or extreme lateral approach) had emerged as a workhorse approach for foramen magnum meningiomas, particularly those located ventrally or ventrolaterally. With the far-lateral route, patients with anterior foramen magnum meningiomas (once considered among the most difficult skull base tumors to resect) experienced higher rates of complete removal and lower morbidity. The pathway offers optimal exposure of meningiomas that develop near the OC or anterior dura of the foramen magnum that would otherwise require either a more dangerous transoral approach or additional unnecessary brainstem mobilization. Lower cranial nerve schwannomas (hypoglossal neurinomas) and jugular foramen tumors extending toward the foramen magnum are similarly well served by far-lateral exposures. Another crucial indication is resection of the lower clivus and upper cervical spine chordomas or chondrosarcoma. These malignant bone neoplasms are common and are often found at the skull base and in the area of C1-C2, requiring a fairly wide exposure often achievable through the far-lateral route or in conjunction with endonasal or other approaches. Far-lateral trajectories have even been applied for some intramedullary tumors of the high cervical spinal cord or ventrolateral medulla (e.g., anterior cervicomedullary hemangioblastomas) where a dorsal midline approach would not suffice to reach the target.

Vascular lesions
Besides aneurysms, the FLA is used to address other vascular pathologies at the CVJ. For instance, VA-PICA junction aneurysms, which are located near the foramen magnum, are excellent candidates for clipping of the distal VA using a FLA because it directly exposes the VA segment and PICA origin. This approach has even been used for the treatment of some complex brainstem cavernous malformations or ventral arteriovenous malformations in the medulla because it provides access to the anterior area of the medulla oblongata and upper cervical cord. Lesions that in the past may have been very hard to access, or required a riskier brainstem splitting, are more simply managed with a safer lateral entry point using the far-lateral corridor.

Congenital and degenerative conditions
Indications have also expanded to some non-neoplastic, non-vascular cases. For example, irreducible basilar invagination or ventral brainstem compression associated with congenital CVJ anomalies may, at times, be amenable to far-lateral transcervical approaches (often in conjunction with fusion). Historically, transoral odontoidectomies were performed to tackle these; however, in select patients with unidirectional compression, a far-lateral trajectory can provide decompression without transoral morbidity. In addition, FLAs have been described to access pathologies such as a compressive high-riding VA or for reaching the odontoid process when there is no access via the endonasal route. Even some extreme lateral disc herniations at C1-C2 or ventral dural arteriovenous fistulas have been treated via far-lateral exposure. Although these are relatively infrequent indications, they demonstrate the versatility of the FLA.

Rapid expansion and adaptation
Once a niche approach for aneurysms, this versatile technique is now widely used for CVJ lesions of diverse pathology and has allowed surgeons to expand the boundaries of lesions they can safely treat. As promising outcomes were reported in the 1990s and 2000s, the indications for the FLA grew rapidly. Endoscopic assistance has further expanded the utility of the far-lateral technique, with more complex skull base tumors being treated using the far-lateral combined with other approaches (staged or simultaneous). As a result, even lesions deemed benign or those that compress the CVJ in complicated settings are now being treated with the FLA.

Advancing far-lateral neurosurgical techniques

Extreme lateral transodontoid approach

Background

The extreme lateral transodontoid (ELTO) approach extends the classic far-lateral transcondylar approach (Fig. 5). This approach involves a single-stage posterolateral trajectory that requires both drilling of the OC (extending to or beyond the hypoglossal canal where necessary) and resection of the C1 lateral mass, C1 anterior arch, and odontoid process. The surgeon accesses the contralateral aspect of the ventral CVJ through a single operative corridor, while transposing the ipsilateral VA and aggressively resecting the associated bony and ligamentous structures. The ELTO technique was recommended to allow for resection of midline-crossing tumors without entering a contralateral field for a second approach. It has also been shown to be useful for reducing anatomic constraints in pediatric patients. The anatomical exposure is profound—extending laterally from the ipsilateral lower cranial nerves (approximately IX) to the contralateral upper cranial nerves (approximately VII) and inferiorly to the C2-C3 nerve roots—and provides a direct line of sight to the anterior brainstem and upper cervical spinal cord. After resection of the odontoid and associated bony confines, this universal exposure and attack angle transform a unilateral approach into a bilateral ventral exposure of the CVJ.

The ELTO approach is indicated for lesions of the lower clivus, foramen magnum, and upper cervical spine that extend across the midline or demonstrate substantial contralateral involvement ventral to the brainstem (Fig. 6). In these situations, a standard FLA may provide limited access to the contralateral side, whereas the ELTO approach allows wider bilateral exposure through a single unilateral corridor. By combining condylar drilling with resection of the C1 lateral mass and odontoid, the approach enables direct visualization of the brainstem–tumor interface and improved access to contralateral pathology without the need for staged or combined procedures.
An additional advantage of the ELTO approach is early lateral control of the VA and PICA, which improves operative safety when dealing with highly vascular tumors or lesions encasing the vertebrobasilar junction. Because the corridor remains extrasinus and extrapharyngeal, the technique also avoids complications associated with transoral or endonasal routes, including mucosal contamination, infection, and CSF leakage from sinonasal communication. These features allow extensive ventral exposure while preserving the ability to perform direct multilayer dural repair under microscopic visualization.
By allowing a GTR of extensive CVJ tumor in a single setting (rather than multi-stage operations or combined approaches, which may be more likely using more conventional techniques), the ELTO approach offers the advantages of direct access to both sides of the midline and the capability to mobilize tumor over the midline while having good control of critical neurovascular structures.

Occipitocervical instability and fusion strategies

Background
Because the ELTO and associated far-lateral techniques involve the removal of stabilizing osseous and ligamentous structures (e.g., OC, atlantooccipital joint capsule, alar ligaments, C1 lateral mass, odontoid process), occipitocervical instability is a major concern. Biomechanical studies with cadaveric models by Mazur et al. defined biomechanical instability thresholds, providing guidance for when fusion should be included in the case: Generally, resection of approximately 30% of a single OC can lead to quantifiable alterations in occipitocervical biomechanics, and delayed occipitocervical instability becomes significantly more likely with greater extent of resection. The authors proposed the hypoglossal canal as a surgical landmark, denoting the maximum safe limit of drilling. In fact, the hypoglossal canal lies at the anterior third of the OC in as many as 22% of individuals, according to anatomical studies. In these cases, drilling to the hypoglossal canal may remove >50% of the OC, exceeding the limit of stability and necessitating fusion. These biomechanical considerations and intraoperative findings are used to guide clinical decision-making for occipitocervical fusion in practice. When substantial OC or C1 destruction is necessary for surgical access to the tumor, we embrace a low threshold to stabilize the CVJ.

Stabilization strategies
Unilateral occiput-C1 fusion: Instrumented (plate and screws) fusion from the occiput to atlas on the affected side only stabilizes the affected occipital-C1 joint but preserves some motion at the contralateral side and at C1-2; these constructs were the least rigid in testing and notably poor in resisting axial rotation and flexion–extension because stabilization is lacking in C2.
Unilateral occiput-C2 fusion: Occipital plate fixation on the affected side (with screws anchored in the axis (C2) on that same side) affords a longer lever arm of fixation (spanning 2 spinal levels) and was significantly stiffer than occiput-C1 in rotation and flexion. Such a unilateral construct can be entertained in specific circumstances (e.g., temporary stabilization or when the contralateral side is entirely intact), but it still does not match the strength of bilateral fixation.
Bilateral occiput-cervical fusion: Although a bilateral occiput-C1 fusion (fixing both OCs to the atlas) provides stability at the occiput-C1 level, it leaves the C1-2 segment free. A bilateral occiput-C2 construct is more robust. This is the gold-standard construct for maximum stability when needed (e.g., after radical condylectomy or bilateral joint disruption) because it provides solid three-point stabilization (occiput, C1, C2). Yet, only a small number of patients develop frank instability if a less invasive partial condylectomy is performed, so the choice of unilateral or bilateral fusion should be made on a case-by-case basis.
Within an ELTO approach, joint destabilization is almost unavoidable; therefore, concomitant occipitocervical fusion is normally performed, either in the same operation or staged shortly after tumor resection. The occipitocervical fusion has a specific construct that can be tailored to the extent of resection. In 2019, Eli et al. compared the biomechanics of different fixation techniques after complete unilateral condylectomy (entire removal of one OC).
If the tumor involves the OC or if significant resection is required to obtain clearance, we opt for early fusion to avoid delayed issues. In a retrospective series of 61 far-lateral surgeries by Mazur et al., 2 patients (3.3%) who did not initially receive fusion went on to develop delayed occipitocervical instability. Notably, in the immediate postoperative period, those patients had appeared aligned and were discharged in a collar, but over time there was gradual collapse of the area. These cases demonstrate that even if early fusion is not undertaken, careful monitoring is essential, and the development of occipitocervical pain or deformity in the days after surgery should lead to imaging and, if instability is suspected, early occipitocervical fusion. Ultimately, the decision-making paradigm for occipitocervical fusion in far-lateral skull base surgery should minimize harm associated with stabilization while avoiding unnecessary fusions when feasible.

Because far-lateral and extreme lateral skull base surgeries breach the dura near the foramen magnum, careful closure is imperative. A watertight closure of the dura and suboccipital wound using multilayer repair can support healing of the dural defect (Fig. 7). Scoville et al. described a multilayer closure technique to minimize postoperative CSF leaks in which an autologous fascial graft is sutured into position for closure of the dural defect and augmented with a layer of autologous fat placed epidurally on top of the repair and stabilized with fibrin glue. The fat graft acts as a plug and a supplemental sealant, and the fibrin glue assists in attaching the graft to the surrounding tissue and seals any micro-holes. Lumbar CSF drainage decreases the subarachnoid pressure, allowing the dural patch to heal without expulsion by high intrathecal pressure or CSF pulsations.

Comparison with alternative methods
If not meticulously closed, FLAs historically carry a risk of CSF leak. This multilayered closure strategy allows direct suturing of the dura under vision, the gold standard for watertight closure. The use of autologous tissue (fascia and fat) is biocompatible and integrates well, potentially reducing infection risk compared with synthetic patches. By reinforcing the suture repair with a fat graft and glue, the chance of any residual defect is further minimized. The inclusion of a postoperative lumbar drain is surgeon-dependent, but CSF diversion may reduce pseudomeningocele or wound leakage in large dural openings.

Outcomes and long-term experience
The success of this closure technique is reflected in the low incidence of CSF leaks in the published series of patients treated with the ELTO approach, in which a single pseudomeningocele without wound leak or infection was reported at the operative site and there were no external CSF fistulas noted postoperatively. By preventing gross CSF leaks, this technique helps avoid complications such as meningitis, wound breakdown, and prolonged hospital stays. Emphasis on a sound closure is in line with the broader skull base surgery experience, where meticulous reconstruction of the dural defect has been shown to drastically reduce leak rates.

Modern evolution of the FLA

Positioning and operative setup
Positioning strategies for the FLA have evolved over time. Although earlier descriptions included the sitting position, concerns regarding venous air embolism have led most centers to favor the lateral or park-bench position for ventrolateral skull base access. Proper head and neck alignment facilitates muscle reflection, early VA exposure, and a tension-free operative corridor.
In extended variants such as the ELTO approach, precise positioning becomes even more important because the approach relies on a single lateral trajectory to access midline or contralateral pathology. This trajectory allows early visualization and control of the VA and PICA while providing a direct corridor to the ventral brainstem and upper cervical spinal cord.
Preoperative vascular imaging is therefore routinely used to evaluate VA dominance and anatomical variations, particularly in cases in which vascular mobilization or staged occipitocervical fusion may be anticipated.

Incision planning and soft tissue exposure
Several strategies have emerged to reduce morbidity associated with soft tissue dissection. A step-by-step surgical report demonstrated that a linear incision, paired with an interfascial–subperiosteal–interdural dissection, provides direct access while minimizing soft tissue trauma and preserving VA safety. Similarly, an oblique straight incision has shown promise in preserving muscle integrity and facilitating reliable, tension-free closure. In a four-patient series using this technique, GTR was achieved in all cases without any postoperative CSF leaks or scalp hydrops.
The "lazy" FLA, as described in a 44-patient series, allows for lateral subperiosteal dissection of the VA within its sheath, followed by a medial curved dural incision. This modification significantly lowers the risk of VA injury while enabling adequate ventral brainstem exposure. Notably, no vascular injuries or surgical mortalities were reported.
Further emphasizing soft-tissue preservation, the C-shaped incision has been validated as a viable alternative to the traditional reverse hockey-stick incision. It requires less muscle elevation and has been associated with no pseudomeningoceles or CSF leaks in a series of 11 PICA aneurysm cases. These findings support a growing consensus: less is more when it comes to suboccipital exposure.
Additionally, closure strategy has become an integral part of incision and exposure planning. A multilayer closure technique using autologous fascia lata, fat graft, and fibrin glue—combined with layered muscle closure—has been shown to significantly reduce postoperative CSF leak rates, even in high-risk cases. By carefully reapproximating the nuchal fascia and musculature and anchoring myocutaneous flaps to the occiput through chevron-shaped bone tunnels, surgeons have preserved muscle bulk and symmetry while achieving robust watertight seals. This approach underscores the importance of designing the incision not only for access but also for durable closure and long-term contour preservation.

Bony exposure and approach extensions
Anatomical dissection studies have redefined the reach of far-lateral variants. A comparative morphometric analysis of transcondylar and supracondylar techniques demonstrated that the transcondylar FLA provides medial access to up to 58% of the anterior foramen magnum by removing the OC, while the supracondylar FLA allows access to 41% of the lower clivus via jugular tubercle removal. The maneuverability windows ranged between 7 and 17 mm, depending on trajectory. Cadaveric tumor modeling showed that transcondylar and supracondylar FLAs offer significantly greater maneuverability than the basic FLA, particularly for small lesions (< 1 cm3). The endoscope-assisted supracondylar technique performed best for vertical reach, supporting the integration of endoscopy into bony corridor planning.
The ELTO approach expands the traditional FLA by resecting the odontoid, C1 lateral mass, and anterior arch to access bilateral ventral CVJ tumors via a unilateral corridor. This extension enables direct access across the midline while preserving contralateral soft tissue and musculature. Careful tailoring of the bony resection is critical—particularly in relation to the hypoglossal canal. Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that resecting as little as 30% of a OC can result in measurable joint laxity, with the hypoglossal canal located in the anterior third of the OC in up to 22% of patients, meaning that "drilling to the canal" may exceed the stability threshold in a significant subset.
Clinical experience now increasingly supports condylar preservation when possible. In a 10-year surgical series of foramen magnum meningiomas, most resections were achieved without condylar removal. These conservative approaches reduce instability risk and the need for occipitocervical fusion. Additionally, a cadaveric report detailed layer-by-layer muscular dissection that preserves exposure while protecting the VA and enables precise tailoring of condylar drilling. The senior author supports the concept of exposing “far enough laterally,” meaning that the OC removal should be tailored to the pathology and to the amount of exposure necessary for addressing the pathology. Often, the tumor presence itself in a foramen magnum meningioma may provide an adequate corridor to achieve anterior tumor removal; in general, OC resection (posteromedial) is limited to only what is necessary for the particular task at hand.
Modified techniques even omit C1 laminectomy for aneurysms above the lower foramen magnum. A 48-patient series showed that the FLA without C1 removal led to complete aneurysm obliteration, favorable outcomes in 77%, and no compromise in exposure. In more extensive resections, the senior author has advocated staging occipitocervical fusion separately to allow for postoperative MRI before hardware placement, maintaining patient alignment in a cervical collar in the interim. These strategies balance maximal exposure with biomechanical integrity and long-term spinal alignment.

Neurovascular strategy including VA mobilization
The VA poses both an anatomical constraint and a source of risk during the FLA. The ELTO technique improves safety by enabling early lateral transposition of the VA and PICA from the start of the approach. This proactive control helps in vascular tumors and high-flow corridors by providing early visualization of the vertebrobasilar junction and enabling safer debulking. It also facilitates resection of a tumor invading the contralateral OC or encasing the ventral dural ring, which would otherwise be challenging via conventional exposures.
Other authors advocate for subperiosteal dissection of the VA within its sheath, without unroofing the artery, as in the "lazy" FLA. This approach respects the integrity of the arterial adventitia while preserving the safety of surrounding structures. A complementary muscle-based dissection technique has also been described, in which the transverse process of C1 and the OC are identified sequentially through anatomic planes while maintaining respect for the VA's surrounding fascia. This strategy minimizes arterial injury while permitting tailored exposure and condylar drilling based on tumor location.
The combination of early vascular control, meticulous subperiosteal dissection, and targeted exposure has contributed to high rates of GTR in series employing these methods, without vascular complications. These neurovascular techniques reflect a growing shift toward anatomical precision and safety-conscious dissection as foundational elements of the modern FLA.

Tumor resection corridors
A key advance of the ELTO method is transforming a unilateral approach into a bilateral corridor to the anterior brainstem. By removing the odontoid and C1 lateral mass, the surgeon gains direct visualization from ipsilateral cranial nerves IX-XII to contralateral cranial nerve VII, down to C2-3. This obviates the need for staged or bilateral approaches and is particularly useful for midline-crossing chordomas, invasive foramen magnum meningiomas, or ventral CVJ tumors that extend contralaterally.
In this configuration, early control of vascular structures and wide bony decompression allow the surgeon to work with direct visualization across the midline, avoiding blind dissection or excessive retraction. The operative corridor remains entirely extrasinus and extrapharyngeal, thus avoiding morbidity associated with transoral or endonasal alternatives, such as velopharyngeal insufficiency, mucosal contamination, or sinonasal wound healing issues. Other groups emphasize working within the tumor corridor itself. For example, a bone-sparing far-lateral strategy led to GTR in most cases by exploiting tumor-created spaces rather than performing routine condylar drilling. Similarly, a complementary aneurysm series using a OC-sparing FLA demonstrated high efficacy for anterior brainstem aneurysms without requiring VA mobilization.
These evolving strategies underscore a key trend: whether through expanded ELTO-based bony exposure or conservative utilization of pre-existing tumor corridors, the overarching goal remains maximal resection with minimal disruption. The decision to drill or preserve critical structures is now more tailored than ever, based on lesion size, trajectory, and patient-specific anatomy.

Craniocervical instability, the need for stabilization, and stabilization techniques
Instability remains a key concern when the OC or C1 is resected. Biomechanical studies show that resecting as little as 30% of a OC can lead to measurable joint laxity. Intraoperative landmarks such as the hypoglossal canal that were traditionally viewed as safe boundaries are not always reliable: up to 22% of cases had the canal within the anterior third of the OC, meaning “drilling up to the canal” risks > 50% resection. This anterior positioning may falsely reassure the surgeon and inadvertently lead to destabilization.
Stabilization options include unilateral occiput-to-C1 or -C2 fusion, but biomechanical data show that bilateral occiput-to-C2 fixation provides the greatest rotational and lateral bending rigidity. Although unilateral constructs preserve some motion, cadaveric data confirm they underperform in resisting axial rotation and flexion, particularly when C2 is not incorporated. Bilateral occiput-C2 fusion remains the gold standard when extensive bone removal or bilateral joint disruption is involved. This strategy is supported by a clinical series in which two patients who underwent extensive bone removal without fusion developed delayed instability, requiring urgent occipitocervical reconstruction. Both had grossly destabilizing resections with condylar violation, yet appeared aligned postoperatively. However, within weeks, craniocervical collapse led to torticollis and joint subluxation and required urgent fixation. These cases emphasize the critical importance of anticipating instability—even when alignment appears acceptable in the immediate postoperative period.
Within ELTO-based approaches, where resection of the odontoid, C1 lateral mass, and anterior OC is often necessary, many authors now advocate a low threshold for performing occipitocervical fusion, either during the same operation or staged soon after. Some centers prefer to delay fusion by one or two days to obtain a clean postoperative MRI, keeping the patient sedated and immobilized in a rigid cervical collar in the interim. Neuromonitoring is commonly used during both the tumor resection and the fusion to reduce risk of spinal cord or cranial nerve stretch injury.
Together, these data support a risk-stratified approach: in cases in which anterior condylar resection exceeds one-third, extends beyond the hypoglossal canal, or involves the C1 lateral mass, early stabilization—preferably bilateral—is advised to prevent delayed collapse.

Multilayer closure and CSF leak Prevention
Because the far-lateral corridor places the dural repair site in continuity with dependent cervical soft tissues, meticulous reconstruction is essential to prevent postoperative CSF leakage. Contemporary techniques emphasize watertight dural closure reinforced with autologous fascia and epidural fat graft, supplemented with fibrin sealant and layered muscle closure. Temporary lumbar CSF diversion is often used to reduce postoperative pressure on the repair.
Additional refinements include anchoring the myocutaneous flap to the occiput using chevron-style drill holes to preserve muscle bulk and minimize postoperative deformity. With these strategies, modern series report very low rates of postoperative CSF leakage and wound complications, even after extensive ELTO exposures.

Conclusion
The FLA has evolved from a solution for lesions inaccessible through traditional midline or transoral corridors into a modular surgical framework for ventral and ventrolateral CVJ pathology. Modern use of the approach is guided less by fixed nomenclature than by pathology-specific selection of exposure modules, including tailored condylar drilling, jugular tubercle removal, VA mobilization, and, in selected cases, extension to the odontoid and C1 lateral mass. Current evidence supports a conservative principle of removing only the bone necessary to obtain a safe angle of attack: condylar drilling should be reserved for lesions in which tumor location or working angle truly require it, while condyle-preserving strategies remain effective for many foramen magnum lesions. Similarly, VA mobilization is most valuable when proximal vascular control or additional ventrolateral exposure is needed but should be performed with meticulous subperiosteal technique to minimize arterial and venous injury. When bony resection extends beyond stability-preserving thresholds, particularly with substantial condylar or C1 lateral mass removal, early occipitocervical stabilization should be strongly considered. Together with modern microsurgical visualization, endoscopic assistance, neuromonitoring, and multilayer closure methods, these refinements have improved exposure while limiting lower cranial nerve morbidity, instability, and CSF-related complications. Thus, the contemporary FLA is best understood not as a single operation, but as a flexible, outcome-driven strategy that balances access with morbidity for complex lesions of the CVJ.
