Clinical and pharmacovigilance safety evaluation of LUXTURNA® (voretigene neparvovec-rzyl)
Background:
LUXTURNA® (voretigene neparvovec-rzyl) is the first FDA-approved gene therapy for inherited retinal dystrophies caused by biallelic RPE65 mutations. While pivotal clinical trials established its safety and efficacy, there has been no formal analysis of real world data to date.
Methods:
We reviewed published literature and regulatory safety databases, including the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System and EudraVigilance, to assess clinical outcomes and adverse event reporting associated with LUXTURNA®.
Results:
Clinical trials demonstrated durable functional vision improvements, with approximately 70% of patients maintaining gains up to four years post-treatment. Adverse events reported in trials were largely mild to moderate, such as conjunctival hyperemia, cataract, and transient intraocular pressure elevation. Post-marketing surveillance, however, revealed additional safety concerns, most notably chorioretinal atrophy (CRA), which has been reported in 13–50% of treated eyes, particularly in younger patients and often near the injection site. Disproportionality analyses confirmed CRA as the most prominent adverse event, alongside elevated risks for retinal tears or detachment, intraocular pressure changes, ocular hemorrhage, and inflammation. Despite these findings, central visual acuity was generally preserved, and no systemic toxicity was identified.
Conclusions:
LUXTURNA® provides meaningful and durable visual benefits for patients with RPE65-related disease, but long-term monitoring is essential to address its emerging ocular safety profile. Our data represent the first comprehensive pharmacovigilance review of LUXTURNA®, providing critical insights for clinical practice and post-marketing surveillance.

Introduction

Background
Inherited retinal dystrophies (IRDs) are a heterogeneous group of genetic diseases that impair the structure and function of the retina, often resulting in progressive vision loss and ultimately blindness. Globally, approximately 5.5 million individuals suffer from an IRD. Pathogenic mutations in over 300 retinal genes have been identified, contributing to a broad range of IRD phenotypes. Current drug discovery efforts primarily focus on the 15 most frequently mutated genes linked to IRDs, including RPE65.
Biallelic mutations in the RPE65 gene are predominantly linked to two severe IRD subtypes: Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP) and Leber Congenital Amaurosis (LCA). RP has an estimated prevalence of 11.09–26.43 per 100 000 people, with RPE65 mutations accounting for only 0.23%–4.27% of cases. LCA affects approximately 1.26–2.37 per 100 000 people, with RPE65 mutations responsible for 1.26%–22.22% of cases. Consequently, RPE65-related RP and LCA are classified as rare diseases. Both IRDs are primarily autosomal recessive disorders, although autosomal dominant inheritance is observed in a small fraction of cases.
Until the approval of LUXTURNA® (voretigene neparvovec-rzyl), the standard of care for RPE65-related IRDs was limited to supportive treatments aimed at managing symptoms and preserving remaining vision without addressing the underlying genetic defect. Supportive care often includes refractive correction, low-vision aids, increased illumination, mobility training, and therapies targeting developmental delays in pediatric patients. Additionally, patients are encouraged to follow diets rich in antioxidants, carotenoids, and xanthophylls to promote retinal health. However, these approaches do not provide a cure or halt disease progression.
Considering the limited treatment options, the approval of LUXTURNA® has marked a significant breakthrough in the treatment of these conditions. LUXTURNA® was not only the first gene therapy for an IRD or ophthalmic indication, but the first ever gene therapy approved by the FDA. LUXTURNA® addresses the genetic cause of the disease by introducing a functional copy of the RPE65 gene using AAV2 vector-based gene therapy. The approval of LUXTURNA® on December 19, 2017, provided a new therapeutic option for patients aged 12 months and older with biallelic RPE65 mutations, offering hope for improved visual function and quality of life. To date, LUXTURNA® remains the only FDA-approved gene therapy for IRD and the only FDA approved therapy utilizing the subretinal route of administration.
LUXTURNA® is indicated for patients with confirmed biallelic RPE65 mutations who still have sufficient viable retinal cells, as determined by an ophthalmologist’s retinal exam. To minimize immune responses, patients are required to take oral corticosteroids, such as prednisone, starting three days prior to LUXTURNA® administration. The corticosteroid dose is gradually tapered over the next ten days. LUXTURNA® is administered via subretinal injection under general anesthesia at a dose of 1.5 × 1011 viral genomes (vg) per eye. Treatment is usually given to the worse-functioning eye first, followed by treatment of the second eye within six days.

Preclinical and clinical development
The creation of genetically modified ‘super mice’ in 1982 demonstrated the potential of gene transfer, leading to the first gene therapy trial in 1990 for adenosine deaminase deficiency. Around the same time, mutations in the rhodopsin gene were linked to retinitis pigmentosa (RP), and the gene was successfully cloned. These advances have led to the classification of IRDs and the discovery of numerous other disease-causing IRD genes. The Human Genome Project further accelerated the identification of disease-related mutations and laid the groundwork for gene therapy development.
Early retinal gene therapy development faced significant challenges due to the low membrane permeability of nucleic acids, which prevented effective delivery. Although lipid-based DNA delivery has successfully achieved gene transfer, gene expression remains transient. In 1994, researchers demonstrated the prolonged expression of the LacZ reporter gene in mouse retinas using recombinant adenoviruses. Subsequent studies have identified adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors as a safer and more efficient alternative, establishing them as the preferred method for retinal gene therapy.
A landmark study in Swedish Briard dogs with RPE65 mutations demonstrated the efficacy of AAV-based gene therapy. Treated puppies (ages 108–132 days) exhibited improved vision, providing evidence of the potential of this therapy for human application. Preclinical studies in dogs and nonhuman primates optimized dosage and confirmed safety. Toxicology studies showed that high subretinal AAV doses were generally well tolerated, although the observed immune responses were varied. While some structural retinal changes were observed, these were attributed to the surgical procedure rather than the treatment. Taken together, these findings supported the safety and efficacy of AAV-based gene therapy for RPE65-related IRDs, enabling Spark Therapeutics to advance to first-in-human clinical trials.
Several Phase 1 trials assessed the safety and efficacy of AAV-based gene therapy for RPE65-related IRD. In the first trial, three patients (ages 19–26) received a subretinal injection of AAV2.hRPE65v2 (1.5 × 1010 vg) in their worse-functioning eye, showing significant vision improvements, though one patient developed a macular hole due to a surgical complication. A second trial in the United Kingdom (ages 17–23) used a higher dose (1.0 × 1011 vg), yielding modest improvements with no serious adverse events. A third trial (ages 21–24) used 5.96 × 1010 vg, with documented vision gains within 90 days and no reported serious adverse events.
A dose-escalation phase 1 trial included 12 participants (ages 8–44) receiving low, medium, or high doses (1.5 × 1010–1.5 × 1011 vg). All doses were well tolerated, with younger patients showing greater improvement. Follow-up studies confirmed the safety of treating both eyes with a second dose, leading to further vision gains.
Instead of phase 2 trials, an open-label, randomized, controlled phase 3 trial enrolled 31 participants (ages 4–44) with biallelic RPE65 mutations. Twenty participants received bilateral AAV2-hRPE65v2 injections (1.5 × 1011 vg per eye), while 10 controls received treatment after one year. Functional vision was assessed using the Multi-Luminance Mobility Test (MLMT) and other measures. The MLMT required participants to navigate an obstacle course under varying light conditions, ranging from light intensities equivalent to a ‘brightly lit office’ (400 lux, score 0) to a ‘dark moonless summer night’ (1 lux, score 6). After one year, treated participants showed a significant MLMT score increase (1.8 vs. 0.2 in controls), with 65% passing the lowest light level test (1 lux), compared to none in the control group. Improvements persisted for two years and were replicated in the control participants after treatment.
LUXTURNA® received FDA approval on December 19, 2017, thus becoming the first gene therapy in the United States approved for genetic vision loss. Approval was granted via Priority Review, Breakthrough Therapy, and Orphan Drug designations, along with a Rare Pediatric Disease Priority Review Voucher. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) followed suit on November 22, 2018. Subsequent approvals for LUXTURNA® were granted in Saudi Arabia (2019), Australia (2020), Canada (2020), Brazil (2020), and Japan (2023) [].

Mechanism of action and pharmacokinetics
The RPE65 gene encodes an isomerohydrolase, RPE65, which is mainly expressed in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). This enzyme plays a crucial role in the visual cycle, a series of enzymatic reactions essential for the continuous regeneration of visual pigment in the RPE and photoreceptors. Specifically, RPE65 catalyzes the conversion of all-trans-retinyl esters into 11-cis-retinol. RPE enzymes further metabolize this product into a functional chromophore, which is then transported to the photoreceptors, where it binds to opsins, G-protein coupled receptors. Upon light absorption by the chromophore, opsins undergo a conformational change that triggers phototransduction, which converts light into electrical signals that are ultimately interpreted by the brain as vision. Mutations in the RPE65 gene disrupt the critical isomerization step of the visual cycle, leading to toxic accumulation of retinyl esters in the RPE, which damages photoreceptors over time and causes progressive vision loss and eventually blindness.
LUXTURNA® utilizes a replication-deficient Adeno-Associated-Virus type 2 (AAV2) vector containing the human RPE65 cDNA (hRPE65v2) within its transgene cassette. The RPE65 cDNA is modified with a Kozak sequence at the start codon and controlled by a hybrid chicken β-actin promoter and cytomegalovirus enhancer to ensure optimal expression. The AAV2 vector is produced in and purified from HEK293 cells. LUXTURNA® is supplemented with a surfactant to minimize material loss due to surface interactions during storage and administration. The AAV2 serotype efficiently transduces human RPE cells. Therefore, subretinal injection of LUXTURNA® delivers a normal copy of the RPE65 gene to RPE cells, resulting in the long-term expression of the functional RPE65 enzyme. This restores the conversion of all-trans-retinyl esters into 11-cis-retinol in the visual cycle. By reestablishing this pathway, LUXTURNA® was designed to prevent RPE cytotoxicity and the subsequent loss of photoreceptor cells, ultimately improving visual function.
The biodistribution and vector shedding of LUXTURNA® were evaluated to assess the spread and potential excretion of the vector following subretinal injection. Spark Therapeutics used quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) to determine vector DNA levels in various tissues and secretions in animals and humans.
The biodistribution of LUXTURNA® was assessed three months after treatment in nonhuman primates. The highest concentrations of the vector were detected in the intraocular fluids, specifically in the anterior chamber fluid and vitreous humor of the vector-injected eye. Low levels were detected in the optic nerve, optic chiasm, spleen, liver, and sporadically in the preauricular lymph nodes, indicating limited systemic exposure after subretinal administration. Importantly, the vector was not detected in the gonads, suggesting a low-to-minimal risk of germline transmission or direct effects on the reproductive system.
LUXTURNA® vector shedding and biodistribution were assessed in 29 subjects from the phase 3 clinical trial. Tear, serum, and whole blood samples were collected on days 1 and 3 post-injection and analyzed. Low-level vector shedding occurred in tears from the treated eye in 45% of the subjects, and in the untreated eye in 7% of the subjects. Overall, these findings suggested that vector shedding is transient and primarily restricted to the treated eye. Overall systemic exposure was low.

Safety evaluation

Adverse events during clinical trials
The safety profile of LUXTURNA® has been evaluated through clinical trials, where it was generally well tolerated across all phases (Table 1). Ocular adverse reactions were reported in 66% of subjects, with mild-to-moderate adverse events, such as conjunctival hyperemia (22%), cataract formation (20%), and transient elevations in intraocular pressure (15%), being the most common findings. Rare complications, such as retinal tears, macular holes, pain, irritation, inflammation, and corneal stroma thinning, were reported in fewer than 7% of participants. Serious adverse events were rare, with foveal thinning, endophthalmitis, retinal hemorrhage, and foveal dehiscence observed in approximately 1%–2% of cases. Notably, no cytotoxic T-cell responses were detected, even after administration to the contralateral eye, and most inflammatory events were mild and reversible, likely due to prophylactic corticosteroid use. These findings demonstrated the safety and efficacy of subretinally-administered AAV2-based RPE65 gene therapy, marking a major advancement in the development of gene therapies for IRDs.

Postmarketing data
Postmarketing studies have demonstrated the long-term efficacy of LUXTURNA®, with approximately 70% of patients maintaining their MLMT performance at the lowest light level (1 lux) for 3–4 years post-treatment.
However, postmarketing surveillance has revealed new safety signals not captured during the clinical trial phase. Among these, chorioretinal atrophy (CRA) has emerged as a recurrent and clinically significant adverse event. CRA was subsequently added to the FDA label on June 8th of 2022 following accumulating real-world evidence and retrospective analyses. Similarly, the EMA updated the label and reported an increase in CRA incidence from 15.4% at baseline to 55.6% one-year post-treatment, including cases with foveal involvement.
A multitude of studies have since confirmed CRA in 28%–50% of treated eyes, with the highest rates seen in school-aged and young adult populations. Researchers have identified three CRA patterns: perifoveal, nummular, and touchdown, with perifoveal CRA progressing the fastest and touchdown the slowest. Notably, most studies reported that CRA development did not impact the vision gains of these patients. One study reported that CRA often develops in retinal areas that initially show functional improvement after treatment, suggesting a mismatch between restored function and sustained metabolic capacity of transduced cells. Further research is needed to better understand the cellular metabolic needs in the retinal microenvironment to support AAV episome expression and to develop strategies to mitigate metabolic insufficiencies. Despite structural changes, rod function remained preserved on full-field stimulus testing. Using dark-adapted chromatic perimetry, scotopic chromatic pupil campimetry, and fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging, the study showed that CRA occurred in regions with many rescued rods, but not where rod sensitivity gains were highest. The fovea was typically spared, explaining the preservation of visual acuity and contrast sensitivity. These findings support a metabolic origin of CRA rather than direct toxicity or photoreceptor loss. Additionally, a comparative study of eyes treated with different AAV2-based vectors found that CRA patterns varied with vector formulation and delivery method, suggesting that vector-specific or surgical factors may contribute to CRA development.
To further evaluate these emerging safety concerns, we developed a custom real-world pharmacovigilance analysis pipeline using the U.S. FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). This computational pipeline was implemented in Python and designed specifically for this review. FAERS data from quarter 1 of 2018 through quarter 2 of 2025 were analyzed to quantify the strength of association between LUXTURNA® and reported ocular adverse events. Case reports were deduplicated by matching primary ID, normalized drug name, route of administration, and dosage, resulting in the exclusion of 38400 duplicate entries identified as likely identical reports. Adverse event terms were grouped into clinically meaningful categories (1 and Table 2). This analysis also considered the different drug names (LUXTURNA, Voretigene Neparvovec, Voretigene Neparvovec-rzyl). Additionally, since full access to EudraVigilance data was not available to us, we relied on reported case counts. Using a custom Python script, we applied a similar deduplication approach and calculated the frequency of each reported adverse event.
Disproportionality analyses were performed for reports in the FAERS database. For each adverse event group, a two-by-two contingency table was constructed to compare the frequency of reports involving LUXTURNA® with those for other drug reports in the FAERS database. From these data, disproportionality metrics were calculated, including the reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), and relative reporting ratio (RRR), all with 95% confidence intervals (Figure 2). Statistical significance for the ROR was assessed using Fisher’s exact test as well as chi-square testing, with and without Yates’ continuity correction (Table 3).
This analysis identified CRA as the most prominent adverse event, with an ROR of 2930.5 (95% CI: 2167.8–3961.6), far exceeding the conventional threshold of 2.0 typically used to define disproportionate reporting. High reporting odds were also observed for retinal tears/detachments (ROR = 171.3), intraocular pressure changes (ROR = 103.0), eye hemorrhage (ROR = 53.5), eye inflammation or infection (ROR = 34.1), vision issues (ROR = 24.0), lenticular changes (ROR =17.6), eye pain/discomfort/irritation (ROR = 17.5), and procedural/device-related issues (ROR = 14.4) (Figure 1 and Table 3). Most reported events in the European database were similar to the FAERS, where CRA (21.3% of the total number), vision issues (19.5%), retinal tears/detachments (11.6%), and intraocular pressure changes (8.5%) were most prevalent in the reports (Table 3).
When applied to small data sets, frequentist disproportionality metrics, such as ROR, can grossly overestimate the reporting odds. Therefore, to account for bias associated with the small number of reports for LUXTURNA®, the Information Component (IC) was calculated. IC is a Bayesian shrinkage estimator applied to pharmacovigilance analyses making it useful for small data sets. IC quantifies disproportionality between observed and expected reporting of drug and adverse reaction, with a positive IC value indicating that the adverse reaction is reported more than expected. The IC results closely mirrored the ROR analysis (Figure 2B), confirming CRA and other ocular events as disproportionately reported (Figure 2B,D). Procedural/device-related issues produced a slightly negative IC signal (IC025 = −0.28), indicating reporting for LUXTURNA® similar to that for other drugs. Neurologic/psychiatric events and drug effectiveness issues similarly were associated with slightly less frequent reporting (Figure 2D).
Studies continue to expand the safety profile of LUXTURNA®. While visual acuity outcomes vary across patients, most report improved retinal sensitivity and structural stability, with CRA emerging in 13%–50% of treated eyes. Additionally, rare cases of foveal detachment following treatment have been reported.
Together, these results confirm that while LUXTURNA® demonstrates a favorable safety profile in controlled trial settings, real-world data highlight a clinically meaningful risk of CRA, retinal tears/detachments, intraocular pressure changes, and eye hemorrhage, eye inflammation or infection, vision issues, lenticular changes, eye pain/discomfort/irritation, and issues related to the procedure. Therefore, ongoing monitoring is warranted. The temporal and anatomical pattern of CRA development raises important mechanistic questions regarding the interplay between vector delivery, retinal metabolism, and immune-mediated processes. Further studies are needed to determine whether CRA represents a direct consequence of AAV2 vector biology, surgical trauma, or a secondary effect of rapid photoreceptor reactivation in metabolically vulnerable tissue.

Safety in special populations
LUXTURNA® is not indicated for infants under 12 months due to ongoing retinal cell proliferation that could dilute or eliminate the vector. Clinical data on use in pregnant or breastfeeding individuals, patients >65 years, or concurrent use with other medications are lacking. The PERCEIVE post-marketing registry documented three pregnancy events (two female recipients and one male partner of a treated patient), but outcome data were not collected due to a lack of consent for follow-up. Preclinical reproductive toxicity studies in nonhuman primates have shown no adverse effects, and biodistribution studies have demonstrated the absence of vector DNA in gonadal tissues, suggesting a low likelihood of germline transmission. However, due to insufficient human data, both the FDA and EMA advise avoiding pregnancy and breastfeeding during and shortly after treatment.

Drug–drug interactions
No drug–drug interaction studies have been conducted with LUXTURNA®, and none have been reported in clinical or post-marketing settings. Given its local ocular administration, minimal systemic exposure, and lack of systemic metabolism, pharmacokinetic interactions are unlikely. Nonetheless, caution may be advisable when co-administering systemic immunomodulatory agents, as their interaction with AAV-based gene therapies remains poorly characterized.

Comparison with other drugs
At present, LUXTURNA® is the only FDA-approved therapy administered via the subretinal route; consequently, comparative safety assessments with other agents utilizing this route of administration are not feasible. Similarly, LUXTURNA® remains the sole approved gene therapy for IRD.
LUXTURNA® was the first FDA-approved AAV-based gene therapy for a monogenic retinal disease, priced at approximately $425 000 per eye. Despite the high cost, it is marketed as a one-time treatment offering a durable benefit. However, patients currently have no alternative treatment options, as alternative care remains limited to supportive measures such as low-vision aids and mobility training.
Several experimental therapies are in development, including optogenetic gene therapies and gene augmentation strategies such as AGTC-501 for X-linked retinitis pigmentosa. Unfortunately, none are yet approved for RPE65 deficiency. The Argus II retinal prosthesis, once offered for around $150 000 (excluding surgery and rehabilitation), was discontinued due to financial difficulties and a shift toward developing neuro-stimulation technology.
Other approved AAV-based gene therapies are similarly priced or even more costly. Glybera® (alipogene tiparvovec), approved only in Europe for lipoprotein lipase deficiency, was withdrawn due to low demand and a price of €1.1 million (~$1.3 million) per treatment. Zolgensma® (onasemnogene abeparvovec-xioi), an AAV9-based systemic therapy for spinal muscular atrophy, remains the most expensive drug globally at approximately $2.1 million (~$2.5 million) per dose. It may, however, be cost-effective compared to lifelong treatments such as Spinraza® (nusinersen). These examples illustrate both the promise and the economic complexities of AAV-based therapies for rare monogenic diseases.

Benefit-Risk assessment
LUXTURNA® offers the potential for sustained visual improvement in patients with confirmed biallelic RPE65 mutations, a small subset of individuals with inherited retinal disease. In the absence of other treatment options, most individuals become legally blind in the second or third decade.
Safety data from preclinical studies, clinical trials, and pharmacovigilance analyses conducted and reviewed in this study indicate that most adverse events are ocular and procedure-related. Despite reporting of more serious complications, including retinal tears, these adverse events are uncommon and manageable by ophthalmologic care.
Therefore, given the magnitude of clinical benefit in the absence of alternative treatments and the low potential for adverse outcomes, the overall benefit–risk profile of LUXTURNA® is considered favorable for the eligible patient population.

Conclusion
Analyses of adverse event databases and post-marketing studies reveal a more complex safety profile for LUXTURNA® than initially reported in clinical trials. While approximately 70% of treated patients maintain meaningful functional vision (as assessed by MLMT at 1 lux) over 3–4 years, emerging evidence highlights a broader spectrum of ocular adverse events, with CRA being the most prominent long-term concern. Data from the FAERS database show an exceptionally high reporting odds ratio (ROR = 2930.5) and information component (IC = 6.86) for CRA, significantly surpassing conventional pharmacovigilance reporting odds thresholds. These findings are consistent with EudraVigilance data and retrospective clinical reports, which indicate CRA in 13%–50% of treated eyes occurring most frequently in younger patients and typically localized near the injection site. Despite the occurrence of CRA, central visual acuity is often preserved. Nevertheless, the risk of worsened vision post-treatment persists, attributed to other serious ocular adverse events, including retinal tears or detachment, elevated intraocular pressure, intraocular hemorrhage, inflammation or infection, lenticular changes, and various vision disturbances. No systemic toxicity or drug–drug interactions have been observed to date, consistent with LUXTURNA®’s localized mode of administration. However, safety data in special populations remain limited.
LUXTURNA® represents a landmark advancement as the first FDA-approved gene therapy for RPE65-related IRDs, addressing a critical unmet need for patients with progressive vision loss. This gene therapy offers a novel mechanism of action by targeting the underlying genetic cause, thereby enabling improvements in retinal sensitivity and functional vision that were previously unattainable.
Consequently, LUXTURNA® has significantly increased genetic testing for RPE65 mutations in visually impaired patients, leading to the identification of eligible candidates for treatment. Non-eligible patients are now often referred to clinical trials for alternative retinal gene therapies.
Despite its demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in clinical studies, it is important to note that a subset of subjects who received LUXTURNA® experienced modest or no visual improvements, with even a few cases of worsened vision. This raises important considerations regarding the cost-effectiveness of the treatment, especially given the substantial financial investment required. In the United States, LUXTURNA® is priced at $850,000 for treating both eyes, igniting debate regarding its affordability and accessibility. To address these concerns, Spark Therapeutics has introduced various financial assistance programs. These include coverage for travel expenses, installment payment options, and an outcome-based reimbursement model in collaboration with health insurers. Under this model, payment depends on treatment success, evaluated through improvements on the FST tests at 30 and 90 days, and again at 30 months post-treatment. Despite these efforts, critics like David Mitchell, president of Patients for Affordable Drugs, argue that these payment models obscure the underlying issue of excessively high pricing. The Institute for Clinical and Economic Review has suggested that a cost between $214,553 and $755,633 would make LUXTURNA® more cost-effective. Its high cost also sets a precedent for future gene therapies, raising concerns about the financial burden on patients, insurance companies, and the healthcare system as similarly expensive treatments emerge, which may limit accessibility to these treatments.
Currently, 19 specialized centers across the U.S. are authorized to perform LUXTURNA® subretinal injections. While these centers ensure high-quality care, geographic and logistical constraints may limit access, particularly for patients in rural or underserved regions. Furthermore, data on additional costs such as liability insurance remain limited.
Critical data gaps persist, including long-term durability of therapeutic effects, comprehensive patient-reported outcomes, and elucidation of the mechanistic basis of adverse events such as CRA. Addressing these gaps through continued postmarketing and real-world studies is essential to optimizing treatment protocols, thoroughly informing patients about the risks, and informing clinical decision-making.
LUXTURNA®’s approval significantly impacted the treatment and research of rare diseases, particularly retinal disorders. The clinical trials conducted between 2012 and 2017 demonstrated the feasibility of developing a safe and effective gene therapy, even with a small participant cohort and over a relatively short timeframe. These trials also set a precedent by including pediatric participants, underscoring the importance of early intervention in congenital genetic conditions. Building on this success, LUXTURNA® has catalyzed the development of new treatments for retinal degenerative diseases, with approximately 200 ongoing clinical trials exploring innovative approaches such as non-viral delivery methods, optogenetics, alternative viral vectors, and novel administration techniques. Although no additional gene therapies for IRDs have yet been approved, these efforts offer promising avenues for broader treatment options. Advances in AAV vector engineering further hold potential to enhance retinal transduction efficiency and reduce immunogenicity. Novel capsid modifications like AAV2.CPP1 may enable less invasive intravitreal delivery by overcoming anatomical barriers such as the inner limiting membrane.
Over the next five years, LUXTURNA® is expected to maintain its pioneering status while driving the development of next-generation gene therapies that are safer, more effective, and more affordable. Its impact will likely extend beyond clinical efficacy to influence regulatory policies, reimbursement frameworks, and healthcare strategies, ultimately advancing the field of genetic medicine.
