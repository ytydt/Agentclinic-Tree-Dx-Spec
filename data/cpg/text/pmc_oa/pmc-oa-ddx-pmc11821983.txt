Aerobic Exercise and Metformin: A Dual Approach to Enhancing Glycemic Maintenance in Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a widespread metabolic condition characterized by elevated glucose levels followed by deficiency in insulin secretion. Metformin notably decreased the incidence of T2DM by 31% and it exerts its effects through various signaling pathways. Databases searched included PubMed, Google Scholar, and Scopus from 2000 to 2024. One of the primary mechanisms involves AMPK activation which causes reduced lipogenesis and improved fatty acid oxidation in the liver and muscles. Key molecules affected by metformin include acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP-1c), both involved in lipid synthesis regulation. Aerobic exercise has also emerged as a crucial component in managing T2DM due to its improved effects on hyperglycemia and insulin sensitivity. Key signaling pathways affected in T2DM include the PI3K/Akt, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and MAPK/ERK pathways which play essential roles in regulating glucose homeostasis, glycogenesis, and insulin secretion. When comparing the mechanisms and efficacy of aerobic exercise and metformin, it becomes evident that aerobic exercise primarily enhances physical fitness and metabolic function, while metformin exerts its effects through biochemical pathways involving AMPK activation. Aerobic exercise and metformin are effective for managing T2DM, though they operate through different mechanisms. Regular aerobic exercise improves insulin sensitivity, enhances cardiovascular health, and promotes weight loss, while metformin primarily decreases hepatic gluconeogenesis and enhances insulin secretion. Understanding the intricate signaling pathways affected by metformin and aerobic exercise provides valuable insights into its mechanisms of action and clinical implications for treating diabetic patients effectively.

INTRODUCTION
The global prevalence of diabetes is 10.5% in adults and is estimated to affect approximately 530 million individuals in the world. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic condition characterized by chronic elevated glucose resulting from impaired secretion and/or action insulin action. Glycemic control is crucial in managing T2DM to prevent microvascular complications and cardiovascular risk. Impaired insulin action is an essential factor in the pathophysiology of T2DM, traditionally associated with obesity as a significant risk factor. However, there is a growing trend of T2DM diagnoses among non-obese individuals, particularly prominent in some Asian countries where it can account for up to 80% of cases. These non-obese individuals face higher mortality rates than their obese counterparts, indicating unique challenges in managing their condition effectively. Research suggests that lean individuals with T2DM may exhibit more severe insulin secretory defects compared to obese individuals, showcasing the complexity of this disease presentation in non-obese populations. Two common treatments for T2DM are aerobic exercise and metformin. Aerobic exercise improves insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake in skeletal muscles, while metformin primarily reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis and increases peripheral glucose utilization. This essay aims to explore the individual mechanisms of action of aerobic exercise and metformin in T2DM management, discuss their synergistic effects when combined, and present clinical evidence supporting their efficacy.
A search was conducted on PubMed, Google Scholar, and Scopus to locate pertinent literature. The search utilized the following keywords: metformin, aerobic exercise, aerobic training, diabetes, and T2DM. Only articles published in English within the last 24 years were considered.

SIGNALING PATHWAYS AND MECHANISM ACTION OF METFORMIN
Metformin is a first-line oral medication for T2DM through its ability to lower glucose levels without causing hypoglycemia. It exerts its therapeutic effects through various signaling pathways that influence key aspects of glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and inflammation. The primary mechanism underlying metformin includes inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis and increasing peripheral glucose and fatty acids uptake. By activating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) in adipose tissue and muscle, metformin promotes the oxidation of fatty acids. This decreases lipid accumulation in tissues, which is associated with improved insulin sensitivity. On the other hand, Metformin has been shown to change the composition of the gut microbiome, increasing the abundance of certain beneficial bacteria while decreasing others. This shift in the microbiome can impact metabolic processes. The changes in the microbiome may lead to increased production of SCFAs, which are important for gut health and have been shown to enhance insulin sensitivity and reduce inflammation. By modulating the gut microbiome, metformin may help reduce intestinal inflammation and improve overall metabolic health, which can be particularly beneficial for individuals with T2DM.
Recent findings have shown the effects of metformin on these signaling pathways and have shown promising results in terms of glycemic maintenance and general health outcomes for diabetic patients. Foretz et al. demonstrated that AMPK activation by metformin promotes glucose uptake in skeletal muscle cells independent of insulin signaling pathways. Additionally, metformin reduces the absorption of carbohydrates from the gastrointestinal tract, leading to lower postprandial glucose levels. It can alter the composition and levels of bile acids produced in the liver. Bile acids are known to act as signaling molecules that can influence glucose metabolism and lipid homeostasis. Some bile acids activate specific G Protein-Coupled Receptors (GPCRs), such as TGR5 (Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5), which can enhance insulin secretion, promote energy expenditure, and modulate inflammation. Metformin may influence the FXR pathway, which plays a critical role in regulating bile acid synthesis, glucose homeostasis, and lipid metabolism. Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR) activation can lead to lower hepatic glucose production.
Also, metformin suppresses mitochondrial respiration through inhibition of complex I within the electron transport chain, leading to decreased ATP production that triggers an energy stress response. This results in reduced hepatic glucose output through the inhibition of key enzymes of gluconeogenesis. Additionally, metformin improves insulin sensitivity by promoting GLUT4 translocation to the cell membrane, facilitating glucose uptake by peripheral tissues. Another important mechanism through which metformin operates is via inhibition of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling. By blocking mTOR activity, metformin modulates protein synthesis and cell growth processes that are dysregulated in conditions like diabetes. This dual modulation of AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition by metformin highlights its multifaceted approach toward improving metabolic dysfunction in diabetic individuals. Additionally, metformin has been shown to elevate the Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) circulating levels. Enhanced GLP-1 levels stimulate pancreatic insulin secretion following meals, thereby reducing postprandial blood glucose levels. It plays a vital role in modulating key enzymes involved in gluconeogenesis. By influencing the activity levels of enzymes such as phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase, metformin contributes to its overarching mechanism of regulating blood glucose levels. The inhibition of these pivotal enzymes by metformin underscores its significance in curbing excessive endogenous glucose production by the liver (Fig. 1, Tables 1 and 2).

THE OPTIMAL DOSE OF METFORMIN IN MANAGING T2DM
The optimal dose of metformin to achieve the best clinical effects in managing T2DM can vary depending on individual patient conditions such as the severity of hyperglycemia, tolerance to the medication, and presence of any comorbid conditions. However, most clinical guidelines and evidence suggest the following dosing strategy for achieving effective glycemic control while minimizing side effects. Typically, patients start with 500 mg/day once or 500 mg/day twice to decrease the risk of gastrointestinal complications. The dose can be increased by 500 mg every 1–2 weeks based on tolerance and glycemic response. The range of 1,000–2,000 mg/day is commonly reported to provide optimal clinical effects for most patients. The dose of 1,000 mg/day twice (2,000 mg/day) is considered by many clinicians as the highest effective dose for achieving significant glycemic control without unacceptable side effects. The maximum FDA-approved dose is typically administered as 850 mg three times/day (2,550 mg/day). Gastrointestinal side effects (nausea, diarrhea, abdominal discomfort) are dose-dependent and are more common at higher doses. So, gradual dose titration can help minimize these side effects. Metformin is discontinuation in patients with significant renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2). Dose adjustments are required for patients with moderate renal impairment (eGFR 30–45 mL/min/1.73 m2). The maximum recommended dose in this group may be lower, and metformin should be used with caution.

DURATION OF METFORMIN USE IN T2DM PATIENTS
The duration of metformin use required to achieve the best clinical effects in managing T2DM depends on several factors, including the time needed to reach target glycemic control and the maintenance of long-term benefits.
I. Initial Phase (first few weeks to months): metformin starts lowering blood glucose levels within days of initiation, with noticeable improvement in glycemic control often observed within the first few weeks. The full therapeutic effect on HbA1c levels may take up to 3–4 months, as HbA1c indicates average glycemia over approximately three months.
II. Maintenance Phase (long-term use): long-term, consistent use of metformin is crucial for maintaining its glycemic and cardiometabolic benefits. Long-term use is associated with improved lipid profiles, reduced risk of cardiovascular events, lower incidence of microvascular complications, and potentially reduced mortality from cardiovascular diseases. UKPDS (United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study) demonstrated that intensive glucose control with metformin led to reduced diabetes-related endpoints over a median follow-up of 10 years.

MECHANISMS ACTION OF AEROBIC EXERCISE
Aerobic exercise plays a significant role in improving glycemic control in individuals with T2DM through various mechanisms. Regular aerobic exercise increases energy expenditure and activates AMPK signaling pathways similar to metformin. The activation of AMPK during exercise promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and glucose uptake in skeletal muscle cells. These adaptations enhance insulin sensitivity by increasing the expression of GLUT4 transporters on muscle cell membranes, allowing for more efficient clearance of circulating glucose. Particularly PPAR-gamma (PPAR-γ), which is involved in glucose metabolism and fat storage. Aerobic exercise may influence PPAR signaling, promoting insulin sensitivity and improving lipid profiles. Additionally, IRS proteins are critical for insulin signaling. Exercise can enhance the insulin signaling pathway through the upregulation of IRS-1, improving glucose uptake in muscle cells. It has shown that myokines such as IL-6 and irisin are secreted during exercise and can affect glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity positively (Fig. 2, Tables 2 and 3).
Aerobic exercise has a range of beneficial effects on numerous body tissues, involving both brown adipose tissue (BAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), particularly in the context of T2DM. It can stimulate the activity of BAT, which is involved in non-shivering thermogenesis. This process helps in burning calories and improving metabolic health. Also, it may increase the capacity of BAT to oxidize fatty acids, contributing to better lipid metabolism and reducing excess fat stores, increasing the expression of uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) in BAT, which promotes energy dissipation. Regular aerobic exercise can decrease the inflammatory markers related to SAT, ameliorate metabolic profiles and decrease the risk of insulin resistance, cause alterations in the distribution of lipids in SAT, and reduce visceral fat and fat mass. It has shown that aerobic exercise are more efficient in improving biochemical factors, lipid profile, and body weight in diabetic rats compared to treatment with metformin.
Aerobic exercise can increase adiponectin, modulate resistin, and reduce caspase-1 and IL-1β in adipose tissue (Fig. 3).

BENEFICIAL AND ADVERSE EFFECTS OF AEROBIC EXERCISE BASED ON INTENSITY OF TRAINING
The intensity of aerobic exercise that has beneficial effects varies but is generally categorized into three levels: low, moderate, and high. 1) Low-intensity aerobic Exercise such as walking and leisurely cycling, improves basic aerobic fitness, helps with weight management, reduces stress, and is suitable for beginners or individuals with certain health conditions, 2) Moderate-Intensity Aerobic Exercise (about 50%–70% HRmax) such as brisk walking, swimming, and moderate-paced cycling, reduces the risk of cardiovascular disease, helps control blood glucose levels, improves lipid profiles, assists in weight loss, and enhances mood and energy levels. Guidelines recommend performing at least 150 minutes a week of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise spread over several days. It is crucial to tailor the intensity and duration of exercises according to individual fitness levels and health conditions to prevent injuries or adverse effects on health, and 3) High-Intensity Aerobic Exercise (about 70%–85% HRmax) such as running, fast cycling, rowing, high-intensity interval training (HIIT), provides more effective benefits in cardiorespiratory health, helps in more rapid weight loss, improves aerobic capacity and endurance, and can provide similar benefits to moderate-intensity exercise in a shorter amount of time. Guidelines suggest engaging in at least 75 minutes per week if performed at high intensity.
Although some studies showed the beneficial impacts of high-intensity aerobic in T2DM, it may also pose some adverse effects, particularly for those with T2DM or Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM). High-intensity exercise can cause a significant reduction in glucose concentration (hypoglycemia), increase the risk of musculoskeletal injuries and cardiac complications, sweating, and fluid loss, and deplete glycogen stores. A study by Riahy S. showed that moderate (walking/running continuously for 47 min at 60%–70% HRmax) or high (4×4 min at 85%–95% HRmax, 3 min of active rest, 50%–60% HRmax in between) intensity aerobic exercise three sessions/week for 12 weeks, was associated with improved insulin resistance and decreased fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and myostatin in men with T2DM. DURATION OF AEROBIC EXERCISE IN DIABETIC PATIENTS
The duration of aerobic exercise recommended for diabetic patients varies, but general guidelines suggest that regular physical activity is crucial for managing diabetes effectively. Patients can have 30 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per session (five sessions/week) or 25 minutes of high-intensity aerobic exercise per session (three sessions/week). Those who are already active can aim for longer durations or more intense sessions, such as 60 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise most days. It has shown that interval training programs ranging from 5 days to 12 months have a positive impact on health conditions. On the other hand, in the study by Kobayashi Y. on normal-weight diabetic patients, results showed that strength training and/or aerobic exercise for 9 months had no significant effect on HbA1c level reduction.

IMPACT OF AEROBIC EXERCISE ON METABOLIC, INFLAMMATORY, AND LIPID PROFILE BIOMARKERS
Aerobic exercise has a significant influence on various biomarkers, which can offer insights into preventive health strategies. It can lower triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), C-reactive Protein (CRP), and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and apolipoprotein B while increasing high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and apolipoprotein A-I levels. By positively influencing these biomarkers, aerobic exercise can help in the prevention and management of long-term conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and obesity, thereby promoting overall health.

SYNERGISTIC EFFECTS OF AEROBIC EXERCISE AND METFORMIN
The choice between or co-administration of exercise and metformin should be personalized based on the patient's overall health, fitness level, potential side effects, and specific treatment goals. Combining lifestyle interventions (like regular aerobic exercise) with pharmacotherapy (metformin) is often the most effective approach for long-term diabetes management. When combined, aerobic exercise with metformin demonstrates synergistic effects on glycemic control beyond what each intervention can achieve alone. The combination treatment leads to further reductions in blood hyperglycemic peaks due to enhanced peripheral insulin sensitivity from both interventions working together synergistically at different sites within the body - muscle tissue (aerobic exercise) and liver tissue (metformin). Moreover, combination therapy has been determined to enhance beta-cell function and delay disease progression by addressing multiple aspects of T2DM pathophysiology simultaneously.

SIDE EFFECTS
Both aerobic exercise and metformin have been shown to be potent at decreasing glucose concentration; however, their side effect profiles differ significantly. Metformin may cause gastrointestinal disturbances such as diarrhea or nausea, whereas aerobic exercise carries a lower risk of adverse effects when performed correctly.

CONCLUSION
Despite its established efficacy as a frontline therapy for T2DM, challenges remain in optimizing treatment outcomes with metformin due to inter-individual variability in drug response and potential side effects such as gastrointestinal disturbances. Future research directions aimed at addressing these challenges could involve exploring personalized medicine approaches based on genetic variations influencing drug metabolism or developing novel formulations to enhance drug tolerability. While both aerobic exercise and Metformin have individual merits when it comes to combating T2DM, the synergy achieved by combining them enhances their effectiveness and offers a comprehensive approach to managing glycemic control and improving outcomes for individuals with T2DM. Clinical evidence supporting this thesis statement includes studies demonstrating greater improvements in HbA1C levels, insulin sensitivity, cardiovascular fitness, and quality-of-life measures when using these two modalities together. Overall, combining aerobic exercise with Metformin presents a promising therapeutic strategy for optimizing glycemic control and metabolic health in individuals living with T2DM. Looking toward future research directions, there remains ample opportunity to further elucidate the intricacies underlying metformin's effects on liver enzymes and lipid metabolism. Future studies could explore novel targets within these pathways that may enhance treatment outcomes or mitigate potential side effects associated with long-term metformin use. Additionally, investigating ways to optimize patient-specific responses to metformin through pharmacogenomic approaches represents a promising avenue for advancing personalized medicine in diabetes care.

LIMITATION
The duration of the study may be too short to assess the long-term effects of combining aerobic exercise with metformin on glycemic control and other health outcomes. Longer studies are needed to evaluate sustainability and potential long-term benefits or side effects. Also, Differences in the type, intensity, frequency, and duration of aerobic exercise can lead to inconsistent results. Standardization of exercise interventions is necessary for comparability across studies. Furthermore, dose responses of metformin in response to different intensity and duration of exercise can also have interesting results which should focused. Future studies should compare the efficacy of this dual approach against other exercise modalities or pharmacological therapies to establish optimal treatment strategies for T2DM management.
