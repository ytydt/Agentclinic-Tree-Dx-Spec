Optimal approach to standardized documentation in epilepsy clinics: A scoping review
Abstract
Clear documentation and transfer of information between health care providers is key to ensuring the delivery of high‐quality patient care. Our aim was to determine how to optimize and standardize physician documentation in outpatient epilepsy clinics as well as to highlight challenges and barriers to their implementation. We conducted a scoping review of studies implementing standardization and optimization of physician documentation within outpatient epilepsy clinics. The study is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews. The following databases were searched from inception to March 2025: MEDLINE, Embase, APA PsycInfo, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library. All abstracts, full texts, and data charting were completed in duplicate. The search yielded 10 268 studies, of which 16 met eligibility criteria. Studies were primarily from the United States (n = 12, 75.0%) and focused on adult practices (n = 9, 56.25%). Studies were quality improvement framework/consensus guideline investigations (n = 9, 56.3%), observational audits/cross‐sectional designs (n = 2, 12.5%), and retrospective cohort/chart reviews (n = 2, 12.5%), followed by several mixed‐methods development, observational field study, and observational toolkit implementation designs (n = 1, 6.25% each). Most clinical notes were in electronic medical records (n = 14, 87.5%) using free‐text fields (n = 4, 25.0%), structured fields (n = 2, 12.5%), or hybrid approaches (n = 7, 43.8%). Common Data Elements included seizure information and treatment counseling information. Outcomes associated with standardized note implementation included reduced epilepsy‐related adverse events, better seizure control, and more consistent documentation of pertinent patient information. Highlighted challenges to implementation included workflow disruptions, hesitation to initial uptake, and cost‐related barriers such as information technology support. Implementation of standardized documentation was associated with fewer adverse events and better seizure control. Future efforts should prioritize inclusive design, have expanded quality indicators, be easy to use at point of care, and have robust evaluation metrics to optimize their utility for epilepsy care.

Key points
Structured, EMR‐embedded templates dominate in published studies.
Eighty‐one percent of templates evaluated in this review align with AAN Epilepsy Quality Measures; that is, they improved seizure tracking but also captured ASM side effects and counseling.
Documentation improved seizure type capture up to 90%–100% and counseling and side‐effects to 40%–60%.
Documentation halved time to treatment for infantile spasms, reduced epilepsy‐related admissions, and improved 1‐year seizure control.
Clinician acceptance is generally high, but uptake hinges on workflow fit.

INTRODUCTION
Accurate and comprehensive clinical documentation is a key component of high‐quality care. It supports effective communication between all members of the health care team, secures continuity of care, facilitates informed clinical decision‐making, and enables access to long‐term clinical data, which facilitates quality improvement, research, and medicolegal accountability. In the field of epilepsy, a complex neurological disorder requiring long‐term management and a multitude of health care providers, high‐quality documentation is especially crucial to facilitating smooth care and communication. The care of patients with epilepsy requires multidisciplinary teams, multiple treatment approaches, and extended monitoring of symptoms, medication adherence, side effects, psychosocial factors, and comorbid conditions. Given that background, inconsistencies or omissions in clinical documentation can lead to suboptimal treatment, fragmented care, and avoidable complications.
Outpatient epilepsy clinics are a critical point of care delivery for people living with epilepsy. They serve as the main sites for confirming diagnoses, starting or modifying treatment plans, and providing patient education and counseling. As such, the clinical notes generated in these settings are integral to the patient's ongoing management. However, documentation practices across clinics and providers vary extensively. Notes may differ in structure, level of detail, and the type of information captured, leading to inconsistencies that may impact care quality and safety. The process of care transitions between providers and health care settings, and across different life stages makes documentation gaps more likely to occur.
The growing adoption of electronic medical records (EMRs) systems provides opportunities to enhance physician documentation quality through standardization. EMRs provide health care professionals with direct access to embed documentation templates alongside clinical decision support tools and structured data fields into their clinical workflows. Such tools can support more consistent recording of essential information (e.g., seizure frequency, medication changes, side effects, and lifestyle counseling), facilitate guideline adherence, and reduce the cognitive and administrative burden on clinicians. Standardized documentation tools for epilepsy clinics exist in various forms, yet their actual implementation remains inconsistent, and their effectiveness is poorly understood.
To address this gap, we performed a scoping review to examine how documentation standardization and optimization have been implemented and evaluated in outpatient epilepsy clinics. Our objectives were to (1) identify and describe study characteristics of interventions used to improve physician documentation; (2) describe the captured data elements of these interventions; (3) summarize reported outcomes, including impacts on documentation quality and patient care; and (4) highlight challenges and recommendations to successful implementation. This review of current practices aims to guide upcoming initiatives that will enhance clinical documentation to improve epilepsy care safety and effectiveness and operational efficiency.
Operationally, we have defined optimization as “an ongoing process making the implemented [EMR] more efficient and usable for end user clinicians that results in improved efficiency in clinicians' practice and satisfaction” and standardization as “the process of enhancing the structural and linguistic integrity of a clinical note without altering its clinical content. Standardization focuses on improving format, spelling, grammar, terminology, usability, interoperability, and readability.”

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protocol and registration
This scoping review was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews. The protocol was registered with OpenScience https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/H6SVW. A scoping review was chosen over a systematic review to enable a more exploratory evaluation that could broadly map the existing literature, clarify key concepts, and identify gaps in the field.

Literature search strategy and inclusion criteria
We included studies that met the following inclusion criteria: (1) reported specifically on epilepsy outpatient clinics; (2) focused on physician (attending or resident) documentation; and (3) involved any form of optimization, standardization, or outcome improvement. To be explicitly in keeping with the population–concept–context framework, our population includes physicians working in outpatient epilepsy clinics (both adult and pediatric) and, indirectly, the patients with epilepsy whose care is influenced by physician documentation practices. Our concept is the optimization and standardization of physician documentation, including methods, tools, and frameworks aimed at improving documentation quality, completeness, and usability. Our context comprises outpatient epilepsy care settings, typically tertiary epilepsy centers, specialized neurology clinics, or academic institutions using electronic or hybrid documentation systems. All languages and study designs were included along with any form of documentation (i.e., electronic, paper, or hybrids). No date limits were applied. We excluded abstracts, conference proceedings, protocols, case series with fewer than 20 people, case reports, editorials, opinion pieces, dissertations, meta‐analyses, and systematic reviews. We opted to focus on physician documentation because physicians are typically responsible, in most settings, for final diagnostic decisions and management, and limiting the review to physicians minimized heterogeneity. Advanced practice providers and physicians differ in their training and may therefore use different documentation templates and workflows depending on their institution.

Search strategy
We searched MEDLINE, Embase, APA PsycInfo, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library to identify studies relevant for inclusion in this review (Supporting Information File 1). Two reviewers (S.M., M.Ki., B.J., A.Z., D.L.L., C.B.J., and/or N.J.) independently screened study abstracts. Two reviewers subsequently screened in duplicate the full text papers of included abstracts that (S.M. and N.J.). Conflicts were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers and the senior author (N.J.) where necessary.

Data charting and analyses
Data charting was completed by two reviewers (S.M., M.Ki.) for each of the included studies into a Microsoft Excel database. The data charting file was initially pilot tested by the two reviewers using the first three studies and enhanced after the pilot review. Extracted data included demographic information (i.e., location, population, etc.), practice type, study designs, data elements captured within the EMR, methods for standardizing/optimization documentation, the outcome from the initiative, physician satisfaction, and challenges to implementation (Supporting Information Form 2).
Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the following variables: country of study development, population (e.g., adults, pediatrics, both), sample size (i.e., number of notes, physicians, encounters), practice type (community clinic, private clinic, urban clinic, rural clinic), study design (e.g., observational, randomized controlled trial [RCT], cross sectional survey), type of note (e.g., paper vs. EMR), presence of a standardized EMR template, presence of structured versus free‐text fields, and whether there was integration of clinical decision support tools. Qualitative descriptions such as data elements captured (e.g., seizure types, adherence to American Academy of Neurology [AAN] Epilepsy Quality Measures, etc.), methods for standardizing/optimizing documentation, outcome from documentation improvement (e.g., accuracy, reduced diagnostic delays), physician satisfaction with new documentation tools, challenges faced during development/implementation, and recommendations for standardization were placed into tables, and recurrent and common aspects/themes between studies were summarized through descriptive statistics. The findings were synthesized using a narrative analysis process in which the studies were systematically broken down into key components of study characteristics, captured data elements, methods for standardizing or optimizing documentation, outcomes related to documentation implementation, and challenges.

RESULTS

Included studies
The full search strategy was run on September 22, 2024, and updated on March 31, 2025 (see Supporting Information File 1). The search yielded 9173 abstracts that were imported and automatically deduplicated by the software Covidence (Covidence Ltd.). In total, we screened 6547 abstracts, of which 6507 (99.4%) were excluded based on prespecified criteria in Supporting Information File 1. From this, 40 studies were eligible for full‐text review, of which 24 (60%) studies were excluded. The reason for exclusion was most commonly due to wrong study design (n = 12, 50%) and wrong article type (n = 5, 41.7%). Sixteen unique studies (40%) were included in our analysis (Figure 1).

Study characteristics
Of the 16 included studies, nine (56.3%) focused on adult epilepsy populations and five (31.3%) on pediatric populations, and two (12.5%) did not specify the target population. Studies were conducted across four countries: the United States (n = 12, 75.0%), Canada (n = 1, 6.25%), Mexico (n = 1, 6.3%), and Ireland (n = 2, 12.5%), with several of these studies involving cross‐national collaborations between Canada and the United States.
Most studies (n = 11, 68.8%) were conducted at tertiary care epilepsy centers. Additional representation included multicenter academic consortia (n = 3, 18.8%) and specialized outpatient neurology clinics (n = 2, 12.5%). Across all studies, the use of EMRs was nearly universal (n = 14, 87.5%), with one study reporting the use of both EMRs and paper charts (6.25%).
Among the 16 included studies, nine were different forms of quality improvement (QI) projects (n = 9, 56.3%), two were observational audits/cross‐sectional designs (n = 2, 12.5%), and two were retrospective cohort or chart reviews (n = 2, 12.5%), followed by a variety of mixed‐methods development (n = 1, 6.3%), observational field study (n = 1, 6.3%), and observational toolkit implementation designs (n = 1, 6.3%).
Regarding note structure, four studies used predominantly free‐text documentation (n = 4, 25.0%), two used structured fields (n = 2, 12.5%), and seven employed a hybrid approach using both structured and free‐text fields (n = 7, 43.8%). Furthermore, one study utilized the web‐based application tool NeuroMeasures outside the EMR (n = 1, 6.3%). The remaining two studies were QI frameworks that did not involve direct clinical documentation (n = 2, 12.5%).

Captured data elements
Across studies, documentation templates frequently captured core clinical variables relevant to epilepsy management. The most consistently included data elements were seizure type and frequency, captured in 15 studies (n = 15, 93.8%); medication usage and side effects, captured in 12 studies (n = 12, 75.0%); and epilepsy syndrome classification (n = 6, 37.5%). Several studies also included diagnostic test results, such as electroencephalography or imaging findings, and tracked comorbid conditions relevant to epilepsy (intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, psychiatric comorbidities).
Counseling topics were included in five templates, most commonly covering preconception counseling, sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), and general safety education. Structured tools such as NeuroMeasures markedly increased documentation of folic acid prescription and SUDEP counseling for women of childbearing potential, and a worksheet aligned to the AAN quality set boosted safety‐counseling rates in an adult clinic.
In pediatric‐focused documentation, developmentally tailored items (e.g., checkboxes for developmental delay, school‐related functioning, or transition planning, and free‐text space for caregiver concerns) were emphasized in both the national Common Data Elements (CDEs) template and the Pediatric Epilepsy Outcomes and Improvement Program (PEOIP) electronic note, which families reported helped communication with schools and advocacy groups.
Five initiatives embedded established quality frameworks. Most mapped fields to the AAN Epilepsy Quality Measures; others imported National Institutes of Health/National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NIH/NINDS) CDEs or International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) definitions during build‐out. Program‐specific tools such as NeuroMeasures and the pediatric PEOIP dashboard standardized longitudinal capture, enabled real‐time benchmarking, and triggered EMR decision support.

Standardizing/optimizing methods
Studies employed a variety of approaches to optimize and standardize epilepsy documentation. Common strategies included the development of structured templates, integration with existing EMR systems, and training sessions for clinicians. Several studies incorporated predefined fields, drop‐down menus, or SmartPhrases (shortcut to insert standardized text into documentation) to enhance consistency in note structure and promote adherence to documentation standards.
Ten initiatives emphasized iterative, user‐informed design, involving clinicians, caregivers, researchers, or multidisciplinary working groups to develop tools aligned with clinical workflows. Training and real‐time support were frequently paired with template rollouts to facilitate uptake. In some settings, mandatory fields or features such as “note lockout” until completion were used to improve compliance. Others leveraged automated data capture tools or quality dashboards (e.g., NeuroMeasures, structured flowsheets) to link documentation directly to performance metrics.
Templates were often aligned with external frameworks such as the AAN Epilepsy Quality Measures, CDEs, or institutional protocols. A few studies reported collaboration with EMR vendors or health systems (e.g., Epic, Cerner) to embed custom tools within existing infrastructure.

Outcomes from documentation implementation
Ten of the included studies (62.5%) reported outcomes related to documentation improvement. Several demonstrated significant increases in the documentation rates of core clinical variables. This included seizure type/frequency, antiseizure medication (ASM) side effects safety counseling, and preconception counseling, with improvements in some metrics exceeding 40–60 percentage points after documentation capture intervention. One study noted that structured documentation improved rates of quality‐of‐life assessments from <3% to >80%.
One study reported reduced delays in treatment, more efficient diagnostic workflows, and elimination of unnecessary repeat investigations. Improved documentation was also associated with reduced epilepsy‐related adverse hospitalizations (relative risk = .34, p = .01), better seizure control at 1 year (8.1% absolute risk reduction), and enhanced follow‐up outcomes such as improved medication adherence and epilepsy surgery referrals. Standardized templates also facilitated quality improvement tracking, enabled benchmarking of seizure outcomes across centers, and highlighted disparities in care by race, ethnicity, and income. Notably, the results among studies were reported with variable methods and statistical analyses, making their comparability challenging.
With respect to physician satisfaction, feedback was generally positive, although only five studies (31.3%) formally assessed this outcome. Providers reported that structured notes improved clinical efficiency, aided communication, and prompted clinically relevant questions. Multiple studies highlighted the usability of templates, with one reporting that clinicians retrieved documentation fields in less than 1 min. Reported barriers included initial workflow disruption, low uptake, and challenges with EMR navigation. Nevertheless, most providers preferred structured formats over dictation, with several noting improved treatment plan comprehension and longitudinal care continuity.

Challenges with implementation and recommendations for standardization
Challenges during the development and implementation of standardized documentation tools were reported in all 16 studies (100%). This included variability in provider uptake, particularly when use of the template was optional, and workflow disruptions from introducing new EMR components. Several studies highlighted resistance to adopting unfamiliar tools, especially when multiple individualized note templates already existed or there were limited training and support for implementation. Technical barriers were also frequently cited, including EMR integration issues, performance delays, and lack of alignment across institutional systems. Resource‐related limitations, such as programming costs, limited information technology (IT) support, and staffing constraints, were also commonly reported.
Documentation quality was hindered by inconsistencies in data entry, missing data on key clinical variables, and lack of structured fields for elements such as nonseizure symptoms, dietary adherence, adverse events, and quality of life. Additionally, some studies cited disparities in care documentation for high‐risk populations, including individuals with intellectual disabilities and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds such as African Americans, Hispanic populations, and those on Medicaid.
To address these issues, studies recommended several key standardization strategies. These included broader adoption of structured EMR fields, particularly aligned with frameworks like CDEs and QUIET (Quality Indicators in Epilepsy Treatment) indicators. Expansion of quality measures to capture underrepresented areas (e.g., mental health screening, self‐management, dietary efficacy) was suggested, alongside improved interoperability and multidisciplinary involvement in toolkit development. Other recommendations involved mandatory documentation fields for high‐priority data, prepopulated EMRs, and broader rollout of web‐based tools such as NeuroMeasures to reduce manual burden.

DISCUSSION
This scoping review synthesizes evidence from 16 studies that investigated methods to enhance and standardize physician documentation practices in outpatient epilepsy clinics. Despite variability in implementation methods, clinical populations, and outcome measures, our findings underscore the potential benefits of structured documentation systems in improving care quality, efficiency, and provider satisfaction.
Standardized documentation interventions were most frequently implemented at tertiary care centers and typically relied on EMR‐integrated tools and structured templates. These systems effectively recorded fundamental epilepsy‐related data elements (e.g., seizure frequency and type, ASM side effect profiles, and counseling on topics such as SUDEP and preconception planning); this was associated with better clinical record consistency and completeness. Multiple initiatives incorporated elements from established frameworks including AAN Epilepsy Quality Measures and NIH/NINDS CDEs as well as ILAE definitions to support both guideline compliance and long‐term data collection.
Use of structured templates was associated with sizeable gains in documentation completeness and with apparent downstream clinical impact. Postimplementation cohorts demonstrated improvements exceeding 40–60 percentage points in selected quality indicators regarding documentation capture, higher capture of seizure‐type/frequency data, shorter time to treatment for infantile spasms, markedly improved quality‐of‐life assessments (<3% to >80%), and, in one adult cohort, fewer epilepsy‐related hospitalizations and better seizure control at 1 year. Importantly, as it comes to interpretation of these results within the limitations of a scoping review, we cannot conclude causation of these outcomes by the implementation of standardized templates; however, we can see clear positive associations with their use.
These results echo broader chronic disease literature on EMR‐based documentation tools, reinforcing their value for decision‐making and continuity of care.
One such example was an attempt to standardize documentation as it relates to stroke care. An Epic‐based stroke structured clinical documentation support toolkit was developed to capture discrete data elements relating to stroke along with other features such as automatically writing progress notes, monthly data‐quality dashboards, and linkage to the Neurology Practice Based Research Network for ongoing improvement and research. An incredibly promising feature that they included was the concept of a "Best Practice Advisory" according to which if a patient had a positive depression screen, the stroke toolkit would launch a Best Practice Advisory forcing the physician to act (order, refer, or justify no action) with regard to this notice. Analogous systems for high‐priority epilepsy items such as SUDEP discussion, folic acid prescription, and rescue plan medication status could be considered in epilepsy EMRs.
Further attempts in documentation optimization and standardization have been reported in Parkinson disease patients, where such QI initiatives found benefit in creating controlled vocabulary for medications, embedding Best Practice Advisory notifications for pertinent topics, and institutionalizing audit and feedback loops for overall standardization rate and benefits.
Physician satisfaction, although variably reported, was generally positive; five studies formally assessed this outcome and described perceived gains in efficiency, communication, and follow‐up planning. This is encouraging, given previous reports of EMRs commonly contributing to burnout among neurologists; a qualitative investigation found that neurologists believe EMRs should ideally save time and enhance/expedite sharing of medical record information between providers. Common implementation barriers included initial workflow disruptions, low uptake when templates were optional, and EMR navigation difficulties, particularly in settings with limited training or multiple preexisting note formats.
Persistent documentation gaps were noted across studies, including underdocumentation of psychosocial comorbidities, quality‐of‐life assessments, and care details for high‐risk subpopulations such as individuals with intellectual disabilities or from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. These deficiencies highlight the need for broader quality measures encompassing mental health, social determinants of health, and patient‐centered outcomes.
Technical and logistical challenges such as limited IT support, integration delays, high development costs, and resource constraints were prevalent. Addressing these barriers will require sustained institutional investment, stronger EMR vendor partnerships, and the inclusion of end users in template design. Multidisciplinary collaboration was a key enabler of successful implementation, particularly in studies that engaged clinicians, researchers, and caregivers in iterative template development and training. Interestingly in another study evaluating the impact of standardizing notes in general neurology clinics, they found that although initially usage by providers was low, with time the use of standard templates increased, which suggests that as familiarity with these new templates increases, general acceptance does as well.
Recommendations converged on several best practices: broader adoption of structured EMR fields aligned with national frameworks; expansion of quality measures to capture underrepresented areas such as mental health screening, self‐management, and dietary efficacy; mandatory documentation of high‐priority data elements and use of “note‐lockout” functions; and integration of decision‐support dashboards and web‐based platforms such as NeuroMeasures to reduce manual burden and enable real‐time benchmarking. To further guide standardization and implementation of these optimized templates, consensus‐based studies designed to create a specific template of necessary information are warranted.
This review has several limitations. First, as this is a scoping review, our ability to determine or infer causation between documentation improvement and clinical outcomes is limited. The results must therefore be interpreted with caution. To properly evaluate causation, RCTs would be required. Most of the studies were from North America and were conducted at academic centers, which may limit the generalizability to community‐based or resource‐constrained settings. Furthermore, given that most studies were conducted specifically in the United States, there may be limited generalizability to public health care systems. In line with this, all studies were conducted in high‐income countries, which limits the applicability of findings to middle‐ and low‐income countries. Additionally, there was a scarcity of formal assessments of cost‐effectiveness and long‐term sustainability, which are important areas for future research. We also cannot rule out the possibility of publication bias. As more studies are published in the field, meta‐analyses may help assess and quantify this type of bias. Finally, as is often noted in broad exploratory scoping reviews, we did not perform an evaluation of study quality. Because the included studies employed heterogeneous designs and nonstandardized outcome metrics, we did not conduct a formal synthesis, calculate effect sizes, or apply structured thematic coding. As a result, our findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than inferential, and we avoided drawing causal conclusions.
In conclusion, standardized documentation tools in outpatient epilepsy clinics have the potential to improve care delivery and clinical data quality. Although there is still variability in implementation and barriers to implementation remain, targeted interventions and institutional support can help to facilitate widespread adoption. Future efforts should focus on inclusive design, expand quality indicators, be easy to use at point of care without adding administrative burden to physicians, and have robust evaluation metrics to optimize the utility of documentation systems in epilepsy care. Furthermore, the creation of a national or international standardized template using consensus‐based methodology is an important next step for optimizing care uniformly across regions. There is a need to develop standardized documentation frameworks for epilepsy care that incorporate quality standards from organizations such as the AAN and ILAE while ensuring interoperability across different centers. Implementing consensus‐based frameworks in multicenter studies will enable performance evaluation, large‐scale quality improvement, and more meaningful comparison of study results.
