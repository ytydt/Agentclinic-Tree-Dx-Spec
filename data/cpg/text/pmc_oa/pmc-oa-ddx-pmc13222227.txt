Current status and future perspectives of fecal immunological markers in the differential diagnosis of Clostridioides difficile infection in children
Objective
The rising incidence of pediatric Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) and its nonspecific clinical presentation pose a significant diagnostic challenge. Current methods (e.g., NAATs, toxin tests) fail to distinguish active infection from asymptomatic colonization reliably. This review assesses the potential of fecal immunological markers to improve the differential diagnosis of pediatric CDI.
Methods
We synthesized current literature on the epidemiology and diagnostic challenges of pediatric CDI, the limitations of existing diagnostic strategies, and the evidence for fecal immunological markers—including inflammatory mediators, antimicrobial peptides, and immunoglobulins—in distinguishing CDI from non-CDI diarrhea.
Results
Fecal immunological markers, such as IL-8, IL-1β, calprotectin, and human defensin-5, reflect intestinal mucosal inflammation and the host response during CDI. Elevated IL-8 and IL-1β are associated with disease severity, while fecal calprotectin > 500 µg/g shows high specificity for CDI and predicts recurrence risk. Dynamic changes in sIgA correlate with mucosal immunity and relapse. CDI exhibits a distinct “hyper-inflammatory” profile dominated by IL-1β/IL-8, contrasting with the Th1/interferon-driven response typical of viral diarrhea. However, variability in assay methods, lack of pediatric reference thresholds, and insufficient validation in diverse cohorts limit current clinical applicability.
Conclusions
Fecal immunological markers represent a promising shift toward a host-focused diagnostic approach for pediatric CDI. Future efforts should prioritize standardized assays, multi-marker panels, and well-designed prospective studies in children to validate their role in guiding diagnosis and personalized management.

Introduction
Diarrhea is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in children worldwide, and the identification of pathogens in infectious diarrhea has always been a challenge in clinical diagnosis and treatment. Clostridioides difficile (CD), as the primary pathogen of antibiotic-associated diarrhea, has seen a rising incidence of CDI in children, particularly closely associated with community-acquired infections and the misuse of antibiotics. However, the clinical manifestations of CDI highly overlap with those of diarrhea caused by other pathogens, such as viruses, bacteria, or parasites. Current diagnostic methods, including Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAATs) with sensitivity ranging from 93% to 98% and specificity from 93% to 95%, as well as toxin immunoassays with sensitivity between 51% and 63% and specificity from 98% to 99%, exhibit limitations in sensitivity or specificity, making it difficult to reliably distinguish between infection and colonization. Recently, fecal immunological markers, which directly reflect local immune responses and pathological conditions in the intestine, are emerging as promising tools for distinguishing CDI from non-CDI diarrhea.

Literature search and selection
A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase through September 2025 to identify studies regarding fecal immunological markers in pediatric CDI. The search terms combined keywords related to children (“pediatric”, “child”), the infection (“CD”, “CDI”), and relevant biomarkers (“fecal marker”, “calprotectin”, “IL-8”, “sIgA”). We included original studies and reviews that focused on the diagnostic or prognostic utility of these markers in children. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, followed by a full-text review of eligible articles. Data were extracted and synthesized narratively to evaluate the current evidence and future directions.

Epidemiology of CDI
CDI is an intestinal infectious disease caused by toxin-producing CD. CD is a Gram-positive, obligately anaerobic, spore-forming bacillus that spreads through the fecal-oral route, with its spores exhibiting strong resistance to environmental conditions and disinfectants. Nosocomial transmission primarily occurs through contaminated hands, medical equipment, and environmental surfaces. Community transmission may occur through food or zoonotic pathways, although the specific mechanisms remain unclear. Pathogenic strains can produce toxin A (Clostridioides difficile toxin A, TcdA) and toxin B (Clostridioides difficile toxin B, TcdB), which disrupt the intestinal mucosal barrier and trigger inflammatory responses. Mild cases present with diarrhea and abdominal pain, while severe cases can lead to pseudomembranous colitis, toxic megacolon, and even death. Risk factors include antibiotic overuse, prolonged hospitalization, immunosuppression, and gastrointestinal surgery. The incidence of CDI in children is increasing year by year, and infants and young children, due to their unstable gut microbiota, often experience asymptomatic colonization but may serve as transmission sources. Currently, there is limited research on pediatric CDI in China. It is imperative to integrate clinical characteristics with molecular pathogenesis to explore its epidemiological patterns and precise treatment strategies, in order to reduce disease burden and improve prognosis.
In recent years, with the emergence of highly virulent strains (such as NAP1/BI/027), the overuse of antibiotics, and the increasing aging population, the incidence, recurrence rate, and mortality of CDI have continued to rise, making it a significant global public health challenge. Since the early 2000s, there has been a notable increase in the incidence of CDI in North America and Europe. Data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention indicate that there were approximately 453,000 cases of CDI in the United States in 2011, resulting in about 29,300 deaths. Despite the strengthening of infection control measures in hospitals in recent years, the proportion of community-acquired CDI has increased to 25%-30%. Data from the European Hospital Infection Surveillance in 2016–2017 show that CD is the sixth most common pathogen causing hospital infections. The incidence rate in Asian countries is lower than that in Europe and America, but the reported cases in Japan, South Korea, and China have been increasing year by year, which may be related to the improvement in diagnostic capabilities. Traditionally, CDI has been more prevalent among elderly hospitalized patients, but in recent years, there has been a significant increase in the incidence among children. Risk factors for CDI in children include antibiotic exposure, gastrointestinal surgery, Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), and immunodeficiency. CDI significantly prolongs hospital stays and increases medical costs. In the United States, the medical cost of a single CDI episode is approximately $11,000 to $16,000, with costs doubling for recurrent patients. UK research shows that CDI patients have an average extended hospital stay of 21 days, resulting in an additional annual healthcare expenditure of £60 million. The mortality rate of severe CDI is as high as 35%-50%, and even survivors may suffer from intestinal dysfunction. Approximately 15%-30% of CDI patients experience a recurrence within 8 weeks after initial treatment, with the recurrence rate reaching up to 40% after the second episode, and the risk escalating to 60% after multiple recurrences. Recurrence may result from reactivation of the same strain or acquisition of a new strain. Molecular typing reveals that approximately 50% of recurrences are due to the original strain, with the remainder being new infections. Intestinal dysbiosis is a significant trigger for relapse, which explains the high cure rate of up to 90% in recurrent patients through fecal microbiota transplantation. The risk factors for recurrence primarily include host factors (advanced age, hypoalbuminemia, immunosuppression), pathogen factors (infection with highly virulent strains, persistent spore excretion), and treatment factors (delayed restoration of gut microbiota following vancomycin or metronidazole therapy).
Pediatric CDI exhibits unique epidemiological characteristics: (1) High rate of asymptomatic colonization: The colonization rate in infants under 12 months of age can reach 40%, making them a significant reservoir for nosocomial transmission. (2) Differences in risk factors: In addition to antibiotic exposure, underlying conditions such as Hirschsprung’s disease, IBD, and cystic fibrosis significantly increase the risk of infection. (3) Broad spectrum of clinical manifestations: Ranging from asymptomatic colonization to fulminant colitis, approximately 20% of pediatric patients develop recurrent CDI. These characteristics pose significant challenges for the early identification and precise diagnosis of pediatric CDI.

Diagnostic dilemmas in pediatric diarrheal diseases
Diarrhea in children encompasses a broad spectrum of etiologies, with infectious causes predominating. Viral pathogens, such as rotavirus and norovirus, are the most prevalent, followed by bacterial pathogens like Salmonella and Campylobacter, as well as parasites. According to statistics from the World Health Organization, there are approximately 1.7 billion episodes of diarrhea annually among children under the age of 5 worldwide, with 70% of these cases caused by viruses. With the overuse of antibiotics, the incidence of CDI in hospitalized children has increased, but its symptoms (such as watery stools, fever) are difficult to distinguish from viral diarrhea. A single test cannot cover all pathogens, while multiplex Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) or metagenomic sequencing is costly and difficult to popularize. Diarrhea caused by different pathogens exhibits significant symptom overlap and lacks specific symptoms. A study revealed that the accuracy of distinguishing bacterial from viral diarrhea based solely on clinical manifestations is less than 50%. In addition, non-infectious conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, and functional gastrointestinal disorders may also present similarly to infectious diarrhea, further increasing the diagnostic difficulty.

Current status of CD detection technologies
Currently, CD detection technologies primarily encompass three major areas: etiological culture, toxin detection, and molecular diagnostics. Each type of technology has its unique advantages and limitations.
Etiological culture techniques.
Traditional culture methods on selective media, such as Cefoxitin cycloserine fructose agar, exhibit low sensitivity and are unable to distinguish between toxigenic and non-toxigenic strains. Toxigenic culture (TC), which integrates culture techniques with toxin detection, remains the reference standard. However, it requires 3 to 7 days, specialized facilities, and is not suitable for routine application. Glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) immunoassays provide high sensitivity and negative predictive value, making them recommended for initial screening in multi-step algorithms. Nevertheless, GDH detects a conserved antigen and does not indicate toxin production, thus necessitating confirmatory testing.
Toxin detection technology.
The cell cytotoxicity assay (CTA) is known for its high sensitivity and specificity in detecting biologically active toxin B. However, it is a time-consuming (24–48 h) and technically demanding procedure, which restricts its use to reference laboratories. In contrast, enzyme immunoassays (EIAs) for toxins A and B are rapid and straightforward but demonstrate significant variability in sensitivity (ranging from 29% to 86%), which can result in underdiagnosis in mild to moderate cases. Recent advancements in the field have introduced ultrasensitive digital immunoassays that can quantify toxins across a broad dynamic range. Nevertheless, these methods still face challenges in reliably differentiating between symptomatic infections and low-level carriage.
Molecular diagnostic techniques.
NAATs, such as PCR for the TcdA and TcdB genes, provide rapid and highly sensitive detection of toxigenic strains and can identify hypervirulent lineages, such as those carrying the cdt gene. However, NAATs are unable to determine toxin expression, which may lead to the misclassification of asymptomatic carriers as infected individuals. Compared to toxin-positive patients, those who are NAAT-positive but toxin-negative often exhibit similar clinical outcomes, underscoring the necessity of integrating functional correlates of disease.
Diagnostic Strategy Optimization.
To mitigate the limitations of single tests, guidelines recommend multi-step algorithms, typically commencing with a sensitive screening method, such as GDH or NAATs, followed by specific toxin confirmation. While these strategies aim to reduce overdiagnosis, they still encounter challenges: toxin EIAs often lack sensitivity; cost and logistical issues impede implementation in resource-limited settings; and the management of asymptomatic carriers remains ambiguous. Importantly, none of these pathogen-focused methods evaluate the host’s inflammatory status or disease severity, highlighting the necessity for complementary immunological markers that accurately reflect the host response.

Advances in research on fecal immunological markers
The pathophysiological process of CDI involves intestinal barrier disruption, activation of inflammatory responses, and dysregulation of host immune responses. In recent years, fecal immunological markers have demonstrated significant value in the diagnosis, activity assessment, and prognosis prediction of CDI due to their non-invasive nature and ability for dynamic monitoring. Research indicates that various protein molecules released during intestinal inflammatory responses can reflect the extent of mucosal damage and immune status through fecal testing, providing new insights for the precise management of CDI. Table 2 summarizes previous studies on fecal immunological markers. Fecal immunologic markers mainly include the following categories: (1) Inflammatory mediators: Pro-inflammatory factors such as Interleukin-8 (IL-8), Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) can reflect the intensity of intestinal inflammation. (2) Antimicrobial peptides: Human Defensin-5/6 (HD-5/6) and calprotectin directly participate in pathogen clearance. (3) Immunoglobulins: The level of Secretory Immunoglobulin A (sIgA) reflects the state of mucosal immune response. (4) Proteases: Matrix Metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) is associated with the degree of tissue damage. (5) Metabolites: The profile of Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFA) reflects the functional status of the gut microbiota. Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of different fecal immunological markers.

The characteristic pathological changes of CDI are toxin-mediated intestinal epithelial damage and excessive inflammatory response. Studies have shown that the concentration of IL-8 in the feces of children with CDI is significantly higher than that in viral diarrhea, and it is positively correlated with the formation of pseudomembranes under endoscopy. Toxin B can activate IL-1β secretion, and its level is significantly correlated with disease severity scores. TNF-α is elevated by 10–15 times in fulminant CDI and is sensitive to glucocorticoid treatment. The defensin family exhibits unique expression characteristics in CDI. For instance, HD-5 expression is upregulated 3–5 times in colonic biopsy tissues from children with CDI, potentially exerting a protective effect by neutralizing the receptor-binding domain of toxin B. A fecal calprotectin concentration > 500 µg/g demonstrates a diagnostic specificity of 92% for CDI and can predict the risk of recurrence within 30 days. sIgA exhibits dynamic changes in CDI. The decrease in sIgA levels during the acute phase can reflect impaired mucosal barrier function. During the recovery phase, sIgA levels return to baseline, and those with persistently low levels have a significantly increased risk of recurrence.
Non-CDI diarrhea can be categorized into viral diarrhea and bacterial diarrhea, with distinct fecal immunological marker characteristics. Studies have shown that rotavirus infection induces a T Helper 1 Cell (Th1) response, with fecal Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) levels significantly higher than those in bacterial diarrhea. In bacterial diarrhea, lactoferrin increases 5–8 times in Salmonella infection and is sensitive to antibiotic treatment. Shiga toxin induces epithelial damage, leading to the release of MMP-9, whose activity is associated with the incidence of bloody stools.

Fecal immunological markers in pediatric CDI care
To achieve the clinical translation of fecal immunological markers in the management of pediatric CDI, it is essential to accurately embed them at critical decision points in the diagnostic and therapeutic pathways. Accordingly, we propose a host response-oriented integrated diagnostic and management pathway in Fig. 1. When molecular testing is positive, highly specific fecal immunological markers such as fecal IL-1β or calprotectin are utilized to distinguish between active infection and asymptomatic colonization. After diagnosis, the acute phase levels of calprotectin and the dynamic changes in sIgA are used to assess the risk of recurrence. During treatment, continuous monitoring of calprotectin and sIgA provides an objective assessment of mucosal inflammation resolution and immune reconstitution. This integrated model promises a shift in the paradigm of pediatric CDI management from a “pathogen-centric” to a “host-pathogen interaction” approach.

The limitations and challenges of existing research
The insufficient sensitivity and specificity of biomarkers
Current research on distinguishing between bacterial and viral diarrhea has proposed various immunological markers (such as Procalcitonin (PCT), Soluble Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells-1 (sTREM-1), and calprotectin), but their sensitivity and specificity still exhibit significant limitations. For instance, studies have shown that although the combined detection of PCT and sTREM-1 yields an Area Under the Curve (AUC) as high as 0.955, the sample size of the study was small, and it was not validated in multicenter or diverse populations, potentially overestimating its practical application value. Studies have shown that C-reactive protein performs better than PCT in adult patients with fever, but the sensitivity of PCT in the pediatric population is only 60.3%, suggesting that age and underlying diseases may affect the efficacy of biomarkers. Although calprotectin levels are significantly elevated in bacterial infections, they are also influenced by non-infectious inflammatory conditions, which limit their specificity in diarrhea of mixed etiology. In the future, it is necessary to validate the universality of existing immunological markers through larger-scale and diverse sample cohorts, and to explore multi-biomarker combination models to improve accuracy.
The standardization challenges of CDI diagnostic criteria.
The diagnosis of CDI relies on a combination of clinical symptoms and laboratory testing, but the current IDSA/SHEA guidelines recommend a strategy of combining NAATs with toxin detection, which is contradictory. NAATs can detect toxigenic genes but cannot differentiate between colonization and active infection, while toxin detection has low sensitivity (approximately 60%-70%), leading to a high rate of false negatives. Although the ultrasensitive toxin detection with a detection limit of 20 pg/mL proposed in some studies holds promise for enhancing sensitivity, its clinical significance remains unclear, and whether low concentrations of toxins are sufficient to induce symptoms still requires validation. In addition, host immunological markers (such as IL-1β, anti-toxin Immunoglobulin A (IgA), and anti-toxin Immunoglobulin G (IgG)) demonstrated excellent performance in distinguishing CDI from asymptomatic carriers. However, these tests are not standardized and are relatively costly, making them difficult to promote in primary care hospitals. It is imperative to establish unified diagnostic thresholds and operational procedures, and to explore cost-effective detection technologies.
To translate fecal immunological markers into reliable clinical tools, a multi-stage framework is essential. This framework includes standardizing laboratory methods, establishing age-stratified diagnostic thresholds through ROC analysis, and prospectively validating these cut-off values in multicenter pediatric cohorts. Furthermore, all research must adhere to established reporting standards to ensure transparency and reproducibility.
The limitations of treatment and prevention strategies.
Currently, the treatment of CDI relies on vancomycin, fidaxomicin, and fecal microbiota transplantation, but the recurrence rate remains as high as 20%-30%. Studies have shown that probiotics can reduce the risk of CDI, but there is a lack of consensus regarding strain selection, dosage, and treatment duration. Some studies have shown that the efficacy of fecal microbiota transplantation for recurrent CDI exceeds 80%, but its long-term safety and standardized preparation procedures remain unresolved. In addition, there is insufficient evidence for the use of antisecretory drugs in the treatment of diarrhea in children, which may mask the condition. More randomized controlled trials need to be conducted to optimize existing therapies and to develop novel drugs targeting toxins or immune modulation.
Lack of data on special populations and long-term follow-up.
The pathological mechanisms of diarrhea in children, the elderly, and immunocompromised patients may differ from those in the general population, but existing research has paid insufficient attention to this issue. For instance, studies have shown that rotavirus is the predominant cause of viral diarrhea in children, but they have not compared the differences in immune responses across different age groups. The majority of research endpoints focus on short-term symptom relief, neglecting long-term sequelae (such as irritable bowel syndrome and malnutrition). Longitudinal studies targeting special populations still need to be conducted, incorporating assessments of quality of life and economic burden.

Prospect
The precise diagnosis of pediatric CDI requires transcending the limitations of current microbiological testing and shifting towards an in-depth analysis of host immune response characteristics. To achieve the clinical translation of fecal immunological markers in the differential diagnosis of pediatric CDI, future research needs to focus on three core aspects. Firstly, there is an urgent requirement to establish standardized and localized technologies, including the development of testing standards and reference intervals for the Chinese pediatric population. Secondly, diagnostic strategies need to be enhanced, transitioning from reliance on single markers to the exploration of a multidimensional joint diagnostic model that incorporates key indicators such as IL-1β and calprotectin. This approach aims to differentiate between colonization and infection while predicting the risk of recurrence. Ultimately, through large-scale prospective studies, the integration of host immune information into precise diagnostic and therapeutic pathways will significantly advance the diagnostic paradigm of pediatric CDI from a “pathogen-centric” approach to a “host-pathogen interaction” model.
