Evaluation of safety and efficacy: a case series of chronic limb-threatening ischemia treated with endovascular revascularization and umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell therapy
Background
Severe chronic limb-threatening ischemia has significant challenges, leading to poor outcomes, including amputation and death. By guiding angiosome blood flow to the ulcerated foot, a therapeutic combination of endovascular revascularization and mesenchymal stem cells can achieve a good outcome.
Objective
To describe the efficacy and safety of combination endovascular treatment and umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell (UCMSC) treatment for chronic limb threatening ischemia.
Methods
The design of the study was a longitudinal study, serial case report and prospectively observed pilot study. All patients received intramuscular UCMSC with a dosage of 1–2 million cells per kilogram of body weight then followed by endovascular intervention based on angiosome guided. We monitored the patients efficacy and safety for 12, 18, and 24 months.
Result
This study involved six patients. All patients have an ankle brachial index below 0.4, and 83.3% of patients have diabetes mellitus, high creatinine level with an average of 1.64 mg/dl, and severe pain scores based on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) of more than 7. 6th month was the time where the creatinine median was reduced, the ABI median was increased, the wound improvement started to happen. While the pain median score was reaching zero in 12 months of observation. 24 months revealed significant improvements in patient outcomes across pain scores, creatinine levels, and ankle-brachial index (ABI) with p < 0.05.
Conclusion
The new technique, unique and innovative research, for using combination treatment of intramuscular UCMSC and modern endovascular procedures based on angiosome concept showed fast and safe wound healing, reduce rest leg pain, and increase ABI.

Introduction and background
Chronic Limb Threatening Ischemia (CLTI) is the most severe manifestation of PAD; it is caused by narrowing or blockage of the leg arteries due to atherosclerosis and thromboembolism. CLTI is still a global problem, with an average of around 20% of CLTI patients having the risk of amputation and 25% dying after one year without proper treatment. The incidence of CLTI is 220 to 3500 cases per 1 million persons. The incidence is predicted to double in 2050. The prevalence is between 1% and 2%, although it may be as high as 11% among individuals with known peripheral arterial disease. CLTI occurs in up to 1 in 10 people with PAD. It is estimated that over a 5-year period, 5 to 10% of patients with asymptomatic peripheral arterial disease or intermittent claudication will have progression to CLTI.
Revascularization with endovascular or surgical bypass for treatment of ischemia, wound healing, controlling risk factors (diabetes mellitus, etc.), comorbidities (coronary artery disease, etc.), and infection management. Field 5: However, about 25% of patients still progress to limb amputations, the amputation rate is 10%–40%, and infections also sometimes occur. The efficacy of surgical revascularization remains limited because the affected arteries are mostly diffused, segmented, and located peripherally. About 20–30% of patients with CLTI are ineligible for revascularization, or the surgery has failed due to distal flow impairment. One of the promising future therapies for CLTI is regenerative medicine therapy. Regenerative medicine utilizes cell-based therapy to repair, regenerate, or replace damaged tissue or organs. This approach involves using cells, often stem cells, to replace or repair injured tissue, offering promise for treating various diseases.
Stem cell therapy as an alternative revascularization technique for patients with no-option CLTI. Stem cells are currently believed to be the future of medicine due to their considerable therapeutic and biotechnological benefits in treating significant diseases such as diabetes, neurological diseases, cancer, heart disease, and vascular disease. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are multipotent adult stem cells with the capacity to differentiate into several cell types. Mesenchymal stem cells also have characteristics like self-renewal, immunomodulatory, angiogenesis, paracrine effects, and regenerative cell capabilities. The use of MSCs in CLTI patients has a unique ability to regenerate dead cells. MSCs are cells that can proliferate and are capable of multiplying and replicating. Moreover, they are capable of differentiating and producing specialized cells from different body tissues. MSCs in CLTI can release angiogenic growth factors and undergo endothelial differentiation. Therefore, they can induce angiogenesis, restore blood circulation to ischemic sites, and promote tissue regeneration and functional recovery.
Navarro LV, et al. showed several clinical trials showed positive effects of tissue regeneration and immunomodulation from MSCs in the treatment of CLTI compared to standard of care or placebo, and Teraa M, et al. showed large clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of mesenchymal stromal cells in the treatment of peripheral arterial disease. The outcomes showed increased ABPI, walking time, pain relief, physical functioning, and wound healing. Otherwise, there is no adverse effect on the preclinical as well as clinical trials.. However, the use of stem cell therapy is still controversial in 2019, but then The European Journal of Vascular and Endovascular Disease (EJVS) in 2021 recommends the use of cell therapy in CLTI. Many studies, from animals to clinical trials, show excellent safety and efficacy. However, there are still many controversies about how to administer it. We are interested in using a combination of conventional or modern endovascular therapy and intramuscular (IM) umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell (UCMSC) administration based on the angiosome concept. In this pilot study, we investigated the outcomes, safety, and efficacy of CLTI patients treated with umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs) during follow up 2 years.

Preparation of UCMSC
The source of mesenchymal stem cells derived from umbilical cords was obtained from the consent of women who gave birth to healthy, full-term babies before taking the umbilical cord. Donor selection is in accordance with FDA standards, 169 and donors must test negative for HIV, HCV, HBsAg, syphilis, and CMV, as well as other infectious diseases or malignancies. Fresh umbilical cords were obtained from healthy full-term neonates after cesarean section, collected in sterile solution, and transported directly to the Prodia Stem Cell (ProStem) or Kalbe stem cell facility (Regenic). The umbilical artery and vein were taken out, and primary cultures were created using the tissue explant method to get mesenchymal stem cells, which were then kept in α-MEM with added nutrients until they reached passage 6 at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The growth medium was without serum, without antibiotics, and without phenol red. We replaced the media every 3 days until we reached confluence, after which we harvested and stored the cells in a cryopreserved state. We analyzed the mesenchymal stem cells using flow cytometry to verify their compliance with FDA standards and to conduct tests for sterility, viruses, and mycoplasma. More than 95% of the MSC population must express CD105, CD73 and CD90, as measured by flow cytometry. Additionally, these cells must lack expression (≤ 2% positive) of CD45, CD34, CD14 or CD11b, CD79α or CD19 and HLA class II. The cells must be able to differentiate to osteoblasts, adipocytes and chondroblasts under standard in vitro differentiating conditions. We thawed the cryopreserved cells upon request and removed the cryoprotectant. Mesenchymal stem cells derived from the umbilical cord are then resuspended in 0.9% NaCl, and then the mesenchymal stem cells derived from the umbilical cord are ready to be administered to the sample to be studied. Mesenchymal stem cells from the umbilical cord will create a mixture that has a lot of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which starts the process of forming new blood vessels.

Steps of umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell therapy in CLTI patients
Patients who underwent endovascular intervention and had significant stenosis in the aortoiliac artery, femoropopliteal artery, and below-the-knee arteries subsequently received debridement of their non-healing wounds. After debridement, The administration of UCMSC proceeds simultaneously with an endovascular approach based on the angiosome guide.
We calculate the UCMSC dosage at a rate of 1–2 million cells per kilogram of body weight. The specified volume of cell suspension was administered through 40–60 intramuscular injections into the gastrocnemius muscle of the ischemic lower limb, followed by angiosome blood vessel criteria. Injection provided in a region of 10 × 6 cm, with 0.5–1.0.5.0 mL of UCMSC delivered to each site. In the management of an ulcer, multiple peri-ulcer injections (4–6 locations; 0.3–0.5 mL of UCMSC) are administered around the ulcer.
Following endovascular revascularization, debridement, amputation in cases of necrosis, and the administration of UCMSC, the patient subsequently get a good control of risk factors (including diabetes mellitus, etc.), wound care, nutritional support, antibiotic administration, and the management of other comorbid conditions. Patients were followed up for safety and efficacy for 12, 18, and 24 months.

The statistical analysis using a general linear model and multivariate analysis for repeated measures over a 24-month period revealed significant improvements in patient outcomes across pain scores, creatinine levels, and ankle-brachial index (ABI). In Table 9, pain scores showed a consistent reduction from a mean of 9.67 at baseline to 0.00 by 12 months, with this trend remaining through 24 months. The multivariate analysis showed statistically significant results for pain score (p = 0.000). Creatinine levels showed a gradual decline from 1.64 at baseline to 1.29 at 24 months. ABI improved steadily from 0.12 at baseline to 0.76 at 24 months, reflecting substantial vascular recovery. However, creatinine levels (p = 0.095) and ABI (p = 0.068) showed no statistically significant possibly due to the small sample size (N = 6). The corrected analyses support the conclusion that the intervention and UCMSC injection was highly effective, especially in reducing pain and improving vascular function. Based on the descriptive analysis of Figs. 5 and 6, and 7, the treatment demonstrated substantial clinical improvements over the 24-month follow-up period. Pain scores showed a marked and rapid decline from a baseline mean of 9.67 to 2.67 at 1 week, 0.83 at 6 months, and complete resolution (score of 0) from 12 months onwards. The ABI improved significantly from a severely ischemic baseline value of 0.12 to 0.45 at 1 week, 0.62 at 6 months, and reached a peak of 0.87 at 12 months, maintaining higher-than-baseline values through 24 months (0.76). Meanwhile, serum creatinine levels, which were 1.64 mg/dL at baseline, exhibited a mild transient increase at 1 week (1.82 mg/dL) before progressively decreasing to 1.29 mg/dL at 24 months, suggesting stable or slightly improved renal function. Collectively, these findings indicate that the intervention was associated with rapid and sustained pain relief, improved lower limb perfusion, and no evidence of renal impairment throughout the two-year follow-up.

Discussion
This pilot study showed that there were no serious adverse event during the period of UCMSC injection and 12, 18, and 24 months follow-up which indicated the safety and efficacy of UCMSC treatment in CLTI patients. The results in this pilot study also showed that UCMSC treatment improved wound healing process of CLTI patients which is indicated by pain score and quality of life score.
Recent studies have investigated the therapeutic potential of umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSC) in treating chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI), focusing on their safety, efficacy, and underlying mechanisms.​.
A phase I pilot study involving 14 patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) and peripheral arterial disease (PAD) assessed the effects of UCMSC administered both topically and intravenously. The treatment led to over 95% ulcer closure within 1.5 months, significant improvements in Wagner scores, Rutherford grades, and pain levels, with no major adverse events or amputations reported over a three-year follow-up period.
In a separate study, UCMSC transplantation post-angioplasty in patients with severe diabetic foot resulted in greater and more stable improvements in skin temperature, ankle-brachial pressure index, transcutaneous oxygen tension, and claudication distance compared to angioplasty alone. Notably, there was a significant increase in neovessel formation and ulcer healing, with no serious complications observed. Similarly, in a tree shrew model, UCMSC promoted angiogenesis, increased ankle-brachial index, transcutaneous oxygen pressure, and capillary/muscle fiber ratio, indicating restored blood supply and alleviation of ischemic symptoms.
In most clinical studies performed, patients receiving MSC-based treatment demonstrated improved clinical symptoms and tolerance. Despite these encouraging findings, several remaining concerns and challenges emphasize the need for preclinical studies to extend the period of animal trials to prove the regenerated micro/macro capillaries’ long-term patency. The most efficient administration route of MSCs still controversial. While new research suggests that MSCs enhance collateral vessel development in individuals suffering from severe PAD, considerations about the best form of administration must be regarded. Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 showed combination endovascular procedure and intramuscular UMSC followed by angiosome administration as a more feasible and less harmful. Intramuscular injection is based on generating a cell depot with paracrine activity in the ischemic site (into the gastrocnemius muscle). Systemic delivery, such as intravenous (IV) or intra-arterial (IA) infusion, is used less frequently. The intramuscular injection, studied more extensively, is easy to use, less invasive, and has demonstrated safety and effectiveness. One study compared the safety and efficacy of single intramuscular BM cell therapy (n = 15) to combination intra-arterial plus intramuscular BM cell delivery (n = 12) in patients who were not surgical or endovascular candidates. While this research found no statistically significant difference between the two approaches, it could not distinguish between ABI, ulcer healing, and walking distance advancement. ABI and TcPO2 levels increased after intramuscular cell administration but not following intra-arterial cell treatment. Both significantly improved rest pain and pain-free walking distance, even though there was no distinction between them.
According to Takahara et al. and Shatnawi et al., showed CLTI patients with type 2 DM most commonly have lesions in the infrapopliteal blood vessels compared to other locations, such as femoropopliteal and aortoiliac artery. This finding is also consistent with this study, which reports that 100% of the cases involve infrapopliteal blood vessel lesions. Infrapopliteal disease in patients with diabetes mellitus and critical limb-threatening ischemia often presents significant challenges, and outcomes can vary. Factors that contribute to poor outcomes in infrapopliteal lesions include older age, a low BMI, dialysis for chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, vascular hardening, infections, insufficient blood flow to the foot, and inadequate nutrition. While infrapopliteal endovascular revascularization can be successful, it’s associated with higher rates of major amputation and recurrent reintervention compared to patients without DM. Another method, aside from endovascular techniques, for achieving rapid wound healing involves the use of cellular factors and genetic modification, such as stem cells, to promote new cell regeneration.
SW. Kim et al. showed clinical trial on the intramuscular administration of human leukocyte antigen-matched UCB-MSCs, which included four patients diagnosed with thromboangiitis obliterans (TAO). The study demonstrated that arterial reconstruction or prevention of arterial obstruction using UCB-MSCs directly mitigates rest pain and accelerates the healing process of ischemic ulcers. All treated patients exhibited a rapid improvement in rest pain before the formation of vascular structures. It is posited by researchers that the secretion of growth factors or pain-relieving substances by implanted stem cells prior to the formation of vessels in ischemic areas may account for the observed pain relief. Building on the findings of prior research, a subsequent clinical experiment was developed to assess the safety and feasibility of intramuscular transplantation of UCB-MSCs into the affected limbs of patients with atherosclerosis obliterans (ASO) or thromboangiitis obliterans (TAO). Angiographic scores showed improvement in three out of eight patients, accompanied by complete ulcer healing. All patients exhibited increasing digital subtraction angiography scores; however, three developed collateral arteries in the injected limbs. This study demonstrated that intramuscular injection of UCB-MSCs is a safe and well-tolerated therapeutic option for patients with end-stage CLI.
Angiosome concept, which defines specific areas of tissue supplied by individual arteries, is increasingly used in endovascular procedures to improve wound healing in patients with critical limb ischemia. The Focussing revascularization on the feeding artery of a particular angiosome, surgeons aim to restore direct arterial flow to the ischemic area, potentially leading to better wound healing and limb salvage. Generally, six angiosomes originate from the three main source arteries and their branches in the foot and ankle. The anterior tibial artery (ATA) becomes the dorsalis pedis artery (DPA), which supplies to the dorsum of the foot. The posterior tibial artery has three main branches and three angiosomes as follows: the medial plantar artery (MPA) supplies to the instep, the lateral plantar artery supplies to the lateral midfoot and forefoot, and the calcaneal branch supplies to the heel. The peroneal artery (PA) has two main branches and two angiosomes as follows: the anterior perforating branch supplies the lateral anterior upper ankle and the calcaneal branch supplies the heel. Between the arteries feeding the angiosomes, there are numerous arterial–arterial connections, i.e., the collateral artery. These connections allow the blood flow to bypass the direct route if it is disrupted. Each angiosome is linked with very small vessels, i.e., choke vessels that allow a given angiosome to provide blood flow to an adjacent angiosome if the latter’s source artery is damaged. Direct Angiosome Revascularization showed significantly improves wound healing and major amputation rates after endovascular treatment in patients with chronic limb ischemia, supporting the angiosome theory. In the presence of collaterals, outcomes after indirect angiosome revascularization are similar to outcomes after direct angiosome. Alternatively, patients without collaterals may benefit even more from direct angiosome as a primary treatment strategy. The angiosome theory is less applicable in bypass surgery, because bypasses are generally anastomosed to the least affected artery, with runoff passing the ankle to maintain bypass patency.
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) stimulate angiogenesis, facilitate immunomodulation, reduce inflammation through anti-inflammatory effects, paracrine effects and promote tissue regeneration and repair. Mechanistically, UCMSC have been shown to modulate immune responses and trigger growth factors, and this will stimulate the formation of angiogenesis. in CLTI patients. Post-treatment analyses revealed reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL 8, IL 10) and T-cell populations, along with an increase in circulating endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), suggesting enhanced vascular repair mechanisms.
The wound healing process of five patients was under six months, but one patient, number 1, took a year. Patients 2 and 3, as shown in the images, demonstrated good control of infection, blood sugar levels, and lesion weight during their healing process. UCMSC can avoid major amputation, sepsis infection, reduce leg rest pain and regulate blood sugar.
These results show that UCMSC can be a safe and effective option for treating CLTI, as they help create new blood vessels, adjust immune responses, and lead to better health outcomes. However, larger randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these preliminary findings and establish standardized treatment protocols.
This study was conducted over a period of 3 years, with the results of all 6 patients showing rapid wound healing, rapid improvement in ABI, greatly reduced leg rest pain, and no serious complications such as death, Major Adverse Limb Event (MALE), Major Adverse Cardiovascular Event (MACE), cancer, infection, severe kidney failure, retinopathy, stroke, and allergies. We can conclude that the case study of the 6 patients demonstrates excellent safety and efficacy.

Study limitations
However, there are limitations due to the small sample size of this trial, high cost because the source of financing is based on private insurance, and the absence of a controlled study. This study aims to utilize a larger sample size to advance to Phase 1–3 clinical trials. A further issue is identifying financial sources for extensive studies.It is anticipated that a comprehensive assessment of these factors will significantly advance and contribute to the scientific understanding of peripheral artery disease in the future.

Conclusion
This research is new technique, unique and innovative used revascularization and molecular cell regeneration. The combination of intramuscular UCMSC and endovascular procedure guided by angiosome guide showed rapid wound healing, free leg pain, and increased ankle-brachial index, with no evidence of death, MACE, major amputation, cancer, retinopathy, or increasing creatinine level.
