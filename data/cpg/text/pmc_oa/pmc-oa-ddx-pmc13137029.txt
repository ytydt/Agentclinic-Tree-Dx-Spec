Comparative analysis for optimal LSD1 inhibitors evaluation techniques: pros and cons
Aberrant expression of lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) has been consistently implicated in a broad spectrum of malignancies, underscoring its relevance as a therapeutic target. Despite growing interest, the development of LSD1 inhibitors continues to face significant challenges, in part due to the enzyme's dual role in catalysis and as a scaffolding protein within chromatin-remodeling complexes. Recent insights into the non-enzymatic functions of LSD1 have shifted the focus toward disrupting its protein–protein interactions, particularly with chromatin-modifying enzymes, as a complementary or alternative therapeutic strategy. In light of the limited systematic evaluation of available technologies, this work provides a critical overview and comparative analysis of current screening platforms and binding affinity assays, with particular attention to approaches capable of identifying LSD1 scaffold inhibitors. These efforts aim to accelerate the discovery of next-generation LSD1-targeted therapies with improved translational potential in oncology.

Introduction
Reversible lysine modifications play crucial roles in regulating chromatin accessibility, and gene expression within the histone code, dating back to the discovery of the first histone demethylase lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) in 2004. LSD1, a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent amine oxidase homolog, comprises 852 amino acids and contains three conserved domains: the SWIRM domain (derived from Swi3p, Rsc8p, and Moira domain), the amine oxidase domain (AOD), and the TOWER domain. Functionally, LSD1 acts predominantly as a transcriptional repressor through demethylation of mono- and di-methylated histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4me1 and H3K4me2) in a FAD dependent manner. Under specific contexts, such as in hormone signaling, LSD1 also promotes transcriptional activation by removing repressive marks like H3K9me2 on nuclear receptor target genes, including those regulated by androgen and estrogen receptors. Meanwhile, the specific LSD1 isoform due to the existence of E8a exon in nervous tissues-LSD1n which associates with learning and long-term memory formation could demethylate H4K20me2/1 specifically. Beyond histone substrates, LSD1 demethylates a variety of non-histone proteins involved in oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways, such as TP53, DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), and E2F transcription factor 1 (E2F1).

Disruption of this interaction can profoundly affect function, for example, the R251Q mutation in LSD1 impairs assembly of the LSD1-CoREST-HDAC1 (LCH) complex and diminishes demethylation of H3K4me2. Interestingly, Lee et al. have found that the LC complex exhibited stronger demethylase activity compared to the LCH complex. Undoubtedly, the role of the LC complex and LCH complex is not limited to maintaining or enhancing demethylase activity. The LC complex can be further assembled into multicomponent platforms via interactions with zinc finger proteins and long noncoding RNAs (forming the LSD1-CoREST-X complex), thus regulating transcription, as seen in the case of zinc finger protein 516 (ZNF516), Snail/Gfi-1 (SNAG) family, myelin transcription factor 1 (MYT1), sex comb on midleg (Scm) with four malignant brain tumor (MBT) domains (SFMBT1), insulinoma-associated protein 1(INSM1), and zinc finger MYM-type containing 1 (ZMYM1). SNAG family often tethers the LC complex to chromatin via direct interaction with its SNAG motif, exemplified by INSM1 and growth factor independent 1b transcriptional repressor (Gfi1b). Similarly, the LCH complex engages in transcriptional repression through interactions with proteins such as zinc finger MYM-type containing 2 (ZMYM2), highlighting the broader epigenetic regulatory potential of LSD1-containing complexes beyond histone modification alone.
Mounting evidence has implicated LSD1 as a key oncogenic driver across diverse malignancies, where it promotes tumor progression through multiple mechanisms, including impaired differentiation, aberrant proliferation, metastasis, invasiveness, poor clinical prognosis, and therapeutic resistance. Beyond its role in tumorigenesis and progression, LSD1 is also involved in skin inflammation, neurodevelopmental disorders, follicle formation and mouse embryonic stem cell growth and differentiation. These findings have established LSD1 as a compelling therapeutic target, and numerous small-molecule compounds have been developed to pharmacologically suppress its function or direct ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation. Many literatures have indicated LSD1 inhibition enhanced tumor immunogenicity and T cell infiltration, reduced T cell exhaustion and overcame the anti-programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) resistance in melanoma. Above all, the screening and synthesis of novel LSD1 inhibitors hold importance because of their role in hindering the progression of cancers.

To date, eleven LSD1 inhibitors have been identified and entered clinical trials since the discovery of LSD1 (Table 1). Nine of them are irreversible inhibitors and have been used for the treatment of leukemia, solid tumors and Alzheimer's disease. Although these inhibitors have superior LSD1 inhibitory activities, numerous drawbacks still exist including strong adverse reactions such as thrombocytopenia, vomiting, and anemia, potential selectivity for monoamine oxidase, and synthetic difficulties. Reversible LSD1 inhibitors present higher selectivity and improved safety profiles, but most of them are still in the preclinical research stages. Importantly, increasing evidence suggests that targeting LSD1's demethylase activity alone may be insufficient, given its scaffolding functions within multi-protein chromatin remodeling complexes. For instance, OG86 has been reported to reduce high-mobility group protein 20B (HMG20B) levels—a component of the LC complex—thereby impairing complex stability and assembly. Similarly, the reversible inhibitor SP2509 is capable of preventing LSD1 interacting with ZNF217, HDAC2 and T-box transcription factor 2 (TBX2), independent of its enzymatic inhibition.
Consequently, novel techniques are required to aid in identifying LSD1 scaffold inhibitor and enzymatic inhibitor. This review provides a comprehensive overview of current screening methodologies for enzymatic LSD1 inhibitors and proposes that these platforms may be extended or adapted to assess compounds targeting non-enzymatic functions. Furthermore, we discuss approaches to distinguish reversible from irreversible inhibitors and outline emerging techniques for discovering LSD1 interaction disruptors with therapeutic potential.

LSD1 enzymatic activity assay
During the demethylation of substrates by LSD1, each catalytic cycle of methyl removal produces a molecule of formaldehyde (HCHO) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which provides inspiration for inhibitor screening methods. In principle, the quantities of HCHO, H2O2, and demethylated substrates produced are directly proportional to LSD1 enzymatic activity and inversely correlated with inhibitor potency. Accordingly, the inhibitory efficacy of candidate compounds can be quantified by measuring either the levels of demethylated or mono-methylated histone substrates, or the accumulation of reaction byproducts. Based on this rationale, screening platforms can be broadly categorized into two classes: (a) direct detection of substrate demethylation, and (b) indirect detection via enzymatic byproducts.

Direct quantification of demethylated or mono-methylated substrates
As LSD1 is a lysine specific demethylase, detection of demethylated histone is the most straightforward approach that can be used to quantify the activity of LSD1. The following experiments such as antibody-specific recognition and mass spectrometry (MS) could be utilized for H3K4 or H3K4me1 quantification.

MS is a powerful label-free analytical technique that enables the direct and quantitative detection of specific molecular species based on their mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios. By resolving ionized particles—ranging from small molecules to macromolecular complexes—MS not only determines molecular weights with high precision, but also permits comprehensive profiling of reaction components, including substrates, products, and enzymes (Fig. 2). As such, MS provides a holistic assessment of LSD1 enzymatic activity under physiologically relevant conditions.
Despite its strengths in sensitivity and specificity, conventional MS workflows often involve labor-intensive sample preparation steps that limit throughput and increase operational costs. To deal with the drawbacks and improve the screening efficiency, Mrksich et al. have reported an innovative platform which combines self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) of alkanethiolates on gold with matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization–time of flight (MALDI-TOF) MS, a technique termed SAMDI, for enzyme activity assays. The substrates are immobilized onto SAMs coated array either before or after treatment with an enzyme. Following simple washing steps to remove unbound species, the reaction is analyzed with a commercial MALDI-TOF instrument. The inhibition activity of promising LSD1 inhibitors can also be quantified with SAMDI. After the demethylation of biotinylated H3K4me1, the whole reaction content could be loaded on the SAMDI array coated neutravidin. The conversion of substrates would be calculated by area under the curve of biotinylated H3K4.
Despite the high sensitivity and specificity of MS, several limitations constrain its broader application in histone demethylation assays. A major challenge lies in the inherently low ionization efficiency of demethylated histone peptides, which often exhibit minimal structural variation and poor ionization characteristics. The ionization efficiency is affected by a series of factors, such as flow rate, interface design, solvent composition, and analyte properties. Besides, the salts in solution will affect the instrument adversely, thus reducing the sensitivity and temporal resolution on H3K4 conversion. Most importantly, the application of MS is limited by complicated operation and expensive equipment.

Europium-labeled assay
Lanthanides are a class of fluorescent metallic elements characterized by their long-lived excited states, sharp emission peaks, and large Stokes shifts, making them well-suited for use in time-resolved fluorescence (TRF) applications. Their extended fluorescence lifetimes allow for temporal separation of signal from background autofluorescence, significantly enhancing detection sensitivity. The large Stokes shift further minimizes spectral overlap between excitation and emission, reducing non-specific background noise and improving signal-to-noise ratio. Based on the fact that all processes contained light emission do not occur at the same time, TRF could record and discriminate the long-lived light and short-lived light from the pulsed light. This property has been widely adopted in assay development, including the screening of LSD1 inhibitors. TRF-based detection strategies could be divided into two categories: (a) heterogeneous assay that needs separate the bound and unbound components, and (b) homogeneous assay that reactants and products are in the same phase and separation is not required during measurement.

Dissociation-enhanced lanthanide fluorescent immunoassay (DELFIA) is a heterogeneous time-resolved fluorescence assay developed on a similar principle and workflow as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Both of them works on the principle that specific antibodies bind to the target antigen and detect the presence and quantity of antigens binding. During the LSD1 inhibitory activity test, the substrate, protein, and LSD1 inhibitor underwent pre-incubation before being placed on the plate coated with H3K4 antibodies (Fig. 3A). Post-incubation with certain Eu3+ chelates-tagged antibodies and subsequent washing, the separated lanthanide signal correlates with the LSD1 inhibitor's efficacy. DELFIA with high sensitivity mainly relies on long-lived photoluminescence of lanthanide chelates whose limitations include inferior photochemical stability, long-term cytotoxicity, high cost, etc. Despite these challenges, DELFIA has proven valuable in secondary screening and validation. For example, Schmitt et al. employed this assay to identify and rule out a false-positive LSD1 inhibitor initially flagged by virtual screening and validated by mass spectrometry.
More recently, DELFIA and ELISA-based formats have also been adapted to detect LSD1 protein–protein interactions, particularly with transcriptional repressors such as Gfi1b and INSM1. Given that LSD1 engages with Gfi1b via the SNAG domain, the direct binding between SNAG peptides and LSD1 can be quantitatively assessed using SNAG peptide–immobilized ELISA platforms. These approaches provide a basis for screening LSD1 scaffold inhibitors that disrupt key protein–protein interactions, extending the application of immunoassay-based methods beyond catalytic inhibition alone.

Homogeneous assay
Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)—also referred to as fluorescence resonance energy transfer—is a widely utilized homogeneous assay format for monitoring molecular proximity, particularly in high-throughput screening (HTS). Among its derivatives, time-resolved FRET (TR-FRET), such as homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence (HTRF), has emerged as a powerful tool in drug discovery pipelines. This evaluation technology has also been widely applied in the screening various compounds, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitor and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) kinase inhibitors, demonstrating its broad applicability in drug discovery. FRET is non-radiative energy transfer that occurs by dipole–dipole coupling from an excited state donor fluorophore to a ground state acceptor. Due to its inherent inverse-sixth-power distance dependence, the energy transfer could occur with a typical range of roughly 1–10 nm. In TR-FRET systems such as HTRF and lanthanide chelate excite (LANCE), lanthanide ions serve as long-lived energy donors—commonly Eu3+-cryptates (HTRF) or Eu3+-chelates (LANCE)—paired with compatible acceptors to enable time-gated signal detection.
Kinds of commercial LSD1 demethylase kits have been designed according to the principle that biotinylated H3K4 is recognized by the Eu3+ cryptates-labeled antibody (Eu-Ab) and XL665-streptavidin (XL665-SA). XL665 was the first acceptor developed for HTRF, a phycobiliprotein pigment purified from red algae, which is fully compatible with Eu3+ cryptates. Utilizing a biotinylated H3K4me1 as the substrate, the Eu3+-cryptates donor and XL665 acceptor dye molecules are brought into proximity because of the combination of biotin and streptavidin in LSD1 demethylase assay (Fig. 3B). The strength of XL665 emission signal is proportional to the concentration of biotinylated H3K4.
While both HTRF and DELFIA rely on Eu3+-labeled antibodies, their detection mechanisms differ fundamentally. HTRF measures energy transfer from the excited Eu3+-cryptate donor to the acceptor fluorophore, enabling homogeneous, no-wash detection. In contrast, DELFIA involves dissociation and quantification of Eu3+ chelates after antigen recognition, necessitating washing and separation steps. What's more, HTRF also integrates the benefits such as sensitivity and conveniency and does not require additional separation steps, such as centrifuging, washing, filtrating or magnetic partitioning which is dissimilar with DELFIA. Additionally, compared with HTRF, the lanthanide chelates in LANCE have the disadvantage that the stability of chelate does not fit the low pH and high concentration of proteins and ions which may cause the loss of signal.
This technology is particularly well-suited for the identification of LSD1 scaffold inhibitors that disrupt protein-protein interaction within the LC complex. For example, HTRF-based assays can be employed to monitor interactions between LSD1 and zinc finger transcription factors such as TBX2 or members of the SNAG family. Inhibitors that perturb these interactions—without directly targeting LSD1's catalytic activity—represent a promising class of allosteric or scaffolding modulators with potential therapeutic relevance. Notably, optimal protein concentration is necessary to avoid the hook effect which may lead to an unreal readout of the HTRF ratio.

Amplified luminescent proximity homogeneous assay (Alpha)
Alpha is a technique based on the chemiluminescent signal detection, which is also consisted of the acceptor beads and donor beads. Alpha has been successfully used to evaluate compounds targeting coronavirus and HDAC, suggesting that the technology is robust across different molecular contexts. The principle of Alpha screen is based on the composition described as follows. The phthalocyanine photosensitizer contained in the donor beads could excite the ambient oxygen into the singlet state following high energy irradiation. As the range of the oxygen singlets diffusion is limited, the acceptor beads could only receive the oxygen singlets that comes into close proximity. Afterwards a series of the energy transfer happens among three chemical dyes contained in the acceptor beads. The singlet oxygen initially reacts with thioxene to generate light that is transferred to anthracene and then to rubrene, resulting in a broad emission from 520 to 620 nm. With the biotinylated H3K4me1/2 as substrates, this reaction detects unmodified substrates by incorporating streptavidin donor beads and acceptor beads conjugated to H3K4 antibody. The intensity of light emission is proportional to the level of biotinylated H3K4.
Like the europium-labeled method described above, this method only requires less amount of H3K4me1/2 and LSD1 to achieve efficient, high-throughput screening.

Byproduct detection
The aforementioned direct approach serves as an inspirational tool for measuring the substrates. HCHO and H2O2, two byproducts formed during the reaction, are equally crucial elements. The ultimate measurements of two byproducts depend on microplate readers, which impose restrictions on maximum and minimum fluorescence intensities that is capable of detecting. The detection of both byproducts necessitates the presence of additional compounds or enzymes, which restrains the accuracy of the inhibitory activity measurement and makes varieties of methods unsuitable for certain conditions.

H2O2 detections
Various extracellular methods for the detection of H2O2 have been reported in the literature. The main methods used in the determination of LSD1 inhibitor activity include (a) fluorescent probe-based methods, and (b) spectrophotometric methods.

Fluorescent probe-based methods
Fluorescent probes are designed and utilized for H2O2 quantification based on different detection mechanism. One of the mechanism is that the fluorophore is exposed or released after the protective group reacts with H2O2 and is removed, such as HKPerox-1/2 and HKPerox-Red. Another mechanism is that H2O2 reacts with the suitable substrate to produce a detectable fluorescent substance under the catalysis of horseradish peroxidase (HRP), such as Amplex red (AR).

In LSD1 inhibitors screening assay, AR may be the most common and widely used method, which is a colorless non-fluorescent probe and utilized to detect nanomolar concentration H2O2 produced during compound screening procedure. With the presence of HRP and H2O2, AR gives rise to a fluorescent product, resorufin (Fig. 4A). The AR-HRP system allows the quantification of the byproduct-H2O2, which serves as a highly sensitive indicator for screening LSD1 inhibitors. However, further researches revealed that AR could also be converted to resorufin by exposing to ambient room light in absence of H2O2 and HRP. As previously mentioned, resorufin is also a peroxidase substrate and can undergo catalytic reaction to yield a colorless product potentially impacting the determination of H2O2. For methods in which HRP involved, some compounds exhibit HRP inhibition and reduce the production of H2O2, thus leading to false positive results. Besides, Tama et al. found that the compounds containing phenolic decreased the yield of resorufin, especially with the low concentration H2O2. Compared with AR, the fluorescent probe like HKPerox-1/2 is more sensitive and enzyme-free, but it is more expensive and there is a lack of literature reports on its application in LSD1 inhibitor screening. All in all, the application of fluorescence probe is restrained by the following drawbacks, such as short emission wavelength, low quantum yield, and poor structural tunability.
This strategy is particularly effective for identifying reversible LSD1 inhibitors, primarily through the use of jump dilution analysis. The underlying principle of this approach is that, upon substantial dilution of the enzyme–inhibitor complex, reversible inhibitors dissociate, allowing the enzymatic activity of LSD1 to recover over time. During the test, the recovery of LSD1 activity is indicated by the enhancement of resorufin fluorescence. Nonetheless, the fluorescence of resorufin itself also increases over time, potentially causing incorrect positive outcomes. While, in principle, jump dilution can be integrated with a variety of detection modalities to evaluate inhibitor reversibility, resorufin-based fluorescence detection remains the most widely adopted method due to its simplicity, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness.

Spectrophotometric methods
4-Aminoantipyrine (AAY) assay is a spectrophotometric method in LSD1 inhibitors screening, whose principle is that AAY and 5-dichloro-2-hydroxybenzenesulfonic acid can be catalyzed by HRP in the presence of H2O2 and yield quinonimine dye detected at the wavelength of 515 nm by spectrophotometer. Both method used HRP have the same shortcomings, such as interference from other compounds, low stability, limited dynamic range.

HCHO detections
Same as the H2O2 quantification method classification, the method for HCHO detection could also be classified into two groups: (a) fluorescence detection method and (b) spectrophotometric method.

Fluorescence detection method
Formed in the demethylation, the HCHO could be catalyzed by HCHO dehydrogenase (FDH) and result in the conversion of HCHO into formic acid through the reduction of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) to NADH. The fluorescence of the NADH excited at 355 nm can be monitored by a plate reader to represent the amount of HCHO (Fig. 4B). Same as HRP, the FDH enzyme can be inhibited by other compounds. Although the methods to byproduct detection are label-free, low cost, false positive or negative consequences should be highly thought of.
Purpald, a formaldehyde-selective reactive probe, is another method to quantify the HCHO formation in the LSD1 inhibitor screening based on the surface enhanced raman (SER) scattering. Under the alkaline conditions, purpald reacts with HCHO and forms purpald-formaldehyde adduct which could self-assemble on the surfaces of peptide-stabilized gold nanoparticles to release a SER signal. What's more, the production of HCHO is proportional the SER signal. The method detects quickly and can prevent interference from intrinsic fluorescence. However, this method may be limited by the SER itself, for low signal causing issues on selectivity, accuracy and interference from the matrix components.

Spectrophotometric method
Oxidized o-phenylenediamine (OPDox), a groundbreaking fluorescent and colorimetric sensor, is commonly employed due to its distinctive physicochemical property. Deng et al. found that the addition of formaldehyde can lead to a decrease in the fluorescence formed by 2,3-diaminophenazine (one of the OPDox), which is caused by various oxidants such as Cu2+, Ag+, and H2O2, and exhibits the fluorescence and absorbance signal. This is due to the fact that Ag+ may generate silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) in OPD containing buffer which could promote the redox reaction between Ag+ and OPD. If HCHO is added to the reaction, the catalytic ability of AgNPs will be decreased and the yield of OPDox is also decreased. However, this method is clearly not suitable for LSD1 inhibitor screening, as H2O2, another byproduct, also reacts with OPD and promotes the formation of OPDox which increases the fluorescence instead and causes the quantification error. At the same time, compared AR method for measuring H2O2, it may require higher quantities of LSD1 and H3K4me2 and extended duration of time.

Scintillation proximity assay (SPA)
SPA, a homogeneous scintillant bead-based platform for measurement of biological processes, played an important role in identification of active chemical entities in drug discovery. SPA beads are designed to capture molecules, such as DNA, protein A, avidin, and streptavidin. Upon binding of a radiolabeled ligand to the scintillant-infused beads, β-particles emitted by the isotope transfer energy to the scintillant within the beads, leading to light emission. When the distance between unbound radiolabel and bead is shorter than the maximum path length of radio isotype, the dissipation of β-particle energy to the solution occurs. For LSD1 activity assay, SPA is adapted to detect demethylation indirectly through a two-step enzymatic cascade. After LSD1 has demethylated the biotinylated H3K4me1 and been quenched by the heat shock, the methyltransferase such as lysine methyltransferases 7 (KMT7) would transfer the methyl from S-(5′-adenosyl)-L-methionine (H3-SAM) to the only demethylated substrates. Then the mixture would be incubated in the streptavidin/scintillant coated microplate and the SPA occurs. Beyond LSD1 inhibitors, this method has proven effective in the evaluation of other targets, including enhancer of Zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) and alanine-serine-cysteine transporter-1, further supporting its reliability and versatility. Despite these strengths, SPA does have notable limitations. Compared with the previously mentioned method, the steps of SPA are complicated and time-consuming which is not beneficial to high-throughput screening. What's more, the signal-to-noise ratio will be impacted by the concentration of beads, as well as the ratio of protein to bead.

In conclusion, to screen compounds with better enzymatic activity, various detection techniques can be devised based on the products after the reaction. For a clearer comparison, the strengths and weaknesses of each method are outlined in Table 2. While direct quantification of H3K4 methylation provides high specificity, it often necessitates costly equipment (e.g., mass spectrometry) or highly selective antibodies, limiting its utility in high-throughput settings. Conversely, methods that detect enzymatic by-products offer a more rapid and cost-effective solution, rendering them better suited for primary screening. Integrating complementary approaches may enhance both sensitivity and throughput, offering a more comprehensive assessment of compound efficacy during early-phase discovery.

The virtual screening (VS) of LSD1 inhibitors
Traditional methods of drug discovery, which entail a large amount of compounds activity detection, are typically expensive and time-consuming. Instead of conducting tests on each compound within given library, VS applies computational tools to predict compound properties and prioritize those most likely to bind effectively to the target of interest. Previous studies on tyrosine phosphatase inhibitors and ubiquitin specific protease 7 (USP7) inhibitors have also demonstrated the efficiency of this technology, supporting its application in the screening of LSD1 inhibitors. With the development of artificial intelligence and the supplement of database, VS has been gradually applied to develop novel compounds, reuse drugs and improve pharmaceutical productivity. Broadly, VS can be divided into two main types including (a) structure-based virtual screening (SBVS) which is suitable for target proteins with known crystal structures to perform virtual screening through competition for active sites, and (b) ligand-based virtual screening (LBVS) that capitalizes on the fact that the ligands similar to active ligands are more likely to be active than random ligands.

SBVS
SBVS uses the three-dimensional (3D) structure of target protein to predict the binding affinity of candidate compounds and evaluate their compatibility with the binding pocket. Molecular docking is an essential method in structure-based design. Based on the protein structure, molecular docking algorithms aim to predict both the binding mode of a ligand and the affinity of the complex. For example, the crystal structure of LSD1 reveals three main domains, among which the AOD forms a large, negatively charged substrate-binding pocket. This pocket serves as the catalytically active site and accommodates the N-terminal tail of histone H3, making it a logical target for structure-based docking studies. The screening process could be developed through different compound ligand libraries within the Glide, Iinternal coordinate mechanics (ICM), GOLD scoring. The assorted scoring system is also capable of executing subsequent screenings post the initial round. Recently a new binding mode target allosteric bending of LSD1 has been reported which induces significant allosteric bending of Tower/CoREST domain, disrupting the nucleosome substrate binding. SBVS can now be directed towards identifying molecules that specifically target this specific, newly characterized allosteric pocket and induce the desired conformational change.

LBVS
Different with SBVS, the first step of LBVS is to find the optimal pharmacophore model. For LSD1 utilizes FAD as an essential cofactor for catalytic activities, the docking protocols could be conducted with the adenosine phosphate fragment of FAD, and riboflavin fragment of FAD. Both of them have been showed that they played an important role in anchoring the flavin to enzyme. Small molecules inhibitors like ORY1001, IMG7289, and GSK2879552, capable of creating an adduct with FAD for competitive attachment to the FAD binding site, are viable as ligands for developing a pharmacodynamic model. Another pharmacophore model is usually constructed based on the substrates binding site. Importantly, the quality of the pharmacophore model depends heavily on the training set, which should typically include 20 or more structurally diverse active compounds. The suitable training set acquired by 20 compounds or more is crucial for pharmacophore model. The following steps are database-based screening and drug-likeness analysis which help us filter out non-drug-like compounds in hits.
Utilized approaches concurrently improve the design and screening of LSD1 inhibition significantly. However, discrepancies between predicted and experimental inhibitory activity are frequently observed. Such inconsistencies may result from limitations in scoring algorithms or an incomplete understanding of the interaction modes that affect enzymatic activity. Additionally, the success of VS is highly dependent on the quality and quantity of available input data, as well as the predictive power and specificity of the applied algorithms. Given that SBVS relies on the physicochemical attributes of compounds rather than their biological characteristics, the risk of false positives or false negatives remains.

LSD1 scaffold inhibitors screening and binding affinity assay
As a molecular scaffold, LSD1 orchestrates the assembly of diverse coregulators and chromatin-modifying enzymes, including ZNF516, MYT1, SFMBT1 and ZMYM1. Through these interactions, LSD1 contributes to the formation of repressive chromatin structures, thereby exerting broad influence over transcriptional programs and cellular functions. Importantly, these protein-protein interactions are not merely structural but are essential for the pathological roles of LSD1 in cancer progression and other diseases.
While traditional LSD1 inhibitors have predominantly focused on targeting its catalytic activity, emerging evidence suggests that disrupting the critical interactions between LSD1 and its binding partners may represent a novel and potentially more selective therapeutic strategy. This has led to increasing interest in the development of LSD1 scaffold inhibitors, which specifically interfere with the assembly or stability of LSD1-associated complexes, offering a promising alternative to catalytic inhibition.

FP is based on the principle that binding of a fluorescently labeled molecule to target alters its rotational diffusion. This, in turn, affects key photophysical parameters—such as fluorophore molecular volume, local rotational mobility, and fluorescence lifetime—all of which are detected by polarization-sensitive sensors. When environmental variables like temperature and solvent viscosity are constant, the fluorescence polarization signal is primarily governed by the size of the fluorescent species. According to the principle, the binding affinity could be detected as the change of fluorophore molecular volume which causes the increase of motion speed, and the emitted fluorescence will maintain a high degree of polarization (Fig. 5A). Besides, the binding affinity determination of a potential LSD1 inhibitor is designed as fluorescence labeled substrates competition experiments. Inhibitors that occupy the substrate-binding pocket compete with the labeled substrate, leading to a measurable decrease in FP signal, thereby reflecting binding affinity. What's more, fluorescence polarization assay has been utilized to discover DNA 6 mA demethylase ALKBH1 inhibitor and tetratricopeptide repeat-containing Rab8b interacting protein (TRIP8b) and hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gate channel 1 (HCN1) interaction inhibitors.
Because of the simple operation, fast response, and no background interference, FP has become more and more popular. However, one important consideration is that high concentrations of protein or small molecule inhibitors may interfere with polarized light detection, potentially confounding signal interpretation. Careful assay optimization and control experiments are therefore essential to ensure data accuracy and minimize false-positive or false-negative outcomes.

BLI
BLI is an efficient, label-free, and high-throughput technique widely employed to investigate protein–protein and protein–small molecule interactions. The method relies on detecting real-time changes in optical interference patterns that occur when an analyte binds to a biomolecule immobilized on the surface of a biosensor tip. These changes are translated into binding curves, from which both kinetic parameters (association and dissociation rates) and equilibrium binding affinities can be derived. In the context of LSD1 inhibitor screening, BLI is typically performed by immobilizing LSD1—often in its biotinylated form—on a streptavidin-coated biosensor (Fig. 5B). Varied dissociation trajectories are achievable through the pre-biotinylated LSD1 and the incubation of LSD1 inhibitors at diverse concentrations. For screening multiple candidate compounds, a cross-competition matrix can be designed to obtain compounds with better affinity by recording changes in signal values when two compounds are added to buffer in turn. BLI has been successfully applied in numerous drug discovery programs. For example, it facilitated the characterization of the interaction between lipopolysaccharide transport proteins LptA and LptC, and helped validate IMB881 as a selective interaction inhibitor. In summary, BLI represents a real-time, label-free platform for measuring biomolecular interactions, offering operational simplicity and compatibility with high-throughput formats. However, its utility is not without limitations. Key challenges include nonspecific binding artifacts, sensor-to-sensor variability, limited compatibility with certain buffer systems, and difficulties in accurately modeling complex kinetic behaviors. Additionally, the recurring cost of biosensor chips can pose financial constraints, particularly in large-scale screening campaigns. Given these considerations, BLI experiments must be carefully optimized and results interpreted with caution.

SPR
Similar to BLI, SPR is another important method to small molecule screening and binding affinity measurement by enhancing the optical absorption. Plasmon is illustrated as a phenomenon which depicts the collective longitudinal oscillation of electrons when the system is disturbed from equilibrium. In a classical SPR setup, the target biomolecule is immobilized on a sensor chip, and interaction with potential ligands in the flowing solution is monitored based on changes in the refractive index near the chip surface. In practice, the biomolecule interaction analysis core technique (BIAcore) senor chips help us to detect the different binding affinity between protein and other molecules, such as drug, protein, and DNA. When exploring LSD1 interaction with different inhibitors, the immobilized LSD1 using amine coupling will combine with the promising inhibitors. The buffer containing inhibitors will keep moving swiftly, and with the rise in inhibitor concentration, the quantity of inhibitors attaching to the chip also escalates (Fig. 5C).
In addition, this technique can also be used to distinguish between reversible and irreversible inhibitors of LSD1. Reversible interactions are characterized by a return of the SPR signal to baseline upon removal of the inhibitor. Compared to the jump dilution analysis, SPR provides greater precision with direct detection. However, the higher amounts of the inhibitor and protein involved also result in escalated costs. What's more, the LSD1 scaffold inhibitor T-3775440 was identified by SPR to demonstrate its suppressive effect on the molecular interaction involving the LC complex and Gfi-1b or INSM1.
However, a fundamental limitation of SPR lies in its sensitivity to molecular mass, as the response signal is directly proportional to the mass of the bound ligand. This presents a significant challenge when analyzing the binding of low-molecular-weight compounds, particularly in systems involving nanodisc-encapsulated membrane protein complexes, which often exhibit low surface immobilization density due to their large structural footprint on the sensor chip.

Other affinity evaluation methods for LSD1 inhibitors
The accurate identification of drug–target interactions is a fundamental step in the drug discovery process. Among the various tools available, affinity assays serve as a cornerstone of compound screening, enabling not only the confirmation of compound–protein binding, but also providing mechanistic insights into the nature and strength of these interactions. These assays play a critical role in triaging low-affinity or nonspecific compounds, thereby streamlining the progression of high-quality candidates. Although numerous techniques exist for conducting affinity assays, published literature indicates a scarcity of LSD1 inhibitor affinity assays, predominantly for assessing LSD1's binding capacity and its interaction with other proteins, whereas affinity for LSD1 inhibitors is mostly obtained in virtual screening. To address this gap and facilitate future efforts in LSD1-targeted drug discovery, we have compiled and critically summarized the commonly used affinity assay platforms, outlining their respective advantages and limitations. This overview is intended to guide the selection and application of appropriate techniques for accurate and efficient evaluation of LSD1 inhibitor interactions.

Given that heat change is closely linked to chemical interaction, the quantification of the heat generated or utilized, leading to temperature fluctuations, is directly proportional to the alteration in enthalpy during the reaction. ITC has been chosen as the first choice to investigate the thermodynamics biomolecular equilibria, involving protein–ligand, protein–protein, protein–metal ions and protein–DNA interactions. The broad use of this technique in evaluating other compounds, such as human soluble epoxide hydrolase inhibitors and mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2) inhibitors, provides additional evidence of its versatility and strengthens its value in LSD1-related studies. A range of thermodynamic parameters can be determined either during or after the reaction, including the melting transition temperature (Tm) and the equilibrium binding constant (Kb) (Fig. 6).
In LSD1 inhibitors’ affinity applications, LSD1 is placed in the sample pool and negative pool (Fig. 6). With the inhibitors loaded in the syringe injector titrating, the calorimetric data will be recorded. Researchers are able to optimize the structure of these compounds by assessing the binding affinity of various LSD1 inhibitors, thereby boosting compounds effectiveness in pharmacology. On top of that, it also helps us understand the mechanism that how LSD1 inhibitor function. For example, the calorimetric data was detected to analyze binding affinity difference when the different domains of LSD1 were placed in the pool.
With modern calorimetric instrumentation capable of detecting heat changes as low as 1μ calorie, ITC offers exceptional sensitivity. However, several limitations restrict its broader application. Massive protein consumption and interfering reaction occurring during binding such as oxidation and reduction also limits the scope of ITC utilization. Furthermore, challenges remain when characterizing interactions with low binding enthalpy or very slow association/dissociation kinetics, which may compromise data quality and interpretability.

Fluorescence thermal shift assay (FTSA)
TSA assays are mostly based on the determination of intracellular LSD1 and compound affinity, but the results take a long time to generate, which is disadvantageous for high-throughput assays. Instead of detecting intracellular affinity, the fluorescence thermal shift assay (also known as differential scanning fluorimetry or ThermoFluor) is a method of monitoring hydrophobic region of proteins while heating. The whole assay is based on the principle that the protein would be more stable after the combination with compound for the formation of additional non-covalent interactions, like hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces. Fluorescence dye such as SYPRO orange will be used to probe with hydrophobic region. In LSD1 affinity assay, SYPRO orange binds to the exposed hydrophobic regions of LSD1 undergoing thermal denaturation and corresponding fluorescence emissions are recorded as melt curves. The enhanced stability caused by the compound will be recorded as the right shift of the melt curve.
Compared with ITC which has described above, FTSA devotes to less time and sample consuming, and is not limited to sophisticated devices and complicated data analysis.

Micro scale thermophoresis (MST)
MST leverages the principle of thermophoresis—a phenomenon in which molecules migrate along a temperature gradient. This movement is highly sensitive to changes in molecular properties such as size, charge, hydration shell, and conformational state. The binding of a ligand to the target molecule would lead to changes in its molecular properties described above. Depending on the instrumentation used, MST enables both labeled and label-free detection. For instance, Monolith NT.LabelFree allows intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence-based analysis without the need for labeling, while Monolith NT.115Pico facilitates detection of binding events at the low picomolar range, offering high sensitivity. In the binding affinity assay, the interaction between red fluorescent dye labeled LSD1 and its inhibitors can be inspected by Monolith NT.115. Notably, MST is capable of detecting binding events even in the absence of pronounced changes in size or charge, owing to its sensitivity to alterations in solvation entropy induced by ligand engagement. However, the signal changes detected by MST are not specific, the reason is that the changes in temperature can also cause corresponding signal changes. Moreover, in experiments utilizing intrinsic fluorescence (e.g., tryptophan-based detection), background fluorescence and sample autofluorescence must be carefully controlled to avoid confounding results.

Discussion and perspective
Detection of LSD1 activity is a key step in the development and evaluation of LSD1 inhibitors targeting cancer and neurodevelopmental disorder. In this review, current LSD1 inhibitor screening strategies are categorized into two major approaches: LSD1 enzymatic activity assay and LSD1 scaffold inhibitor assay. Each method presents distinct advantages and limitations, as detailed above. Future progress will depend on addressing several critical challenges in the following: 1) the development of reversible LSD1 inhibitors, 2) mitigation of false positive results in the screening process, 3) reduction of on-target and off-target effects in clinical studies, 4) design and discovery of inhibitors based on the LSD1 scaffold function, such as allosteric inhibitors and LSD1-CoREST-X complex inhibitors, and 5) exploration of non-histone substrate-based screening strategies.
Although reversible inhibitors show better selectivity and therapeutic advantages, researches on the design and discovery of reversible inhibitors are relatively lacking. This review outlines relevant techniques for evaluating reversible inhibitors. The jump dilution analysis combined with resorufin fluorescence detection and the SPR method in affinity measurements are commonly used. For jump dilution analysis, enzyme activity restoration is indicated by the enhanced fluorescence signal, yet over time, this signal's value inherently rises. Moreover, this technique fails to negate the compound's effect on the detection mechanism. Conversely, SPR offers a more straightforward approach to detection, yet it incurs higher costs. Consequently, identifying and uncovering techniques for reversible inhibitors will be a primary focus in upcoming research.
False-positive results, particularly in high-throughput screening, present a major bottleneck in drug discovery, often arising from pan-assay interference compounds (PAINS). These artifacts are linked to factors such as protein reactivity, photophysical interference, light-induced reactivity, singlet oxygen quenching, metal chelation, and redox activity. Consequently, multi-technology integration is required to tackle PAIN, for example H2O2 detection combined HTRF.
In clinical research, both reversible and irreversible inhibitors face the dilemma of on-target or off-target effects. Although there are currently no solutions to address such issues in practical applications and research, the use of technologies like nanomedicine, peptides conjugation, nucleic acids conjugation, and antibody conjugation for targeting cancer tissue specifically and delivery of LSD1 inhibitors should be another priority in the future.
The third focus of future work is to discover allosteric inhibitors and inhibitors that interrupt LC complex interactions, which are independent of enzymatic activity. For proteins that have already been identified to interact with the LC complex, such as the SNAG family, methods like HTRF can be used to screen inhibitors interrupting protein-protein interactions. However, identifying unknown interaction partners and elucidating intracellular mechanisms remains a long-term challenge requiring further technological refinement and mechanistic insight. Utilizing non-histone substrates might face limitations owing to challenges in the synthesis and purification of methylated non-histone substrates. Current researches indicate that LSD1 inhibitors, discovered via histone substrate screening, also show demethylation inhibition effects. Therefore, the practical use of non-histones substrates is on the decline.
