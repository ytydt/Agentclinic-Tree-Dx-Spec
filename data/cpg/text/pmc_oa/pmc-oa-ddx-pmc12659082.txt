Usability evaluation methods for hospital information systems: a systematic review
Background
Hospital Information Systems (HIS) play a pivotal role in improving healthcare delivery by minimizing errors, increasing efficiency, and enhancing user satisfaction. Post-implementation usability evaluation is essential for optimizing these systems. This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis and comparative analysis of usability evaluation methods and tools applied across different HIS subsystems, offering evidence-based recommendations to inform future system design and research directions.
Methods
A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, SID, Magiran, Elmnet, and Google (first 10 pages) on January 14, 2024, following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. From 21,592 records, 41 studies met inclusion criteria. Eligible studies applied quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods to evaluate HIS or its subsystems (e.g. NIS, LIS, CPOE). Data extraction and quality appraisal (using a modified Downs & Black checklist) were performed independently by two reviewers.
Results
Among 41 included studies, user testing (UT) and heuristic evaluation (HE) were the most frequently applied methods (each in 34% of studies). The most commonly used tools were standardized questionnaires (40%) and checklists (36%), with SUS and Nielsen’s heuristics being predominant. Usability criteria assessed included user satisfaction (45%), efficiency (45%), error reduction (40%), learnability (19%), and flexibility (16%). HIS subsystems such as NIS (20%), CPOE (12%), and LIS (7%) were the most frequently evaluated. Geographically, 61% of the studies were conducted in low- and middle-income countries, with Iran accounting for the majority (56%), and most research was published between 2016 and 2020 (41%).
Conclusion
This review highlights methodological trends and gaps in HIS usability research. Combining qualitative and quantitative methods enhances the accuracy of usability assessments. Future research should adopt AI-based evaluation tools, simulate clinical workflows, and address underexplored subsystems and criteria (e.g. learnability, flexibility) to improve HIS design and implementation.

Introduction
In today’s technology-driven healthcare landscape, digital information systems are central to enhancing care quality, minimizing medical errors, and supporting clinical decision-making. Among these technologies, Hospital Information Systems (HIS) serve as core components of Health Information Technology (HIT), facilitating integrated workflows, improving data management, and ultimately optimizing patient outcomes. As healthcare organizations increasingly adopt these systems, the usability and functional adequacy of HIS have emerged as critical determinants of their operational effectiveness.
Although HIS implementation has demonstrated significant benefits in terms of process efficiency and error reduction, persistent challenges remain concerning usability, interface complexity, and end-user satisfaction. Inadequate system design and technical limitations can disrupt clinical workflows, increase cognitive burden for healthcare professionals, and even contribute to adverse patient outcomes. Moreover, the substantial financial investments required for HIS procurement and maintenance underscore the necessity of rigorous usability evaluations to ensure cost-effectiveness and sustainable adoption.
Usability—defined as the extent to which a system enables users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction—is a fundamental concept in human-computer interaction (HCI). Numerous evaluation techniques have been employed to assess HIS usability, including user testing (UT), heuristic evaluation (HE), cognitive walkthroughs (CW), and standardized questionnaires. However, previous research in this domain is marked by methodological heterogeneity, lack of standardized evaluation frameworks, and limited subsystem-specific analysis, making it difficult to generalize or operationalize findings across diverse clinical environments.
Given the structural complexity and functional variability among HIS subsystems—such as Nursing Information Systems (NIS), Laboratory Information Systems (LIS), Clinical Decision Support Systems (CDSS), and Computerized Physician Order Entry (CPOE)—there is a growing need for a systematic, evidence-based approach to match usability evaluation methods with specific subsystem characteristics and use cases. Addressing this methodological gap is crucial for developing tailored evaluation strategies that align with the unique goals, workflows, and end-user profiles associated with each subsystem.
This systematic review builds upon and extends previous literature by providing a comprehensive comparative analysis of usability evaluation methods across a wide spectrum of HIS subsystems. In contrast to prior reviews that have focused on isolated methods or narrow system contexts, our study offers a structured mapping between evaluation techniques and subsystem domains. This framework aims to reduce methodological fragmentation, enhance comparability across studies, and guide both researchers and practitioners in selecting appropriate evaluation approaches.
In addition to synthesizing current evidence, this review provides preliminary recommendations regarding the suitability of various usability methods for different HIS components, offering practical insights for system designers, usability specialists, and healthcare policymakers. The outcomes are intended to support informed decision-making and promote more user-centered, efficient, and context-sensitive HIS development and implementation in clinical settings.

Methods

Study design and reporting standards
This systematic review was conducted to identify, analyze, and compare usability evaluation methods for HIS and its subsystems. The study was designed and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and replicability.

Search strategy
A comprehensive search was conducted on January 14, 2024, across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, SID, Magiran, Elmnet, and the first ten Google result pages, without time limits. Search terms were developed using MeSH and Emtree, based on expert input, and categorized into two groups: (1) HIS-related terms and (2) usability evaluation terms. These were combined using the Boolean operator AND, with syntax tailored to each database to maximize retrieval precision (Appendix 1).

Eligibility criteria
To ensure the inclusion of relevant and high-quality studies, predefined criteria were applied. Eligible studies focused on the usability of HIS or its subsystems (e.g., NIS, LIS, RIS, PIS, CPOE), employed quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method approaches, and assessed metrics such as efficiency, effectiveness, or user satisfaction.
Exclusion criteria included studies unrelated to usability (e.g., technical performance, cost), those focused on systems other than HIS (e.g., generic EHRs), lacking methodological clarity, or not published in English or Persian.

Study selection and data extraction
Study selection followed a three-step process: initial title/abstract screening, full-text review, and final inclusion. Two reviewers conducted the process independently, with a third resolving disagreements.
Data extraction was performed using a standardized form and included: author, year, country, user characteristics, HIS subsystem type, evaluation methods (e.g., user testing, heuristic evaluation), usability criteria, and key findings. Two reviewers completed extraction independently.

Quality assessment
Study quality was assessed using a modified version of the Downs & Black checklist, adapted for usability evaluation in health information systems. The original tool comprises 27 items across reporting, validity, bias, confounding, and power domains. However, since many items are not applicable to non-interventional studies such as HIS usability evaluations, we adopted a 10-item modified version proposed by Wronikowska et al.. This usability-oriented version focuses on clarity of objectives, methodological transparency, relevance of evaluation methods, participant representativeness, and alignment between methods and results. Studies were classified as high (scores ≥8), moderate (6–7), or low quality (≤5). No study was excluded based on quality rating. Quality appraisal was independently conducted by two researchers with domain expertise in health informatics and usability evaluation. In cases of disagreement, a senior investigator with over 10 years of experience in digital health and Health Technology Assessment (HTA) resolved conflicts and provided the final judgment to ensure methodological consistency and rigor.

Data synthesis and analysis
Due to significant methodological heterogeneity across the included studies—such as variations in evaluation designs, HIS subsystems, and usability metrics—a meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, a thematic synthesis approach was employed to identify, compare, and organize recurring patterns and usability insights across the selected studies.

Frequencies and proportions were used to quantify evaluation trends. This structured thematic analysis provided a comparative understanding of how various usability evaluation methods are applied across different HIS subsystems and healthcare contexts, in accordance with PRISMA-based qualitative synthesis guidelines.
Additionally, temporal trends in publication years were analyzed to explore the evolution of research focus over the past two decades. The geographical distribution of included studies was also assessed to highlight regional contributions and disparities in HIS usability research.

Results

Study selection
Out of an initial pool of 21,592 articles, a total of 41 studies were selected for final analysis following the screening process. The findings revealed that UT (34%) and HE (34%) were the most commonly employed usability assessment methods for HIS. The primary evaluation tools consisted of standardized checklists (36%) and questionnaires (40%), both of which predominantly focused on assessing key usability components, including user satisfaction (45%), efficiency (45%), and error reduction (40%).

Temporal trends in published studies
Among the 41 included studies, most were conducted in Iran (n = 23, 56%), followed by the United States (n = 7, 17%) and the Netherlands (n = 3, 7%). Other countries—including France, Germany, Canada, Finland, Brazil, South Korea, China, and Spain—each contributed one study (2%). Overall, 56% of studies originated from developing countries, indicating a notable research interest in HIS usability in resource-limited settings, alongside continued activity in technologically advanced nations. This geographical pattern reflects both global engagement and regional disparities in HIS usability research (Fig. 2).

Temporal trends in published studies
Temporal analysis revealed a steady increase in HIS usability research over the past two decades. Initial activity was limited, with only 3% of studies published between 2000–2005 and 5% between 2006–2010. A more substantial rise occurred from 2011–2015 (20%), followed by a peak between 2016–2020 (41%), coinciding with widespread HIS adoption and heightened awareness of usability challenges. From 2021 to early 2024, research remained active (32%), with growing emphasis on innovative solutions to usability problems. These trends reflect a sustained and evolving interest in HIS usability evaluation (Fig. 3).

Hospital information system subsystems
Before presenting the results per HIS subsystem, it is important to contextualize the evaluation methods and tools employed in the reviewed studies. The most commonly used evaluation methods included HE (34%), UT (34%), TA (15%), and CW (5%). Evaluation tools varied, with standardized checklists—particularly Nielsen’s heuristics—being the most prevalent (36%), followed by structured questionnaires (40%) such as the System Usability Scale (SUS) and the Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire (PSSUQ). These tools were selected based on their ability to measure core usability criteria such as efficiency, satisfaction, error prevention, learnability, and system flexibility. The combination of qualitative and quantitative approaches across studies enabled comprehensive assessment of real-world usability in diverse HIS subsystems.

Nursing information system (NIS)
A total of 8 studies (20%) focused on NIS usability evaluation, highlighting key challenges related to efficiency, effectiveness, and user error reduction. The findings suggest that a combination of user-based methods such as think-aloud protocols and standardized questionnaires, alongside expert-based approaches like HE, is the most effective strategy for assessing NIS usability. This integrated approach enables a comprehensive identification of usability issues, facilitating system redesign and enhancing user experience.

Laboratory information system (LIS)
Three studies (7%) examined LIS usability, emphasizing its role in workflow optimization and user interaction enhancement. The evidence suggests that direct user observation, standardized questionnaires, and HE together provide the most effective usability assessment. This approach helps identify practical challenges, informs system redesign, and contributes to improving efficiency and user satisfaction.

Computerized physician order entry system (CPOE)
Five studies (12%) explored CPOE usability, confirming its effectiveness in reducing medication errors and improving system efficiency. The recommended evaluation approach includes HE, UT, and standardized tools such as the System Usability Scale (SUS) and usability questionnaires. This combined methodology enhances system efficiency, minimizes prescribing errors, and improves user satisfaction.

Radiology information system (RIS)
Four studies (10%) focused on RIS usability, primarily examining its impact on reducing image data processing time and increasing user satisfaction. The most effective usability evaluation approach combines UT, HE, SUS, and standardized questionnaires. These methods help streamline image processing, optimize workflow efficiency, and enhance overall system usability.

Admission, discharge, and transfer system (ADT)
Five studies (12%) assessed ADT usability, indicating that usability evaluation tools mainly focused on effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction. The integration of UT, HE, and standardized usability tools (SUS, questionnaires) is recommended for comprehensive usability assessment. This approach allows for precise problem identification, improved system efficiency, and enhanced user experience.

Pharmacy information system (PIS)
Only one study (2%) evaluated PIS usability. The recommended approach includes UT combined with qualitative tools, such as satisfaction questionnaires and user feedback analysis. This methodology contributes to system design optimization and enhanced user experience.

Emergency information system (EIS)
Three studies (7%) focused on EIS usability. The most effective approach integrates UT to identify performance issues, combined with HE to analyze interface design factors. These methods support system designers in better understanding user needs, leading to improved usability and user-centered design enhancements.

Picture archiving and communication system (PACS)
Two studies (5%) examined PACS usability. The preferred evaluation method involves UT to identify operational issues, combined with task analysis to understand complex workflow requirements. These approaches facilitate the development of more intuitive PACS systems, ensuring improved usability and enhanced user experience.

Clinical decision support system (CDSS)
Four studies (10%) focused on CDSS usability assessment. The most effective evaluation approach includes HE for identifying usability issues in early design stages, complemented by UT to assess real-world user interactions. These methods aid in improving system design and ensuring more effective decision support for healthcare professionals.

Electronic patient records (EPR, EHR, EMR)
Eight studies (17%) investigated EPR, EHR, and EMR usability. Findings emphasize the importance of user-centered design and system usability improvements. The best approach integrates both qualitative and quantitative usability assessment methods, including user feedback surveys and task analysis, to evaluate real-world workflow efficiency. This methodology ensures optimal usability enhancements, improving system accessibility and user satisfaction.

Usability assessment tools used in HISs

Checklists
Checklists were employed in 18 studies (40%), with the Nielsen usability checklist being the most commonly used, appearing in 16 studies (36%). Additionally, a custom-designed checklist was used in one study (2%).

Questionnaires
A total of 18 studies (40%) utilized questionnaires as a usability evaluation tool. While some studies relied solely on questionnaires, others combined them with interviews and direct observations to enhance the depth of usability assessments.

Interviews and observations
Interviews and direct observations were employed in 6 studies (14%). These studies often incorporated questionnaires alongside interviews or combined interviews with observational methods to gather more comprehensive usability insights.

System usability scale (SUS)
The SUS was used in 5 studies (12%), highlighting its role as a standardized tool for assessing the overall usability of HIS.
Overall, checklists and standardized questionnaires were the most frequently used usability evaluation tools in HIS research, indicating their broad applicability in structured and systematic usability assessments.

Usability evaluation methods in HIS
Distribution of usability evaluation methods used in studies related to hospital information systems
The findings revealed a wide range of usability evaluation methods, each serving a distinct role in assessing different aspects of system usability. These methods included HE, UT, TA protocols, and CW. Some studies implemented a combination of multiple methods to achieve a more comprehensive usability analysis. Figure 4 illustrates the distribution of usability evaluation methods applied in HIS-related studies.

Usability testing (UT)
The most frequently used method, applied in 14 studies (34%), was UT, which focuses on real-world user interactions with the system. This method is particularly valuable for evaluating system performance in practical environments, allowing researchers to analyze actual user experiences and system functionality.

Heuristic evaluation (HE)
The second most commonly used method, HE, was applied in 14 studies (34%). This method relies on predefined usability principles to identify usability issues and is predominantly used in early-stage system development to refine design components.

Think-aloud protocol
Used in 6 studies (15%), the think-aloud method focuses on analyzing users’ cognitive processes while interacting with the system. This approach enables researchers to uncover deeper usability challenges and gain insights into decision-making processes.

Cognitive walkthrough (CW)
This method was applied in 2 studies (5%), emphasizing users’ cognitive processes when performing specific tasks. CW is particularly effective for evaluating complex workflows and task-driven interactions in HIS usability assessments.

Combination of multiple methods
Three studies (7%) employed a combination of two or more usability evaluation methods to ensure a more holistic analysis of system usability. This multi-method approach allows for a more comprehensive assessment of different usability dimensions, strengthening the reliability of findings.
The selection of evaluation methods in the reviewed studies appears to be influenced by the specific goals of each subsystem’s usability assessment. For instance, heuristic evaluation was typically applied in early-stage design or redesign processes, especially for subsystems with static interfaces such as RIS and PACS. In contrast, user testing was preferred for dynamic systems like CPOE and ADT, where real-time task execution and workflow integration are critical. Similarly, think-aloud protocols were often used to explore user decision-making in high-risk environments such as CDSS and Emergency Information Systems. This methodological alignment indicates an intentional strategy to tailor usability evaluations to subsystem complexity, user roles, and clinical risk levels.

Usability evaluation criteria for HISs
This study identified several key criteria used to evaluate the usability of Hospital Information Systems (HIS). Among these, user satisfaction and system efficiency received the highest attention, with 19 studies (45%) emphasizing their significance. This finding highlights the importance of positive user experience and optimal system performance in HIS usability.
User errors were examined in 17 studies (40%), reflecting a strong focus on minimizing human errors and enhancing system accuracy. Additionally, system effectiveness was analyzed in 12 studies (28%), underscoring the ability of HIS to deliver precise and meaningful outcomes that support clinical and administrative workflows.
Other usability dimensions received comparatively less attention. Learnability, assessed in 8 studies (19%), focused on user training and ease of adoption, while ease of use, discussed in 7 studies (16%), examined how intuitive and user-friendly HIS interfaces are. Flexibility, also explored in 7 studies (16%), emphasized the system’s adaptability to evolving user needs and clinical requirements.

Discussion

Interpretation of main findings
This systematic review synthesized 41 studies on usability evaluation in Hospital Information Systems (HIS), highlighting considerable variation in research focus across different subsystems. NIS (20%), CPOE (12%), and LIS (7%) received the highest attention—likely due to their high clinical workload, frequent user interaction, and direct influence on patient outcomes. The greater research focus on these modules may also reflect broader institutional priorities such as medication safety (CPOE), documentation accuracy (NIS), and diagnostic workflow optimization (LIS).
Notably, NIS studies combined TA and HE techniques to uncover both cognitive bottlenecks and design violations, while standardized instruments such as SUS and QUIS captured satisfaction and perceived effectiveness. This methodological triangulation appeared especially effective for capturing the dual nature of usability—both in terms of system functionality and user perception.
For CPOE, the use of HE helped identify early interface issues such as visual complexity and non-intuitive menu structures, while UT offered insight into workflow integration and user burden during real-time prescription tasks. Studies reported that systems with intuitive navigation scored higher on SUS, and were linked to fewer prescribing errors, underscoring the need for seamless human-computer interaction in time-sensitive contexts.
LIS-focused evaluations—though fewer in number—demonstrated the utility of direct user observation and task-based assessments for capturing operational challenges specific to laboratory workflows. However, the limited evidence base suggests an urgent need for broader investigations into LIS usability, particularly in resource-constrained environments.
Similarly, evaluations of RIS, EIS, and PIS revealed critical usability challenges in image data handling, emergency workflow responsiveness, and medication interface complexity, respectively. Despite their specialized nature and relatively lower user base, these subsystems carry significant clinical risks if poorly designed, and therefore merit expanded investigation using simulation-based and multi-method evaluations.

Methodological considerations and tool suitability
Our review identified a broad spectrum of usability evaluation tools and methods, with clear patterns of alignment between method choice and system type or usability objective.
For example, checklists—especially Nielsen’s principles—were prevalent across studies (40%), valued for their systematic identification of UI issues, especially during early system development. Their consistent application across subsystems such as NIS and CPOE suggests a recognized utility for benchmark-based expert evaluations in healthcare informatics.
Meanwhile, questionnaires such as SUS and QUIS were applied in another 40% of studies to capture subjective user experience, with several studies combining them with interviews or observations to deepen contextual understanding. However, findings suggest that questionnaires alone may overlook task complexity or cognitive strain, especially in high-stakes modules like CDSS or PACS.
In terms of methods, UT led the field (34%), reflecting its effectiveness in evaluating real-world interaction with live systems in clinical environments. This was particularly evident in time-sensitive subsystems like ADT and CPOE. Conversely, HE—used in 32% of studies—was most common in early-stage design, offering a cost-effective way to surface violations before deployment.
Interestingly, TA and CW were used in more cognitively complex or high-risk systems, such as EIS and CDSS. These methods provided insights into mental models, navigation strategies, and user decision-making, especially when standard metrics (like satisfaction) were insufficient.
Collectively, these findings indicate that the choice of method is not arbitrary, but rather closely tailored to system complexity, clinical stakes, and study goals. Multi-method designs were especially powerful in triangulating usability dimensions across cognitive, behavioral, and perceptual domains.

Usability criteria and research focus imbalance
The most commonly assessed criteria were efficiency and user satisfaction (each in 45% of studies), followed by error reduction (40%). These metrics are inherently aligned with hospital performance benchmarks—such as throughput, task completion, and patient safety.
However, learnability (19%) and flexibility (16%) were substantially underreported, despite being critical to long-term system adoption, especially among non-technical users or in settings with high staff turnover. This signals a research gap: systems may function well once mastered, but initial usability hurdles and rigidity to workflow variations remain poorly understood.
Future usability studies should broaden their evaluative lens to include adaptability, customization capacity, and workflow fit—all essential in modern, dynamic hospital environments.

Geographic trends and generalizability concerns
A striking finding was that 56% of studies originated from Iran or similar LMICs. This reflects both research momentum and operational focus in these settings, where institution-level HIS (rather than national EHR platforms) remain the primary digital infrastructure.
In contrast, high-income countries are increasingly centered on interoperable, longitudinal EHR systems, integrating data across hospitals, primary care, and public health. Thus, many studies in these contexts no longer focus on HIS modules in isolation, but rather evaluate cross-sectoral usability, often through population-level analytics.
This divergence introduces a limitation in generalizability. Usability findings in centered, modular HISs may not extrapolate to distributed EHR systems with different user roles, data models, and interoperability demands. Cross-context usability models are needed to bridge this gap.

Limitations
This review included only studies focused on usability of HIS components and excluded studies that addressed technical performance, data privacy, or system architecture. Due to methodological heterogeneity, a meta-analysis was not feasible. Also, publication bias may have led to underrepresentation of negative usability findings, especially from vendor-driven implementations.

Recommendations for future research
Future work should move beyond conventional usability metrics and pursue: Subsystem-specific frameworks, aligning methods and metrics to functional context. AI-enhanced usability monitoring, such as heatmaps, keystroke dynamics, or error tracking. Simulation-based evaluations, particularly in high-risk subsystems (e.g., EIS, CDSS). Longitudinal usability studies to capture changes in user satisfaction and efficiency over time. Cross-national comparative studies, mapping how usability varies by infrastructure, regulation, and digital literacy. By adopting holistic and adaptive approaches, researchers and developers can ensure that HIS solutions not only meet design standards but also fit seamlessly within diverse and evolving clinical workflows.

Conclusion
This review underscores the importance of tailored usability evaluation methods—particularly heuristic evaluation, user testing, and mixed—method approaches—in improving the performance and user experience of Hospital Information Systems. Subsystems such as NIS, CPOE, and CDSS benefit the most from these evaluations due to their clinical criticality and high user interaction. Despite progress, usability gaps remain in areas like interface design and workflow alignment. To address these challenges, future research should explore AI—enhanced evaluation techniques and scenario—based simulations to support more adaptive, efficient, and user—centered HIS.
