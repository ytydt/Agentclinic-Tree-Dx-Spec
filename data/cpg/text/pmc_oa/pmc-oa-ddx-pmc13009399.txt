Ultrasound-Guided Radiofrequency Ablation of the Ilioinguinal Nerves to Treat Ilioinguinal Neuralgia: A Description of a Novel Approach
Introduction
Ilioinguinal neuralgia is a commonly underdiagnosed and often refractory cause of chronic groin and lower abdominal pain. This technical report outlines an ultrasound-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA) technique for ilioinguinal nerve (IIN) pain, summarizing key anatomical considerations to enhance procedural accuracy.
Technique
Anatomical studies show that the IIN most commonly arises from L1 and traverses between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles within 1–2 cm of the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). Under ultrasound guidance, an 18–22-gauge radiofrequency cannula is advanced into the interfascial plane to target the IIN. Sensory stimulation is used to elicit the patient’s typical symptoms, while motor testing confirms the absence of distal muscle activation. A diagnostic block is performed before lesioning. Continuous RFA is then applied at 80 °C for 60–90 s with additional lesions placed as needed based on anatomical variation.
Discussion
Previous published data demonstrate significant pain reduction and sustained benefits following ilioinguinal RFA, with improved function and minimal complications. Ultrasound-guided probe placement and stimulation testing enhance the reproducibility, accuracy, and safety of the procedure.
Conclusions
Ultrasound-guided RFA of the IIN offers a minimally invasive treatment option for patients with refractory ilioinguinal neuralgia. Recognizing anatomical variability and utilizing stimulation testing, along with real-time imaging, can help clinicians improve the consistency of outcomes and reduce complications.

Key Summary Points
Ilioinguinal neuralgia is a chronic neuropathic pain condition affecting the lower abdomen, groin, and inner thigh that commonly arises from nerve injury during surgeries such as inguinal hernia repair or cesarean delivery. Conventional treatments, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antidepressants, and physical therapy, often fail to provide long-term relief in refractory cases.	 	The long and variable course of the ilioinguinal nerve (IIN) through multiple abdominal fascial layers makes it highly susceptible to entrapment or injury and difficult to target, thus prompting the need for a standardized, image-guided intervention to improve diagnostic and therapeutic outcomes.	 	The primary aim here is to describe and standardize a novel technique involving ultrasound-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA) for the IIN as a minimally invasive treatment for chronic ilioinguinal neuralgia refractory to conservative therapy.	 	Ultrasound-guided RFA of the IIN provides significant pain reduction, with minimal and self-limited adverse effects, and improved accuracy through real-time visualization and stimulation testing.	 	Even with an anatomically variable nerve, standardized ultrasound targeting combined with sensory and motor testing increases the consistency and safety of RFA lesioning, supporting its use as a minimally invasive interventional option after diagnostic nerve block confirmation.	 	These findings highlight the need for future controlled studies comparing continuous, pulsed, and cooled RFA to refine lesioning parameters and optimize long-term outcomes in ilioinguinal neuralgia management.

Introduction
Ilioinguinal neuralgia is a chronic neuropathic condition that causes pain in the lower abdomen, groin, genitals, and inner thigh. Although ilioinguinal neuralgia is less commonly a result of stretch or entrapment neuropathy, it is a known complication of surgical repair of inguinal hernia, with an estimated 2% of US patients developing significant neuralgia after this procedure. While inguinal hernia repairs are more common in men, ilioinguinal neuralgia is slightly more common in women, often as a result of damage to the ilioinguinal nerve during cesarean section or hysterectomy. As a result, the worldwide lifetime incidence of ilioinguinal neuralgia is reported to be as high as 18–24% While conventional treatments such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), opioids, antidepressants, lidocaine patches, physical therapy, and transcutaneous electric nerve stimulation provide pain relief to some patients, many fail to respond to these treatments and experience lasting pain symptoms. Thus, alternative, minimally invasive techniques are warranted.
The ilioinguinal nerve (IIN) is a mixed nerve that originates from the anterior rami of the T12 and L1 nerve roots and descends inferiorly through the anterior abdominal wall before becoming superficial as it exits through the external inguinal ring. Functionally, the IIN provides motor innervation to the transverse abdominis and internal oblique muscles. It conveys sensory information from the anterior surface of the scrotum and root of the penis in male patients or the labia majora and mons pubis in female patients, as well as a small region of the upper anteromedial thigh. Due to its extensive anatomical course, the IIN is particularly susceptible to entrapment or injury following lower abdominal surgical procedures, often resulting in pain, paresthesia, and sensory disturbances within its cutaneous distribution as well as muscle weakness of the upper anterior medial thigh
Ilioinguinal pain can be caused by inflammation, nerve entrapment, direct surgical injury, or thermal damage from cautery. If the nerve becomes compressed, blood flow can be compromised, leading to progressive ischemia and resulting symptoms. Ilioinguinal neuralgia can be clinically assessed by targeted palpation medial and inferior to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) or on direct pressure to the external ring on the affected side. A diagnostic anesthetic nerve block can confirm the diagnosis of ilioinguinal neuralgia if injection of a local anesthetic temporarily relieves that patient’s pain.
Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is an innovative and minimally invasive technique that utilizes thermal lesioning of nociceptive sensory nerve fibers to destroy the nerve tissue responsible for the sensation of pain. This technique has been used to treat a variety of other chronic pain disorders involving peripheral sensory nerves, including intercostal, lateral femoral cutaneous, superior cluneal, pudendal, and sural nerves. Although further research is needed to confirm its efficacy, preliminary case studies have shown promising results in the use of RFA on the IIN for ilioinguinal neuralgia.
Traditional RFA techniques have been used for decades for various chronic pain conditions, including chronic back pain, but the use of RFA for ilioinguinal neuralgia has only recently been explored. Though previous studies have investigated the use of pulsed RFA or published case series to support the use of RFA for the treatment of ilioinguinal neuralgia, to the best of our knowledge, Dr. Alaa Abd-Elsayed is the first to publish the use of both conventional and cooled RFA for the treatment of peripheral neuralgias, including ilioinguinal neuralgia. This intervention introduces a new therapeutic modality targeting the IIN, as no prior studies have specifically documented its application or outcomes for ilioinguinal neuralgia, making this the first reported technique to target this nerve using RFA directly. This novel procedure involves the percutaneous insertion of a radiofrequency cannula under ultrasound guidance, targeting the IIN. Real-time sonographic visualization allows for precise identification of the nerve and surrounding anatomical landmarks, ensuring accurate probe placement and minimizing the risk of injury to adjacent structures. After confirming the appropriate positioning and administering local anesthesia, thermal energy is applied to ablate the nerve, typically at 80 °C for 60–90 s. Accurate probe orientation and lesion size selection are critical for reproducibility and optimal outcomes. Current evidence suggests that both thermal and pulsed RFA can provide meaningful pain relief in peripheral neuropathies. Meanwhile, cooled techniques are being investigated to create larger, more precise lesions that may enhance clinical efficacy.
Although RFA has demonstrated efficacy in alleviating pain mediated by the IIN, accurate targeting of this small and anatomically variable nerve remains challenging. Variations in its course, depth, and relationship to adjacent structures necessitate precise imaging guidance to achieve optimal lesion placement and patient safety. Ultrasound has emerged as a critical modality for this purpose, offering high-resolution, real-time visualization of the ilioinguinal nerve and surrounding anatomical landmarks as well as adjacent vascular structures. The dynamic capabilities of ultrasound enable direct needle guidance, continuous monitoring of probe position, and improved procedural safety compared with landmark-based or fluoroscopic techniques. This report delineates a reproducible, ultrasound-guided technique for RFA of the IIN, emphasizing relevant sonographic anatomy, probe orientation, needle trajectory, and lesioning parameters to ensure accurate targeting and consistent clinical outcomes.

The IIN originates predominantly from the ventral ramus of the L1 spinal nerve, with occasional contributions from T12. It arises at the lateral border of the psoas major, travels anteriorly across the quadratus lumborum, and pierces the transversus abdominis muscle near the iliac crest. The IIN then proceeds between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles and closely accompanies the iliohypogastric nerve within the same fascial plane. After passing between these muscles, it exits through the superficial inguinal ring, where it lies over the spermatic cord in male patients and the round ligament in female patients before giving off terminal branches to the mons pubis, labia majora or scrotum, and the upper medial thigh (Fig. 1).
Cadaveric studies have demonstrated substantial anatomical variation in the IIN, mainly due to its origin, branching pattern, and anatomical relationship to the ASIS. The nerve originates from L1 in about 65–100% of cases, but it may also share a common trunk with the iliohypogastric nerve before diverging near the iliac crest. In most people, the nerve enters the abdominal wall above or below the iliac crest and travels within 2 cm of the ASIS, depending on anatomical variation. In approximately one-third of cadaveric anatomical specimens, the IIN perforates the external oblique aponeurosis before entering the superficial inguinal ring, which is a variation that is associated with increased risk during surgical procedures.
Histological studies show that the IIN is composed predominantly of small myelinated and unmyelinated fibers, which is consistent with its sensory and mixed motor–sensory roles. The nerve provides motor branches to the internal oblique and transversus abdominis, which aid in facilitating lower abdominal wall stability, and provides sensory branches to the skin of the groin, pubic region, and medial thigh. However, minor contributions to the rectus abdominis have been observed in a few cases, which indicates functional crossover with the iliohypogastric and genitofemoral nerves.
Because the IIN runs superficially and extends across the lower abdomen, it is vulnerable to entrapment or injury during procedures such as inguinal hernia repair, appendectomy, cesarean delivery, and other abdominal surgeries. Entrapment of the nerve most commonly occurs 1–2 cm medial and inferior to the ASIS, specifically at a point where the nerve transitions from the muscular layers into the subcutaneous tissue; this change increases its susceptibility to compression or tethering. The resulting ilioinguinal neuralgia often produces a radiating neuropathic pain involving the groin, genital region, and proximal thigh.
Ultrasound imaging and magnetic resonance (MR) neurography provide valuable tools for identifying the fascial plane of the IIN and adjacent structures. On ultrasound, the nerve appears as a hypoechoic oval fascicle between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis, near the deep circumflex iliac artery. Color Doppler improves vessel distinction, and the ASIS serves as a consistent sonographic reference point. MR neurography provides detailed imaging of the IIN from its origin near the psoas to its terminal branches; this can aid in diagnosing and guiding interventions for chronic groin pain syndromes.
Anatomical variations, such as duplication, absence of a distinct trunk, or a shared origin with the iliohypogastric nerve, are observed in 25–35% of cadaveric specimens. These variations underscore the importance of precise localization before performing nerve-targeted interventions. Hence, a clear understanding of these variations is essential to minimize procedural complications and optimize outcomes during ultrasound-guided RFA.

Procedure

After proper patient identification, obtaining written informed consent, and initiation of standard cardiopulmonary monitoring, the patient is placed in the supine position on the procedure table, allowing the physician to easily access and visualize the ilioinguinal nerve. The lower abdomen, iliac crest, and groin area on the affected side are prepared with three rounds of 2% chlorhexidine solution in 70% isopropyl alcohol and sterile draping. The skin and subcutaneous tissues 2 cm medial and inferior to the ASIS are anesthetized with 1–2 mL of 1% lidocaine using a 25-gauge needle to numb the skin before needle insertion. A high-frequency (7–11 MHz) linear array ultrasound transducer is sheathed in sterile plastic and aligned in an oblique plane over the bony prominence of the patient's ASIS to the umbilicus (Fig. 2A). The probe is then slowly swept superiorly and inferiorly over the ilioinguinal canal while keeping the bony iliac crest visible in the lateral part of the image until the IIN is visualized in the fascial plane between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles, often appearing as part of a bundle with the iliohypogastric nerve and the deep circumflex iliac artery (Fig. 2B). The nerve is typically visualized as a small hypoechoic oval structure in short-axis transverse ultrasound views and as a hypoechoic linear structure when viewed in the long-axis longitudinal plane. Under ultrasound guidance, a disposable radiofrequency cannula, 18–22-gauge, 100–150 mm in length, with a 5 or 10 mm active tip, is then advanced into the fascial space between the internal oblique and transversus abdominus muscles, where the IIN runs. The cannula is advanced using an in-plane ultrasound approach, which allows continuous visualization of the needle tip as it enters the fascial plane. A brief long-axis view may be used when needed to confirm cannula alignment before returning to the short-axis view for lesioning.
To ensure accurate placement of the needle within the correct pain-signaling nerves while avoiding damage to motor nerves, sensory stimulation at 50 Hz is performed to reproduce the sensation corresponding to the patient’s primary pain complaint. Suppose the sensory testing is negative for reproduction of paresthesia. In that case, the cannula tip is repositioned, and sensory testing is repeated until the patient experiences reproduction of pain or sensation in the area of symptoms. After positive sensory testing, motor stimulation is performed at 2 Hz up to 2 V and is considered appropriate if there is no motor response distal to the needle insertion site. A diagnostic nerve block with 2% lidocaine or 0.25% bupivacaine with or without a steroid is typically administered before radiofrequency lesioning to ensure short-term pain relief.
RFA is then performed using continuous radiofrequency (lesion) mode. For consistency, continuous RFA is performed at 80 °C for 60–90 s, which reflects established RFA technique recommendations and aligns with published procedural parameters. This conventional technique delivers sustained energy at 80 °C for 60–90 s to create a thermal lesion. If the patient received complete pain relief for the initial round of RFA, the needle is removed, and a sterile dressing is applied to the insertion site. However, if pain persists, the needle is repositioned a few millimeters medially or inferiorly from the initial target to identify the iliohypogastric and genitofemoral nerves, as anatomical variation and overlap of these nerves can contribute to the sensation of pain. After another sensory and motor testing round, RFA lesioning is performed again. Needles are removed after the procedure, and the skin is cleaned and prepared with a sterile dressing. Patients remain in the recovery room for 15 min after the procedure, during which a nurse monitors their vitals and checks for any adverse events, including temporary pain, swelling, numbness, bleeding, or neuritis at the needle insertion site, as well as signs of possible infection and fever. Patients are educated on returning if symptoms worsen or if signs of infection develop, scheduled for a follow-up appointment approximately 6–8 weeks after the procedure, and discharged on the same day as the procedure.
Dr. Alaa Abd-Elsayed has expanded upon the use of RFA by investigating the application of cooled RFA techniques. Although the procedural framework parallels that of conventional thermal RFA, the cooled method employs 17-gauge, 100-mm radiofrequency probes with 4-mm active tips to deliver lesions at 80 °C for approximately 2 min and 30 s, demonstrating consistent and reproducible efficacy. Considering the anatomical variability of the ilioinguinal nerve, needle placement should be guided by the patient’s reported area of pain, with sensory testing performed before ablation to confirm accurate targeting.
Alternatively, other providers have explored the use of pulsed radiofrequency (RF) as a therapeutic modality for ilioinguinal neuralgia. This technique utilizes a 27-gauge needle to deliver short, intermittent bursts of electrical energy, allowing the nerve to cool between pulses. For this procedure, the IIN is treated with 20 ms radiofrequency pulses at a frequency of 2 Hz for 120 s at a maximum temperature of 42 °C. Additionally, other providers have adapted this procedure using fluoroscopic guidance instead of ultrasound techniques for proper needle placement. Although the IIN is more easily identified on ultrasound, it can be visualized under fluoroscopic guidance by first locating the ASIS and iliac crest and then placing the needle 0.5–1.0 cm medial to the ASIS. The needle is directed slightly inferomedially toward the expected course of the nerve, advanced until it contacts bone (the ilium), then retracted 1-2 mm to reach the fascial plane. While fluoroscopy can be used as an alternative to ultrasound, it is instead often used in conjunction with ultrasound to confirm needle placement before RFA. However, there is a lack of evidence in the literature supporting the use of fluoroscopy to identify the IIN.

Key Technical Tips
Important technical considerations include maintaining continuous visualization of the cannula tip by using an in-plane ultrasound approach whenever possible. Identifying the deep circumflex iliac artery with color Doppler early in scanning helps to confirm the correct fascial layer containing the ilioinguinal nerve. Sensory stimulation should be performed before final cannula advancement, since adjusting the needle by only a few millimeters can significantly improve targeting when there is anatomical variability. When the nerve is difficult to visualize, sweeping cranially and caudally from the ASIS improves the likelihood of locating the correct fascial plane. Briefly switching to a long-axis view may also help to confirm the needle path before returning to the short-axis view for lesioning.

Common Pitfalls
Several common challenges may affect procedural success. The iliohypogastric nerve may be mistaken for the ilioinguinal nerve because they frequently travel together in the same fascial plane; therefore, sensory stimulation is essential to ensure accurate targeting. Omitting motor testing increases the risk of inadvertently affecting nearby motor branches, including the genitofemoral nerve. Lesioning too superficially can result in incomplete ablation, especially in patients with increased soft tissue depth. Scanning too narrow an area near the ASIS may miss variant nerve branches, so a broader cranial-caudal sweep is recommended. Additionally, relying solely on fluoroscopy introduces limitations because the nerve itself cannot be visualized, which increases the chance of suboptimal lesion placement.
These recommendations, which consist of a standardized sequence of sterile preparation, ultrasound-guided needle placement, sensory and motor testing, diagnostic block, and continuous RFA in lesion mode, consistently yield significant and sustained pain relief in patients with ilioinguinal neuralgia.

Discussion
Ilioinguinal neuralgia is a chronic neuropathic condition characterized by burning or stabbing pain localized to the lower abdomen, inguinal crease, and upper medial thigh that often radiates to the genital region. It most commonly develops due to nerve entrapment, direct surgical injury, or postoperative fibrosis following inguinal hernia repair, cesarean delivery, appendectomy, and other pelvic procedures. Because it runs superficially through multiple fascial layers, the IIN is prone to traction and compression injuries. The resulting neuralgia is a significant cause of postoperative groin pain, with iatrogenic injury reported in approximately 2% of hernia repairs and 20–30% of chronic groin pain cases.
Conservative management, such as NSAIDs, opioids, neuropathic agents, and physiotherapy, is the initial line of therapy for ilioinguinal neuralgia. Still, they often provide insufficient relief in refractory and postsurgical neuropathy cases. Consequently, image-guided interventional therapies have gained importance for diagnostic testing and long-term pain control. RFA is increasingly being recognized as a reliable option due to its ability to selectively denervate afferent nociceptive fibers while preserving nearby motor function. Similarly, RFA techniques have also been established for other peripheral neuralgias, such as superior cluneal, intercostal, and pudendal nerve disorders, which have shown persistent clinical benefits with minimal complications.
Ultrasound guidance provides significant advantages for procedural accuracy. Because the IIN is small and follows a variable pathway between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles, accurate image-guided localization is essential for effective lesioning. High-frequency ultrasound enables real-time visualization of needle placement and minimizes the risk of injury to the iliohypogastric or genitofemoral nerves. Cadaveric and imaging studies demonstrate that the distinct fascial planes surrounding the IIN enable consistent identification within the transabdominal plane, thereby establishing the reliability of ultrasound as the primary modality for RFA needle placement.
Histopathological data from resected IIN segments in herniorrhaphy patients show significant fascicular narrowing and collagen infiltration, which is consistent with chronic compression neuropathy and microvascular ischemia. These findings support the concept that RFA induces thermocoagulation of pathological nociceptive fibers to interrupt pain transmission at the site of lesion formation while preserving myelin fibers in unaffected regions. Continuous RFA applied at 80 °C for 60–90 s generates reproducible lesion morphology and reliable clinical results when accurately guided.
Clinical studies of RFA for ilioinguinal neuralgia have demonstrated pain reduction of 60–80%, with relief maintained for 6–12 months; these results are comparable to those reported for lateral femoral cutaneous and superior cluneal nerve interventions. Compared to surgical neurectomy, RFA provides safe repeatability along with lower complication rates and shorter recovery periods. This supports its use as a first-line procedural intervention after a successful diagnostic nerve block. Reported adverse effects, such as numbness, neuritis, and bruising, are typically self-limiting and resolve within a few days.
Despite these promising results, anatomical variability continues to influence procedural success significantly. Variability in the nerve’s branching or intersection with the iliohypogastric nerve frequently requires multiple lesion attempts to achieve complete analgesia. Furthermore, despite current studies supporting both thermal and pulsed RFA, standardized recommendations for lesion parameters, probe orientation, and appropriate follow-up intervals are not yet established. Therefore, future studies should focus on prospective, randomized comparisons of pulsed versus continuous RFA, as well as expanded follow-ups beyond 1 year, to confirm long-term safety and efficacy.

Limitations of the Technique
This technique has several limitations. anatomical variability of the ilioinguinal nerve such as branching differences and shared trunks with nearby nerves may require needle repositioning or multiple lesions despite careful ultrasound guidance. Ultrasound visualization may be reduced in patients with obesity or significant postsurgical scarring, which can make localization more challenging. Additionally, standardized lesioning parameters for continuous, pulsed, and cooled RFA have not been established in the existing literature, which limits direct comparison between modalities. As a result, long-term outcome data are limited since most studies report pain relief that lasts 6–12 months, and durability beyond 1 year remains unclear. Finally, fluoroscopic guidance offers only indirect localization of the nerve, which may reduce the consistency of procedural outcomes compared with ultrasound.

Conclusions
In summary, ultrasound guided RFA of the IIN provides a reproducible, minimally invasive approach for patients with chronic ilioinguinal neuralgia that is refractory to standard therapies. By combining high resolution anatomical mapping, electrophysiological confirmation, and controlled radiofrequency lesioning, this technique achieves targeted nerve ablation with minimal morbidity. This technical report supports the incorporation of RFA into multimodal pain management frameworks, while also reinforcing the need for controlled trials better to understand optimal lesioning parameters and patient selection criteria.
