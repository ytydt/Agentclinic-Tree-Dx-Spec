The electric brain: approach to suboptimal DBS parameters
Highlights
Programming errors underlie 40 % of post-DBS patient dissatisfaction cases.
Chronic stimulation correction requires tolerance-optimized protocols.
Suboptimal leads: directional steering enables gradual VTA correction.
High-TEED cases: 10–15 % weekly energy reduction prevents rebound.
Misplaced leads: Structured deactivation precedes revision decisions.
Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) has revolutionized Parkinson’s disease treatment, yet post-operative challenges persist, including reversible programming errors caused by insufficient clinician training. Approximately 40 % of suboptimal outcomes referred to tertiary centers stem from correctable programming errors, highlighting the need for standardized approaches. This article addresses chronic overstimulation syndrome in Subthalamic DBS(STN-DBS), where patients with sustained high-energy stimulation leads poorly tolerated rebound effects when abruptly adjusted. We present a structured, image-guided algorithm combining gradual energy titration with advanced volume of tissue activated (VTA) modeling to optimize therapeutic windows. Three characteristic scenarios are detailed: (1) suboptimal lead placement, managed via directional steering to refine stimulation focus; (2) excessive stimulation energy, addressed through decremental total electrical energy delivered (TEED) reduction; and (3) misplaced leads, requiring systematic deactivation and candidacy assessment for surgical revision. Our tolerance-optimized framework emphasizes spatial precision (leveraging imaging reconstruction) and temporal adaptation (gradual parameter adjustments), offering a paradigm shift for managing chronic DBS complications. While focused on STN-DBS, these principles may extend to other targets facing analogous challenges. The integration of advanced imaging with clinician expertise underscores the dual importance of technology and specialized training in improving DBS outcomes.

Introduction
Since FDA approval in the early 2000s, DBS has been a transformative therapy for Parkinson’s disease (PD). While technological advances have increased accessibility, evidenced by a nine-fold rise in annual surgeries at major centers, this expansion has revealed significant challenges in post-operative management.
These challenges are particularly pronounced in regions with limited access to specialized care, where insufficient training opportunities contribute to suboptimal outcomes and patient dissatisfaction. Approximately 40 % of unsatisfactory results referred to tertiary centers stem from correctable programming errors, highlighting a critical need for standardized management approaches.
This article specifically addresses chronic overstimulation syndrome in STN-DBS, where conventional programming strategies often prove ineffective due to neuroadaptive changes from sustained high-energy stimulation. Unlike acute stimulation-induced side effects that respond readily to parameter adjustments, these complex cases demonstrate a paradoxical dependence on suboptimal settings due to maladaptive neuroplastic changes.
When clinicians attempt to abruptly correct parameters in these cases, patients frequently experience severe withdrawal phenomena that mimic disease progression, often leading to the erroneous conclusion of DBS failure. This creates a self-perpetuating cycle where harmful stimulation parameters are maintained due to the patient's inability to tolerate correction attempts, resulting in progressive functional decline.
To address this critical gap in DBS management, we present a structured, tolerance-optimized approach developed through the systematic evaluation of three characteristic clinical scenarios. Our protocol recognizes that successful reprogramming of chronically overstimulated patients requires both spatial precision in target engagement and temporal sensitivity to neuroadaptive processes. The framework provides clinicians with specific decision pathways for suboptimally placed leads, excessively high stimulation parameters, and frankly misplaced electrodes.
This work challenges the prevailing notion that chronic DBS complications represent irreversible treatment failures. By introducing novel concepts of stimulation withdrawal phenomena and neuroadaptive dependence, we provide both a physiological framework for understanding these complex cases and practical algorithms for their successful management. Our approach represents a paradigm shift in DBS programming, emphasizing the need for gradual, tolerance-guided parameter adjustments in chronically stimulated patients. While this article is focused on STN-DBS, the fundamental may extend to other targets experiencing similar chronic stimulation challenges.

The anatomic problem: Subthalamic nucleus neighborhood
The STN is a densely populated structure functionally divided into associative, limbic, and sensorimotor subregions. The dorsolateral sensorimotor STN represents the optimal stimulation target, or ¨sweet spot¨, due its motor-related projections. In contrast, stimulation of medial associative and limbic territories frequently induces cognitive and behavioral adverse effects, while the nucleus’s proximity to major white matter tracts creates additional therapeutic challenges.Three critical surrounding structures significantly influence stimulation outcomes: pallidofugal fibers, medial lemniscus and internal capsule.
Pallidofugal fibers, comprising ansa lenticularis and lenticular fasciculus, connect thalamus to globus pallidus internus. Their activation mediates anti-dyskinetic effects but may worsen gait when overstimulated. The medial lemniscus, situated ventroposteriorly, provides useful feedback when mild paresthesia occur during programming, though persistent symptoms or cerebellar fiber activation often indicates problematic current spread leading to ataxia.
Most notably, the internal capsule’s anterior and lateral position relative to STN produces distinct stimulation effects depending on duration. Acute activation induces focal muscle contractions, such as facial or thumb twitching, while chronic overstimulation, leads to gait spasticity and impaired balance. Theses chronic manifestations often present as diagnostic challenges, as they may be misinterpreted as disease progression by clinicians unfamiliar with DBS-related complications.

STN therapeutic window
The STN is a complex anatomical challenge with its sensorimotor sweet spot representing only approximately 24 mm3 (33 % of total nucleus volume) surrounded by structures whose stimulation may lead to disabling side effects, necessitating precise identification of optimal stimulation parameters. Two complementary methods to target identification and parameter optimization commonly used post-surgery are monopolar review and advanced image reconstruction.
Monopolar review, considered the gold standard for selecting the optimal contact, involves exploratory testing of each contact by gradually increasing the current amplitude while maintaining fixed frequency and pulse width. The therapeutic window is defined as the range between the minimum energy producing beneficial effects (lower limit) and the threshold triggering side effects (upper limit). Optimal contacts are those with the largest therapeutic window that also achieve beneficial effects with the lowest energy. Usually clinical improvement is associated with a stimulation threshold of 2.2 ± 0.7 mA and a side-effect threshold of 4.68 ± 0.98 mA (60 ms; 130 Hz).
Modern image-guided reconstruction techniques provide complementary anatomical precision through computational integration of preoperative magnetic resonance imaging with postoperative computed tomography. These methods enable both precise contact localization within STN subregions and predictive modeling of the VTA through biophysical simulations. The VTA models incorporate both the physical parameters of current spread (governed by electrode geometry and tissue conductivity characteristics) and the neurophysiological parameters of neuronal activation thresholds. While these computational predictions demonstrate minor spatial variance compared to actual neural activation patterns, they have proven clinically valuable for both initial target identification and side effect prediction.
Recent comparative clinical studies have demonstrated that image-guided programming approaches achieve equivalent therapeutic outcomes to traditional monopolar review while significantly reducing programming time requirements. This convergence of anatomical precision and functional assessment now represents the prevailing paradigm for STN-DBS parameter optimization, particularly in cases requiring careful balancing of therapeutic benefit against potential stimulation-induced complications.

The challenge: Wrong chronic stimulation
Stimulation-induced adverse effects in STN-DBS manifest temporally as either acute (≤48 h post-adjustment) or chronic (developing over months) phenomena. While acute side effects permit straightforward parameter correlation, chronic manifestations often mimic disease progression or DBS failure, delaying recognition, especially among less experienced clinicians.
The management of chronic overstimulation presents a unique therapeutic paradox still not discussed in the literature. While these patients unequivocally require parameter correction, they exhibit marked intolerance to abrupt adjustments. Attempts at rapid energy reduction or VTA reshaping frequently precipitate severe symptom exacerbation, including generalized distress and worsened parkinsonism that forces reversion to previous settings. This clinical phenomenon reflects fundamental neuroplastic adaptations to sustained supratherapeutic stimulation, including both local tissue changes and reorganization of broader network dynamics.
At the pathophysiological level, chronic overstimulation establishes new neural equilibria through three key mechanisms: (1) modulation of perielectrode impedance properties, (2) remodeling of local and downstream neural circuits, and (3) development of dependent relationships between symptom control and pathological network engagement. When standard acute programming approaches are applied to this adapted system, the simultaneous deactivation of both therapeutic and maladaptive pathways produces profound rebound phenomena.
To address these clinical complexities, we developed a systematic approach for chronic overstimulation management. Our model accounts for the temporal dynamics of neural adaptation through gradual parameter titration, contrasting with conventional acute management strategies. The framework addresses three characteristic clinical scenarios depicting patients that had new-onset gait complaints after STN-DBS surgery.

The first scenario: Suboptimal lead Location

Chronic stimulation through suboptimally positioned electrodes produces a distinct clinical syndrome characterized by delayed-onset neurological complications. As stimulation amplitudes are progressively increased to compensate for lead misplacement, current spread to adjacent structures initiates a gradual deterioration of motor function that typically becomes clinically apparent several months after initial programming.

The underlying pathophysiology involves two interdependent mechanisms. First, the dorsolateral subthalamic nucleus develops a dependence on elevated stimulation intensities due to local neuronal reorganization. Second, unintended activation of neighboring structures − particularly corticospinal fibers in cases of lateral lead displacement − becomes functionally integrated into the therapeutic response. This neuroadaptive process creates a paradoxical clinical scenario where patients simultaneously experience partial therapeutic benefit and progressive stimulation-induced side effects, most commonly manifested as worsening gait and speech disturbances.Conventional abrupt parameter adjustments prove ineffective in these cases, as rapid withdrawal of stimulation from either the intended target or inadvertently activated pathways precipitates clinical deterioration. Our protocol addresses this challenge by first performing directional current steering towards dorsolateral STN of ≤ 25 % weekly (or 12.5 % for sensitive patients) without altering stimulation amplitude. This gradual correction allows progressive network reconfiguration while maintaining therapeutic stability, typically requiring 4–6 weeks to achieve optimal VTA repositioning. Final TEED optimization then further refines stimulation parameters within the target zone. (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).
This management paradigm fundamentally differs from acute side effect strategies by explicitly accounting for both the spatial distribution of stimulation effects and the temporal dynamics of neural adaptation safely restoring optimal therapeutic outcomes.

The second scenario: Very high chronic stimulation
“First months after surgery tremor disappeared, but within a year his gait worsened, he’s been frequently falling and his speech worsened as well”
Chronic supratherapeutic stimulation in STN-DBS manifests clinically through a progressive symptoms of gait deterioration, behavioral changes, and dysarthria, emerging when TEED exceeds neurophysiological tolerance thresholds. This condition may arise from multiple active contacts, excessive current, elevated frequency, increased pulse width, or combinations of these factors. TEED, calculated as (Voltage2 × Pulse Width × Frequency)/Impedance, becomes clinically significant when exceeding the equivalent of 4.31 mA at standard parameters (60 μs pulse width, 130 Hz frequency) in STN monopolar stimulation.
Therapeutic correction requires a systematic, tolerance-optimized approach that begins with prioritized reduction of the parameter most responsible for excessive energy, maintaining existing contacts configurations to preserve network stability. If current amplitude exceeds 3.5 mA, it is addressed first due to its quadratic influence on the volume of tissue activated. Otherwise, the most elevated pulse width or frequency is targeted. The selected parameter is then reduced in weekly decrements of 10–15 % of TEED, with ultra-gradual adjustments (e.g., 0.1 mA daily) for particularly sensitive cases. Following successful TEED normalization, typically achieving approximately 30 % total reduction, stimulation focus is refined through image-guided contact reselection and directional current steering when available. (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).
This dual-phase approach addresses both the quantitative excess of energy delivery and qualitative refinement of stimulation targeting, ultimately establishing optimal therapeutic parameters while avoiding destabilizing withdrawals.

The third scenario: Misplaced lead
“I did not notice any improvement in my symptoms after surgery, instead my gait worsened and I constantly freeze”
The acute onset of stimulation-induced dysarthria, gait impairment, or behavioral changes during initial programming, coupled with inadequate parkinsonian symptom control, strongly suggests lead misplacement. This clinical presentation typically reflects intraoperative lead deviation, confirmed electrophysiologically through monopolar review demonstrating low-threshold adverse effects without therapeutic benefit − a pattern indicative of non-target structure stimulation.
For patients presenting with chronic stimulation outside the STN proper, comprehensive evaluation begins with high-resolution image reconstruction to precisely quantify lead position relative to the dorsolateral STN target. Surgical revision becomes clinically indicated when quantitative analysis reveals > 2 mm deviation from the planned trajectory. However, even in confirmed cases of significant misplacement, immediate cessation of stimulation proves clinically untenable due to established neuroadaptive dependence. Our protocol therefore mandates gradual deactivation, implementing weekly stimulation reductions of 10–15 % TEED similarly as high chronic stimulation scenario, until complete energy cessation, thereby minimizing withdrawal phenomena while preparing for potential revision.
Determination of surgical candidacy requires rigorous assessment through standardized levodopa challenge testing. Repositioning is justified when patients exhibit both ≥ 33 % improvement in UPDRS Part III scores and persistent eligibility per CAPSIT-PD selection criteria. This dual requirement ensures revision is pursued only when objectively justified by both the technical need for repositioning and preserved responsiveness to dopaminergic therapy, thereby optimizing patient safety and clinical outcomes (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).
The protocol emphasizes that management of misplaced leads represents a critical exception to conventional DBS programming principles, requiring specialized consideration of both the technical aspects of lead position and the neurophysiological consequences of chronic stimulation withdrawal. This approach provides a structured framework for navigating the complex decision-making process surrounding lead revision in chronically stimulated patients (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Discussion
Our experience at a DBS reference center reveals that chronic stimulation mismanagement represents a critical yet underrecognized challenge in movement disorders therapeutics. The growing prevalence of these cases—where patients develop neuroadaptive dependence on suboptimal or frankly harmful stimulation parameters—demands a fundamental rethinking of conventional programming paradigms. While often mischaracterized as simple “programming errors,” these scenarios in fact represent a distinct clinical syndrome requiring specialized management approaches fundamentally different from acute side effect correction.
The three clinical scenarios presented—suboptimal lead placement, excessive stimulation energy, and frankly misplaced leads—collectively demonstrate how chronic overstimulation creates unique therapeutic challenges through maladaptive neural reorganization. In each case, standard reprogramming attempts fail because they disregard the patient's physiological dependence on established stimulation patterns, regardless of their therapeutic appropriateness.
Several key insights emerge from our protocol development. First, the temporal dimension of parameter adjustment proves equally critical as spatial targeting—gradual weekly reductions of 10–25 % (depending on clinical scenario) prevent the destabilizing withdrawals that occur with abrupt changes. Second, directional steering capabilities, when available, provide essential flexibility for safely correcting suboptimal lead placement without immediate hardware revision. Third, even clearly misplaced leads require structured deactivation protocols before surgical intervention, as immediate cessation of chronic stimulation consistently produces severe clinical deterioration.
These findings carry important implications for the field. They challenge the binary classification of DBS outcomes as either “successful” or “failed,” instead identifying chronic stimulation syndrome as a third distinct category requiring specific intervention strategies. Our tolerance-optimized approach demonstrates that most patients abandoned as “non-responders” can in fact achieve meaningful improvement through systematic, image-guided reprogramming respecting neuroadaptive timelines.
The protocol's success hinges on two equally critical components: (1) advanced technical tools (directional leads, high-resolution imaging, VTA modeling), and (2) clinician expertise in recognizing and managing neuroadaptive phenomena. This dual requirement underscores the importance of specialized training programs to address the growing gap between DBS technological complexity and real-world programming expertise. Future developments should focus not only on hardware improvements but also on decision-support systems that help clinicians navigate these challenging cases.
An additional perspective relates to adaptive DBS (aDBS). By continuously monitoring neural signals and dynamically adjusting stimulation delivery, aDBS has the potential to minimize one of the main issues described in this work: chronic overstimulation. Unlike conventional systems, which deliver fixed parameters regardless of clinical state, aDBS can identify periods of adequate control and reduce stimulation accordingly, thereby preventing the cumulative maladaptive changes that emerge from sustained excessive energy delivery. This technology may ultimately represent an important safeguard against the type of neuroadaptive dependence outlined here.
Ultimately, this work provides both a conceptual framework and practical algorithms for rescuing patients from chronic stimulation mismanagement—a population that has historically fallen through the cracks of conventional DBS care paradigms.

Limitations
We acknowledge several important limitations. This is a single-center experience without direct head-to-head comparison with conventional programming strategies, and our outcomes are presented descriptively rather than with systematic percentage-based improvement data. The small sample size and lack of longitudinal neurophysiological markers also limit the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, these results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, requiring rigorous validation in larger, multicenter cohorts with standardized outcome metrics. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to address this specific clinical challenge, presenting to the international community a practical solution for a problem that, until now, has not been discussed in the literature.
