Radiographic evaluation of key anatomical markers in discoid lateral meniscus: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Purpose
The lateral discoid meniscus (DLM), a common anatomical variant in Asian populations, is often linked to knee pain, restricted motion, and an increased risk of meniscal tears. Due to limitations of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in certain patients, this study aims to assess the efficacy of X-ray parameters in diagnosing DLM, focusing on key features: lateral tibial spine height (LTSH), lateral joint space distance (LJSD), fibular head height (FHH), lateral tibial plateau obliquity (LTPO), and the condylar cut-off sign.
Methods
A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted using studies from PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science (January 2004 - October 2024). Studies with observational designs, X-ray measurements, and data on DLM were included. Mean difference (MD) and 95% CI analyses were performed using Review Manager and Stata.
Results
Nine studies involving 788 discoid menisci and 761 normal knees were included. LJSD and FHH were significantly larger in the DLM group (MD = 1.18 and − 3.03, P < 0.01), while LTPO was smaller (MD = -2.18, P < 0.01). LTSH did not show significant differences in children but was smaller in adults with DLM (MD = -0.65, P < 0.01). The condylar cut-off sign also showed significant correlation in both adults and children (MD = -0.10, P < 0.01).
Conclusion
This meta-analysis identifies LJSD, FHH, LTPO, and the condylar cut-off sign as key radiographic markers for diagnosing DLM, with accuracy enhanced when combined, particularly in the absence of MRI. LTSH appears diagnostically relevant mainly in adults, with limited value in pediatric populations.
Level of evidence
IV

Introduction
The lateral discoid meniscus (DLM) is a common anatomical variant of the meniscus, characterized by an abnormal increase in the width of the lateral meniscus. The DLM predominantly occurs in Asian populations, with a prevalence rate of approximately 10.9%−16.6%. Due to its larger surface area and thicker structure, patients with DLM often experience knee pain, locking, clicking, and limited range of motion. Moreover, the disruption of the annular collagen fiber system in the DLM makes it more susceptible to tearing, with the tear rate being about twice that of a normal meniscus. Chronic DLM tears can lead to the progression of osteoarthritis in the knee joint, affecting its function and significantly impacting the patient’s quality of life. Therefore, a simple and rapid diagnosis of DLM can help prevent meniscal injuries and reduce the occurrence of knee osteoarthritis.
Currently, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the primary diagnostic method for DLM. However, the lengthy examination time, high cost, and low availability of MRI limit its widespread use for large-scale DLM screening. MRI is also challenging for patients with magnetic metal implants, pacemakers, or claustrophobia. The high patient compliance required for MRI, combined with motion artifacts caused by limb movement during the scan, can severely affect the image quality and ultimately influence the diagnostic outcome. Hence, exploring a faster and more convenient diagnostic method is essential. Knee X-rays, with their simplicity, speed, low cost, and wide availability, present a feasible alternative. Structural abnormalities in the knee bones of DLM patients, particularly the femoral condyle and tibial plateau, make X-ray diagnosis and morphological classification of DLM possible by comparing parameters such as fibular head height (FHH), lateral joint space, tibial plateau slope, and the condylar cut-off sign. However, as a low-cost and practical method, routine X-rays may not always produce accurate and standardized measurements due to variations in patient positioning, compliance, and the experience of radiology technicians. This inconsistency has led to varied results across studies for the same diagnostic parameters.
Therefore, this study aims to explore the feasibility and effectiveness of these parameters in diagnosing DLM through a meta-analysis comparing the radiographic features of knee joints in different populations. Specifically, the study focuses on key knee structural parameters such as lateral tibial spine height (LTSH), lateral joint space distance (LJSD), FHH, lateral tibial plateau obliquity (LTPO), and the condylar cut-off sign. The variations in these parameters may be closely related to the presence of DLM. Therefore, by systematically reviewing and analyzing relevant literature, it can help identify the typical radiographic features of DLM. Additionally, the study will include age as a factor for subgroup analysis to better understand how DLM presents in different populations and provide more targeted diagnostic criteria for clinical practice. This study can promote the broader and more effective application of X-ray screening techniques in clinical practice, thereby reducing the incidence of knee joint damage and osteoarthritis caused by DLM.

Methods

Search strategy
This study follows the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement for conducting meta-analyses. A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science was conducted from January 1, 2004, to October 15, 2024, without language restrictions, to identify studies investigating the relationship between the DLM and X-ray imaging. The following search strategies were adopted: (lateral discoid meniscus[Title/Abstract] OR discoid meniscus[Title/Abstract] OR discoid lateral meniscus[Title/Abstract]) AND (radiography[Title/Abstract] OR X-ray[Title/Abstract] OR radiographs[Title/Abstract]) for PubMed; TS=(“lateral discoid meniscus” OR “discoid meniscus” OR “discoid lateral meniscus”) AND TS=(“radiography” OR “X-ray” OR “radiographs”) for web of science and (‘lateral discoid meniscus’:ab, ti OR ‘discoid meniscus’:ab, ti OR ‘discoid lateral meniscus’:ab, ti) AND (‘radiography’:ab, ti OR ‘x-ray’:ab, ti OR ‘radiographs’:ab, ti) for Embase.

Protocol and registration
Study has been registered with the PROSPERO systematic review platform, registration ID: CRD42024590880.

Data extraction
The two authors (X.J. and X.W.) independently and in duplicate extracted the relevant data from each included study using a standardized data extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel (Office 2019). The extracted information included the first author’s name, publication year, mean patient age, sample size for both experimental and control groups, and study design. In this review, the presence of a DLM was considered a positive outcome. Data on the radiographic measurements of anatomical parameters, including the height of the LTSH, LJSD, FHH, LTPO, and the condylar cut-off sign, were extracted. Discrepancies between reviewers were initially resolved through discussion and consensus. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (H.W.) independently assessed the article, reviewed the rationale provided by both reviewers, and made the final decision.

Eligibility criteria
Studies meeting the following criteria were included: (1) observational design, including case-control studies, prospective controlled studies, and relevant case series; (2) measurement of knee anatomy using X-rays; (3) the outcome of interest was the DLM; (4) reported means and standard deviations for injured and control groups (or calculable data).

Risk of bias assessment
The methodological quality of included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). This tool assesses three domains: Selection (maximum 4 points); Comparability (maximum 2 points); Exposure (maximum 3 points).
Two reviewers (X.J. and X.W.) independently assessed each study. Scores ranged from 0 to 9. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer (H.W.). Studies scoring ≥ 7 were considered high quality, 5–6 as moderate quality, and < 5 as low quality.

Statistical analyses
All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan software (version 5.2) and Stata software (version 11.0). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, unless otherwise stated. The mean difference (MD), a measure used to compare the average values between groups, was calculated for the anatomical parameters reported in all included studies, along with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). If standard deviations were not reported, they were estimated based on sample size, standard error, or 95% CI. Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated using the Q statistic, with P < 0.05 considered indicative of heterogeneity. The I² statistic was also used to assess heterogeneity. MD were estimated using a fixed-effects model when statistical heterogeneity was considered low (I² < 50%), under the assumption of a common effect across studies. In contrast, when heterogeneity was substantial (I² ≥ 50%), a random-effects model was adopted to incorporate between-study variation and provide more conservative estimates. Publication bias was assessed using Galbraith plots, where studies far from the regression line may indicate bias. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the robustness of the results. If conclusions remained unchanged after excluding individual studies, the overall results were considered reliable.

Results

Study selection
A total of 164 articles were initially identified from the PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases, and nine studies were ultimately included in the final meta-analysis. The initial database search identified 164 records (PubMed: 40, Web of Science: 76, and Embase: 48). After removing eight duplicates, 156 articles were screened. Title and abstract screening excluded 74 articles. The remaining 82 articles underwent further abstract review, during which 64 were excluded for the following reasons: irrelevant topics (n = 59), review article (n = 1), annual meeting abstract (n = 1), and case studies (n = 3), leaving 18 articles for full-text evaluation. 18 studies were selected for full-text evaluation, and eight were subsequently excluded. In cases of overlapping populations (e.g., Ha et al. 2017 and 2020), only the most recent and comprehensive dataset was retained. Finally, nine studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. The detailed selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Risk of bias assessment
All included studies underwent quality assessment using the NOS. NOS scores ranged from 5 to 8 (Table 1). Six studies were rated as high quality (score ≥ 7), and three studies were rated as moderate quality (score = 5–6). No study was deemed to have poor quality (score < 5). Selection domains were generally well satisfied. However, variability existed in comparability due to inconsistent adjustment for potential confounders.

Study characteristics
All included studies were observational in nature and utilized X-rays to evaluate knee anatomy. Study populations comprised patients with DLM and matched control groups with normal meniscal morphology. Four studies included pediatric or adolescent participants with open growth plates, while five studies involved adult patients with closed growth plates. The studies involved X-ray measurements from 788 discoid menisci and 761 normal knees. As all studies were cross-sectional, follow-up duration was not applicable. LTSH
Seven studies met the criteria for assessing the relationship between LTSH and the risk of DLM injury, involving a total of 1,253 knee joints. Three studies focused on children, and four on adults. The overall results did not show a significant difference between the experimental group and the control group (MD = −0.33 [95% CI: −0.73, 0.07], P = 0.10), although the heterogeneity was relatively high (I² = 75%, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2A). Subgroup analysis revealed that heterogeneity in the adult group was acceptable, with the Q-test indicating non-significant heterogeneity (I² = 42%, P = 0.16). The MD was − 0.65 [95% CI: −1.01, −0.28] (Fig. 2A), showing that LTSH was smaller in the experimental group than in the control group, with a significant difference between the two groups (P < 0.01). In contrast, heterogeneity in the children group was higher than in the adult group, with the Q-test indicating significant heterogeneity (I² = 74%, P = 0.02). The MD was 0.09 [95% CI: −0.50, 0.68], showing no statistically significant difference (P = 0.76) (Fig. 2A). The Galbraith plot analysis showed that most of the study points were near the regression line, although two studies deviated from the center line, indicating some publication bias (Fig. 2B). However, the overall bias was within acceptable limits. Sensitivity analysis revealed that, after excluding each study individually and re-performing the meta-analysis, the combined effect estimates remained stable. Each line in the sensitivity plot represents the effect size and CI after excluding the corresponding study. From the plot, it can be observed that the sensitivity analysis supports the robustness of the meta-analysis results. The horizontal axis represents the effect size, ranging from − 0.34 to 0.00. Each point (yellow circle) indicates the overall effect estimate after the exclusion of a particular study. It is evident that when different studies were excluded, the overall effect size varied minimally, clustering between − 0.27 and − 0.05, with CI remaining relatively unchanged across studies, showing no significant expansion or narrowing (Fig. 2C). This suggests that no single study had a considerable impact on the overall results, indicating that the meta-analysis results were stable, and individual studies had little influence on the final outcome.

LJSD
Seven studies evaluating the association between LJSD and DLM injury risk involved 1,253 knee joints. Three studies focused on children, and four on adults. Overall, there was a significant difference between the experimental group and the control group (MD = 1.18 [95% CI: 0.86, 1.50]; P < 0.01), with high heterogeneity (I² = 63%, P = 0.01). Subgroup analysis indicated a significant difference between the experimental and control groups in adults (MD = 1.04 [95% CI: 1.67, 1.40]; P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A), showing that LJSD in the experimental group was larger than that in the control group, with non-significant heterogeneity according to the Q-test (I² = 62%, P = 0.05). A significant difference was also found in the pediatric group (MD = 1.42 [95% CI: 0.96, 1.88]; P < 0.01), with lower heterogeneity in the children group (I² = 38%, P = 0.20) (Fig. 3A). The Galbraith plot further supported this conclusion, as most of the study points were near the regression line and within the 95% CI, indicating no apparent publication bias and that the study results were robust (Fig. 3B). The sensitivity analysis confirmed that the overall effect size and CI remained relatively stable regardless of which study was excluded, demonstrating the robustness of the meta-analysis results. No single study significantly influenced the overall findings (Fig. 3C).

FHH
Seven studies met the criteria for evaluating the association between FHH and DLM injury risk, involving 1,253 knee joints. Three studies focused on children, and four on adults. The overall effect size was − 3.03 (MD = −3.03 [95% CI: −3.83, −2.23]; P < 0.01) (Fig. 4A), indicating a significant difference between the experimental and control groups, with high overall heterogeneity (I² = 77%, P < 0.01). Subgroup analysis showed significant differences between the experimental and control groups in adults (MD = −2.47 [95% CI: −3.59, −1.35], P < 0.01), with high heterogeneity (I² = 81%, P < 0.01). Similarly, significant differences were found in the pediatric group (MD = −3.77 [95% CI: −4.79, −2.75], P < 0.01), but the Q-test for heterogeneity was non-significant (I² = 62%, P = 0.07) (Fig. 4A). This study consistently suggests that fibular head height is significantly lower in the DLM group compared to the normal control group, a finding confirmed in both the adult and pediatric groups. In the Galbraith plot, most data points were aligned along the regression line, indicating consistency in effect sizes across studies. However, one data point deviated from the regression line, suggesting some degree of publication bias (Fig. 4B). The sensitivity analysis supported the stability of the meta-analysis results. Although some studies exhibited heterogeneity, their influence on the overall results was relatively manageable. Excluding any individual study led to only slight variations in effect estimates, which remained between − 1.04 and − 0.66 (Fig. 4C). This demonstrates that these studies had a limited impact on the meta-analysis and that the results are robust. Even when excluding any one study, the overall effect size remained stable, indicating high reliability of the study’s findings.

LTPO
A total of six studies met the criteria for evaluating the association between the LTPO and the risk of DLM injury, involving 1,122 knee joints. The pediatric group included three studies, with high heterogeneity (I² = 87%, P < 0.01), suggesting substantial variability between the studies. The adult group also included three studies, and the effect size was MD = −2.58 [95% CI: −3.60, −1.55], indicating that LTPO in adult DLM patients was significantly lower than that of the normal control group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5A). The Q-test for heterogeneity in the adult group was non-significant (I² = 0, P = 0.43), indicating homogeneity among the results in the adult group (Fig. 5A). The Galbraith plot analysis showed that most of the study points were distributed along the regression line, indicating a certain level of consistency in the effect sizes across the studies. However, Choi et al. deviated from the regression line, indicating notable publication bias (Fig. 5B). This is further corroborated by the forest plot results, which demonstrate that some studies’ findings diverged from the others. The sensitivity analysis also showed that excluding Choi et al. resulted in a significant shift in the effect estimate, beyond the 95% CI of the estimated effect size (Fig. 5C). Therefore, a reanalysis of the forest plot was conducted after excluding this study.

After excluding Choi et al., the forest plot for the pediatric group showed homogeneity, with a non-significant Q-test for heterogeneity (I² = 0, P = 0.44), MD = −1.65 [95% CI: −2.85, −0.45, P < 0.01] (Fig. 5D), indicating that LTPO in children with DLM was significantly lower than in the normal control group (P < 0.01). The adult group results remained unchanged. The overall effect size was − 2.18, MD = −2.18 [95% CI: −2.96, −1.40, P < 0.01], demonstrating a significant difference in LTPO between the experimental group and the control group. The overall heterogeneity was also non-significant, confirming the study’s homogeneity (I² = 0, P = 0.46) (Fig. 5D).

Condylar cut-off sign
A total of six studies met the inclusion criteria for evaluating the association between the condylar cut-off sign and the risk of DLM injury. In total, 1,127 knee joints were included in the analysis. The adult group included three studies, and the pediatric group included four studies (Fig. 6A). The Galbraith plot showed that two study points significantly deviated from the regression line, indicating that these studies might have some bias (Fig. 6B). The sensitivity analysis suggested that Choi et al. had a considerable impact on the overall heterogeneity (Fig. 6C). After excluding this study, the effect estimate exceeded the 95% CI, indicating that this study significantly influenced the overall results. Therefore, a forest plot analysis was performed again after excluding Choi et al. to ensure more accurate results.

After excluding Choi et al. based on the sensitivity analysis, the remaining five studies were included in the final meta-analysis, involving 945 knees. For the adult group, three studies were included. The effect size for the adult group was MD = −0.11 [95% CI: −0.22, −0.01, P = 0.04], with high heterogeneity (I² = 96%, P < 0.01) (Fig. 6D). Significant correlation between the experimental and control groups was observed (P = 0.04). For the pediatric group, two studies were included. The effect size was MD = −0.09 [95% CI: −0.11, −0.07, P<0.01] (Fig. 6D), indicating that the condylar cut-off sign in the pediatric group was significantly lower compared to the control group. The heterogeneity was non-significant (I² = 18%, P = 0.27). Combining the results from both adult and pediatric studies, the overall effect size was MD = −0.10 [95% CI: −0.15, −0.06, P < 0.01] (Fig. 6D), indicating that the condylar cut-off sign in DLM patients was significantly lower than in the control group. However, the total heterogeneity remained high (I² = 89%, P < 0.01), suggesting variability among the included studies.

Discussion

Clinical implications and future research
The most important finding of this study is that combining multiple radiographic signs can enhance the diagnostic accuracy of complete DLM. Key predictive factors for DLM include LJSD, FHH, LTPO, and the condylar cut-off sign. Results confirm several earlier studies that identified these critical radiographic features associated with DLM.
Regarding LTSH, although subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore the substantial heterogeneity observed (I² = 75%), further elaboration is necessary to clarify the potential sources of clinical heterogeneity and their implications. The high level of heterogeneity may stem from differences in study populations (pediatric vs. adult), variations in imaging modalities (e.g., radiographs vs. MRI), measurement protocols for LTSH, and diagnostic criteria for DLM. In particular, anatomical and developmental differences in the pediatric population, such as incomplete ossification and growth plate variations, may influence the measurement accuracy and reliability of LTSH, thereby affecting the comparability of results with adult studies. In studies by Park et al. and Song et al. on adult DLM, no correlation was found between LTSH and DLM. In other studies, LTSH has been suggested to have a correlation with DLM. These factors suggest that while a significant association between decreased LTSH and DLM was observed in the adult subgroup, such findings may not be directly extrapolated to pediatric populations or universally across clinical settings. Consequently, clinicians should exercise caution when interpreting these results, and consider patient age, imaging methods, and contextual clinical factors in applying the findings to practice.
In this study, a significant correlation between LJSD and DLM was observed in both pediatric and adult populations. According to Park et al., the lateral joint space is larger in knees with DLM compared to normal lateral menisci, and the probability of having a DLM increases as the lateral joint space widens. Rohren et al. published a retrospective study of 87 patients to identify radiographic features associated with DLM. Rohren et al. further reported that 53.9% (34 of 63) of patients exhibited bony changes related to DLM, with the most common being lateral joint space widening. This study emphasizes the relevance of LJSD as a non-invasive marker for DLM, particularly in early diagnosis.
In this study, FHH was consistently lower in the experimental group than in the control group across all subgroups. Potential sources of heterogeneity (I² = 77%, P < 0.01) may include differences in imaging techniques, measurement protocols, patient demographics, and definitions of DLM across studies. Previous research has identified FHH as an important sign for predicting DLM. Additionally, the reduction in FHH not only serves as a notable marker of DLM but also reflects changes in the mechanical stress distribution within the knee joint, which could affect joint stability and function. Kim et al. were the first to demonstrate significant radiographic differences in FHH between DLM and normal menisci. This anatomical characteristic is considered a distinctive feature of DLM, making FHH measurement crucial for diagnosing and managing DLM.
This study has shown that increased LTPO inclination is strongly correlated with the presence of DLM in both adults and children. Park et al. demonstrated that combining LTPO with clinical symptoms significantly improves the diagnostic accuracy of complete DLM. Furthermore, Minami et al. found that changes in LTPO were closely related to abnormal lateral meniscus morphology, particularly in pediatric and adolescent patients. This supports the clinical application of LTPO as a diagnostic tool, especially in patients with subtle symptoms, where LTPO measurements provide additional diagnostic evidence. The presence of significant initial bias—particularly stemming from Choi et al. has notable implications for the clinical interpretation of LTPO in diagnosing DLM. Prior to excluding this outlier, the pooled data exhibited substantial heterogeneity (I² = 63%, P = 0.01), which could have led to misleading conclusions about the diagnostic value of LTPO. Surgeons and radiologists should interpret increased LTPO values cautiously and always in conjunction with other radiological signs and patient history, particularly when the findings are borderline.
Regarding the condylar cut-off sign, significant correlations were observed in both the adult and pediatric groups. Park et al. pointed out that although the occurrence rate of the condylar cut-off sign in adult patients with DLM is relatively low, the radiographic feature, when combined with other clinical presentations, can still assist in diagnosis. The study further emphasized the role of MRI as a supplementary tool for diagnosing DLM. Ha et al. demonstrated that, in pediatric patients, the condylar cut-off sign could be a key radiographic marker for early identification of DLM. The study also noted that the specificity and sensitivity of this feature were particularly significant in this population, especially when evaluated using tunnel view X-rays. This sign correlates with morphological changes in the lateral femoral condyle and widening of the lateral joint space, making it particularly useful for the early diagnosis of complete DLM. Nonetheless, the remaining variability (I² = 89%, P < 0.01) imposes important limitations on the clinical applicability of this radiographic sign. Therefore, clinicians should interpret the condylar cut-off sign cautiously and in the context of other diagnostic information. Further standardized studies are needed to validate its diagnostic accuracy.
Compared to normal menisci, patients with complete DLM are more prone to meniscal tears, instability and lateral compartment osteoarthritis. Therefore, close monitoring of patients with suspected complete DLM, particularly for the onset of meniscal tear symptoms (such as pain, dysfunction, and joint locking), is crucial for determining the optimal timing for further evaluation and surgical treatment. Given the high sensitivity of LJSD and FHH, which do not require specialized X-ray techniques, it is recommended to use LJSD and FHH as screening tools for DLM. The predictive equation can then be used as a supplementary diagnostic tool to decide whether further evaluation (such as MRI) or treatment is necessary, enabling earlier detection and treatment of DLM.

Limitations of the evidence
Although this meta-analysis provides valuable insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, there was significant heterogeneity in studies involving FHH (I² = 77%, P < 0.01) and the condylar cut-off sign (I² = 89%, P < 0.01). This heterogeneity may be due to variations in study design, imaging protocols, and patient demographics. The inclusion of different radiographic techniques and imaging angles may have led to measurement inconsistencies across studies. Future research should focus on standardizing radiographic examination methods to ensure data consistency and reliability. Second, the small number of studies in certain subgroups, particularly in the adult group, limited the statistical power of our findings. Although our analysis demonstrated significant differences in several radiographic parameters, the small sample size increased the risk of Type I (false positive) and Type II (false negative) errors, reducing our confidence in generalizing these results to broader populations. Future research should prioritize larger, multicenter studies that can confirm these findings and reduce the impact of study-level variability. Finally, reliance on observational studies introduces the possibility of selection bias. Although this study used Galbraith plots and sensitivity analyses to assess publication bias, these methods may not fully eliminate its influence, as publication bias was observed in studies on LTSH and the condylar cut-off sign. To address this issue, future studies should aim to publish all results, including negative and non-significant findings, to provide more comprehensive data for meta-analyses.

Conclusion
This meta-analysis identifies LJSD, FHH, LTPO, and the condylar cut-off sign as key radiographic markers for diagnosing DLM, with accuracy enhanced when combined, particularly in the absence of MRI. LTSH appears diagnostically relevant mainly in adults, with limited value in pediatric populations.
