Repurposing antibiotics: A dual-action approach against bacteria-induced cancer
Antibiotic resistance and the growing burden of bacteria-induced cancers highlight the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches. Drug repurposing, leveraging pre-approved antibiotics for novel applications, is a promising strategy to address this challenge. Antibiotics designed to combat bacterial infections can inhibit microbial activity and target cellular mechanisms associated with oncogenesis. Chronic bacterial infections, such as those caused by Salmonella typhi, Helicobacter pylori, and Escherichia coli, contribute significantly to gallbladder, gastric, kidney, and bladder cancers. These infections induce inflammation, DNA damage, and the disruption of cellular pathways, promoting the development of cancer. Antibiotics such as doxycycline, rifampicin, and azithromycin demonstrate anticancer properties by inhibiting angiogenesis, inducing apoptosis, and regulating key pathways including the interleukin (IL)-6 signaling pathway and autophagy-related pathways. This dual action enhances chemotherapeutic efficacy and addresses bacteria-induced oncogenesis, offering a cost-effective and time-efficient alternative to traditional drug discovery. Herein, we review the intricate mechanisms by which bacteria-induced cancer arises and explore the groundbreaking potential of repurposing antibiotics as dual-action therapies in oncology. By elucidating the pivotal role of biofilms in persistent infections and highlighting untapped therapeutic opportunities in antibiotic repurposing, this review underscores a transformative approach to cancer treatment. This article explores the potential of repurposing antibiotic drugs for cancer treatment and highlights the prospects of drug repurposing strategies.

Introduction
Currently, humanity faces numerous health challenges, ranging from infectious diseases such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), typhoid, and tuberculosis to non-infectious conditions such as cancer. Among these, cancer remains one of the leading causes of human mortality. Uncontrolled cell growth is a hallmark of cancer, and its onset is attributed to various factors, including genetic predisposition, environmental influences, chronic inflammation, immune suppression, chronic bacterial and viral infections, and radiation exposure. Interestingly, several bacterial infections have been implicated in oncogenesis. Pathogens such as Salmonella typhi, Escherichia coli, Helicobacter pylori, and Staphylococcus aureus are associated with cancer development. These bacteria often induce chronic infections facilitated by biofilm formation that act as protective barriers, creating a favorable environment for bacterial survival under stressful conditions, including environmental, antibiotic, and nutritional stresses. This biofilm-mediated persistence can lead to chronic infections, which in turn contribute to cancer development. Given this connection, there is an urgent need to address bacteria-induced cancers. However, the current landscape of cancer therapies and drugs offers limited curative results. The design and discovery of novel anticancer drugs is a lengthy and expensive process, typically requiring 8–10 years with a high risk of failure. This underscores the need for alternative strategies that are time- and cost-effective. Drug repurposing has emerged as a promising solution for this challenge; it involves the application of preexisting drugs to new therapeutic targets and indications. Repurposed drugs have already undergone rigorous testing for pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and toxicity, bypassing many hurdles associated with new drug development. This approach reduces the risk of failure and accelerates the availability of effective treatments. This review focuses on the potential of antibiotics as repurposed agents to combat bacteria-induced cancer. Antibiotics, originally designed to combat microbial activity, exhibit dual-action mechanisms, functioning as both antibacterial and anticancer agents. Notable examples are doxycycline, azithromycin, rifampicin, rifabutin, clarithromycin, minocycline, and ciprofloxacin, making them a compelling subject for cancer research. In this review, we explored the mechanistic roles of antibiotics in bacterial oncogenesis, emphasizing their contribution to cancer prevention and treatment. In addition, we provided recent updates on the repurposing of antibiotic drugs for cancer therapy, paving the way for innovative strategies to integrate antibiotics into cancer treatment paradigms.

Cancer involves multiple processes such as alterations of cell growth, mutations, and deoxyribonucleic acid DNA damage. Bacteria promote oncogenesis by disrupting the host immune system, altering the cell cycle, and inducing chronic inflammation. Various types of cancer showed a relationship with bacterial infections, such as gallbladder, urinary bladder, lung, and kidney cancers. Herein, we discussed gallbladder, gastric, kidney, and bladder oncogenesis because they are strongly correlated with bacterial infections [Figure 1 and Table 1].

Gallbladder cancer
The gallbladder is a pear-shaped organ that is located beneath the liver. Though it does not produce it, the gallbladder helps to release bile into the small intestine. Simultaneously, bile can be stored. When food reaches the small intestine, the gallbladder releases bile to facilitate digestion. Some bacterial infections create chronic conditions inside the gallbladder that provoke oncogenesis, such as typhoid infections, E. coli infections, and gallstone disease. Typhoid infections and gallstones are the most common causes of gallbladder cancer. Every year, 11–12 million people suffer from typhoid infections caused by S. typhi; it enters the human body through contaminated food and water and reaches the small intestine. Then, it crosses the epithelial layer of the small intestine, enters the microphase (phagocytic and non-phagocytic cells), and is transferred to various organs, such as the liver and gallbladder. In the gallbladder, S. typhi infections persist for a long time and are asymptomatic. At this condition, S. typhi released various types of toxic molecules that show carcinogenic behaviors and provoked the oncogenesis, such as nitro-chemical, pertussis toxin (Plt), effector proteins, and typhoid toxin. It also releases the cytolethal distending toxin (Cdt), which is encoded by three genes: CdtA, CdtB, and CdtC. Cytolethal toxins cannot enter host cell DNA, and therefore, they combine with Plts (Plts A and B) to form a unique combined triplet (CdtB-PltA-PltB) that enters the host cell. As a result, it causes host cell DNA damage and cell cycle alterations. Additionally, Salmonella outer protein E (SopE), Salmonella outer protein E2 (SopE2), and Salmonella outer protein B (SopB) enhanced the survival capacity of the bacteria in host cells and altered the mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinase and protein kinase B (Akt) pathways. These events enhance proto-oncogene expression. In contrast, all typhoid toxins combined with bile juice form a toxic environment that has a direct impact on the epithelium of the gallbladder, causing enhanced inflammation. Consequently, these events may provoke the transformation of normal gallbladder cells into cancerous cells. If a gallstone is present inside the gallbladder, which provides a favorable surface for bacterial colonization and biofilm formation, it also has a direct relationship with oncogenesis.

Gastric cancer (stomach cancer)
Gastric cancer is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, accounting for approximately 1,000,000 new cases and 700,000 deaths annually. The primary risk factor for gastric cancer is infection with H. pylori. This Gram-negative microaerophilic bacterium uniquely colonizes the gastric epithelium. H. pylori is recognized as the most prevalent bacterial infection globally and has been classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the World Health Organization (WHO). Infection with H. pylori initiates chronic non-atrophic gastritis, which can progress sequentially to intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and eventually gastric cancer. Even asymptomatic H. pylori infections can lead to complications such as peptic ulcers, which may further evolve into malignancy. The bacterium's pathogenicity begins with its ability to penetrate the gastric mucosa using flagella, allowing it to evade the harsh acidic environment of the stomach. Moreover, H. pylori neutralizes gastric acidity by producing urease, creating a more hospitable niche for its survival. The adherence of H. pylori to the gastric epithelium is facilitated by specific adhesion factors, including blood group antigen-binding adhesin (BabA), sialic acid-binding adhesin (SabA), outer inflammatory protein A (OipA), and adherence-associated lipoproteins (AlpA/B). These outer membrane proteins bind to receptors on epithelial cells to firmly anchor the bacterium to its host. Following adhesion, H. pylori secretes virulence factors such as cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA) and vacuolating cytotoxin A (VacA), which are crucial in the pathogenesis of gastric cancer. The cagA gene encodes a component of the type IV secretion system (Cag T4SS) that injects CagA protein into host epithelial cells. Once inside, CagA undergoes phosphorylation by host tyrosine kinases, such as Src and Abl. Phosphorylated CagA interacts with key host proteins, including SH2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase (SHP-2), growth factor receptor-bound protein 2 (Grb2), and CT10 regulator of kinase (Crk). These interactions activate oncogenic signaling pathways, notably those of Ras and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, which drive uncontrolled cell proliferation and tumorigenesis. Additionally, T4SS facilitates the delivery of VacA toxin into host cells. VacA forms pores in the cytoplasmic membrane, enabling its entry into endosomes and mitochondria via endocytic vesicles. Once inside mitochondria, VacA disrupts cellular function by releasing the cytochrome c, which triggers apoptosis. This dysregulation of cell death and proliferation caused by CagA and VacA is critical in the development of gastric cancer. However, the combined action of CagA and VacA generates chronic inflammation through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), leading to oxidative stress and DNA damage. These events also provoke the devolvement of gastric cancer. The multifaceted virulence mechanisms of H. pylori ranging from evasion of the gastric environment to induction of oncogenic signaling and apoptosis, underscore its central role in gastric carcinogenesis.

Kidney cancer
Kidney cancer is a significant health challenge in humans that primarily manifests in two major forms: renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and transitional cell carcinoma (TCC). According to the GLOBOCAN 2020 database, kidney cancer is projected to account for approximately 1.8% of all cancer-related deaths globally, highlighting its substantial impact on human mortality. Several factors contribute to the development of kidney cancer, including bacterial infections, genetic predispositions, and acquired cystic kidney disease. Few bacterial species have been implicated in the onset and progression of kidney cancer, including Comamonadaceae, Streptococcus, Romboutsia, Peptostreptococcaceae, Allobaculum, and Candidatus Aquiluna rubra. Emerging evidence suggests that malignant kidney tissues often harbor diverse microbial populations. For instance, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Blautia, and Streptococcus are frequently observed in RCC, where they contribute to oncogenesis by altering signaling pathways and inducing DNA damage. One study demonstrated that F. nucleatum can promote cell proliferation in colorectal cancer by binding to the fatty acid synthase-associated domain (FADA) of E-cadherin. E-cadherin is a vital cell adhesion molecule responsible for maintaining cell-to-cell adhesion and epithelial integrity. It forms adherent junctions and interacts with cytoskeletal components to maintain tissue structure. When FADA binds to E-cadherin, it inhibits its function, triggering epithelial–mesenchymal transition and fostering interactions within the tumor microenvironment, compromising epithelial integrity and enabling cancer progression and metastasis. This process facilitates cancer cell detachment and migration, enhances interactions within the tumor microenvironment, and creates a microenvironment favorable for tumor progression and metastasis. Thus, F. nucleatum modulates key molecular pathways that drive colorectal cancer development. Additionally, bacterial byproducts such as p-cresol sulfate, indoxyl sulfate, phenylacetylglycine, trimethylamine oxide (TMAO), and phenyl sulfate contribute to kidney cancer development by inducing inflammation and releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines exacerbate DNA damage and promote tumorigenesis. Particularly, TMAO has been implicated in accelerating renal tubular interstitial fibrosis and kidney dysfunction. Nonetheless, the exact mechanisms underlying bacteria-induced kidney cancer remain poorly understood. Further in-depth investigations are required to elucidate these processes and uncover new therapeutic opportunities for managing bacteria-induced kidney cancer.

Bladder cancer
Bladder cancer is the fourth leading cause of cancer in men worldwide. It is estimated that 614,000 new cases and 220,000 deaths occurred worldwide in 2020. Generally, bladder cancers are divided into non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC). The most significant symptom of bladder cancer is microscopic examination or hematuria. In bladder cancer, 75% of cases confined to mucosa (NMIBC) and 25% are MIBC. Studies suggest that various bacterial infections in the urinary bladder cause severe inflammation in the urinary tract and promote oncogenesis, such as those of Streptococcus, Anaerococcus, Aerococcus, and Pseudomonas. One study suggests that Sphingobacterium, and Aerococcus work as markers for urinary infections. These bacteria produce various enzymes and toxic molecules, including N-nitrosamines, polyaromatic hydrocarbons, free radicals, and proteases. Bacterial proteases are crucial for damaging host tissues, leading to significant inflammation. Chronic inflammation triggers the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and bacterial toxins such as N-nitrosamine. These processes stimulate the release of IL such as IL-6 and IL-10, which suppress the ability of the immune system to target and eliminate cancer cells, thereby increasing cancer risk. Furthermore, IL-6 and IL-10 disrupt the cell cycle and activate the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT)3 signaling pathway, which promotes cancer cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis, thereby contributing to tumor progression. These interconnected events lead to DNA damage in host cells and disruptions in the cell cycle, ultimately increasing the likelihood of oncogenesis. A study also suggests that few cancer patients show a high expression of cyclooxygenase, which is induced by bacterial infections. The cyclooxygenase acts as a regulator of tumor growth, showing that bacteria play a major role in the formation of oncogenesis in bladder cancer.

Need of the repurposing from antibiotics to the anticancer agent
Drug repurposing can significantly shorten the drug discovery timeline due to its features such as preexisting toxicity profile, reduced cost, targeting the common pathway, combat the drug resistance, time-saving, dual mechanistic action, synergistic behaviors, and overcoming drug shortage.
Fig. 2
Drug repurposing has emerged as a promising strategy to address the challenges associated with traditional drug discovery. Developing new drugs is a time-consuming and costly process with low success and high failure rates. In contrast, drug repurposing leverages existing drugs for new therapeutic applications and offers numerous advantages. This approach is based on the application of “old drugs” to “new therapeutic uses.” Some of the key advantages of antibiotic drug repurposing include its preexisting toxicity profile, reduced cost, ability to target common pathways, potential to combat drug resistance, capacity to address drug shortages, synergistic effects with anticancer drugs, and dual mechanistic actions [Figure 2], making this approach advantageous to prevent cancer. Collectively, these features make drug repurposing a highly attractive strategy for accelerating drug development. One major advantage is that repurposing utilizes approved drugs with established safety profiles, such as Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved antibiotics. This reduces the need for extensive safety evaluations, significantly shortening the time required for drug development. Consequently, the cost of drug discovery decreases, enhancing the economic feasibility of this approach. Another valuable aspect is the ability of repurposed antibiotics to target shared pathways in both bacterial infections and cancer. For example, antibiotics such as rifampicin, which inhibit DNA synthesis and protein production, can also target pathways implicated in cancer progression. This overlap underscores the utility of repurposing to address oncogenesis. Another feature, the synergistic behavior, enhances the efficacy of antibiotics in cancer treatment. For instance, azithromycin combined with a chemotherapeutic agent has been shown to amplify anticancer efficacy. Such combinations open new avenues for cancer prevention and treatment. The dual mechanism system is another essential feature of drug repurposing, such as a drug that has already been designed against a bacterial target. If this bacterium is responsible for oncogenesis, as in S. typhi. This drug works against bacteria-induced oncogenesis with new targets in the same species during the repurposing process. This enables the drug to work against preexisting and newly discovered targets. Therefore, preexisting drugs will show new therapeutic applications to overcome drug shortages. These features are responsible for many drug discovery processes and facilitating entry into clinical trials. Therefore, drug repurposing is an important avenue for drug discovery. It also opens new therapeutic applications for preexisting drug resistance. Currently, drug repurposing is required because drug discovery is slow compared to the disease formation rate. Thus, there is only one option for using preexisting drugs to control chronic diseases, making drug repurposing an effective strategy for drug discovery. Various antibiotic drugs have shown anticancer effects due to drug repurposing, which is discussed in the following sections.

Doxycycline
Doxycycline is a well-known FDA-approved antibiotic used against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial infections. It is a tetracycline-based second-generation antibiotic discovered in 1967. The chemical formula of doxycycline is C22H24N2O8. It is also effective against bacterial protein synthesis. It interacts with the 30S prokaryotic ribosomal subunit and inhibits protein synthesis, exhibits an anti-inflammatory response by blocking nitric oxide synthase, and is used in bacteria-induced cancer, as inflammation promotes oncogenesis (Bacteria-Induced Cancer section). In addition, nitric oxide synthase, which contributes to oncogenesis, is effectively targeted by doxycycline. Doxycycline induces cancer cell apoptosis and inhibits metastasis. A recent study by Ghasemi and Ghasemi suggests that doxycycline is critical for inhibiting colorectal cancer by inducing apoptosis. This process involves both caspase-dependent and -independent pathways. Another study by Rok et al. suggests that doxycycline exerted anticancer effects against amelanotic melanoma cell lines by inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis in amelanotic melanoma cells. Another study by Zhang et al. suggested that doxycycline plays a major role in inducing apoptosis in cancer cells, which is necessary to prevent cancer. It may promote the release of cytochrome C, which is an essential event for apoptosis. Cytochrome c release activates apoptosome formation by binding to apoptotic protease activating factor-1 (Apaf-1) and procaspase-9, ultimately leading to the activation of effector caspases, such as caspase-3 and caspase-7, which execute programmed cell death. It also plays a significant role in preventing the transition of breast cancer cells. A few proapoptotic factors lead to the release of cytochrome C, such as Bax, Bak, Bid, and Bim. A study suggests that matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) enzymes participate in cellular processes, such as cell motility and proliferation. A study also suggests that the MMPs expression is upregulated in cancer tissue, making them a possible cancer marker because of their involvement in cell proliferation and metastasis. Doxycycline acts as an MMPs inhibitor, showing a highly positive view as an antibiotic in humans. In contrast, bacterial cells have a unique way of communicating with each other known as quorum sensing, wherein bacteria release specific molecules or chemicals that act as quorum sensing molecules; this process is crucial in bacteria-induced cancer. However, doxycycline is an anti-quorum-sensing molecule. Therefore, it is useful against bacteria-induced cancers.

Rifampicin
Rifampicin is an FDA-approved antibacterial drug used to treat tuberculosis and leprosy. Its molecular formula is C43H58N4O12. Rifampicin exerts its action by targeting the DNA-dependent ribonucleic acid (RNA) polymerase, resulting in cell death. This may lead to resistance to a range of antibiotics. Furthermore, it demonstrated efficacy against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The approximate half-life of rifampicin is 3.35 h (±0.66). Rifampicin showed the anticancer potential to prevent cancer cell growth due to its antiangiogenic effect. Using in vitro models, Shichiri et al. suggested that rifampicin prevents cancer growth in 39 different human cancer cell lines. A recent study by Saad et al., suggested that administering rifampicin at a dose of 0.107 mg/kg effectively reduces alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase levels while enhancing antioxidant defenses in vivo in a model of Ehrlich ascites carcinoma. A recent study by Elshahawy et al. suggests that rifampicin demonstrated enhanced anticancer potential when combined with doxorubicin. This synergy significantly boosts apoptosis, highlighting the effectiveness of their combined use against hepatocellular carcinoma. Rifampicin also shows anticancer effects through the inhibition of angiogenesis because it targets vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), focal adhesion kinase, endothelin-1, and endothelin receptor genes. Rifampicin shows anticancer potential due to cancer apoptosis, cancer cell DNA damage, and antiangiogenic effects.

Rifabutin
Rifabutin is an FDA-approved antibiotic used to treat mycobacterial infections. Additionally, it exhibits broad-spectrum activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Its chemical formula is C46H62N4O11. Rifabutin functions by interacting with DNA-dependent RNA polymerases, thereby inhibiting bacterial infections. It also causes DNA damage during bacterial infections. These properties demonstrate their significant potential for applications in drug repurposing. Lee et al. suggested rifabutin's potential for repurposing as a treatment for drug-resistant cancer. Rifabutin significantly enhances the cytotoxicity of vincristine (VIC) in P-glycoprotein (P-gp)-overexpressing drug-resistant cancer cells by inhibiting P-gp activity and reducing VIC efflux. The combination (rifabutin and VIC) also promoted early apoptosis, G2 arrest, and DNA damage, demonstrating its unique anticancer efficacy. Another recent study by Gisbert suggests that rifabutin also possesses antibacterial properties against H. pylori infection, which is directly associated with gastric cancer. Thus, rifabutin showed potential for controlling bacteria-induced cancer. Li et al., suggested that rifabutin participates in preventing lung cancer by targeting the translation initiation factor 4E (eIF4E), which is essential in the development of cancer through the activation of the β-catenin. However, the exact mechanism of the β-catenin-induced cancer is unknown. The rifabutin showed a suppression effect on the eIF4E, which led to inhibitions of the phosphorylation of the β-catenin. As a result, cell growth is inhibited. It also enhanced apoptosis and inhibited cell proliferation. Rifabutin showed anticancer effects, and while it is not widely used for cancer prevention, its strong antibacterial action provokes apoptosis, inhibits translation, and inhibits cell proliferation factors, providing new hope to control non-bacteria- and bacteria-induced cancers, such as those of the gallbladder, lung, and kidney. Therefore, in-depth experimental studies are required to identify new therapeutic applications of this drug.

Clarithromycin
Clarithromycin was approved by the FDA in 2000. It has antibacterial activity against a wide range of bacteria, including Gram-positive species such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and S. aureus, and Gram-negative bacteria such as Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis. Its mechanism of action involves an interaction with the 50S subunit of the bacterial ribosome, thereby inhibiting aminoacyl transfer-RNA translocation activity. Consequently, bacterial protein synthesis is hindered, leading to bacterial cell death. As a result, clarithromycin is used in cancer treatment because of its effective mechanisms, such as inhibition of protein synthesis, making it an excellent example of drug repurposing. A recent study by Nardo et al. suggests that clarithromycin showed an anticancer effect due to the induction of apoptosis and increased ROS in cancer cells. Increased ROS levels cause oxidative stress, leading to the damage of cellular components such as lipids, proteins, and DNA. Oxidative damage activates stress-response signaling pathways, including those of c-Jun amino-terminal kinase (JNK) and p38 MAPK, which drive apoptosis. Excessive ROS also disrupt mitochondrial function, amplifying the release of proapoptotic factors such as Bax, while downregulating anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2. Takemori et al. suggested that clarithromycin has a suppressive effect on IL-6 and possesses immunomodulatory properties, making it a potential candidate for cancer prevention. Growth factors such as IL-6 are crucial in the development of cancers such as myeloma by promoting uncontrolled cell growth. Clarithromycin not only suppresses IL-6 but also enhances anticancer effects when used in combination with other drugs, such as protease inhibitors, surpassing the efficacy of individual treatments. Additionally, the study by Petroni et al. suggests that clarithromycin exhibits anticancer activity against colorectal cancer by regulating autophagy, a critical process that maintains cellular homeostasis by managing defective proteins and organelles. Autophagy is often dysregulated in cancer cells during tumor growth and progression. Clarithromycin interacts with the ether-à-go-go related gene 1 (hERG1) and inhibits the formation of complexes, thereby preventing Akt phosphorylation. This disruption induces cancer cell death through a p53-dependent mechanism. Similarly, the study by Amani et al. suggests that clarithromycin regulates autophagy while exhibiting anti-proliferative and proapoptotic effects when combined with doxorubicin. This combination demonstrated enhanced anticancer activity due to its synergistic effect. Consequently, clarithromycin holds promise as an anticancer drug owing to its regulation by autophagy and ILs. However, it is not effective against all types of cancers. Therefore, it is imperative to comprehensively understand the potential of antibiotics for cancer treatment. It can serve as a positive adjunct to cancer prevention with and without chemotherapy.

Azithromycin
Azithromycin is a widely recognized antibiotic used to treat various bacterial infections, including those caused by H. influenzae and S. pneumoniae. Notably, its high tissue penetration capacity makes it a potent antibiotic. It also exhibits anticancer effects against bacteria-induced cancer owing to its high antibacterial activity. It impedes proliferation and induces apoptosis in gastric cancer, particularly because it targets bacteria-induced oncogenic mechanisms directly linked to gastric cancer development. This is a clear example of drug repurposing from an antibiotic to an anticancer drug. Similarly, Chu et al. suggested the potential of azithromycin in combination with chemotherapeutic agents such as paclitaxel. Azithromycin was found to inhibit IL secretion, resulting in immune modulation, which enhanced its anticancer efficacy. Another study by Qiao et al. suggests that azithromycin showed an inhibitory action on colon cancer by autophagy inhibition. In the same direction, a recent study Takano et al. suggests that azithromycin worked as an autophagy inhibitor effect in cancer. Autophagy is crucial in cancer cell growth and chemotherapy resistance. Therefore, autophagy is an important target for cancer treatment. Azithromycin interacted and disrupted keratin-18, leading to autophagy inhibition. Thus, azithromycin may be used as an anticancer drug; however, more in-depth studies are required to understand its anticancer mechanisms. This will provide a new direction for the treatment of bacteria-induced cancer, owing to its antibacterial properties.

Tigecycline
Tigecycline is an FDA-approved antibacterial drug used to treat a broad range of infections caused by organisms such as E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, S. aureus, and Enterococcus faecalis. It belongs to a novel class of antibiotics known as the glycylcyclines, which are derived from the tetracycline class of antibiotics. The mechanism of action of tigecycline is similar to that of tetracycline. It targets the 30S ribosomal subunit and thereby inhibits protein synthesis. Tigecycline demonstrated a greater binding capacity to its bacterial target than that of tetracycline. Moreover, although tigecycline demonstrates anticancer effects, it has not been extensively explored.

Minocycline
Minocycline is an antibiotic that was approved by the FDA in 1971. It is a tetracycline-based antibiotic with certain modifications, such as a D-ring modification at the 7–9 carbon positions, which enhances its effectiveness. Consequently, it is used for the treatment of a wide array of bacterial infections, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections. Minocycline acts by interacting with the 30S ribosomal subunit, thereby impeding protein synthesis in bacterial infections. It has many anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective effects. Minocycline is used to treat various cancers, such as those of lung, ovarian, and breast. Tone et al. suggested the effects of minocycline on the outcomes of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) receiving first-line epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs). The results showed that patients who received minocycline had a significantly longer progression-free survival (PFS) than those who did not, with a median PFS of 714 and 420 days, respectively. Multivariate analysis confirmed that minocycline administration for ≥30 days improved both PFS and overall survival independently of skin rash, suggesting its potential to enhance the efficacy of EGFR-TKIs in NSCLC treatment. Another study by Ataie-Kachoie et al. suggested that minocycline drugs showed anticancer activity against ovarian cancer by suppressing IL-6, which has been implicated in the growth and progression of ovarian cancer through the activation of the STAT3 and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)1/2 pathways. Minocycline works against IL-6, which inhibits the JAK-STAT or MAPK pathway, showing an anticancer effect. This is a positive sign for the control of cancer using antibiotics. However, this requires an understanding of the cancer prevention mechanisms. Thus, some modifications may improve therapeutic outcomes.

Ciprofloxacin
Ciprofloxacin is a well-known FDA-approved antibiotic used against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial infections. It targets bacterial DNA replication specifically by inhibiting DNA supercoiling. Additionally, ciprofloxacin plays direct and indirect roles in cancer prevention by inhibiting efflux pumps. In cases of multidrug-resistant cancer, efflux pumps, such as ATP-binding cassette subfamily B member 1 (ABCB1), are often overexpressed, leading to the failure of chemotherapeutic drugs to accumulate within cells, posing a significant challenge for cancer prevention. Gupta et al. suggested that ciprofloxacin enhances the chemosensitivity of cancer cells by inhibiting the efflux function of ABCB1. Further, ciprofloxacin significantly increases the cytotoxic effects of ABCB1 substrates in cells overexpressing ABCB1. Ciprofloxacin enhances the intracellular accumulation of paclitaxel while reducing its efflux without altering ABCB1 protein expression. Consequently, chemotherapeutic drugs facilitate the action of ciprofloxacin and indirectly contribute to cancer prevention. In another study, Chrzanowska et al. suggest that ciprofloxacin also exhibits toxicity toward cancer cells, inducing apoptosis. Moreover, it inhibits the expression of IL-6, thereby impeding cancer cell signaling and directly participating in cancer treatment. Nevertheless, the precise role of ciprofloxacin in cancer treatment remains inadequately investigated, as its full anticancer mechanism is not yet understood. A recent study Ali et al. suggests that ciprofloxacin derivative showed significant suppression of cancer growth on HCT-116 and LOX IMVI cancer cells by acting on topoisomerases I and II. Ciprofloxacin derivative-induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase, and are associated with the upregulation of proapoptotic genes (Bax and caspase-9) and the downregulation of the anti-apoptotic gene (Bcl-2), which leads to the cancer prevention. Hence, ciprofloxacin may be used in cancer prevention but there is an urgent need to explore its potential.

Cefepime
Cefepime is an advanced, fourth-generation FDA-approved antibiotic. It is used against a wide range of bacterial infections, including those caused by both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Its mechanism of action involves the targeting of the bacterial cell wall via penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), thereby impeding the formation of new bacterial cells. Interestingly, the study of Pfab et al. suggests that cefepime exhibits anticancer effects in colon cancer. Cefepime showed anticancer effects in combination with ceftazidime owing to its ability to induce S-phase arrest and apoptosis in colon cancer cells. Zhang et al. suggested that cefepime induces apoptosis through the formation of FEP-manganese (Mn) complexes. This is because Mn is overexpressed in cancer cells, and cefepime forms a complex that inhibits cell proliferation and induces apoptosis in cancer cells. Cefepime antibiotics' anticancer potential has not been widely explored; thus, further studies are required to determine its anticancer mechanisms.

Similarly, the study by Chen et al. highlights the significance of doxycycline in cancer prevention through its inhibitory action on integrin αvβ3, which has been identified as a promising target for cancer treatment because of its critical involvement in key processes, such as cancer cell proliferation and migration. Doxycycline exerts its anticancer effects by inhibiting integrin αvβ3 activity. Furthermore, it suppresses the expression of cell cycle regulators, such as cyclin D1 and cellular myelocytomatosis oncogene (c-Myc), which are essential for cancer cell proliferation. These findings underscore the potential of doxycycline as a therapeutic agent in targeting integrin αvβ3-related cancer pathways. Another recent study by Takano et al. study suggests that azithromycin is crucial in the prevention of cancer by inhibiting autophagy by binding to keratin-18 and disrupting its dynamics. As a result, tumor cell growth was suppressed. Therefore, azithromycin is a potent inhibitor of cancer treatment. Notably, mitomycin is also utilized as a chemotherapeutic agent in cancer prevention and treatment. Advantages and disadvantages of the antibiotic treatment of cancer as compared to the traditional cancer treatment
Antibiotic treatment offers several advantages over traditional cancer treatment regimens, particularly for bacteria-induced cancers. Antibiotics like doxycycline and azithromycin not only combat infections but also exhibit anticancer properties by targeting key pathways such as integrin αvβ3 or disrupting cancer cell proliferation. Their dual mechanisms of action allow them to address bacterial causes and oncogenesis. Additionally, antibiotics often have well-documented safety profiles and are more cost-effective than novel cancer drugs, significantly reducing the time and expense required for drug development. Some antibiotics also show synergistic effects when combined with traditional chemotherapeutic agents, enhancing treatment efficacy. However, antibiotic-based cancer treatments have several disadvantages. Their spectrum of action is limited, as they are particularly effective only in cancers linked to bacterial infections or the specific pathways they target. Unlike traditional targeted therapies, antibiotics lack precision in acting on cancer-specific mutations, which can lead to broader, less-specific effects and potential harm to healthy cells. Additionally, antibiotics may contribute to the emergence of resistant bacterial strains, complicating future infections in patients with cancer, which may be considered a hazardous condition. Furthermore, antibiotics are rarely as effective as standalone therapies and typically require a combination with existing cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy, for optimal results. Additionally, there is a concern that the widespread use of antibiotics in oncology might contribute to antibiotic resistance, potentially undermining their efficacy in treating infections. These factors make antibiotics a complementary rather than primary solution in cancer treatment, highlighting the need for tailored applications based on the type and etiology of cancer.
Drug repurposing has emerged as a promising strategy for managing bacterial oncogenesis. However, this study has certain limitations that must be addressed. Although antibiotics demonstrate a dual-action approach in combating bacterial infections and oncogenesis, these findings rely primarily on in vitro and preclinical models, necessitating further experimental validation. Additionally, the potential of antibiotics to induce drug resistance and other side effects highlights the need for more extensive investigations in preclinical models. Lastly, although this study emphasizes the potential of antibiotics in preventing bacterial oncogenesis through their dual-action mechanism, supporting evidence from human clinical trials is lacking. Future research that addresses these gaps will provide a more comprehensive understanding and facilitate the translation of these findings into clinical applications.

Future directions
Drug repurposing presents an attractive and alternative approach in the realm of drug discovery, operating on the principle of “old drug, new target.” This serves as a cost-effective and timesaving method for uncovering new therapeutic applications of existing drugs. The process of discovering new drugs is complex. Drug repurposing offers a novel avenue for exploring diverse applications of drugs. Humans suffer from various infectious and non-infectious diseases, some of which are novel because of self-generated bacteria and mutated viruses, with significant negative impacts on populations. Designing and discovering new drugs to combat these infections are challenging and time-consuming. Therefore, repurposing existing drugs for new infections and chronic diseases is a more straightforward approach than traditional drug discovery methods. Existing drugs contain approved information that is beneficial to human health. In the field of cancer prevention, many chemotherapeutic drugs fail to provide curative results in all cases owing to cancer cell resistance and other cellular phenomena. Repurposing antibiotics has shown improved efficiency in chemotherapy and has yielded more effective results in cancer prevention. Drug repurposing extends beyond antibiotics to anticancer treatments and may even explore antiviral applications such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infections. This method has the potential to unveil multiple therapeutic applications and redefine existing drugs for new therapeutic uses. Consequently, it will open new avenues for research, shaping the future of preexisting drugs in the medical field.

Conclusions
Bacteria-induced cancers, including gastric, gallbladder, renal, and bladder cancers, represent significant global health challenges and often arise from chronic infections and inflammation caused by pathogenic bacteria. Addressing these cancers requires innovative approaches that go beyond conventional therapies. Drug repurposing offers a promising frontier for combating bacteria-induced cancers by identifying new therapeutic applications of existing antibiotics. This strategy leverages pre-approved drugs with established safety profiles, thereby reducing both the cost and time required for drug development. Antibiotics such as doxycycline, rifabutin, clarithromycin, rifampicin, azithromycin, and ciprofloxacin have demonstrated anticancer effects, showing their potential to target cancer pathways, disrupt bacteria-induced oncogenesis, and synergize with existing anticancer therapies. By targeting common pathways and exhibiting dual mechanisms of action, repurposed antibiotics can effectively address both bacterial infections and the associated cancer risks. These examples underline the transformative potential of drug repurposing to enhance cancer treatment options. Understanding the full potential of antibiotics as anticancer agents represents a new frontier in therapeutic development, and bridges the gap between infectious disease management and oncology. This approach not only paves the way for potential treatments but also provides a cost-effective and timely solution to address the rising burden of bacteria-induced cancers.
