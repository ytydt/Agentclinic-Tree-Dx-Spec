A systems approach to sustainable finance: Actors, influence mechanisms, and potentially virtuous cycles of sustainability
Summary
Over the past decade, corporate investors have increasingly recognized that responsible environmental and social practices are essential to long-term financial success. Despite growing interest, corporate practices remain largely unchanged, and planetary trends are deeply concerning. This review applies a systems analysis lens to understand how financial sector structures and actors influence sustainability outcomes, often in counterproductive ways. Key barriers include the lack of science-based metrics, poor integration of environmental risks, and limited capacity to evaluate complex system dynamics. Current financial practices frequently miss or misinterpret systemic sustainability risks. We identify three critical areas where collaboration between sustainability science and finance is urgently needed: (1) translating scientific insights into financial decision contexts, (2) supporting science-based corporate sustainability reporting, and (3) strengthening environmental impact assessment. Systems thinking helps clarify where financial leverage can drive meaningful, cross-scale change—an essential step toward aligning capital flows with long-term ecological resilience and sustainability goals.

Introduction
Finance, economies, and human societies are embedded within the biosphere, but the continued increase in scale, connectivity and speed of the global economy is undermining the capacity of these living systems to reliably support human societies and economic activities. Economic activity is steered by finance, as investments and regulations determine which activities grow or shrink. How the financial system works, therefore, shapes the future of humanity and the biosphere, but connections between the financial system and social and environmental sustainability have only begun to be explored.
Over the past decade, interest in sustainable finance has grown exponentially. Many financial actors have begun to recognize dependencies of humans and societies on living systems and consequently are developing strategies to address increasing risks from climate change and degrading ecosystems. In various ways, these responses are influencing capital flows and investor engagement with sustainability. For example, the UN Principles for Responsible Investment (UNPRI) has seen a rapid increase in signatories, from 100 (with US$6.5 trillion AUM) in 2006 to more than 4900 financial institutions in 2023 (estimated AUM at US$121.3 trillion in 2021). There have also been increases in the variety and volume of sustainability-related financial products, such as loans or bonds that allow companies to access lower interest rates if they commit to deliver stipulated environmental outcomes or performance improvements.
Despite this growth and the undeniable importance of sustainable investments, sustainable finance has experienced recent setbacks. One key factor limiting the expansion of sustainable finance is the lack of standardized methods of assessing whether an investment is sustainable or not. Such assessments have relied heavily on ESG-related information, but ESG ratings have come under increasing scrutiny and critique due to the lack of quality control of self-reported information that goes into ESG measures, and large inconsistencies between alternative ESG metrics that undermine ESG credibility. Furthermore, environmental scientists have criticized the business-centric perspective that permeates ESG disclosures by companies; a perspective that means corporate disclosures currently fail to identify or reliably assess aggregate and cumulative corporate impacts on the planet (i.e., environmentally material information) (see Wassénius et al. for a deeper discussion). The lack of reliable and consistent data on environmental and social impacts makes it challenging for investors to assess the true impact of investments and to make informed decisions. It has also resulted in perceived greenwashing of both businesses and sustainable investment products, resulting in reduced consumer trust of sustainable investments. Many investors also worry that prioritizing ESG factors might compromise financial performance, and indeed, recent evidence does not identify any notable financial return for individual ESG investment strategies. However, as the risks associated with climate change and environmental degradation rise, the imperative for truly sustainable investments continues to increase.
Some fundamental shifts have occurred in the policy domain that governs financial actors. Two examples are the increasing climate-related financial risk focus of financial regulators and a growing recognition by central banks that biodiversity loss is a source of financial risk. Despite this, the potential for private capital to contribute to a sustainable transformation remains debated. Some still argue that a more fundamental transformation of the financial sector and its logics is necessary. However, at a time of rapid materialization of climate change, nature degradation and the compounding effects of these, exemplified through frequent and intense forest fires, convective storms, extreme hailstorms and droughts, we cannot wait for a full transformation of something as complex as the financial industry. We need to identify meaningful ways in which already existing investment practices can be improved and leveraged to amplify sustainable outcomes. This can be done by identifying two complementary goals that will help sustainable investments deliver on their ambitions. These goals are to (i) invest in and promote sustainable solutions, while also (ii) simultaneously working to reduce the harm created by existing firms and investments.
In this article, we depart from these two complementary goals and identify how financial industry actors are connected through their investment practices and what this means for their ability to leverage current investment practices to create virtuous cycles of sustainability, while addressing barriers that appear to lock in unsustainability and hamper progress toward greener economies. We do this through a qualitative conceptual review that synthesizes diverse scholarship on the most prominent private actors in the financial services sector and their primary means of influencing the sustainability outcomes of investments within their current roles and mandates. We adopt a systems analytical lens to examine how these actors are interconnected, and how their actions can reinforce or block one another’s sustainability ambitions. This allows us to identify potential reinforcing feedback loops that can create either virtuous or vicious cycles. While systems approaches have long guided sustainability studies—from local social-ecological systems to global Earth system dynamics—they have not, to our knowledge, been systematically applied to analyze how the internal dynamics of the financial sector can drive sustainability transitions. By addressing this gap, our study provides a foundation for deeper discussions on transforming the financial system and underscores the critical role of sustainability science and scientists in fostering such a transformation. We conclude with reflections on how this transition can be fruitfully pursued.

Methodological approach
Our analysis is informed by “systems thinking,” an analytical perspective that aims to understand the complexity of any given phenomenon by looking at it in terms of wholes and relationships between individual components, and in doing so identifying internal dynamics that promote or prevent certain systems outcomes. Applying a systems lens to understand the financial system translates into a focus on who the principal actors in the system are, what they typically do, and how they directly or indirectly interact and influence each other.
The article is developed in two parts. The first part is a literature review that maps out each actor’s broadly established mandate and practice. We take a scoping review approach, broadly following the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) (see supplemental information). The second part analyses the results of the scoping review to identify feedbacks among financial actors and their actions. This is done by evaluating how the practices of individual financial actors can reinforce or hinder the sustainability-enhancing potential of the other actors in focus. Feedback loops that indicate synergistic opportunities and where the sustainability-promoting actions of one actor reinforce the effectiveness of other actors’ actions are referred to as virtuous cycles. In contrast, when an actor’s current actions are likely to prevent or hamper systems change, these are akin to balancing (or stabilizing) feedback. Both reinforcing and balancing feedback can indicate barriers to systemic change, but reinforcing dynamics can be used to drive systemic change, and we outline how this can happen in the financial sector. For more details on the selection of actors, review protocol, and so forth, see methods.

By reviewing and analyzing the general practices of each actor type, we identify and outline the specific practices that are necessary for each actor to credibly be able to influence the sustainability outcomes of investments. We refer to these as mechanisms of influence (Table 1; see Fichtner et al. for a similar approach). We also identify barriers that currently undermine or prevent this influence from materializing.

Institutional investors in public equity
While short-sightedness has characterized large parts of the investment community, institutional investors have a longer time-perspective since they are unable to diversify away from systemic risks (Table 1). Strategies available to equity investors, and to so called Universal Owners in particular, to promote sustainability include active ownership through board engagement, filing shareholder resolutions, voting against the board, or as a last resort divesting (Table 1). Divestment, has become commonplace for industries that deliver blatantly harmful products/services for which there is international consensus on phasing out, as they are viewed as “stranded assets.” Investors also use it to signal discontentment with individual companies, but on its own, it is not a feasible tool to transform entire economies. see for review The main reason is that stocks are traded on a secondary market, and when sold, other less sustainability-oriented investors are likely to buy the stock. Hence, the shares change hands with no significant effect on the company itself. Rare exceptions exist, if (1) investors hold large proportions of the company shares, (2) the market for the firm’s shares is highly illiquid, (3) investors coordinate to send a strong market signal that restricts the firm’s access to capital. If these conditions hold, divestment may cause the firm’s cost of capital to increase. Otherwise, the impact of divestment is generally unidentifiable in the noisiness of stock prices. More impactful actions by patient, long-term investors, therefore include active engagement with the boards of portfolio companies. Influence can be enhanced by joining forces with other investors, and multiple initiatives exist that aim to create platforms where investors can share experience, adopt similar practices and create alliances for more effective pressure on companies (see e.g., Net-Zero Asset Owner Alliance, Climate Action 100+, Finance for Biodiversity). However, without instigating changed corporate practices by asking companies to set clear targets and then monitor progress and openly disclose it, active ownership is unlikely to contribute to any significant change. Institutional investors, therefore, have a duty to act when progress fails, by filing resolutions or voting to replace boards that block or slow change.

Banks as lenders and underwriters of bonds
Debt is a powerful tool to change company practices because it imposes financial discipline and accountability, compelling companies to adhere to specific performance metrics and governance standards (Table 1). Further, as opposed to equity traded on secondary markets, debt directly affects borrowing companies’ liquidity and cost of capital. Corporate banks, therefore, have a critical role to play in achieving a shift toward a sustainable economy. The potential for bonds and loans to deliver real change lies in determining access to and the cost of capital, as well as holding borrowers accountable or liable to pay if targets are not met. Ensuring that clear sustainability targets are set and used to evaluate performance and costs of capital for sustainability-linked bonds and loans (SLB/SLL) and, likewise, ensuring a trustworthy assessment of the sustainability (or “greenness”) of the green loans or bonds, is therefore paramount.
A lack of clear definitions of what counts as a sustainable or green outcome currently presents a major barrier for green debt. Also closely connected is the issue of post-issuance impact verification. Such verification is essential if green debt is to address sustainability challenges, but currently, impact verification post project completion rarely occurs. This absence of verification severely limits investor capacity to assess whether a particular bond actually delivers environmental benefits. It also hampers investors’ ability to identify significant trade-offs associated with green bond projects. For example, bonds may perform well on water use or emissions, but have a negative impact on waste and land use. Currently, green bonds are often used to re-finance existing projects, and in such cases, the sustainability benefits are naturally limited. However, evidence suggests that green bond issuance can lead to reduced GHG emissions, but reductions are only pronounced when the bonds are certified by third parties, are issued in countries with strong institutions and low corruption levels, and when the bonds issued for refinancing purposes are excluded from analysis.

Venture capital providers
Venture Capital (VC) can be seen as a subcategory of private equity, and the two share many mechanisms of influence. While there are some differences in their capacity to generate impact, we follow Caldecott et al. and treat them as one category and focus primarily on VC for its explicit aim to catalyze innovation.
New ventures tend to be most constrained in their growth by external financing conditions. For companies that offer sustainable solutions or products that promise to contribute to a greener economy, VC therefore has a fundamental role to play yet it is an under-researched actor in the financial system. One feature of VC investors makes their potential role for promoting sustainability particularly interesting. VC investments generally have strong, continuous monitoring and contractual mechanisms to guard against uncertainty and insufficient information about a new company. This is intended as a protection for VC investors, but also represents a key lever through which sustainable corporate practices can be encoded into the DNA of portfolio companies through informed, hands-on involvement or guidance by VC investors. This requires active engagement by the VC investor, but as sustainability start-ups grow and increase their market share, such sustainable corporate practices could have ripple effects on the wider market. However, this requires that investment evaluation criteria and corporate governance practice among a wider set of venture capital funds integrate the credible evaluation of environmental impact. Such evaluations are currently only applied by “green” VC funds, and these represent only a small portion of VC funds globally.

Insurance companies
The investment side of the (re)insurance industry is essentially a form of institutional investor (Table 1). However, we treat insurers as a distinct type of actor because of their role as a risk carrier and manager and because globally the insurance sector is one of the financial actors most exposed to climate- and nature-related risks. This means that, similarly to Universal Owners, they have strong incentives to mitigate systemic risks from climate and environmental changes, and there are two main mechanisms by which they can do that. The first overlaps with that of institutional equity investors who affect economies through their capital allocation and who can engage with the companies they invest in (Table 1). With $40.6 trillion in assets under management globally in 2021, insurers are among the largest institutional investors. Their direct risk exposure could (and arguably should) also position them as strong advocates for data disclosures that allow for reliable analysis of environmental impacts that translate into risks of high relevance to their clients. Second, insurance companies can promote sustainability by restricting insurance provision or raising premiums for unsustainable activities or vice versa, lowering them for sustainable ones (Table 1). The competitive dynamics of the industry currently appear to hamper the latter influence mechanism because systemic risk mitigation is a collective action dilemma. This means that free-rider problems arise as all actors benefit from global risk reduction, disincentivizing individual insurance companies to act.

Development banks
Development banks, such as the World Bank or the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, play a crucial role in driving sustainable change through their capacity to provide funding for sustainable activities and de-risking innovative projects and stimulating private investor participation (Table 1). They can exert authority and enable sustainable corporate practices by setting environmental and social standards that projects must comply with to receive funding. Similar to other investors, they can and do apply criteria when allocating capital, such that projects that do not meet standards receive less or no funding, or at less advantageous rates. However, several barriers impede their effectiveness at promoting sustainability. Limited capacity of banks to assess the relationship between nature and economy often results in investments that do not adequately address environmental challenges. Furthermore, a lack of well-developed green markets presents a significant obstacle, as this translates into limited investment opportunities for development banks. Political instability can also undermine sustainable efforts by creating an unpredictable environment that discourages long-term planning and investment, due to shifts in the priorities or funding available to development banks.

Providers of market infrastructure
The role of stock exchanges in providing transparent, regulated markets and setting standards for disclosure practices leaves them uniquely placed to promote large-scale shifts in corporate practice for sustainability (Table 1). This could happen in at least two ways. One is through listing requirements, which could include sustainability-relevant information that would facilitate investors to factor this information into capital allocation decisions when buying shares. Listing requirements would thus also represent a clearance beyond which companies that do not comply with minimum sustainability criteria do not pass, thus increasing the lowest sustainability level of all companies traded on the exchange. Second, stock exchanges can create and promote sustainability-related indices or bonds that promote the mobilization of capital for sustainable practices. Companies that meet certain sustainability criteria may be eligible for inclusion in such indices or investment products, which can help attract more investment and improve their reputation among investors.
A barrier preventing stock exchanges from taking this more pronounced role in the sustainability shift of the financial sector is their increased corporatization. From being largely not-for-profit organizations (broker clubs), they have become predominantly for-profit corporations, many of them publicly listed companies. This creates a conflict of interest because listed stock exchanges still have regulatory power, and this means they become self-regulatory organizations (SRO) that set rules while simultaneously being subject to those rules. Corporatization thus reduces the legitimacy which traditionally stemmed from the mutual not-for-profit status of stock exchanges, and the idea that they acted in the public interest.

Brokers of information
Data are a fundamental requirement for any investor aiming to make a deliberate decision to promote sustainability through their investments (Table 1). Until recently, ESG raters were the unrivalled providers of such information. However, as discussed in the introduction, they have come under increasing scrutiny and critique. Current measures of environmental performance often fall short for several reasons. Their methodologies tend to be untransparent; and their concepts are measured in inconsistent ways, with data often unverified. perhaps even more important from an environmental science perspective, they often focus narrowly on GHG emissions, at the expense of other equally important environmental dimensions; and they are biased toward corporate environmental impacts directly linked to short-term financial risk and therefore do not capture information that is important to assess absolute environmental impacts (environmentally material information) and inform systemic risk assessment; As a result, ESG ratings are highly divergent, and fail to provide accurate information about the actual environmental impact of firms, rendering them unreliable. Despite these criticisms, ESG rating agencies continue to wield significant influence over how sustainability is defined and enacted in the financial domain.

Providers of information and advice
By centralizing the costs of research and preparation needed to make informed voting decisions and selling the service to thousands of funds (Table 1), proxy advisors have come to gain significant influence over the voting decisions of institutional investors and the governance of publicly traded companies. If this advice were sound and well-informed with regards to sustainability concerns, then this could represent a powerful lever for change. Mainstream voting behavior has, in fact, tended to be very close to the advice promoted by the largest proxy advisors. This is likely the outcome of a market where two actors (ISS and Glass, Lewis & Co.) dominate. Together, they held over 97% of the market share in 2014. This extreme dominance, combined with limited transparency of the process used to develop their proxy voting guidelines, has, however, raised concerns that their advice may not be verifiably sound. Furthermore, advisory firms have no fiduciary duty to anyone and can therefore not be held accountable for any negative results ensuing from the voting recommendations. There is also currently a disconcertingly strong link between proxy advisors and ESG rating agencies, as some ESG raters and proxy advisors form part of the same conglomerate. This means that the same criticized ESG measures noted above are highly likely to make their way into voting recommendations, if sustainability information is at all considered.

Reinforcing virtuous cycles or locking-in unsustainability
From the perspective of a sustainability transition, finance must stop funding negative activities and start funding positive activities. To achieve these goals, investments must aim to mitigate harm and negative impact, but capital must also be allocated to novel business ventures that can “do good” or at least “do better” by offering products and services that help reduce the environmental harm of the mainstream economy, and contribute to net-positive impacts. Both approaches—reducing harm and doing good—are necessary as neither is sufficient in isolation.

Influence mechanisms can be complementary, such as equity investors and creditors demanding similar sustainability disclosures of companies, and thereby also reinforcing the effectiveness of the original action. In systems terminology, this is referred to as a reinforcing feedback, and it represents a virtuous cycle. However, influence mechanisms of actors can prevent or hamper systems change, dampening the effects of initial action and acting as “balancing feedbacks.” An example is providers of ESG information that do not reliably and accurately communicate environmental impacts of companies and therefore misrepresent risks to investors and undermine sustainable investment decisions. Analyzing how the influence mechanisms of financial actors interact reveals opportunities to strengthen virtuous cycles of progress and also identifies how these same mechanisms may inadvertently obstruct a broader sustainability transition (Figure 1).
We identify five potentially virtuous cycles where each actor has the potential to reinforce the respective ambitions of other actors and speed up a sustainable transformation of our economies simply by using their specific influence to pursue improved sustainability in their respective investments (Figure 1).
The first such reinforcing feedback involves public equity investors, venture capital providers, and lenders of capital (Figure 1B). Institutional public equity investors, such as pension funds, own the negative environmental externalities created by the economy because these externalities translate into risks to the economy. Their interest is therefore to reduce such risks, and this ambition often aligns with the public at large. Their main mechanism of influence is practicing active ownership through board engagement to incentivize improved corporate practices to reduce harm, and also filing shareholder resolutions or voting against the board of directors if they hinder the mitigation of systemic risks. Large, listed companies may devote significant resources to R&D to develop new technologies, but history shows that innovative solutions to solve challenges faced by incumbent companies often emerge from smaller entrepreneurs. Start-ups that provide genuinely novel, disruptive and regenerative business models are therefore a critical part of a sustainability transition. These start-ups are often funded by public or private venture capital (VC). However, while they may offer solutions to observed environmental challenges, they are often challenged by their high level of innovativeness and require patient and mission-driven funders and to grow, they need sustainability-minded business “ecosystems” in which their innovative ideas can plug in. Pressure on established companies from both credit providers and equity investors to mitigate environmental impacts therefore creates the necessary markets for goods/services that sustainable start-ups need to scale. Institutional equity investors and creditors, therefore, have the potential to significantly amplify the effect of VC investments and vice versa. Over time, VC, through the companies they help build, also promote novelty and contribute to growing the investible universe of sustainable companies available to both equity and lenders (Figure 1B). There are a number of barriers currently preventing this reinforcing feedback from being set in motion. From the public equity investors' perspective, barriers include the current inability to accurately assess the extent of systemic risks stemming from climate change and nature degradation, and the agency and collective action problems of investment managers, which are exacerbated by the rise of passively managed funds. On the other hand, the VC industry is hindered by a lack of frameworks and data on sustainable investing, as well as a mindset focused primarily on financial performance, partly driven by pressure from limited partners (LPs). Though there seems to be a trend toward increasing the consideration of non-financial impact.
A second feedback potentially reinforcing and amplifying sustainability efforts includes development banks, as opposed to venture capital (Figure 1C). These (most often) public actors fill the important function of partially de-risking investments that may otherwise deter private investors with a lower risk appetite (Table 1). They do so to achieve a set of goals (such as greening our economies) deemed important for society. Development banks, therefore, provide novel investment opportunities for private capital and can catalyze further private investments by demonstrating the viability of green initiatives (Figure 1C). Their de-risking practices help fill the “financing gap” identified by multiple organizations in relation to sustainable development but it is also yet another way to expand the aforementioned investable pool of sustainable projects and companies. Despite this potential, there has been a lot of discussions around the limited evidence of blended finance mechanisms catalyzing private finance investments, with the most common barriers being low leverage ratios (i.e., percentage of private capital mobilized in relation to development banks investment) in low income countries, lack of investable opportunities, high cost of capital, unpredictable policy environments, low risk appetite of development banks and limited data availability and transparency. These would have to be addressed to unlock the power of this feedback.
A third opportunity for a virtuous cycle arises from the combined efforts of capital lenders and insurers, which can facilitate the efforts of institutional equity investors (Figure 1D). Insurers can impose underwriting restrictions or apply raised premiums for environmentally harmful sectors that do not show commitment and progress toward mitigation targets. Similarly, banks can apply concessionary terms linked to sustainability targets and increase the cost of capital for companies that do not begin and progress on a sustainability transition plan (Table 1). They can even refrain from underwriting new issuances. Combined, the rising costs to companies can nudge or even force companies to address unsustainable practices, thus reducing their environmental impact and growing the universe of sustainable and investible assets for equity investors, as noted above. The rising costs of operation outlined are also likely to make it easier for active owners to get buy-in from other shareholders for improved sustainability practices through shareholder resolutions or management dialogue. One barrier to this reinforcing feedback appears to be the collective action problem that individual insurance companies and banks are faced with. If a singular insurer charges higher premiums or restricts underwriting to environmentally harmful companies, the result is that those clients will seek insurance from a different insurer (similar mechanisms would apply to banks charging higher interest or withdrawing liquidity from harmful companies).

For insurers, poor assessments refer to the lack of information about environmental impact, which undermines their ability to accurately assess future liabilities (∗ in panel B).
Limited knowledge and understanding among financial actors of what is actually environmentally sustainable is a fundamental impediment for finance to be a force for sustainability (Figure 2B). It means that despite good intentions, some of the sustainable investment products and practices developed to date will struggle to play a meaningful role in a green transformation, simply because they do not move beyond the firm-level perspective of risk to also include risks to the environment. Green and sustainability-linked debt provides a concrete example. Setting sustainability targets that issuers must deliver on is central to such instruments and evaluation of target achievement is therefore paramount. However, to date, these debt instruments struggle with a lack of a universal definition of what is sustainable, as well as limited post-issuance verification of impact. Scholars increasingly conclude that borrowers appear to enjoy financial benefits regardless of whether sustainability targets are credible or even met. Companies can thus continue with business-as-usual yet enjoy green debt at a discount price, while investors also stand to gain from this approach since it still increases the “sustainable flavor” of their books but with negligible increases in risk. Lack of financial penalties for companies failing to achieve improved sustainability fundamentally undermines the important role of debt as a lever to shift capital from unsustainable to sustainable investments, and exemplifies the current lock-in of unsustainability that has emerged in sustainable finance.
But the lack of definitions of what constitutes sustainable corporate targets and of scientifically anchored environmental performance measures is not limited to the debt sector. This presents a barrier to understanding, evaluating and comparing sustainability outcomes of different capital allocation decisions, and evaluating trade-offs across the entire financial system. A fourth reinforcing feedback loop therefore centers on ESG raters and proxy providers. Currently, ESG raters serve as the primary providers of sustainability-related information, shaping investment decisions and corporate behavior. However, low accountability and unreliable assessments of environmental and social performance create a fundamental barrier to sustainability progress (Figure 2B). Lenders require accurate impact data to assess risk exposure, while insurers depend on it to evaluate environmental risks and liabilities. Without credible data, investors may misallocate capital, undermining sustainability ambitions and resulting in a vicious cycle that inhibits action. Currently, the lack of reliable ESG information hampers these processes, weakening financial sector alignment with sustainability goals (Figure 2B). If, on the other hand, ESG raters supplied accurate information that clearly linked environmental impacts to financial risks, and proxy providers incorporated this information into voting recommendations, investor support for corporate sustainability actions could increase significantly. This, in turn, could drive companies to improve sustainability performance, and if institutional investors acted on more credible environmental performance data, companies would face stronger incentives to improve sustainability practices. A virtuous cycle would thus emerge (Figure 1E).
The fifth potential virtuous cycle promoting sustainability centers on stock exchanges (Figure 1F). Listing requirements can drive widespread reductions in environmental harm by mandating standardized environmental impact disclosures (see Wassénius et al.). Enforcing transparent and verified reporting as a first step (Table 1) would enhance comparability of corporate environmental performance across investment phases, from venture capital to public listing and post-listing operations. Aligning disclosure requirements throughout these phases would not only provide investors with a consistent basis for evaluating sustainability but also ease the transition for start-ups entering mandatory reporting frameworks. Additionally, it would create a more level playing field by giving companies emerging from “green” venture capital investments an advantage, as they would already have environmental impact reporting embedded in their governance structures.
Stock exchanges could further reinforce sustainable investment by establishing specialized secondary boards focused on sustainability, addressing the limited exit options for VC investors. The lack of viable exit pathways currently dampens green VC investments due to perceived high risks by investors. By creating structured market mechanisms that support sustainability-focused firms, stock exchanges could strengthen investor confidence and encourage greater capital allocation toward environmentally responsible businesses. However, this feedback loop is disrupted and weakened by the same barriers affecting ESG information providers, including the increasing corporatization of stock exchanges and the absence of strong incentives to enforce stricter reporting requirements. Overcoming these barriers is critical to unlocking stock exchanges’ potential role in promoting sustainability across financial markets.

Levers for change
Our analysis of financial sector actors’ influence mechanisms reveals that these mechanisms can be divided into three broad levers for effectuating change: funding, exerting authority, and filtering (Figure 2A). Some actors can achieve change by leveraging the need for funding (loans) or services (such as insurance) to achieve some authority to promote changed practices. For example, banks can adjust the cost of capital or could also refrain from underwriting new issuances. Venture capital investors can employ sustainability criteria to guide which ventures to enter into, and insurers can adjust insurance premiums for unsustainable operations (Table 1). Here, “funding” as a lever refers to both providing access to liquidity and affecting firms’ cost of capital.
Other actors, such as public equity investors who have no direct financial influence over companies since they acquire ownership on a secondary market, have to work on exerting authority by engaging with the boards of companies (Table 1). Their primary lever for change, therefore, lies in their ability to influence board decisions or company practice through information, education, and by putting pressure on companies through investor alliances, as noted above (see e.g., Net-Zero Asset Owner Alliance, Climate Action 100+, Finance for Biodiversity). Proxy providers are also providers of advice, and their potential to promote sustainable change is therefore similarly reliant on the extent to which their advice is taken up and acted on (Figure 2A).
A third lever for change lies in the ability to filter by deciding who receives funding, whose stocks to invest in and thus include in an investment fund, and who gets access to markets to trade their shares (Figure 2A). Lenders and underwriters of bonds can control who gains access to capital, while public equity investors and fund managers decide which companies to own or include/exclude in their funds. Stock exchanges have a potentially important but not widely discussed potential to filter, by setting listing requirements and determining who gains access to the marketplace. ESG raters arguably contribute to filtering through the non-financial information they provide about companies, which forms the basis for capital allocation decisions by investors.
The sheer necessity of direct funds and insurance for company operations arguably places actors who provide these in a relatively stronger position of influence vis-à-vis individual companies. However, a classic prisoner’s dilemma can undermine this strong influence. In situations like we are currently witnessing, where ESG considerations are either actively opposed or not widely valued, it can be risky for individual actors to attempt to exert influence, as clients can access capital or marketspaces elsewhere. That is precisely why identifying how financial actors actions and strategies can be mutually supportive and reinforcing is important. The analysis presented in Figure 2 begins to clarify how alignment of purpose and action across multiple seemingly disparate segments of the investment community can be achieved and how this could potentially reinforce virtuous dynamics.
For example, the power of institutional equity investors to drive change may seem low in comparison with providers of direct capital. However, their universal ownership model and their long-term ownership have given them large corporate control, leaving them well-placed to push companies across all sectors to deliver on sustainability. If this is done in concert with similar-minded investors in various “alliances of the willing,” it can support the efforts of creditors and insurers by sending a strong signal that the sustainability demands they place on clients are aligned with a direction supported by a wide coalition of financial actors.

Breaking barriers: the role of regulators and sustainability science in facilitating virtuous cycles
Using the concept of reinforcing feedback loops from systems thinking, we have identified five interrelated feedback loops that emerge when sustainable investment activities mutually reinforce one another. These virtuous cycles have the potential to accelerate the sustainable transformation of our economies. However, their potential is blocked by a variety of barriers that are outside the control of individual financial actors. These barriers stem from entrenched norms and practices within the financial services sector, including short-termism, an excessive focus on price efficiency and shareholder value maximization, a narrow conception of risk, and reliance on models and tools rooted in past performance that are ill-suited to enable actors to navigate the novel risks of our uncertain future.
By mapping out these potential virtuous feedback loops, we provide a framework for financial actors to capitalize on emerging opportunities while actively reducing incentives that sustain vicious cycles. Understanding these feedback dynamics can identify opportunities to remove the barriers currently blocking the emergence of virtuous cycles. Overcoming obstacles such as free-riding and first-mover disadvantages, which discourage individual actors from initiating change, can be achieved through coordinated partnerships that align incentives, regulatory interventions that facilitate collective action, or the identification of strategic niches where early adopters have a competitive advantage, potentially triggering broader systemic shifts. Moreover, by considering multiple feedback loops simultaneously, actors can identify opportunities to connect virtuous cycles in ways that reinforce one another, lowering barriers to change and increasing the resilience of feedback dynamics that align profit-seeking behavior with public welfare and environmental sustainability.
A key barrier to improved sustainability of private investments is the lack of standardized and science-based disclosures on environmental impact and exposure. The dearth of data hinders accurate assessments of contributions and exposures to systemic risks for Universal Owners, banks and insurance companies (Figures 1 and 2B). While a deeper treatment of the role of regulators in addressing these issues is beyond the scope of this article,see there is also an important role for sustainability science to play. This has been much less discussed, and we therefore end by highlighting four important ways in which sustainability science and scientists can contribute to overcoming the barriers that block these potentially virtuous cycles.

Knowledge translation
Finance is currently far from promoting a sustainable transition. Building the capacity to do so may require entirely new approaches to finance, but in the short term it requires bridging sustainability science and financial practice knowledge systems. Concretely this includes developing science-based definitions of what sustainable investments are and providing scientific expertise into the development of corporate sustainability disclosures. Sustainability science can also provide expertise, training, and models to address the sustainability competency gap within finance. It can and should help develop monitoring systems, data, models, and metrics to include environmentally material information in financial decision-making (Figure 2C). Conversely, sustainability science can better incorporate investment dynamics and financial dimensions of development in its empirical research, in models and in education practices. Individuals with competencies in both sustainability and finance are needed to implement change within the financial system. Pathways for sustainability scientists to engage actively with investors exist within coalitions such as the Principles for Responsible Investment, and Finance for Biodiversity. These coalitions provide platforms that enable ongoing, structured, and legitimate engagement between researchers and finance professionals.

Environmental impact assessment
Developing environmental impact assessment techniques grounded in science is essential for evaluating corporate sustainability impacts and associated risks to both investments and society. Parts of the sustainability science community have begun mobilizing by developing scientifically grounded environmental impact metrics and tools that can assist in analyzing company and portfolio impacts, but much more is needed. Examples include improving transparency and scientific anchoring of biodiversity impact tools, including location and company-specific information, but also the development of methodologies to assess the positive impacts of innovative business models.

Leveraging scientific expertise for improved sustainability disclosures
Access to high-quality, information that is both environmentally and financially relevant is essential for informed decision-making by investors but is currently largely lacking. Such information is unlikely to be delivered by current untransparent proprietary ESG ratings based largely on self-assessed data.cf.  Corporate reporting frameworks and standards (e.g., Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TFND), International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI), and European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)), have been developed largely to meet corporate requirements. Scientists should strive to engage in these processes to ensure that disclosure requirements capture environmentally material information, that auditing practices are informed by sustainability science, and that methods to assess impacts (whether by corporates, consultants, or investors) from disclosed data are based on open and transparent models.

Changing norms and perceptions of risk
The financial services sector is shaped by deeply rooted norms and institutions, but even deep-rooted norms can change. As the compounding effects of a changing climate and degraded ecosystems are materializing into financial risks, understanding the full breadth of such risks and how to mitigate them is becoming obvious to increasing numbers of financial scholars and professionals. Sustainability science draws on decades of research into the social, environmental and connected social-ecological impacts of anthropogenic pressures that could and should inform the expanding conception of risk in the financial domain. Sustainability researchers could promote this by developing methodologies to help corporations, banks, and insurance companies better assess their asset-level exposure to nature-related risks, in ways that go beyond current tools that rely too heavily on industry and country-level averages. Sustainability science can also play a role in supporting decisions on how to best mitigate those risks and build resilience across a portfolio of assets.
By addressing these three interrelated issues, sustainability science can leverage the expertise of both sustainability scientists and financial professionals to promote the development of environmentally sustainable economies.

Methods
Our focal actors are identified and selected based on their connection to financial markets and their role in facilitating private investment flows, providing market infrastructure, and influencing sustainable investment decisions. Financial markets play a fundamental role in channeling funds from actors with surplus capital to those in need of additional financing. This capital flow occurs through direct finance—where borrowers issue securities such as bonds or stocks—or indirect finance, in which financial intermediaries facilitate transactions between savers and borrowers. These intermediaries help allocate resources efficiently within the economy. Key financial intermediaries are institutional investors in public equity, banks as lenders and underwriters of bonds, venture capital providers (an important form of private equity investor), and insurance companies.
Financial markets cannot function without essential supporting services that provide infrastructure and information necessary for investment decisions. Stock exchanges provide the marketplace infrastructure for the trade of stocks and bonds. Furthermore, investors with sustainability ambitions require non-financial information about the companies and projects they plan to invest in. Brokers of information are therefore critical actors in sustainable finance, supplying investors with environmental, social, and governance (ESG) metrics, but also advice to shareholders—particularly institutional investors—on voting at shareholder meetings and facilitating the vote execution process. Given their influence on investment decision-making, we include these providers of information and advice in our analysis.
We do not include hedge funds as a separate actor due to their highly heterogeneous strategies, which make it difficult to generalize any impact they have. Hedge funds trade primarily on secondary markets, and except for the trade in distressed debt, their mechanisms of influence largely overlap with those of public equity investors. For example, activist hedge funds that seek to exert influence over companies typically do so by building coalitions with other shareholders, as seen in the case of Engine No. 1. We also do not include regulators. While regulators shape the financial system as a whole, our analysis focuses on how the investment practices of private financial actors influence the actions and outcomes of other investors. Central banks are thus excluded since their primary mandate is to maintain monetary and financial stability. However, we make one exception: development banks. These banks, whether state- or privately owned, have a mandate to promote sustainable development by financing infrastructure projects and supporting small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Their role in providing funding to companies closely mirrors that of private institutional investors, justifying their inclusion in this study.
There is no consistent definition of a scoping review, but they are generally conducted to meet various objectives, including mapping the extent and variety of evidence on a topic, but also the nature (characteristics) of the evidence on a topic, and to summarize findings from a body of knowledge that is heterogeneous in methods or discipline; or identify gaps in the literature to aid planning and commissioning of future research. We follow the growing number of studies that use it as a way of mapping evidence and key concepts from several fields and bring these together. The PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) protocol was developed for health sciences, and we have therefore adjusted it to fit our purpose (see supplemental information).

Limitations of study
The article is not intended as a guide to transform the financial system, nor a political economy analysis of the existing system. A rapid shift in how private capital is allocated is urgently needed to support a sustainability transformation of our economies. But transformations do not happen overnight. They happen as a result of many different changes that, at some critical juncture, “tip a system” into a new domain. This review should therefore not be seen as a roadmap to transforming the financial system, rather it offers a means to identify and make explicit the interconnectedness of financial sector actors, and provides an analysis of how, within the confines of existing investment mandates, even relatively small shifts in financial sector practices can nonetheless dock into changing practices of other actors to generate virtuous dynamics.
We recognize that regulators influence all investors. However, since our aim is to analyze how investment practices of private financial actors influence the actions and outcomes of other such investors, we limit the focus of our analysis to private companies with the exception of development banks as explained above. This means that we also do not include central banks, as their primary mandate is to maintain monetary and financial stability, which are macroeconomic goals.
