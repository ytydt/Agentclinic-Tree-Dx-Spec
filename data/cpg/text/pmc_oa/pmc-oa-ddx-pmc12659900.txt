A patent is a form of intellectual property protection granted by a government to an inventor for a limited period, usually 20 years from the date of filing. The purpose of a patent is to give the inventor exclusive rights to prevent others from using, selling, or manufacturing their invention without their permission. In exchange for this protection, the inventor must publicly disclose their invention so that others can learn from it and build upon it.
Obtaining a patent can be a complex process, but it provides the inventor with a few benefits, including:
Exclusive rights to their invention, allowing them to prevent others from using or selling it without their permission
Recognition and validation of their invention can lead to increased credibility and prestige
The ability to license or sell their invention for financial benefit
The opportunity to stop others from infringing on their rights through legal action
The patent process typically involves conducting a prior art search, preparing and filing a patent application, cooperating with the patent examiner, and paying maintenance fees to keep the patent in force.

HISTORY OF PATENTS
The concept of patents has a long history, dating back to ancient civilizations such as ancient Greece and Rome, where monopolies were granted for certain goods or services. The modern system of patents, as we know it today, however, has its roots in medieval England. In the 16th century, the English government began granting letters of a patent to inventors, allowing them to monopolize the production of their inventions for a limited period. This system was later adopted in the United States, with the passage of the first federal patent law in 1790. Since then, the patent system has evolved and expanded, with new laws and international treaties being established to regulate and protect patents on a global scale. Today, patents play a critical role in the protection of intellectual property and the promotion of innovation in a wide range of industries, including dentistry. By providing inventors exclusive rights to their creations, patents incentivize investment in research and development and encourage the creation of new products and technologies that can improve people’s lives. HOW LONG HAVE YOU BEEN PROTECTED AFTER THE PATENT?
The time a patent is protected depends on several factors, including the jurisdiction, in which the patent was granted and the type of invention covered by the patent. In the United States, for example, the length of protection for a utility patent (which covers most inventions) is generally 20 years from the date of filing, while the length of protection for a design patent (which covers ornamental designs for an article of manufacture) is 15 years from the date of grant. It is important to note that the length of protection for a patent can be affected by various factors, such as changes in the law, the filing of continuation or divisional applications, and the payment of maintenance fees. In addition, the effective duration of patent protection may be reduced by challenges from third parties, such as invalidation proceedings or compulsory licensing actions. To ensure that a patent remains in force, it is important for patent owners to stay informed about changes in the law and to take all necessary steps to maintain their patent rights.

FILING A PATENT APPLICATION BEFORE DISCLOSING THE INVENTION IN DENTISTRY
In dentistry, as in many other fields, it is advisable to file a patent application before disclosing the invention to the public. This is because once an invention is publicly disclosed, the inventor may be prevented from obtaining a patent in some countries. This is because of the “first-to-file” principle, under which the first person to file a patent application for an invention has the priority to obtain a patent. It is important to note that filing a patent application before disclosing the invention does not guarantee that a patent will be granted. The patent application must meet the requirements of the patent office and must show that the invention is new, nonobvious, and useful. In addition, the inventor should consider whether they need to file a provisional or nonprovisional patent application, depending on their specific needs and the requirements of the patent office. In any case, it is recommended to consult with a patent attorney or agent before disclosing the invention or filing a patent application, to ensure that all legal and procedural requirements are met and that the inventor’s rights are protected.
