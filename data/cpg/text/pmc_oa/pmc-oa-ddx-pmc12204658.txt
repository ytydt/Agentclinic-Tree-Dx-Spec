Diagnostic microbiology for intraocular infections: A comprehensive approach
Ocular infections, particularly intraocular infections, face challenges due to low sample volumes and their paucibacillary nature (low pathogen load) compared to other anatomical sites. This often leads to culture-negative cases, complicating the identification of the causative pathogen. Intraocular sample collection is mainly invasive and difficult to repeat. Ocular microbiology diagnostics require specialized expertise developed over years of experience, along with effective communication between clinicians and microbiologists, to ensure accurate pathogen diagnosis. This review offers an overview of ocular microbiology practices employing both conventional and molecular techniques for pathogen identification, including unusual pathogens primarily affecting the eye, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, result interpretation, emerging diagnostic techniques, challenges in intraocular sample processing, troubleshooting strategies, serological approaches, and basic operating room sterility assessments in eye care institutions. The review aims to assist both ophthalmologists and microbiologists in their daily practice of ocular microbiology diagnostics.

Introduction
The timely identification of causative pathogens in infectious endophthalmitis, retinitis, or uveitis is essential for the early initiation of appropriate therapy and for preventing irreversible and severe complications, such as blindness or loss of the globe. Ocular microbiology, in general, presents numerous challenges, particularly for intraocular samples. This distinguishes it from microbiological diagnostics for other parts of the body. Some of the common challenges in ocular microbiology include limited sample volume; a lower pathogen load compared to other systemic infections, which can lead to occasional culture negativity even with strong clinical suspicion; pathogens that are specific to ocular infections, which require particular culture conditions and rapid transport; and difficulties in collection, especially from intraocular compartments, making them irreplaceable. Moreover, prior antimicrobial use (such as empirical antibacterial and antifungal eye drops readily available on the market) before patients present to clinics equipped for ocular microbiology complicates pathogen culture efforts. Additionally, there is often a lack of proper training for ophthalmologists regarding sample collection, bedside or operating room (OR) inoculation for culture, and transport methods to yield effective microbiology results.
While sampling and diagnosing ocular surface infections (such as microbial keratitis, blepharitis, conjunctivitis, and lid abscess) is somewhat easier, microbiological diagnostics of intraocular samples (like those from infectious endophthalmitis, retinitis, uveitis, etc.) pose more challenges due to the paucibacillary nature of these samples. Additionally, the difficulty of sample collection is often invasive, making it frequently unrepeatable, and they are obtained in tiny aliquots that complicate the application of various conventional and molecular microbiological techniques.
This manuscript addresses the challenges of microbiology diagnostics for intraocular samples, particularly those associated with uveitis and related infectious conditions such as endophthalmitis and retinitis. We present a protocol for the collection, transport, inoculation, and processing of samples linked to these and similar ocular infections. Additionally, we offer practical tips for ocular microbiology diagnostics.

Types of Samples
The intraocular samples used to rule out or diagnose intraocular infections include vitreous biopsies (diluted and undiluted), anterior chamber (AC) tap/exudate/wash, intraocular lens (IOL), lens matter and exudates, iris tissue, retinal biopsies, eviscerated contents, enucleated eyes, and optic nerves. If a patient has an ocular infection that has spread from the surface of the orbit, additional samples include corneal scraping/biopsy, conjunctival swab, scleral scraping, sutures, and foreign bodies (FBs, such as vegetative matter, iron/stone particles, etc.), scleral buckle, and any drained abscess pus. The undiluted vitreous provides a better sample than the diluted one, which may be cytocentrifuged, with the sediment collected and processed for diagnostics. Any parasites collected from intraocular compartments (such as microfilaria) can be placed in normal saline in a sterile glass vial and sent to microbiology for processing, or they can be directly placed on a sterile slide with a drop of normal saline, covered with a cover slip, and examined under a microscope.

Collection of Samples and Direct Inoculation at Bedside/Operating Room
AC taps, washes, exudates, and vitreous biopsies are collected in sterile syringes and sealed with silicone caps after removing the needle to prevent contamination and maintain anaerobiosis if any anaerobic pathogens are suspected. Samples such as iris tissue, retinal biopsies, IOLs, lens material, exudates, FBs, scleral buckles, and samples from the external part of the eye are directly inoculated in the OR into solid media, such as blood agar (BA) or chocolate agar (CA), due to the very low sample quantity. These samples are subsequently transported to the laboratory. The sample quantity is typically very low, making transportation challenging; they risk being lost, especially FBs, if not inoculated and sent promptly. Prior to sample collection, the laboratory should be notified for sending all necessary sterile media in clean collection boxes, which must be transported back to the laboratory as quickly as possible after sample collection and inoculation. Having an in-house microbiology diagnostics facility significantly reduces transportation time (preferably within 2 h postcollection), thereby enhancing the likelihood of effectively isolating the causative pathogens and reducing the chances of contamination of the inoculated media during transport. If samples take longer than this to arrive at the laboratory, they should be placed in transport media (such as viral transport media) and sent or kept refrigerated at 4°C until they can be delivered to the laboratory (within 2–4 h). Ambient room temperature (18°C–40°C) during transport is ideal, as excessively high or low temperatures can harm the survival of pathogens (most aerobic pathogens, bacteria, or fungi thrive well within this temperature range).
In cases of suspected endogenous endophthalmitis, the source of systemic infection should be investigated through at least a urine analysis and culture, along with a blood culture. An early morning urine sample or unpassed urine collected in the urinary bladder for at least 6–8 h is ideal for culture. Midstream urine should be collected in a sterile container, avoiding contamination from surrounding skin flora, and should be submitted to the laboratory immediately. For blood cultures, a minimum of 10–15 ml of blood should be collected while ensuring utmost asepsis – cleaning the skin over the vein thoroughly with 70% ethyl alcohol and allowing it to dry before collection – into a 50 ml biphasic blood culture bottle. For neonates (1.5–2 ml) and pediatric patients (5–10 ml), blood should be collected and inoculated into pediatric blood culture bottles (20 ml volume).
If any unusual pathogens or pathogens requiring special culture conditions (beyond what is available for most commonly isolated pathogens) are suspected, the laboratory should be informed beforehand. Communicating with the microbiologist about this will help in processing such samples more effectively. The laboratory should also be consulted about the most appropriate artificial media to inoculate in case the collected sample was significantly less. However, we recommend bedside inoculation of one CA (or BA if CA is unavailable) and one liquid medium along with a direct smear (preferably for Gram stain) as the minimal requirement for low-volume samples to provide a proper report based on “significant pathogen” criteria (details in the next section). Additional samples, such as corneal scrapings, are collected using a scalpel blade or spatula. A scalpel blade is a better choice than cotton swabs when applicable, as it does not retain any samples during inoculation. In contrast, much of the valuable and minuscule sample gets lost by being absorbed into the fibrils of swabs, and some components of cotton swabs, such as fatty acids or glue, may inhibit microbial growth. To summarize: The eye, although a small organ, provides a variety of samples, the microbiological processing of which differs to certain extent from that of other human systems. The environment of the eye is also distinct from other body sites; therefore, the collection, inoculation, and transportation of ocular samples, as well as the interpretation of microbiology results, require special handling.

Sample Collection for Molecular Techniques
If molecular testing is needed, such as nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) such as conventional or real-time polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) or sequencing, at least 200 μl of the liquid sample (e.g., vitreous biopsy) should be collected in a microcentrifuge tube (MCT) and sent. For other samples (e.g., corneal scraping), the scalpel blade with the scraping should be swirled in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) in an MCT. Additionally, any tissue pieces (e.g., biopsy of orbital mass) should be sent directly in PBS in an MCT for such molecular testing.

Choice of Media for Inoculation of Samples from Intraocular Infections
Intraocular samples, such as vitreous biopsy, are liquid and often paucibacillary. Therefore, their detection requires enrichment and optimal growth conditions to ensure that the pathogens multiply rapidly and are easily detectable. Nonselective solid media, such as BA and CA, support the growth of most culturable microbes, including bacterial and fungal pathogens (both molds and yeasts), while selective media, such as Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) or potato dextrose agar (PDA), facilitate fungal growth exclusively.
Liquid media such as thioglycolate broth (TGB), brain–heart infusion (BHI) broth, and Robertson’s cooked meat media (RCM) facilitate the robust growth of pathogens that may be present in very low numbers in intraocular liquid samples. Additionally, RCM provides enhanced anaerobiosis for vitreous biopsies to support the growth of anaerobic pathogens like Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes. TGB also promotes anaerobic growth in its lower section, while the upper part of the media in the tube supports aerobic pathogen growth.
Detecting the growth of pathogens in inoculated media as early as possible aids in targeted therapy and improves clinical outcomes in conditions such as infectious endophthalmitis. The BACTEC system (bioMerieux, France), which is commonly utilized for early pathogen detection in blood cultures, also assists in endophthalmitis. The pediatric blood culture bottles (20 ml base media) can be inoculated with up to 500 μl of the vitreous biopsy and incubated in BACTEC. Meanwhile, the remaining sample can be used for smear, inoculation of other media, and PCR. The scanner in BACTEC examines the media every 15 min for even the slightest signs of growth, which can be detected as early as 6 h and then subcultured onto solid media with microscopy of smears to identify the pathogen. Newer techniques, such as using a mucus extractor during pars plana vitrectomy for vitreous biopsies, may enhance the yield of pathogens from such low-load samples. Deep vitreous biopsy taken under air has also demonstrated higher culture positivity rates compared to conventional anterior vitreous biopsy in endogenous endophthalmitis cases.
To summarize, a variety of nonselective and selective artificial solid and liquid media are available in the ocular microbiology laboratory for processing different ocular samples. Some of these media are better suited for isolating specific pathogens than others. Therefore, prior communication with the laboratory about the clinical suspicion of such pathogens aids in selecting the most appropriate media, particularly when the sample volume is low. Paucibacillary samples can first be incubated in liquid media within systems like BACTEC, which are highly sensitive to detecting minimal growth in culture. The liquid media promote abundant growth of low-load pathogens and dilute any antimicrobial agents previously administered in the sample. New techniques, such as using a mucus extractor or performing deep vitreous biopsy under air, may enhance culture positivity rates for low-load samples like vitreous.

Routine Processing of Samples

Conventional microbiological methods
As already mentioned in the above sections, the intraocular samples are mostly collected in very low volume, and often, the pathogen load is very low. The samples from the ocular surface or orbit are relatively more in volume or pathogen load (though all ocular samples are low volume when compared to most other sites of infection of the human body). Diluted vitreous biopsy (as collected from cassette) must be centrifuged, sediment collected, and then processed for microbiology for any yield of pathogens. Hence, undiluted vitreous is a better choice. The following is practiced in most ocular microbiology diagnostic laboratories for the processing of ocular samples:

Direct smears and microscopy
Direct smears are prepared from the samples or the sediment of cytocentrifuged diluted samples and stained with Gram stain and 1% calcofluor white (CFW) fluorescent stain for bright field microscopy and fluorescence microscopy, respectively [Figure 2a]. Common pathogens observed in microscopy of direct smears from vitreous biopsy, stained with various methods (CFW, Gram, Giemsa, and Ziehl–Neelsen [ZN] or modified ZN stains), reveal different bacteria (Gram-positive cocci, Gram-negative bacilli, Nocardia, and Mycobacterium spp.) and fungi (molds and yeasts), as shown in Figure 2b (i-xii).

Conventional culture
For intraocular liquid samples, both solid and liquid media are inoculated, including nonselective BA and CA (which support the growth of most culturable aerobic pathogens) as well as selective fungal media such as SDA/PDA. Liquid media such as TGB, BHI broth, and Robertson’s cooked meat media are also used, and the same applies to other samples like corneal scrapings. All inoculated media are incubated aerobically at 37°C, preferably in a carbon dioxide incubator, except for SDA/PDA, which are incubated at room temperature (26°C–28°C) in a biological oxygen demand incubator with added humidity. For anaerobic culture, a separate set of Brucella-BA plates must be inoculated with the sample and incubated anaerobically in a Genbag [Figure 3a] or McIntosh-Fildes jar. After overnight incubation, all inoculated media are checked daily for any significant pathogen growth. Any such “significant growth” is then processed for identification through biochemical tests and confirmed by automated systems such as the Vitek2 compact system or matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time of flight. The BACTEC culture system [Figure 1] (commonly used for blood cultures) aids in the rapid detection of pathogens in liquid samples such as vitreous fluid, facilitating early diagnosis, as discussed in detail above.

Incubation period
All inoculated media should be incubated for at least 7 days postinoculation before being reported as culture negative and discarded. If a fungus is clinically suspected or observed on smears, the inoculated media should be incubated for a minimum of 14 days before being reported as culture negative and discarded. This extended incubation is necessary because fungi are slow-growing pathogens compared to rapidly growing bacteria. In cases of clinical suspicion of pathogens that may be overlooked due to their very thin morphology on direct smears, such as Nocardia spp. or Mycobacterium spp., it is essential to inform the laboratory of such suspicion. This allows for special stains, such as ZN acid-fast staining (with a 20% sulfuric acid decolorizer for Mycobacterium spp.) and modified ZN staining (with a 1% sulfuric acid decolorizer for the weakly acid-fast Nocardia spp.), to be performed to avoid missing these pathogens. Anaerobic culture plates, sealed in an anaerobic environment (e.g., Genbag), can be examined after 7 days of incubation and discarded as culture negative if no growth is observed until that time.
Few tips
In cases of suspected endogenous endophthalmitis, the blood and urine samples from patients should also be sent for culture, in addition to the ocular samples, to detect any systemic infections and initiate early treatment. If the vitreous biopsy appears thick like pus, the pathogen may become trapped in the polymorphonuclear leukocytes and may not grow even if present in sufficient quantities. Intraocular fluid samples, such as vitreous, are paucibacillary and must be enriched before the pathogen can grow on solid media such as BA/CA/SDA/PDA. Liquid media such as RCM with meat particles provide additional nutrition for aerobic pathogens and create anaerobic conditions for anaerobic pathogens. All liquid media facilitate robust growth, even from a few pathogen cells present in the samples, resulting in good growth on solid media during subculture for subsequent processing. On the downside, liquid media are easily contaminated, and there is no way to determine if the turbidity of the liquid media is due to a “significant pathogen” or merely a contaminant unless the criteria for “significant growth” of pathogens are met, as outlined below.
To summarize: Conventional microbiology for ocular samples, which involves standard methods such as microscopy of direct smears and culture in artificial media, accounts for the special culture conditions and media requirements for difficult-to-isolate pathogens. This approach is a universal method for detecting causative pathogens. For other unusual, nonculturable, or difficult-to-culture pathogens, molecular techniques, as discussed below, may be employed.
Criteria for defining “significant growth” of pathogens in ocular samples help differentiate whether a growth on an inoculated medium is a true pathogen that requires further processing or a contaminant.
The criteria for “significant growth” of pathogens, as mentioned above, for vitreous biopsy are as follows:
Bacteria are observed on direct smear and cultivated in at least one solid medium at the inoculation site or one liquid media
The same pathogen was grown on two or more solid media at the inoculation site, or on one solid and one liquid medium, even if it was not observed in direct smears
Confluent pathogen growth on solid media at the inoculation site
Two or more fungal colonies/yeasts are at the inoculation site on solid media, even if not seen on direct smears.
The examples of significant pathogens observed through direct smear microscopy and grown on culture from intraocular samples are shown in Figures 2b (i-xii) and 3b-l.
If there is any evidence of fungus or yeast in vitreous biopsies (whether from direct smear or positive culture), the laboratory sends a critical alert to the clinician to take appropriate action (e.g., to discontinue intravitreal corticosteroids and administer intravitreal antifungals immediately).
The criteria for “significant growth” of pathogens from samples, such as corneal scraping, are as follows (Jones’s criteria for significant growth):
Bacteria are observed on direct smears and cultured on at least one solid medium at the inoculation site or one liquid medium
Growth on two or more solid media at the inoculation site or on a solid and a liquid media, even if it is not visible on a smear
Confluent growth (>10 colonies) was observed at the inoculation site on solid media
Repeated growth of the same pathogen from the same infection site upon retesting when none of the first three criteria apply.
To summarize, the criteria for “significant growth” of pathogens aids in the better interpretation of results in ocular microbiology by distinguishing between commensals and contaminants that do not require unnecessary treatment and actual pathogens that do require treatment.

Fungal pathogens in vitreous biopsy and other intraocular samples
Fungal filaments in the vitreous often clump together and settle in one location. Consequently, they can be overlooked during microscopy of direct smears and may not grow in culture if the inoculated vitreous lacks that clump. Any detection of fungal filaments or yeasts on direct smears of the vitreous, or any fungal growth in culture, must be urgently communicated to the concerned clinician to halt steroid administration and initiate intravitreal antifungal treatment. If the microbiology laboratory is equipped, in vitro antifungal susceptibility testing using standard methods would assist in selecting the most appropriate antifungal for the patient. This is especially important as resistance to both azoles and polyenes is increasing in Asian countries. Voriconazole and amphotericin B are two of the most commonly used intravitreal antifungals. To summarize: Fungal ocular infections can be difficult to diagnose and treat. With the gradual rise of fungal infections worldwide and increasing antifungal resistance, maintaining a strong clinical suspicion and being vigilant during microbiology processing, while considering the aforementioned points in diagnostics, can aid in accurately diagnosing and managing fungal ocular infections in a timely manner or in ruling out fungal infections. This approach helps to avoid the unnecessary use of costly antifungals and reduces the exposure of commensal flora to antifungals.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing, which includes antibacterial and antifungal susceptibility testing
If laboratory facilities are available and the pathogen can be isolated from the samples, in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) serves as an effective method to predict whether the causative pathogen is susceptible to the empirically administered antimicrobials or resistant. This indicates that treatment may need to be adjusted to include susceptible antimicrobials or additional therapy may be required.
The various methods for conducting AST in the laboratory adhere to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute or the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing guidelines. The basic concept is to evaluate whether a standard suspension of the pathogen (obtained from the pathogen’s growth in ocular sample cultures) is inhibited by a specific amount of the drug. Accordingly, cutoff or breakpoint standard charts are available (e.g. for the minimum inhibitory concentration [MIC] of various antimicrobials) to which our results are compared, allowing us to predict if a pathogen is susceptible or resistant to a specific drug and whether it will be effective if administered.
In brief, the various methods of AST are as follows:
Qualitative methods, such as the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method, can only predict whether a pathogen is susceptible or resistant to a specific antimicrobial
Quantitative methods, such as the E-strip test and the broth microdilution method, enable us to predict the precise MIC of the antimicrobials that can kill our pathogens, and thereby determine whether they are susceptible or resistant to the tested antimicrobials. Discrepancy between Clinical Outcomes and In vitro Antimicrobial Susceptibility Results
The in vitro AST for ocular isolates has several disadvantages, as outlined.
The cutoffs for MICs are based on serum values, and none exist for ocular tissues or fluids separately. The eye is a unique and small organ, and the serum MIC values noted for the various AST methods may not always be reflected in the eye. However, establishing separate ocular standards would require large-scale worldwide studies for standardization, and there may also be geographical variations
Topical or intraocular antibiotics used in the eye are fortified to several times their required concentration in the eye. These medications rely on their concentration in the serum to effectively eliminate these microbes in blood or serum, whereas the eye is a smaller organ with a significantly lower volume
There is a lack of adequate pharmacodynamic-pharmacokinetic studies for systemic antibiotics used in ocular infections; therefore, the safety profile for some of these drugs in the eye is not well understood
Nevertheless, AST testing for ocular pathogens can identify the most appropriate antibiotics in urgent situations like ocular infections.
To summarize: In vitro AST serves as a guide for predicting how the pathogen may behave in the eye and respond to treatment, determining whether there is a need to alter the currently prescribed antimicrobials or introduce new drugs into the regimen. There may be a discrepancy between the in vitro susceptibility results and the actual clinical response to therapy observed in the eye, and these issues have been discussed in detail above. However, the availability of an AST report for the isolated pathogen aids the ophthalmologist in making a better-informed decision for the patient.

Processing for Intraocular Parasites
If there is a clinical suspicion of intraocular parasites, the cut rate during vitrectomy should be kept low to preserve the anatomy of the parasite (e.g. the mouth and tail parts of a filarial nematode, which are helpful for identification). Most parasites are nonculturable (except for the protozoa Acanthamoeba spp. and Leishmania spp.), so identification relies on direct microscopy of the parasite and molecular methods such as sequencing and PCR for select parasites with available specific primers for target genes. The vitreous biopsy should be sent for centrifugation, and the pellet should be used for microscopy or molecular techniques to avoid missing any part of the parasite. If the parasite is collected whole from the vitreous, it should be immersed in a small amount of sterile normal saline in a sterile glass vial and sent for microbiology to prevent drying out, which can distort its morphology. Some of the parasites that infect the eye (inner coats and orbit) include species of Gnathostoma, Toxoplasma, Toxocara, Acanthamoeba, Onchocerca, Leishmania, Plasmodium, Taenia solium (cysticercus cellulose), and Echinococcus (hydatid cyst). Occasionally, fixing the smear and staining it with hematoxylin and eosin stain in histopathology may reveal the cut cross-sections of the parasite.

Common Molecular Methods in Routine Microbiology of Samples

Nucleic acid amplification tests
Nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT), such as PCR, can help identify various pathogens (bacteria, fungi, viruses, or parasites) when they do not grow in culture. All types of PCR, including conventional, nested, multiplex, or real time (kit-based for Toxoplasma gondii), can be employed for this purpose. The DNA of the pathogen is found in the vitreous, which can be extracted and amplified using specific primers for detection via gel electrophoresis, as done in conventional PCR (e.g. conventional PCR for bacteria or fungi using eubacterial 16S rRNA or panfungal internal transcribed spacer conserved region primers; specific primers for herpes simplex virus-1, Epstein–Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, or varicella-zoster virus; 193 base pair of the B1 gene for detecting T. gondii; MBP64 gene for Mycobacterium tuberculosis; specific primers for Treponema pallidum or Propionibacterium acnes, etc.) [Table 3].

While conventional PCR can indicate whether a patient is infected with a specific pathogen (qualitative), real-time PCR can determine the actual load of the pathogen present at the infection site (quantitative), thus providing prognostic guidelines for therapy as well.
NAATs are particularly useful for viral infections, where culturing viruses in cell lines or animal models poses significant challenges, and for parasitic infections, as most parasites (excluding a few protozoa such as Acanthamoeba or Leishmania spp.) are nonculturable. PCR methods can be used for both DNA and RNA viruses. For RNA viruses, the RNA is first converted into complementary DNA using the reverse transcriptase enzyme before proceeding with PCR.
In brief, the principle of PCR is as follows: the double-stranded DNA of the pathogen (if present in the sample) is denatured at high temperatures into single-stranded DNA. The specific primers in the PCR master mix, which contain complementary sequences of the target gene from the pathogen, then bind to the target gene sequence of this DNA. From there, the synthesis of the complementary strand is completed with the help of the Taq polymerase enzyme and deoxynucleotides present in the master mix to synthesize a complete double-stranded DNA of the pathogen. This process results in exponential amplification of the pathogen DNA, which is tagged with SYBR green-fluorescent dye in the master mix and can be detected through gel electrophoresis under ultraviolet light.
The pathogen band on the gel is matched with the positive control band and the ladder to confirm the presence of the pathogen in the patient’s sample. Multiplex strip polymerase chain reaction test
Easy-to-use multiplex strip PCR tests can detect up to 24 common ocular pathogens, including viruses and hard-to-culture bacteria like the spirochete T. pallidum. These tests require a smaller sample volume, offer rapid diagnosis, and provide results comparable to conventional real-time PCR.

Sequencing techniques
Shotgun metagenomic sequencing or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of intraocular samples, such as vitreous biopsies, may help identify all microbes present in the sample, including parasites, if cultures are negative. Some studies have shown that sequencing techniques like metagenomic next-generation sequencing (NGS) have very high sensitivity (detecting all positives, but also some false positives) and a high negative predictive value (accurately predicting true negatives) in diagnosing causative pathogens of infectious uveitis, including various common bacteria, fungi, viruses, and the less common Bartonella spp. and T. gondii, compared to routine diagnostic tests.
The disadvantage of the aforementioned molecular techniques is that any contaminating microbial DNA may also be amplified and detected through PCR and sequencing. This can lead to confusion regarding the significance of such a finding and whether treatment is necessary, particularly when conventional diagnostics fail to identify such a microbe. Additionally, it may necessitate repeat sample collection and testing for confirmation and must be correlated with clinical findings. Moreover, the presence of live pathogens at the infection site cannot be predicted by molecular tests based on nucleic acid extraction, as microbial nucleic acids may be present even if the pathogen is dead. A robust bioinformatics pipeline and efficient bioinformatics analysis support are needed to fully leverage sequencing techniques in diagnostic laboratories in the future.
To summarize, molecular techniques such as PCR and sequencing are valuable for diagnosing pathogens in ocular infections that conventional microbiology often fails to detect. This failure can occur for reasons including low pathogen load, small sample volume, or the presence of difficult-to-culture or nonculturable pathogens, as well as various viruses. A significant barrier to the widespread adoption of these molecular techniques is their high cost, limited availability at healthcare facilities, and the challenges associated with standardization for different sample types. However, the rapid progress in ongoing research in these areas may soon address these challenges, bringing these techniques into the mainstream of routine diagnostics.

Serology Practices in Ocular Microbiology
Serology plays a crucial role in diagnosing both viral and parasitic infections that lead to uveitis or retinitis, among others. Ocular samples from these patients often yield negative results when assessed with conventional microbiological diagnostics. By detecting virus-specific or parasite-specific antigens or antibodies in serum or ocular fluids (e.g. IgM for acute infections and IgG for chronic infections), we can indirectly confirm such ocular diseases in patients. This diagnosis is mainly achieved through enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Some of the commonly utilized serology diagnostic tests for ocular diseases include the following:
ELISA in serum: detection of antigens or antibodies through ELISA for syphilis, tuberculosis (TB; cell-mediated immune response), toxoplasmosis (primarily via clinical diagnosis, confirmed by specific IgM/IgG detection), and other infections where conventional microbiology often yields negative results (Bartonella, Lyme disease, rickettsiosis, and leptospirosis)
ELISA in ocular fluids and serum: The Goldmann–Witmer coefficient, as calculated in cases of acute retinal necrosis and intraocular parasitic infections such as toxoplasmosis. The GWC is the ratio of Toxoplasma-specific IgG antibodies found in the ocular fluid (e.g. AC) compared to those in the serum. A GWC of 2 or more indicates the production of intraocular Toxoplasma-specific IgG antibodies
Immunoblotting techniques, such as Western blot, detect specific antibodies for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), or human T-cell lymphotropic virus (HTLV) as a confirmatory test following the rapid card-based Tridot test for HIV
Lateral flow assays, such as rapid tests for detecting HIV, HCV, or hepatitis B, should be used as a screening procedure before any ocular surgery, which can then be confirmed by ELISA or immunoblotting tests
Immunofluorescent assays (IFAs), such as kit-based IFAs, are used for the detection of herpes simplex virus type 1 antigen in corneal scrapings or intraocular fluids, like AC taps [Figure 5b (i and ii)]
The interferon-gamma (IFN-Y) release assay-6, including Quantiferon Gold In-tube and T-SPOT TB tests for ocular TB, involves stimulating patient lymphocytes in serum with M. tuberculosis antigen in vitro. This stimulation leads to the release of IFN-Y in patients with TB, which can be quantified and compared against a negative (NIL) sample for TB diagnosis. It is rapid and highly sensitive, and it is also not affected by previous Bacillus Calmette–Guérin vaccination or the presence of atypical mycobacteria; however, it does not differentiate between latent and active diseases. Combining this assay with the tuberculin skin test may improve the detection rate of ocular TB
Tests for excluding noninfectious uveitis caused by collagen vascular diseases: for instance, the antinuclear antibody test to rule out systemic lupus erythematosus or juvenile idiopathic arthritis [Figure 5b (iii and iv)]; antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) tests to exclude Wegener’s granulomatosis (C-ANCA) and polyarteritis nodosa (P-ANCA); rheumatoid factor to rule out rheumatoid arthritis or human leukocyte antigen B27 to exclude ankylosing spondylitis
Weil–Felix test: This test utilizes the fact that certain antigens of the bacteria Proteus spp. have cross-reactivity with the antigens of Rickettsia, such as OXK, OX 2, and OX 19, which cross-react with the antibodies in the serum of a rickettsiosis patient. The antibody titers are thus measured (positive titer: >1:80–1:160), aiding in the diagnosis
Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) test and Treponema pallidum hemagglutination assay (TPHA) for ocular syphilis: VDRL is a nontreponemal test used as a screening tool for syphilis by measuring the T. pallidum-specific antibody titer in a patient’s serum, and it may yield false-negative results (as in early disease, or with a high antibody titer, which requires further serum dilution for positive results). TPHA is a treponemal test that has both high sensitivity and specificity and is used as a confirmatory test for syphilis with ocular symptoms. A combination of both VDRL and TPHA is preferred for diagnosing ocular syphilis
Erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein in serum are elevated in any systemic infections that lead to ocular symptoms such as endophthalmitis, uveitis, and retinitis. These metrics serve as additional investigations and therapeutic markers for such patients.
To summarize, the increase of pathogen-specific antibodies and inflammatory markers, such as IFNs, during ocular infections can be detected in serum and, in some cases, in ocular fluids. These serve as indirect indicators of the presence of such pathogens in the eye and/or the body and help in confirming the diagnosis. This approach is particularly valuable in situations where obtaining samples directly from the site of infection is challenging (e.g. uveitis, retinitis, and choroiditis) and for pathogens that are difficult to culture.

Operating Room Sterility Checks for Infection Control Practices
Rigorous infection control practices in hospitals are essential for preventing postoperative infections, such as endophthalmitis and surgical site infections. It is crucial to perform monthly or bi-monthly cultures of swabs collected from OR equipment, including microscope handles, OR tables, instrument trolleys, air conditioners, and settle plates, to ensure proper sterility checks for the OR room air quality. Additionally, testing the scrub water through cultures every month and monitoring various biological indicators daily is vital for verifying the proper functioning of autoclaves, steam sterilizers, plasma sterilizers, or ethylene oxide sterilizers to guarantee adequate sterilization of the OR load before surgeries.
Particle counting for the proper functioning of high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters must occur annually (where HEPA filters are available), and filter changes should be recommended as needed. Regular surveillance of the OR for appropriate infection control practices by surgeons and OR staff – such as hand hygiene, scrubbing procedures, and proper draping for patients – is also mandatory. If any patient experiences a postoperative infection within 6 weeks of surgery, the infection control committee should thoroughly review all infection control practices, the surgery, and patient management, along with patient compliance, to identify any procedural breaches and lessons to be learned to prevent similar occurrences in the future.

Common Problems in Processing These Samples and Interpreting Basic Microbiology Reports
Because ocular samples are paucibacillary, the pathogen may be missed on direct smears and may also fail to grow in culture. Unusual pathogens, which are uncommon in other areas of the body, can frequently be found in ocular infections, including Haemophilus, Microsporidia, Acanthamoeba, and Pythium. Identifying these pathogens may require additional special media (other than the nonculturable microsporidia), specific incubation conditions for culture isolation, tailored AST, as well as the microbiologist’s expertise and specialized staining techniques for detection in microscopy of direct smears.
Prior communication with the microbiologist and the laboratory regarding the clinical suspicion of such pathogens aids in selecting the appropriate media, culture conditions, and special stains for these pathogens. For intraocular samples, such as vitreous biopsies, the presence of uveal pigments resembling cocci or bacilli, or crystal violet and safranin stain deposits (components of Gram stain, which may appear as Gram-positive or Gram-negative cocci, respectively), or even crystals from CFW stain can complicate pathogen detection, potentially leading to false-positive results. The unstained bacilli of Mycobacterium spp. on Gram stain may be overlooked, as can the very thin filaments of Nocardia, particularly if the pathogen load is low. Therefore, communicating with the laboratory regarding such clinical suspicions may facilitate better detection of these pathogens by allowing for the use of special stains (e.g. ZN stain) if they are missed on routine stains. Additionally, informing the laboratory about the suspicion of anaerobic pathogens helps prevent the omission of anaerobic culture for such samples.

Challenges of Culture-negative Samples with Strong Clinical Suspicion of Infection
Infected ocular samples are primarily paucibacillary, with an even lower pathogen load for intraocular samples compared to other systems within the human body. The amounts obtained for diagnostics are also minuscule compared to other infection sites, adding to the challenge of completing all relevant diagnostic tests within those small sample limits. Frequently, such sample collections are invasive in nature, especially intraocular samples, thus limiting the possibility of repeated collections. Compounding these challenges is the fact that intraocular samples, such as vitreous biopsies, are often culture negative, even when there is a strong clinical suspicion.
Molecular microbiology diagnostics have sought to address challenges related to culture negativity and low sample volumes. The utility of matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS) for the direct detection and identification of pathogens in monomicrobial infectious endophthalmitis, bypassing culture steps, has been studied and shown to be beneficial. Implementing MALDI-TOF-MS for routine diagnostics in ocular microbiology requires further research and standardization. WGS, utilizing sequencing techniques such as NGS or nanopore sequencing technology, has also been explored for the direct detection of pathogens in both mono- and polymicrobial infections associated with infectious endophthalmitis, and it may be integrated into routine diagnostics with additional research. One further advantage of sequencing techniques like WGS is the identification of antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes in pathogens, which aids in determining appropriate therapy for affected patients.

Conclusions
Diagnosing ocular infections in the laboratory, particularly intraocular infections, is more challenging than diagnosing infections in other parts of the body. In this review, we discuss the routine processing of ocular samples, focusing on intraocular samples, their analysis and report interpretation, the various challenges in these diagnostics, and the new techniques developed to address these challenges.
