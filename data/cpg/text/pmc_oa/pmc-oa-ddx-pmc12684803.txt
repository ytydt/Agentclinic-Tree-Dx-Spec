Risk of Ischemic Stroke After Transient Ischemic Attack in Patients Receiving Comprehensive Versus Noncomprehensive Diagnostic Workup: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Background
Although a timely comprehensive diagnostic workup is recommended for patients with transient ischemic attack (TIA), few studies have examined its impact on post‐TIA ischemic stroke risk. In this study, we hypothesized that patients receiving more comprehensive diagnostic workup have better outcomes, regardless of their care setting.
Methods
Studies on the workup process of patients with TIA and the 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke incidence were searched. Eligible studies were scored 1 to 4 based on the number of diagnostic workups (head computed tomography scan, brain magnetic resonance imaging, and cerebrovascular imaging) and the involvement of a neurology provider in the diagnosis process.
Results
Thirty‐eight studies were included for meta‐analysis. The overall 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke risk was estimated to be 5% (95% CI, 4.9%–5.1%). Our meta‐analysis showed that studies with higher scores—provided more comprehensive diagnostic workups by neurology providers—had a lower risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke (P<0.001). The post‐TIA ischemic stroke risk was 8.4% (95% CI, 6.4%–10.8%) among studies with a score of “1, 5.3% (95% CI, 5.2%–5.5%) among studies with a score of 2, 2.9% (95% CI: 2.7%–3.2%) among studies with a score of 3, and 3.2% (95% CI, 2.5%–4%) among studies with a score of 4. The difference between studies with a score of 3 and ”4 was not statistically significant (P=0.524). Moreover, studies with more comprehensive cardiac examinations had lower 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke risk (P<0.001).
Conclusions
Our findings highlight that a more comprehensive diagnostic workup—neuroimaging, cerebrovascular studies, and cardiac examinations—of patients with TIA delivered by neurology providers might improve their outcomes by reducing the risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke, regardless of the care setting.

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?
This meta‐analysis demonstrates that more comprehensive diagnostic workups—including neuroimaging, cerebrovascular, and cardiac evaluations—are associated with progressively lower 90‐day post‐transient ischemic attack ischemic stroke risk, regardless of care setting.
Our meta‐analysis also highlighted that involvement of neurology providers in the diagnostic process of patients with transient ischemic attackis associated with lower 90‐day post‐transient ischemic attack ischemic stroke risk.

What Are the Clinical Implications?
A timely, comprehensive diagnostic evaluation of patients with transient ischemic attack—including brain, vascular, and cardiac imaging with neurology provider involvement—should be prioritized across all health care settings to reduce the risk of subsequent ischemic stroke.
Each year, approximately 240 000 people in the United States experience a transient ischemic attack (TIA), a heralding sign of ischemic stroke. Timely management of TIAs is essential to prevent a subsequent ischemic stroke. Many TIA clinical models, such as observation units, hospital inpatient units, and outpatient clinics, have been previously introduced. In the United States, hospitalization is the primary pathway for delivering full workups for many patients with TIA. However, some studies have shown that observation units and urgent TIA outpatient clinics are as efficient in providing timely TIA care.
Urgent neurological assessment, neurovascular and brain imaging, and cardiac evaluation play essential roles in TIA evaluation. A recent study showed that despite improvements in nationwide adherence to TIA imaging guidelines, the number of patients with TIA receiving a complete neurovascular diagnostic workup during their initial encounters is still below the optimal level. A similar follow‐up study using Medicare databases demonstrated that about 70% of patients with TIA discharged from emergency departments do not complete their diagnostic workup in outpatient settings.
In this study, we hypothesized that patients receiving more comprehensive diagnostic care are at lower risk of 90‐day post‐TIA stroke regardless of their care setting. We systematically reviewed and analyzed published studies that report the 90‐day post‐TIA stroke rate. We estimated and compared the pooled incidence of subsequent ischemic stroke between different study cohorts based on their reported diagnostic workup.

METHODS
This study was prepared and reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses, Meta‐analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology, Methodological Expectations of Cochrane Intervention Reviews, and Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research guidelines. The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its online supplementary file. As this work is a systematic review and meta‐analysis, the requirement for institutional review board approval and informed consent was waived.

Search Strategy
We explored the following databases: (1) Medline, (2) Web of Science, (3) Scopus, (4) Embase, (5) International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, (6) ClinicalTrials.gov, (7) Trip Medical Database, (8) CINAHL, and (9) all Evidence‐Based Medicine review series, including Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, ACP Journal Club, Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects, Cochrane Clinical Answers, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Cochrane Methodology Register, Health Technology Assessment, and NHS Economic Evaluation Database (Data S1).
The research queries were primarily conducted from the beginning of each database until August 1, 2023, without limitation on study type, design, language, or the publishing institute’s socioeconomic and health‐expenditure indicators. Besides peer‐reviewed publications, unpublished documents and gray literature sources were also evaluated to minimize the risk of publication bias. We improved the search outcomes by manually forward and backward citation tracking (in Google Scholar) and by contacting selected authors.

Eligibility Criteria
All reports documenting the incidence rate of ischemic stroke at 90 days after index TIA were collected. We selected cohorts that provided information about the detailed diagnostic workup of the patients and their care providers. The considered diagnostic workups included brain imaging and cerebrovascular imaging. Neuroimaging was defined as brain magnetic resonance imaging and head computed tomography scans. Vascular imaging modalities consisted of intracranial or extracranial magnetic resonance angiography, head computed tomography angiography, color Doppler imaging, or conventional angiography. A neurology provider visit refers to a visit by either a general neurologist, vascular neurologist, stroke physician, neurology resident, or neurosurgeon.
The aimed studies consisted of both retrospective and prospective reports of adult patients (>18 years old) who received proper antithrombotic therapy following the index event. Both the time‐based and tissue‐based definitions of TIA were used to select the patients. Exclusion criteria included (1) unavailable or unclear patients’ diagnostic workup, (2) unknown evaluation time of the post‐TIA stroke, (3) retrospective diagnosis of the index TIA after stroke diagnosis, (4) inclusion of patients other than TIA, such as stroke, (5) duplicate reports of 1 data set, (6) inclusion of nonconsecutive (selected) patients, such as those studies that included only high‐risk patients with TIA.

Study Population
The included study cohorts were categorized based on a neurology provider’s involvement in the patients’ diagnostic process and the number of diagnostic workups. For this purpose, studies were assigned with a total score ranging from 1 to 4. Although patients in a cohort with lower scores were least evaluated, those with higher scores were most comprehensively evaluated after the index TIA. The detailed method used for the studies’ scoring is presented in Table 1.

Outcome Measure
Our primary study objective was to compare the pooled risk of 90‐day post‐TIA stroke across study cohorts with different workup scores. We sought to address the question of whether patients who underwent more comprehensive diagnostic evaluations and had the involvement of a neurology provider exhibit a reduced overall risk of 90‐day post‐TIA stroke, irrespective of their treatment setting. As a secondary analysis, we compared the pooled risk of 90‐day post‐TIA stroke between studies with negative and positive neurology provider visits.

Screening and Data Extraction
The output of the search was aggregated in Mendeley version 1.19.8. The same software was used to find and delete duplicate reports. The evaluation and screening of distinct titles and abstracts for full‐text assessment was done by 2 independent reviewers (S.S.H and S.S.). A third reviewer (R.Z.) was consulted in case of any discrepancy or disagreement.

Risk‐of‐Bias and Publication Bias Assessment
We used the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies—of Exposures tool to conduct an in‐depth evaluation of the included studies. The assessment is categorized as low, moderate, or high risk of bias or no information. Following current methodological guidance for proportional meta‐analysis, the evaluation of publication bias was deemed unnecessary, as standard methods such as funnel plots and Egger’s test are considered unreliable in this context.

Statistical Analysis
We qualitatively described the data and conducted a meta‐analysis. Due to the lack of random sampling and the nonexchangeability of underlying study rates, pooled estimates for all studies and subgroups were calculated using the Exact fixed‐effects method. This approach provides standard exact binomial CIs without assuming homogeneity across study rates. Heterogeneity between studies was explored based on the SD of rates. The subgroup analyses were performed via contingency tables. Forest plots are used to visualize the variances between studies. All rates presented in tables and figures are shown in their original (untransformed) format. Meta‐analyses and visualization of forest plots were performed in R version 4.4.3. In addition, the forest plots for the random effect model analyses are provided in Figures S1 through S8.

RESULTS

Literature Review and Study Selection
The search protocol resulted in 30 377 records (Figure 1). After removing 15 934 duplicate records, the titles and abstracts of 14 443 discrete search results were screened. Of the 432 potentially eligible studies, 400 articles were excluded after full‐text review (Table S1). Reviewing the reference lists, citation tracking, and communication with authors led to the inclusion of 6 additional studies. Therefore, 38 studies conducted between 2004 and 2023 were included.

Outcome of Meta‐Analyses
The forest plot based on the DerSimonian and Laird estimator is shown in Figure 2. The pooled risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke was 5% (95% CI, 4.9%–5.1%, SD=4.06%) across all studies. Considering the “most patients” threshold of 50%, among studies with a diagnostic workup score of 1, the risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke was 8.4% (95% CI, 6.4%–10.8%, SD=2.32%). Among reports with a diagnostic workup score of 2, the risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke was 5.3% (95% CI, 5.2%–5.5%, SD=5.78%). The same estimate among cohorts with a diagnostic workup score of “Three” and “four” was 2.9% (95% CI, 2.7%–3.2%, SD=3.36%) and 3.2% (95% CI, 2.5%–4%, SD=1.46%), respectively. The estimated pooled risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke was significantly lower among studies with higher scores. The only exception was the difference between a score of 4 versus score 3 studies (P=0.524, Table S3).

Further analysis showed that the aggregated risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke is significantly lower among studies with positive neurology provider visits compared with those with negative neurology provider visits (3.06% [95% CI, 2.8%–3.3%] versus 5.41% [95% CI, 5.3%–5.5%], P<0.001) (Figure S9).

Sensitivity Analyses
In a sensitivity analysis, we used “most patients” thresholds other than 50%—including 66% and 75% (Figures S10 and S11). The findings under both alternative thresholds aligned with the primary analysis, demonstrating that studies with higher diagnostic workup scores were associated with lower risks of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke. When the 75% threshold was applied, 1 study—with the highest 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke risk among all included studies (9.69% [95% CI, 7%–13.1%])—did not receive any points on the diagnostic workup score (score=0).
To assess the impact of the TIA definition on the results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using only studies that employed the time‐based definition of TIA. This analysis yielded results consistent with the main analysis, which used the 50% threshold for “most patients” (Figure S12) and indicated that studies with higher diagnostic workup scores have a lower risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke. Similar to the main analysis, the only exception was the insignificant difference between studies with a score of 4 and 3 (P=0.558, Table S3).
In another sensitivity analysis, we categorized studies based on their care setting, where this information was available (Figure 3). Comparing the pooled risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke between inpatient and outpatient studies showed a significantly higher rate among inpatient studies (5.24% [95% CI, 4.1%–5.4%] versus 3.48% [95% CI: 3%–4.1%], P<0.001). Besides, inpatient studies had significantly higher pooled risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke than TIA clinic studies (5.24% [95% CI, 4.1%–5.4%] versus 2.74% [95% CI, 2.1%–3.6%], P<0.001) and emergency department studies (5.24% [95% CI, 4.1%–5.4%] versus 4.05% [95% CI, 3.3%–4.9%], P=0.010) (Figure S13).

To underscore that a more comprehensive diagnostic workup is associated with a better outcome among patients with TIA, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis. For that purpose, we first developed a scoring system based on the extent of cardiac evaluations reported in studies with available data (12 out of 38). This system accounted for the use of Holter monitoring and echocardiography (transthoracic or transesophageal), assigning scores of 0, 1, or 2 depending on whether neither, 1, or both modalities were used in at least 50% of the study population. ECG, performed in nearly all studies for almost all participants, was excluded from this scoring system due to its ubiquity. Our analysis showed that studies with a cardiac score of 1 (3.38% [95% CI, 2.7%–4.2%], P<0.001) or 2 (2.25% [95% CI, 1.4%–3.3%], P<0.001) had a significantly lower risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke compared with those with a score of 0 (6.02% [95% CI, 4.9%–7.3%], Figure 4). The difference between studies with a cardiac score of 1 and those with a score of 2 was not statistically significant (P=0.091).

Risk‐of‐Bias Assessment
Table S4 summarizes the results of the risk of bias assessment according to the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies—of Exposures. Among the 38 included cohorts, 22 cohorts (57.9%) had a low risk of bias, and 6 cohorts (5.8%) had a moderate risk of bias. No study had a high risk of bias. In 10 studies, there was not enough information to assess the publication bias in at least one domain of the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies—of Exposures tool.

DISCUSSION
Our results suggest that the rates of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke are lower among patients with a more comprehensive diagnostic workup—including brain neuroimaging, cerebrovascular studies, and cardiac examinations—who were worked up by neurology providers, regardless of their care setting, as each diagnostic workup score category included both inpatient and outpatient studies; however, the number of studies was insufficient to further compare them by their diagnostic workup scores within each setting category.
It has been previously established that implementing early treatment among patients with TIA can prevent subsequent stroke. However, despite recommendations for timely comprehensive diagnostic workups of patients with TIA by the European Stroke Organization 2021, the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association 2021, and many other guidelines and studies, few studies have investigated the impact of adhering to these recommendations and the risk of post‐TIA ischemic stroke. Our findings provide strong evidence that incorporating cerebrovascular imaging and cardiac examinations, besides brain imaging techniques under the supervision of neurology providers, may significantly improve the outcome of patients with TIA by lowering the risk of ischemic stroke.
Although many studies have demonstrated that TIA clinics are as effective as inpatient units, the concept of providing outpatient TIA care is still controversial. Our results highlight that the primary determinant of post‐TIA ischemic stroke risk is the proper evaluation, diagnostic workups, and the health care professionals who deliver these services, not the care setting—inpatient or outpatient—in which patients with TIA are treated. However, the wide variations in infrastructure and capacity across different inpatient and outpatient settings are inevitable, often leading to inconsistencies in diagnostic approaches and treatment decisions. Moreover, modifying each center to fully comply with the most comprehensive diagnostic approach is accompanied by significant logistical and financial challenges. As a result, a priority for future research should be developing decision‐support systems that can accurately identify patients most likely to benefit from an extensive diagnostic workup. When integrated with an efficient referral pathway, such systems could optimize resource allocation and ensure that high‐risk patients receive the level of care they need.
Although our findings support improved outcomes with a more comprehensive diagnostic workup for patients with TIA, the availability of key modalities—such as neuroimaging, cerebrovascular imaging, and cardiac evaluation—remains limited across many health care centers, especially in remote areas or poor countries. Therefore, a personalized diagnostic strategy can be adapted based on individuals’ risk for stroke, and further workup can be completed in a timely manner in outpatient centers that have access to resources. Nevertheless, our results underscore the potential value of investing in broader access to diagnostic workup for patients with TIA, as such investments could significantly reduce the incidence of subsequent ischemic stroke. The return on investment for stroke prevention is high, with estimates suggesting that every $1 spent may yield up to $10 in health care savings.
Our more detailed analysis highlighted that the risk of post‐TIA ischemic stroke is lower among studies in which neurology providers were involved. Because most patients with TIA arrive after the index event with no residual neurologic deficit, the diagnosis of TIA is challenging. Many studies have shown low interobserver agreement in accurate TIA diagnosis. More important, the tendency of misdiagnosis by nonneurology providers is toward underestimating the stroke risk, which in turn increases the risk of underrecognition and undertreatment of patients with true TIA. Moreover, in another study, the agreement between neurology residents and more specialized neurology providers regarding the age, blood pressure, clinical features, duration of symptoms, and diabetes scoring system was “excellent,” proposing that short‐term training of physicians may enhance their performance while treating patients with TIA.
Our study had several limitations. First, most studies did not provide detailed patient medication data (eg, statins). Although therapeutic decisions are directly affected by the results of diagnostic workups, we could not evaluate the impact of medication on our results. Second, we had limited access to the timing of diagnostic workups used in most studies. Therefore, we could not adjust the results of our study based on patients’ medication and the timing of diagnostic workups. Third, our study identified high levels of heterogeneity among specific study groups, and further research is needed to corroborate these findings. Fourth, although cardiac examinations are crucial for determining the cause of TIAs, we could not include them in the diagnostic workup scoring system due to the lack of data on these procedures in most studies.
Despite these limitations, our study provides new insight indicating that the comprehensiveness of diagnostic evaluations—incorporating modalities such as neuroimaging, cerebrovascular studies, and cardiac examinations—is more critical to improved outcomes in patients with TIA than the specific care setting in which they are managed, especially when these workups are performed by neurology providers. However, this study represents a preliminary step in this direction, and future longitudinal research is warranted to validate and expand upon its findings. Some of the questions that are worth researching in future studies include whether patients with varying TIA subtypes and demographic characteristics—such as age, sex, and race—derive equal benefit from comprehensive post‐TIA diagnostic workups and whether the identification of a positive finding through 1 diagnostic modality diminishes or maintains the value of pursuing additional modalities.

CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, our findings suggest a lowered risk of 90‐day post‐TIA ischemic stroke among patients receiving more comprehensive diagnostic workups, including neuroimaging, cerebrovascular studies, and cardiac examinations, especially when delivered by a neurology provider, regardless of the care setting these workups are provided. Further studies are needed to tailor an individualized approach for managing patients with TIA and to identify those who would benefit most from comprehensive diagnostic workups.
