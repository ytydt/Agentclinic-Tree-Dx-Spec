AJCC 8th Edition Staging of Oral Cavity Cancer: Radiologic Evaluation
Accurate staging is essential for establishing treatment strategies and predicting the prognosis of patients with head and neck cancer. Radiologic imaging plays a pivotal role in evaluating deep tissue invasion and regional nodal metastasis that extend beyond the limits of endoscopic evaluation. This review examines the revised staging system for oral cavity cancer in the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition, focusing on radiologically relevant parameters, particularly the tumor depth of invasion, and on key considerations in image interpretation. This article serves as a practical guide for radiologists to ensure accurate staging and facilitate effective communication with clinicians.

INTRODUCTION
The head and neck region encompasses intricate anatomical structures in close proximity that support diverse and vital functions. Consequently, clinical presentation, therapeutic approach, and prognosis vary substantially according to tumor location. Accurate staging is therefore indispensable for appropriate treatment planning and reliable prognostic assessment. The tumor (T)-node (N)-metastasis (M) staging system developed by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) provides a widely accepted and standardized framework for staging head and neck cancer malignancies.
Radiologic imaging constitutes a fundamental component of head and neck cancer staging. Although endoscopy provides a valuable assessment of mucosal surfaces, it has limitations in evaluating submucosal extension, invasion of adjacent structures, bone and cartilage involvement, regional lymph node metastasis, and extranodal extension (ENE). By contrast, cross-sectional imaging with CT and MRI provides critical anatomic information, enabling accurate assessment of both primary tumor extent and nodal disease for precise T and N categories.
This review, based on the AJCC 8th edition staging system, comprehensively examines the revised TNM classification criteria for oral cavity cancer and outlines detailed imaging evaluation strategies and interpretative considerations. The aim of this review is to assist radiologists in providing accurate image-based assessments that enhance communication with clinicians and support optimal patient treatment planning.

CLINICAL AND PATHOLOGICAL CHARACTERISTICS
In the AJCC 8th edition, oral cavity cancer encompasses epithelial cancers and minor salivary gland cancers arising within the oral cavity. Squamous cell carcinoma is the most prevalent histological subtype and is associated with high morbidity and mortality, with reported 5-year survival rates in Korea of approximately 50%–70%. Minor salivary gland cancers, although rare in the oral cavity, warrant particular attention during staging and diagnosis owing to their high malignant potential and marked histological heterogeneity, including adenoid cystic carcinoma and mucoepidermoid carcinoma. Perineural spread is particularly common in these tumors, conferring a heightened risk of recurrence; consequently, long-term surveillance is essential.
Risk factors for oral cavity cancer include poor oral hygiene, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Clinically, patients may present with a non-healing oral ulcer, color changes of the oral mucosa (such as leukoplakia), recurrent bleeding, pain, and dysphagia; in advanced cases, patients may also present with cervical lymphadenopathy.

ANATOMICAL STRUCTURES AND PATHWAYS OF TUMOR SPREAD
The oral cavity extends from the skin-vermilion junction of the lips anteriorly to the junction of the hard and soft palates superiorly, the circumvallate papillae inferiorly, and the anterior tonsillar pillars laterally. Anatomically, it consists of the mucosal lip, buccal mucosa, alveolar ridge, retromolar trigone, floor of the mouth, hard palate, and oral tongue. The mucosal lip extends posteriorly to the attached gingiva of the alveolar ridge. The buccal mucosa extends from the line of lip contact to the alveolar ridge and the pterygomandibular raphe, forming the inner mucosal surface of the cheeks and lips. The alveolar ridges consist of mucosa overlying the alveolar processes of the maxilla and mandible. The retromolar trigone is the attached mucosa covering the ascending ramus of the mandible, located posterior to the last mandibular molar. The floor of the mouth is a crescent-shaped surface overlying the mylohyoid and hyoglossus muscles, extending from the mandibular alveolar ridge to the undersurface of the tongue. The hard palate forms a semi-laminar region between the maxillary alveolar ridge and the palatine process of the palatine bone. The oral tongue comprises the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, anterior to the circumvallate papillae, and represents the freely mobile portion within the oral cavity.
Owing to these anatomical characteristics, oral cavity cancers have a strong propensity for direct invasion into adjacent soft tissues and osseous structures. Lesions arising in the floor of the mouth and oral tongue may spread rapidly along the muscular planes. Mandibular invasion occurs relatively frequently in lesions originating from the alveolar ridge or retromolar trigone.
Oral cavity cancers most commonly metastasize to cervical lymph node levels IA, IB, and IIA. Lesions located near or crossing the midline exhibit a higher likelihood of bilateral nodal dissemination; however, for staging purposes, midline nodes are generally classified as ipsilateral.
In addition, perineural spread represents an important pathway for tumor dissemination in oral cavity cancers. The inferior alveolar nerve (branch of cranial nerve V3) is frequently involved in mandibular and gingivobuccal lesions, whereas the greater and lesser palatine nerves (branches of cranial nerve V2) are typically affected in hard palate lesions.

AJCC 8TH EDITION STAGING SYSTEM

T CATEGORY
In the T category of oral cavity cancer (Table 1), the revised AJCC 8th edition, tumor involvement of the extrinsic tongue muscles has been removed as a staging criterion. A key modification is the inclusion of tumor depth of invasion (DOI) alongside the greatest tumor dimension. DOI is defined as the perpendicular distance from the closest normal mucosal basement membrane to the deepest point of tumor infiltration, measured in millimeters. DOI must be distinguished from tumor thickness. Notably, exophytic tumors that grow outward may have a relatively shallow DOI despite substantial tumor thickness, whereas endophytic tumors can exhibit considerable DOI even with limited tumor thickness, conferring a potentially worse prognosis (Fig. 1).

• Tis: Carcinoma in situ
• T1: Tumor ≤2 cm in greatest dimension with a DOI of ≤5 mm
• T2: Tumor ≤2 cm with 5 mm < DOI ≤ 10 mm, or tumor >2 cm but ≤4 cm with DOI ≤10 mm (Fig. 2)

N CATEGORY
The N category incorporated ENE into the classification (Table 2).
• N1: Single ipsilateral metastatic lymph node ≤3 cm in greatest dimension without ENE
• N2a: Single ipsilateral metastatic lymph node >3 cm but ≤6 cm in greatest dimension without ENE
• N2b: Multiple ipsilateral metastatic lymph nodes, all ≤6 cm in greatest dimension, without ENE
• N2c: Bilateral or contralateral metastatic lymph nodes, all ≤6 cm in greatest dimension, without ENE
• N3a: Metastatic lymph node >6 cm in greatest dimension without ENE
• N3b: Metastatic lymph node with clinically overt ENE

M CATEGORY
The M category followed a binary system:
• M0 (no distant metastasis)
• M1 (distant metastasis present)

IMAGING EVALUATION AND INTERPRETIVE CONSIDERATIONS
Cross-sectional imaging is essential for assessing the DOI of the primary tumor and detecting bone erosion, perineural spread, and lymph node or distant metastases.
CT is particularly effective for evaluating cortical bone erosion and destruction. However, caution is required to avoid misinterpreting dental pathology, periodontal inflammation, or edentulous changes, such as mandibular invasion. Artifact-reduction techniques can be employed to minimize interference from dental prostheses, and MRI may serve as an alternative when CT is compromised. The puffed-cheek technique, in which imaging is performed with inflated cheeks, separates the mucosal surface from the alveolar bone and improves the delineation of lesion contours and margins, thereby facilitating the evaluation of subtle lesions.
Contrast-enhanced MRI is preferred for lesions of the tongue and floor of the mouth due to its superior soft tissue resolution. MRI provides a more precise assessment of DOI, tongue muscle invasion, midline extension, and neurovascular involvement than CT. Among MRI sequences, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging has been shown to have the highest correlation with pathological DOI. Additionally, the selection of imaging planes for DOI measurement can be flexibly determined based on the location of the long axis that represents the tumor thickness. However, radiologically measured DOI may differ from pathological findings. Careful interpretation is warranted, as peritumoral edema or inflammation may mimic tumor tissue on imaging. Tang et al. recommended high-resolution three-dimensional isotropic contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging because it enables precise multiplanar reformation and facilitates the delineation of the normal mucosal surface. Similarly, when evaluating mandibular bone marrow invasion using MRI, chemical shift artifacts or inflammatory changes as tumor involvement may be misidentified. MRI is also used to evaluate perineural spread, identified by nerve thickening, abnormal enhancement, and widening of the neural foramina.

CONCLUSION
The AJCC 8th edition head and neck cancer staging system represents the current clinical standard, integrating anatomical tumor extent with key prognostic factors. A notable feature of the AJCC 8th edition is the incorporation of DOI in the oral cavity cancer T category.
Cross-sectional imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced CT and MRI, plays a central role in head and neck cancer staging. CT offers advantages in the assessment of bone invasion and overall lesion extent owing to its superior spatial resolution and rapid acquisition. MRI is used for evaluating submucosal infiltration, bone marrow invasion, and perineural spread owing to its excellent soft tissue contrast resolution.
Although a revision to AJCC Version 9 is anticipated, radiologic staging based on a comprehensive understanding of anatomical structures and tumor spread pathways will continue to play a crucial role in treatment planning and prognostic assessment of patients with head and neck cancer.
