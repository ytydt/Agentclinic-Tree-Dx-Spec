A Comprehensive Approach: Addressing Psychological Needs in Gene Therapy for Haemophilia
ABSTRACT
Introduction
Gene therapy (GT) represents a transformative development in the treatment of haemophilia, offering the potential for long‐lasting symptom control after a single administration. Although the scientific and medical aspects of this innovation have been widely studied, its psychological impact remains under‐addressed.
Aims
This paper explores the importance of providing continuous psychological support to individuals with haemophilia who are undergoing GT, emphasizing its necessity before, during, and after treatment.
Results
Persons living with haemophilia often experience a wide range of emotions, including hope, fear, anxiety, and uncertainty, particularly as they transition from managing a chronic condition to facing the possibility of long‐lasting therapeutic benefit and enduring freedom from regular treatment. Psychological support plays a pivotal role in preparing them to understand and cope with the profound shifts that GT entails. In the pre‐therapy stage, mental health professionals can help assess readiness, align expectations, and mitigate anxiety. During treatment, those with haemophilia may confront emotional stress related to the novelty, irreversibility, and physical effects of the procedure, including adverse reactions and the psychological consequences of steroid use. In the post‐treatment phase, emotional responses to treatment outcomes require careful management. Moreover, those who are ineligible for GT may experience emotional distress that also warrants psychological support.
Discussions
By recognizing these varied psychological dimensions, healthcare providers can implement comprehensive support strategies that enhance treatment adherence, emotional resilience, and quality of life.

Introduction
In recent years, many treatment options have been developed for persons living with haemophilia (PLwH), leading to an increase in their life expectancy and quality of life. However, none of these treatments have eliminated the need for ongoing treatment. Recently, gene therapy (GT) has emerged as a significant advancement in the treatment of haemophilia, offering the potential for durable therapeutic benefit through sustained endogenous factor expression.
GT uses viral factors to deliver the FVIII or FIX gene into the liver cells. This is a novel treatment, and there are still a number of uncertainties regarding its outcomes, including how long its effects will last. Additionally, it is currently a one‐time treatment, and therefore if it is ineffective, it cannot be administered again. Considering these aspects of GT, PLwH will need constant support, both during the decision‐making process and after injection. This support should involve regular sessions with a multidisciplinary team (MDT) to ensure comprehensive care from various areas of expertise. Psychologists play a key role in MDT, as many PLwH who have undergone or are considering GT perceive it as a multifaceted treatment. Although GT offers the possibility of living a more normal life, it also leads to significant emotional and psychological challenges, making psychological support essential. Therefore, due to the importance of the psychological support for PLwH, in this paper, we explore the role of psychological support at different stages of the GT process.

Pre‐Therapy Stage

Initial Psychological Assessment
Before undergoing GT, PLwH should participate in comprehensive psychological assessments. So that at this stage, psychologists assess the mental health status to evaluate whether they are psychologically prepared for a life‐changing treatment and whether they need additional psychological supports in the process. However, there is still no universally accepted protocol for psychological assessment before GT. Nevertheless, in the guideline published by the National Hemophilia Foundation, several key assessment categories were introduced. These include the understanding of GT, mental health history, coping strategies in stressful situations, the presence of a psychological support system, and mental readiness for this significant life change. It is important to clarify that the purpose of the psychological assessment is not to screen for potential psychologically ineligible PLwH; clinical and medical criteria remain the only formal exclusion factors. Rather, the assessment is intended to identify early any psychological vulnerabilities that could affect the GT journey in order to activate a timely psychological support to help vulnerable candidates to manage expectations and to improve their copying skills and adherence to the treatment pathway.

Informed Consent Process
During this phase, PLwH should receive detailed information about potential benefits, risks, and ethical consideration of GT in the sessions with MDT. The acceptance of any medical treatment depends on the trust relationship with the healthcare team. The healthcare team have to ensure that comprehensive and accurate information about GT and other available treatment options is effectively communicated. This is essential to support informed decision‐making that respects PLwH interests and preferences. To achieve this in practice, PLwH and their families should be aware of their health needs, understand the potential impacts of each treatment option, and consider how these align with their lifestyle and personal values.
According to Self‐Determination Theory, PLwH who perceive a given therapeutic option as a self‐directed and informed choice, usually experience a greater sense of autonomy and empowerment. Conversely, when individuals feel uncertain or pressured, this may lead to anxiety or withdrawal from the process. Self‐Determination Theory posits that human motivation and well‐being are fostered when three basic psychological needs are satisfied: autonomy (feeling in control of one's actions), competence (feeling effective), and relatedness (feeling connected to others). In the context of GT, when PLwH feel they are making an autonomous and informed decision, they are more likely to feel empowered and psychologically prepared. On the contrary, if they experience external pressure or lack sufficient understanding, they may feel anxious or disengaged. This model is helpful in suggesting the three main psychological needs to be assessed and if necessary, worked on, to ensure a better the decision‐making processing for PLwH.
However, providing detailed information of the treatment can prevent any dissatisfaction after the treatment. For instance, a possible misunderstanding about GT is the belief that it can also cure pre‐existing damages: in the study by Fletcher et al. (2022), a participant reported a total dissatisfaction with the treatment, as his previous joint damages have not been reversed. These kinds of dissatisfaction can be prevented by providing detailed information about the treatments.
In addition to clinical and emotional readiness, it is important that the pre‐therapy evaluation includes a discussion of potential lifestyle impacts. These may include family planning decisions, employment and financial considerations, and logistical aspects such as travel to specialist centres and time away from work. These practical issues can significantly affect treatment adherence, satisfaction, and emotional well‐being. Addressing them early in the decision‐making process ensures a more holistic and realistic understanding of what GT entails, aligning with the principles of patient‐centred care.

During GT
Although GT is typically considered as an outpatient procedure, PLwH still require close physical monitoring for at least one full day. This period is typically longer than the time usually spent in hospital for other treatment regimens, such as prophylaxis. During the hospital stay, different medical aspects, including blood pressure, heart rate, and temperature, must be carefully monitored. If any unusual physical symptoms are detected during medical checks, hospital stay may be extended. This intensive physical monitoring can be stressful for many individuals and may lead to feelings of anxiety or even doubts about their decision for undergoing GT.
In these cases, psychologists can provide supports for discomfort or emotional vulnerability. The emotional reaction during this stage can be further understood through the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, which emphasizes that psychological stress results from the interaction between the person and the environment. Specifically, individuals appraise (interpret) a situation as either threatening or manageable based on their perceived coping resources. In this context, PLwH may experience GT hospitalization either as a stressful threat or a challenging opportunity, depending on their emotional resilience, past experiences, and access to support.

Post‐Therapy Stage
In the first year after GT, several follow‐up sessions and restrictions are planned for PLwH. These include limitations on long‐distance travel, avoiding alcohol for 6–12 months, and undergoing regular medical and psychological check‐ups. Many individuals find these appointments overwhelming. So that in a study by Fletcher et al. (2021), PLwH who had already received GT reported that the follow‐up process was more time‐consuming and intensive than they had initially anticipated. These prolonged and frequent sessions were often associated with feelings of anxiety and frustration. In this phase, psychological sessions, either individual or as supportive group sessions with other PLwH who have undergone GT, might help not only in dealing with eventual negative psychological side effects, but also to motivate PLwH to regularly attend the necessary follow‐up sessions.
In addition, following GT, steroids might be necessary as part of post‐treatment care. The primary purpose of this treatment is to manage and mitigate immune responses that could compromise the effectiveness of GT. However, this treatment can cause a number of side effects, which many PLwH have identified as a significant concern. The psychological impact of steroid therapy should not be underestimated, as side effects such as insomnia, weight gain, and mood swings can have a profound impact on both individuals and their families. Furthermore, uncertainty about the duration of the treatment outcomes can bring anxiety and anger: a number of studies have reported that the possibility of coming back the haemophilic symptoms is PLwH main source of anxiety
On the other hand, research has shown that long‐term and chronic conditions, such as haemophilia, especially when diagnosed in early life, often become an integral part of one's own identity. As shown by studies on Illness Identity Theory, individuals with chronic illnesses integrate their condition into their self‐concept through four main identity dimensions: rejection (denying or minimizing the illness), engulfment (allowing the illness to dominate their identity), acceptance (acknowledging the illness as a separate but manageable part of life), and enrichment (drawing positive meaning or personal growth from the illness). These dimensions highlight how redefining oneself post‐therapy can influence long‐term psychological adjustment. For instance, for individuals whose haemophilic identity aligns with the engulfment dimension, where the condition dominates their sense of self, this transition may be particularly challenging. The sudden absence of a condition that once defined their daily life and interactions may lead to disorientation or reluctance to fully embrace the post‐treatment ‘healthy’ self. This helps explain why older PLwH usually view younger individuals as more suitable candidates for GT: they might feel a strong attachment to their haemophilic identity and may be reluctant to alter or let go of it.
In addition, GT has significant social implications. As media increasingly consider GT as a permanent “cure,” there is a risk that public perceptions may shift, leading to reduced support for PLwH, such as stricter scrutiny or reduced access to disability‐related benefits. Professional groups should therefore, facilitate learning of PLwH and proactively counter haemophilia GT‐related misinformation by raising awareness of the media's influence on community expectations and supporting them in interpreting press coverage to limit the spread of inaccuracies.
However, the psychological experiences discussed here may reflect the perspectives of individuals in high‐income countries where there has been long‐term access to regular factor replacement therapy. In contrast, many PLwH in low‐ and middle‐income countries might face significant barriers in accessing even basic treatment. For these individuals, GT may represent not a psychological disruption but rather a long‐awaited opportunity for stability or normalization.

Psychological Support Following Decline in Factor Levels Post‐GT
As previously mentioned, there is still uncertainties regarding the long‐term efficacy of GT. Although most individuals maintain therapeutic benefits for up to 2 years post‐treatment, a gradual decline in factor levels has been observed in the majority of individuals. This decline may ultimately necessitate a return to prophylactic treatment. Such a transition can significantly impact their lives, potentially leading again to shifts in identity, increased disappointment and anxiety, since GT cannot be repeated.
Those experiencing these symptoms, particularly those who face challenges in adapting to a redefined sense of self post‐treatment, would benefit from targeted psychological support. This includes individuals who have difficulty letting go of a long‐standing illness identity, those whose condition has dominated their self‐concept (engulfment identity), or those struggling with the emotional implications of transitioning to a ‘healthy’ self. Tailored interventions, such as cognitive‐behavioural therapy (CBT), identity‐focused counselling, or peer support groups, could assist in navigating this psychological adjustment and fostering a more balanced and resilient sense of self. Despite the critical importance of such services in this phase, there is still no research available on the psychological impact of declining factor levels post‐GT. Therefore, investigating these psychological needs and interventions should be a priority for future studies.

Psychological Support for Ineligible PLwH
Currently, there are a number of exclusion criteria for undergoing GT. Some exclusion factors are age 65 or older, liver diseases, or chronic conditions such as HIV. Specifically, a substantial cohort of PLwH with HIV as a direct result of contaminated blood products used in the 1970s–1990s. People living with HIV as a consequence of contaminated blood transfusion tend to have long‐term psychosocial harms, including higher rates of depression and anxiety, persistent stigma, and mistrust to the healthcare systems.
At the same time, GT protocols commonly exclude PLwH with active HIV infection, or who are HIV positive.This exclusion from GT is typically due to the cautious view, as this is a novel treatment and there are a number of unknowns in this area. In addition, in the study by Levy‐Mendelovich et al. (2023), chronic liver damage in those with HIV was considered as a preventive element for receiving GT in those with HIV, as GT is now a liver directed AAV treatment.
As exclusion based on HIV serostatus may reinforce feelings of injustice and reawaken trauma linked to past iatrogenic harms, we recommend that both research protocols and clinical practice (a) clearly explain and justify HIV‐related exclusion on clinical grounds, (b) assess the psychological and social impact of such exclusions, and (c) actively engage affected communities in decisions about eligibility, treatment access, and communication.
Furthermore, for individuals who have invested hope in GT as a transformative treatment, being deemed ineligible can lead to significant emotional distress, including feelings of loss, disappointment, or even grief. Fletcher and colleagues identified two major themes in this context: expectation and loss. Many PLwH enter the process with the expectation of achieving “normality,” freedom from treatment, or even a cure. Some see GT as a way to pioneer better treatments for future generations. When these expectations are unmet, individuals may feel as though their hopes have been taken away, resulting in feeling of loss. Such feelings resemble stages of grief, including denial, anger, and depression. In these cases, psychological support plays a crucial role in helping individuals process their emotions, reframe their expectations, and explore alternative treatment options that may align with their health needs and personal goals. Moreover, psychological interventions can focus on fostering resilience, maintaining hope, and addressing any stigma or self‐perception issues that may arise from being ineligible for GT. Furthermore, patient advocacy groups (PAGs) have an important supportive role for PLwH who are ineligible for treatment. Indeed, they are instrumental in providing accurate and balanced information about the benefits, risks, and limitations of GT, and they also extend support to those who are excluded from eligibility due to clinical or logistical constraints, fostering a sense of inclusion within the therapeutic community. PAGs achieve this by (i) facilitating peer‐support networks to address psychosocial challenges and feelings of exclusion, (ii) advocating for access to alternative treatments and equitable healthcare policies, and (iii) promoting holistic educational resources that include the perspectives of ineligible patients.
As highlighted by Folkers et al. (2019), by incorporating PAGs perspectives into educational resources, decision‐support tools, and care pathways, healthcare systems can better ensure that ineligible individuals receive the support they need and remain fully supported within the broader therapeutic community.

Summary and Future Directions
Each stage of the GT can influence the mental and emotional state of PLwH. The experiences are highly individualized: some PLwH embrace the process with optimism and hope, while others may face challenges related to stress, fear, or disappointment. Therefore, given the emotional burden often caused by uncertainties surrounding outcomes and the one‐time nature of GT, PLwH undergoing GT require additional support beyond the standard MDT care typically provided to PLwH and their families. PAGs play a vital role in supporting individual ineligible for GT by providing balanced information, fostering inclusion, and advocating for equitable access to alternative treatments.
Although some studies have explored the psychological impacts of GT, it remains unclear whether some PLwH are more vulnerable to these psychological changes than others. Moreover, future research should focus on understanding the psychological effects across different stages of the treatment process. This includes examining the long‐term impact, such as experiences 2 years after therapy, which contain lots of follow‐up sessions and potential side effects. Additionally, lifestyle changes, such as restrictions on alcohol consumption, may affect the social lives and relationships of PLwH, contributing to further emotional challenges. Therefore, longitudinal studies are essential to assess how the demand for psychological support evolves over time and varies among individuals.
