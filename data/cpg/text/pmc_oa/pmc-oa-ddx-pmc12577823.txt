Advancements in biodegradable implants: evaluation in large animal surgical models
Biodegradable implants significantly advance regenerative medicine and orthopedic surgery, offering temporary mechanical support while facilitating natural tissue regeneration. Unlike permanent implants, biodegradable materials eliminate the need for secondary removal surgeries, reducing patient risk and healthcare costs. Recent innovations in biomaterials – such as magnesium-based alloys, polymeric composites, and bio-ceramics – have led to the development of implants with enhanced biocompatibility, controlled degradation rates, and improved mechanical performance. Additionally, the advent of 3D (three-dimensions) printing and additive manufacturing has enabled the creation of patient-specific scaffolds with complex geometries tailored for optimized tissue integration. Evaluating these implants in large animal surgical models, including pigs, sheep, and goats, is critical for bridging the gap between laboratory research and human clinical applications. These models provide anatomical and physiological parallels to human systems, offering valuable insights into implant behavior, degradation kinetics, tissue response, and functional outcomes under realistic biomechanical conditions. However, species-specific differences and variability in healing responses present ongoing challenges in directly translating findings. Emerging technologies, such as smart implants embedded with biosensors, bioactive surface coatings, and artificial intelligence-assisted diagnostic tools, continue to enhance implant functionality and monitoring capabilities. Despite these advancements, challenges persist in achieving optimal degradation profiles, managing inflammatory responses, and maintaining mechanical integrity throughout the healing process.

Introduction
Biodegradable implants have emerged as transformative tools in regenerative medicine and orthopedic surgery, offering temporary support to tissues while eliminating the need for secondary removal surgeries. Unlike conventional metallic implants, these biomaterials gradually degrade in vivo, ideally synchronizing tissue healing processes and minimizing long-term complications such as stress shielding, inflammation, or implant rejection. Materials such as polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), magnesium alloys, and bioresorbable ceramics are at the forefront of this innovation. Despite their promise, translating biodegradable implant technologies from bench to bedside requires robust preclinical validation, particularly in large animal models that closely mimic human anatomy and physiological responses. Canines, ovines, porcine, and caprines are commonly employed in these evaluations due to their biomechanical and metabolic similarities to humans. These models enable researchers to assess parameters such as mechanical integrity, biodegradation kinetics, biocompatibility, osseointegration, and immunological response in a clinically relevant setting. Despite their theoretical and laboratory successes, the clinical translation of these implants necessitates rigorous preclinical testing, often conducted in large animals. Using such models allows for a detailed assessment of biocompatibility and efficacy and helps refine surgical techniques and implant designs before human trials. Integrating multidisciplinary technologies, including biomaterials science, tissue engineering, and veterinary surgery, is accelerating progress in this area, bringing biodegradable implants closer to routine clinical use. Recent advancements include the integration of bioactive coatings, controlled degradation mechanisms, and additive manufacturing (3D printing) techniques that allow for customized, patient-specific implants. Coupled with longitudinal imaging modalities and histopathological analysis, large animal surgical models provide invaluable insights into these next-generation devices’ functional and biological performance. Despite these innovations, challenges remain, particularly in predicting human outcomes based on animal studies due to interspecies differences in healing rates and immune responses.
As the demand for biodegradable implants grows in orthopedics, cardiovascular surgery, and craniofacial reconstruction, the rigorous evaluation of translational animal models remains critical to ensure safety, efficacy, and regulatory compliance before human clinical trials.

Materials and design innovations
Recent developments in biodegradable materials have focused on enhancing mechanical properties, degradation rates, and biocompatibility. Polymers such as PLA, PGA, and their copolymers (PLGA) have been extensively studied for their tunable degradation rates and mechanical strength. Innovations in composite materials, which combine biodegradable polymers with bioactive ceramics or metals, have shown promise in improving load-bearing capacity and promoting bone regeneration. Additionally, 3D printing technologies have enabled the fabrication of complex geometries tailored to specific anatomical requirements. This customization allows for better integration with surrounding tissues and improved functional outcomes. Recent studies have focused on various materials and technologies to enhance the performance and biocompatibility of these implants.
Among the most promising materials are magnesium (Mg)-based implants, which have garnered considerable interest due to their biocompatibility and mechanical properties resembling natural bone. In rabbit femoral models, Mg alloy screws demonstrated excellent integration with bone and gradual degradation, aligning well with the healing timeline. These implants also release Mg ions that promote osteogenesis. However, their rapid degradation can lead to the formation of hydrogen gas, potentially resulting in gas pockets and local tissue irritation. Moreover, controlling the degradation rate remains challenging due to magnesium’s high reactivity in physiological environments.
Synthetic polymers such as PLA, polycaprolactone (PCL), and PGA are widely used for biodegradable scaffolds due to their favorable degradation profiles and easy processing. In rat cranial defect models, PLA/PCL scaffolds supported bone regeneration while degrading at a rate suitable for tissue ingrowth. These materials are well-tolerated biologically and can be tailored for various applications by adjusting their chemical composition. Nonetheless, they have mechanical limitations that restrict their use in load-bearing scenarios, and their degradation may produce acidic byproducts that could induce localized inflammation or impair healing in sensitive tissues.

To address limitations in mechanical performance and biological activity, bioceramic composites such as hydroxyapatite (HA) combined with PLA have been developed. These composites are particularly valuable in bone tissue engineering due to their osteoconductivity and ability to mimic the mineral phase of natural bone. In rabbit tibial defect models, HA-PLA implants enhanced bone integration and showed controlled degradation. HA promotes bone cell attachment and bioactivity. However, the brittleness of ceramics and challenges in uniformly distributing ceramic particles within the polymer matrix can affect their structural integrity and consistency during degradation.
In parallel, 3D-printed biodegradable implants represent a cutting-edge approach in the field. Researchers have developed custom scaffolds that precisely match the defect site using materials like poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA). In rat femur models, PLGA scaffolds loaded with growth factors such as bone morphogenetic proteins significantly improved bone healing outcomes. Fabricating implants with controlled porosity and architecture enables better vascularization and tissue infiltration. However, the complexity and cost of 3D printing and challenges related to degradation control and uniform release of incorporated growth factors limit their widespread use.

Evaluation in large animal models
Large animal models, such as sheep, pigs, and goats, are crucial for evaluating the performance of biodegradable implants before clinical application in humans. These models provide a more accurate representation of human anatomy and physiology than small animal models, allowing for better assessment of the implants’ mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and degradation behavior.
Mechanical testing: Large animal studies have demonstrated that biodegradable implants can achieve mechanical properties comparable to traditional materials. For instance, a study by Mohan et al, evaluated a novel PLGA-based implant in a sheep model, showing that it maintained sufficient mechanical strength during the critical healing phase.
Biocompatibility and tissue integration: The biocompatibility of biodegradable implants is essential for successful integration with host tissues. Degradation and resorption: The degradation rate is a critical factor influencing the performance of biodegradable implants. Research has shown that material selection and processing techniques can tailor the degradation rate. A study by Li et al, demonstrated that a novel composite implant exhibited a controlled degradation profile, allowing for sustained mechanical support during the healing process.

Species-specific consideration
The clinical outcomes associated with biodegradable implants in large animals such as sheep, goats, pigs, and rabbits demonstrate significant advancements in veterinary medicine. These outcomes are characterized by enhanced healing processes, effective stabilization of fractures, and overall improved integration of the implants.

Improved healing times

Sheep
Research indicates that biodegradable implants used in orthopedic surgeries lead to faster healing times than traditional metal implants. For instance, studies have shown that sheep receiving biodegradable implants exhibited quicker bone regeneration and reduced recovery periods.

Goats
In clinical evaluations, biodegradable screws for fracture fixation in goats have resulted in accelerated healing, allowing for earlier mobilization and return to normal activity.

Effective stabilization

Pigs
Using biodegradable stents in pigs has effectively stabilized vascular applications. These stents have been shown to maintain structural integrity while allowing for natural tissue integration, which is crucial for successful healing.

Rabbits
Though smaller than other species mentioned, they are frequently used for ease of handling and cost-effectiveness. In dental applications, biodegradable implants in rabbits have effectively stabilized dental structures, leading to successful outcomes in procedures such as tooth replacement and repair.

Biocompatibility
The favorable biocompatibility of biodegradable materials has led to minimal inflammatory responses in various animal models. This characteristic is essential for ensuring the implants integrate well with the surrounding tissues without causing adverse reactions.

Long-term outcomes
Studies have indicated that biodegradable implants integrate effectively and degrade at a controlled rate, allowing for the gradual transfer of load to the healing bone or tissue. This property is particularly beneficial in maintaining the mechanical environment necessary for optimal healing. Biomaterials are materials originating from natural resources or synthesized to design artificial implants and delivering agents due to their biomimetic properties shown in Table 1. The key characteristic features of the biomaterials include biocompatibility, surface functionality, nontoxicity and bio-degradability. Further, the European Society of Biomaterials defines Biomaterial as any substance that can be used in an organ at any time to treat, enhance, or restore body functions other than drugs or mixtures of drugs.
Key characteristic features of the biomaterials for biomedical application.
Besides that, for designing biodegradable implants, the materials must be non-inflammatory, controlled degradability, biomimetic functionality, nontoxic and readily resorbable byproducts release; tissue or organ-specific permeability and processability and sustainable (Fig. 1). In medical applications biocompatibility of the materials is an important aspect. Hence, the degradability of the non-biodegradable polymers is also tailored to improve the controlled degradation over time. Biomaterial-based therapies and tissue engineering approaches are hindered by the lack of a detailed understanding of how biomaterials interact with the immune system. The biomaterial community faces several challenges, including developing multifunctional materials with functionalities like degradability, drug release control, or stimuli sensitivities while overcoming immune barriers, inflammation, and endotoxin contamination. However, the biomedical implant design, natural (Collagen, chitosan) and a few biosynthetic polymers (PLA) are the most focused due to their bio-degradable and replacement of the matrix by the neo-tissue regenerated at the wound site. Due to their flexibility in chemistry, polymers can produce materials with various physical and mechanical properties. Biodegradable polymers are of utmost importance in biomaterials since they can be broken down and excreted or reabsorbed without surgical intervention. The mechanism of implanted graft degradation under in vivo conditions is either hydrolytic breakdown or enzymatic cleavage of the polymeric chain. The material’s degradation rate variability is affected by the polymer chemistry, molecular weight, polarity, corrosion rate, and hydrophilic nature. A great deal of effort must be put into improving current polymers and processing techniques to make polymers that perform better in biological media, among the major trends in related research and development. The development of novel polymers that are more biocompatible and bioresponsive is a significant trend in the field of biomaterials.

Even though modulating biomaterial chemistry to enhance material properties is limitless from a basic science perspective, it takes significant time and resources to accomplish the research. Applying biomaterials in the clinical setting raises several issues that previous in vitro experiments cannot adequately identify and address. However, preexamination of the biomaterial’s physicochemical and biological properties or host response is a must to develop bioengineered graft and drug-delivery agents. The variability of the degradation rate of the biomaterials is a significant issue that moderately changes over time and physiological conditions. The prolonged or long-term effects on the host body can differ significantly from its initial response because of these changes, including a selection of biomaterials (Table 2). These issues are nontrivial and have contributed to the slow evolution of biodegradable polymeric biomaterials as a field of research.
After World War II, the demand for and utility of the material drastically changed, and over time, the global market of biomaterials reached USD 47.5 billion by 2025. The constant demand for new biomaterials for medical implants, therapeutics, and translation research increased the market value of the products.
Metallic biomaterials such as Gold, Cobalt, titanium, austenitic steels, Magnesium alloy, etc., used as implants or delivery systems have long-term stability, nontoxicity, immune-tolerant, and excellent load-bearing capability. In the case of in vivo implantation, adverse outcomes of the metallic biomaterial are due to the corrosive nature of implants and the release of ionic toxicants in the surrounding tissue. In recent advancements to minimize the negative impact of these materials, biodegradable metallic scaffolds have been designed, for example, a 3D porous metallic stent (TiO with BMF; silver-coated carbon nanotubes or graphene sheets) with a polymeric coating. In the current scenario, nanosized or metallic nanoparticle-impregnated polymeric grafts are designed for complex tissue regeneration by improving the implant’s compatibility, degradability, and mechanical stability under in vivo conditions. Moreover, these metallic biomaterials are also used as delivery agents and improve the drug’s therapeutic index by enhancing the enzyme’s catalytic rate or metabolites. A pure zinc stent degraded due to endothelialization caused by water molecules, hydrophilic solutes, and ions, which led to localized corrosion, during degradation microenvironment conversion, zinc phosphate in blood flow becomes zinc oxide and calcium phosphate.
The corrosion resistance of the Ceramic biomaterial in the environment of tissues and body fluids shows a very high bio-tolerance and potential role in implant designing for osteogenesis and dental fixation. A wide range of biocompatible ceramics used in biomedical applications is Calcium, silicate derivatives, hydroxyapatite (HAp), bioactive glass (BG), zirconium oxide (ZrO2), aluminum oxide (Al2O3) to orthopedic and dental implants. The ceramic biomaterials category is inert- nonabsorbable, semi-inert- surface-reactive, and non-inert- resorbable in vivo conditions. The non-inert or resorbable ceramic, for example, calcium phosphate, has potential bio-degradability and nontoxic effect, with time response replacement by the neo-tissue formation. In other studies, the semi-inert ceramic material was also focused on due to their bioactive nature and bio-functionality for hard to soft tissue reconstruction, for example, carbonate apatite. In long-term non-replaceable graft or artificial prosthetic implants, designing neutral bio-ceramics focused on their long-term stability and inert nature, for example, auditory or bone implants. Ancient civilizations also utilized mineral-rich biomaterials such as sea shells to make dental fillings and artificial eyes to replace organ loss.
Polymers are the most focused and utilized biomaterial among all the types due to their tailored, cutting-edge properties, including acceptable mechanical, physical, and biochemical properties. In addition, the sustainable production and processivity of the polymer make it a suitable biomaterial for biomedical applications. Based on their origin, they are either natural (plant, animal, and microbial products) or synthetic (biomass and chemical synthesis). Natural polymeric is widely acceptable for biomedical applications, including surgical material or drug delivery systems, due to its biomimetic composition, cytocompatibility, nontoxicity, negligible inflammatory response and bio-degradable nature. The natural polymeric implant has a higher to moderate degradation rate, and batch variability results in poor mechanical and physicochemical stability under an in situ or host body environment. To overcome these issues, biosynthetic polymers, for example, PLA and PLGA, improved the mechanical stability of the implants. Also, the nontoxic degradation products of these polymers (lactic acid and glycolic acid) are formed by simple chemical hydrolysis of the polymers and are removed by normal metabolic pathways.
In general, polymeric nanoparticles are particles with a size in the range of 10–100 nanometers in one dimension. By incorporating inorganic nanoparticles into polymers, recent developments have altered the polymer’s physical and chemical properties and implemented new features within the polymer network. In various remedial applications, biodegradable polymeric nanostructures have demonstrated extraordinary promise.

Advancement and challenges of biodegradable implants
In 1950, the first pacemaker was developed as a biomaterial-based cardiovascular implant that laid down the platform for new surgical grafts, including cardiac stents, artificial bone implants, etc. These remarkable discoveries significantly improve patients’ survivability rates and are integral to the biomedical sector. Biomedical implants are now used to replace or support the function of organs, tissues, and other bodily structures, providing solutions for previously deemed untreatable conditions. However, in the 20th century, advancements in material science led to the discovery of novel biomaterials and the synthesis of new polymeric compositions based on their specificity for the particular tissue or organ micro-environment. An important factor in determining the outcome of human biology and materials interactions is the surface modification of biomaterials. Researchers have made significant strides in the field of biomaterials, which has resulted in a wide range of orthopedic and dental implants, including total replacement of bone, fracture fixation, dental screws, and joint arthrodesis.

Regarding bioimplants’ performance and reliability, coating choice is significant based on their degeneration and surface properties. It is easy to divide biomedical coatings into bioinerts, bioactives, and bioresorbables shown in Figure 3. Orthopedic and dental implant metallic or polymer composite implants coated with ceramic materials benefit tissue repair and regeneration. Bioinert coating of metal oxides, nitrides, carbides, carbonitrides, and oxynitrides has remarkable properties such as wear, tear, hardness, biocompatibility, and corrosion resistance of bioimplant. Metallic oxides of TiO2, ZrO2 coated bio-implants used for Maxillofacial reconstruction and Ophthalmic surgeries have anti-corrosive, anti-bacterial activity with neo-tissue regeneration potential at the wound site. Anti-corrosive chemically modified metallic implants, for example, Nitrides of TiN, ZrN, TiCN, ZrCN, TiAlN, and their oxide, that is, TiON, ZrON have to minimize frictional coefficient with improved adhesive properties for the dental and joint endoprostheses. In soft-tissue reconstruction, such as cardiac and ligament implants, the chemical composition of carbon-based coatings is excellent, providing excellent thermostability, high blood compatibility, excellent biocompatibility, and excellent hydrophobicity. These coatings include a-C, DLC, NCD, carbides, and carbonitrides. Materials such as calcium phosphates (CaP, HAp) and bioactive glass (biolytes in glass) are used for Spinal, Orthopedic, and Maxillofacial reconstruction. Skull plates have high osteointegration capability, excellent biocompatibility, and bioactivity in the host body. Composite ceramic implants not only enhance the bio-functionality but are also capable of improving the inferior mechanical properties of monolithic ceramics while at the same time reducing the limitations of their components. Composites like HAp and Al2O3 do well with bone, are bioactive, and have a high yield strength. Because of their remarkable weight-to-strength ratio, composites can be used extensively for fillings, bones, and ligaments. It is possible to replace these traditional implants with magnesium (Mg), which is biocompatible and biodegradable. In the physiological environment, Mg has low corrosion resistance. However, surface coatings can improve this resistance and are also helpful in noninvasive radiographic techniques in diagnosing and treating malignant tumors in animals and humans. Since Mg alloys are strong, lightweight, and naturally biodegradable, they have become a research frontier in biodegradable materials. Among Mg alloys for orthopedic implants, Young’s modulus is identical (20–45 GPa) and comparable to cortical bone (20–27 GPa). An HA coating on a magnesium substrate is a bioactive fixation site for new bone formation, as HA promotes osteoblastic cell binding and growth. It is also biocompatible, osteoinductive, and osteoconductive. There was a significant difference in the dissolution behavior of ceramic crystals coating AZ31 (Mg-Al–Zn alloy in vivo compared to the behavior in vitro, demonstrating that the coatings developed for the HAp and OCP (octacalcium phosphate) have great potential for corrosion protection and biocompatibility. Recent approaches focus on additive manufacturing processes to manufacture magnesium (Mg) alloys to overcome conventional manufacturing. Biomaterials can be developed using various additive manufacturing methods due to their ability to achieve complex geometrical shapes and material designs. As a result of tissue adhesion and reduced diffusivity on the surface in vivo, differences in corrosion morphology can be attributed. Polymeric implants contribute to the replacement or repair of the damaged tissue or organ and act as antibacterial, antioxidative and anti-inflammatory material in soft to complex tissue surgeries. Chitosan is widely used for coating the bio-implants and synthesizing drug-delivery vehicles due to sound degradation, biocompatibility, and antibacterial effect, it has a remarkable effect on complex metallic or ceramic implant functionality. Synthetic polymer composite with metallic oxides, for example, polyether ether ketone (PEEK) and TiO2 particle implants, showed higher compressive strength and better fatigue properties than pure PEEK. Therefore, the bio-functionality and physicochemical properties can be altered based on advanced materials’ tissue or organ anatomy, as shown in Figure 4. The biosynthetic implant of multiple polymeric blends, such as chitosan-PLA and Silk-PVP, was developed to make a biodegradable matrix with functionalized ECM proteins to improve cell signaling and better bio-integration.

Polymeric hydrogels for soft-tissue reconstruction provide a highly porous 3D matrix having tissue-specific degradability, viscoelasticity, stiffness, sorption rate, and biocompatibility. Examples of hydrogel synthesized from natural and synthetic polymers are cellulose, alginate, gelatine, and PLA. PEG, etc, for bone and skin tissue engineering shown in Figure 5. Compared with natural biomaterials, synthetic polymeric hydrogel having chemical surface functionalization with short peptides (RGD), GAGs, and other growth factors significantly stimulates 3D cell cultures. The alteration of their surface properties improves the hydrogel’s mechanical strength and chemical stability, which induces the implants’ wound sealing and healing properties. Recent studies showed that applying hydrogels as bio-ink for 3D printing of small organoids to complete organ design under in vitro conditions offers innovative opportunities by conquering the challenges of conventional bio-implants.
Size reduction or miniaturization of the implant to make portable and easy-to-access biomedical devices is the most significant trend in biomaterial designing. The reduction of the implant size not only improves the functioning of the implants but also improves comfort and aesthetics and reduces the risk of post-surgical complications. Since smaller invasive cardiac devices like leadless pacemakers were developed, healing times have been shortened, and infection risks have decreased.

In addition to miniaturization, topological porous absorbable metals have strongly emphasized enhancing biomedical implants’ durability. Durability is a critical factor in the success of any implant, as it determines the lifespan of the device and its ability to function effectively over time. Recent materials science and engineering advancements have added more resistance to wear and tear, corrosion, and mechanical stress. In orthopedic surgery, advanced alloys and ceramics are now used on implants to provide greater resistance to corrosion and can last for several decades shown in Figure 6. As a result, revision surgeries are becoming significantly less frequent, and the durability of the bio-implant has improved.
With the advancement of 3D printing and advanced imaging tools such as MRI and CT scans, the customized biodegradable implant provides the précised tailored material to mimic the patient anatomy of native tissue/organ. The better fitting of the implant reduces the risk of host inflammation and psychological acceptance by the patient. Hence, the advancement of 3D printing technology and material composition with imaging modalities (PET scan) made functional implants bio ink-transformed biomaterial for designing biocompatible grafts, especially in personalized medicine by allowing the creation of complex structures (maxillofacial implants) by layering materials in precise patterns. As a result of this technology, complex and multi-functional implants can be manufactured specifically for a particular patient.
Smart implants, for example, drug-eluting stents or grafts synthesis by incorporating drugs or bioactive molecules, allow précised, controlled and targeted release directly at the localized implantation site. Thus, the effectiveness of bioimplants for chronic inflammation tissue is improved, and post-surgical graft rejection is suppressed by tissue or cell-specific targeting with negligible side effects.

It has been proven that nanotechnology can increase the mechanical properties of implants without compromising their biocompatibility, which is one of its key advantages in implant materials. For example, carbon nanotubes and graphene have been incorporated into polymer matrices to produce composite materials that are strong, flexible, and wear-resistant. Advancement and challenges of use of large animals

Advancement
In recent years, integrating advanced biomaterials and innovative medical technologies into large animal surgical research has significantly enhanced the translational relevance of preclinical studies. Large animals such as pigs, sheep, and goats are now widely used to test and refine next-generation implants that aim to better mimic, integrate, and respond to the human body.

Smart implants
Smart implants are engineered to monitor physiological responses or deliver therapeutic actions after implantation. They include embedded sensors for real-time tracking of biomechanical loading, healing progress, temperature, or infection markers.
Large animal models, particularly pigs and sheep, are ideal for testing such devices due to their body weight, joint mechanics, and bone density, closely resembling human conditions.
Applications include orthopedic implants with load sensors, drug-eluting stents, or stimulus-responsive surfaces that release bioactive agents during healing.

Bioresorbable implants
Bioresorbable Implants, such as magnesium-based screws, plates, and vascular scaffolds, offer the advantage of gradual degradation, eliminating the need for surgical removal.
Porcine and ovine models are commonly used to evaluate biocompatibility, degradation rate, and mechanical integrity of bioresorbable materials under physiological conditions.
These models help simulate human bone turnover and vascular remodeling, critical for long-term implant success.

3D-printed constructs
3D printing technologies (including bioprinting) have created patient-specific scaffolds, implants, and surgical guides, significantly advancing reconstructive and orthopedic surgery.
Large animal models play a key role in testing 3D-printed titanium, ceramic, or polymer implants, especially in craniofacial, dental, and orthopedic applications.
These models provide insight into implant fit, osseointegration, and mechanical performance before clinical application.
Large animals provide the mechanical loads, immune responses, and anatomical challenges necessary to evaluate the clinical readiness of these advanced biomaterials and devices, offering a reliable bridge from bench to bedside.

Challenges

Anatomical and physiological relevance
Despite offering greater anatomical similarity to humans than small animals, large animal models (e.g. pigs, sheep, goats, dogs) still present species-specific anatomical and physiological differences that can hinder the full translation of surgical findings.
Anatomical discrepancies such as the coiled colon in pigs, thick dermis, and variations in skeletal alignment (quadruped vs. biped) may limit direct applicability in procedures involving the gastrointestinal tract, skin grafting, or orthopedics.
Physiological variations, including differences in cardiac conduction systems, wound healing rates, and growth dynamics, can affect the performance and evaluation of surgical implants and regenerative interventions.
Breed variability within species (e.g. Yorkshire pig vs. mini pig) also introduces intra-species differences that can affect data reproducibility unless models are standardized.
These anatomical and physiological challenges must be carefully considered during experimental design, especially when testing human-specific devices or surgical protocols.

Immunological and biocompatibility considerations
Another major challenge in using large animals surgically is their immune response, which may differ significantly from that of humans, particularly in the context of implantation, biomaterials, and regenerative therapies.
Pigs, for example, have strong innate immune responses, which can lead to overreaction or underreaction to foreign materials compared to human patients.
The foreign body response and macrophage activation patterns in large animals often vary from humans, impacting the study of biocompatibility and implant integration.
In regenerative medicine, species-specific immune rejection of stem cells, allografts, or xenografts can confound outcomes unless immunosuppressive strategies are applied.
There’s also limited availability of validated immunological reagents (e.g. antibodies and cytokine assays) for large animals, making mechanistic studies more challenging compared to mice or rats. AI is poised to transform veterinary medical education by enhancing how students learn and understand veterinary anatomy. Addressing immunological barriers is crucial for accurately evaluating how devices and therapies perform in the human immune environment.

Preclinical in vivo study and clinical study
Preclinical large animal in vivo experiments are an essential bridge between bench research and human clinical application, particularly for surgically implanted devices. Large animal models such as pigs, sheep, and goats offer anatomical and physiological similarities to humans, making them ideal for assessing biocompatibility, mechanical performance, immune responses, and long-term integration of implants. In the study, although the primary focus was on AI-assisted dermoscopic diagnostics, the high diagnostic precision (accuracy >95%, sensitivity 94.3%, specificity 96.7%) demonstrates the growing role of AI in enhancing data reliability and real-time analysis, which is increasingly being leveraged in preclinical studies. When applied to surgical implant studies, such AI tools can assist in noninvasive monitoring of implant sites, early detection of complications, and longitudinal assessment of healing processes. Furthermore, integrating smart implants and biosensors in large animal models enables dynamic data acquisition, providing insights into pressure changes, tissue growth, or infection – without sacrificing the animal. These developments reduce the ethical burden while improving the translational potential of preclinical findings. As regulatory agencies demand robust safety and efficacy data for device approval, extensive animal studies remain indispensable for evaluating implant design, surgical technique, and post-operative outcomes in a clinically relevant setting.
In the preclinical evaluation of aortic endografts, large animal models, particularly pigs, have proven invaluable due to their anatomical similarity to human vasculature. A novel study developed a reproducible pararenal aneurysm model in pigs using a bovine pericardial patch to support the testing of fenestrated endografts and renal bridging stents. The procedure involved precise surgical creation of the aneurysm above the renal arteries, confirmed via contrast-enhanced cone beam CT imaging. Eight out of nine animals developed stable aneurysms with no perioperative mortality, minimal complications, and preserved vessel patency throughout a median follow-up of 95 days. This model demonstrates potential for simulating human-compatible aneurysm conditions and could serve as a viable platform for evaluating advanced endovascular devices. Although anatomical discrepancies remain compared to human aneurysms, the model’s stability and scalability underscore its utility until more refined 3D-printed or alternative ethical models become mainstream.

Conclusion
Advancements in biodegradable implants are transforming regenerative medicine and orthopedic surgery by providing temporary mechanical support with active biological integration. Large animal models particularly pigs, sheep, and goats are essential for bridging preclinical research and human applications due to their anatomical and physiological similarities to humans. Innovations in biomaterials, including magnesium alloys, polymers, bio-ceramics, and 3D-printed scaffolds, have improved biocompatibility, degradability, and functionality. Emerging technologies like bioactive surfaces, smart sensors, and additive manufacturing further enhance clinical potential, promoting more personalized and minimally invasive treatments. However, challenges persist in controlling degradation rates, ensuring mechanical stability, and addressing immune responses. Variability among animal models and differences from human physiology also demand better model selection and standardized protocols. Future efforts should focus on optimizing materials, streamlining manufacturing, and aligning testing with regulatory standards. Technologies such as AI, nanotechnology, and biosensing offer promising solutions. Ultimately, ongoing innovation, rigorous animal studies, and interdisciplinary collaboration are key to translating biodegradable implants into effective, patient-specific therapies that advance precision medicine and sustainable healthcare. As the field evolves, it will redefine implantable devices and significantly contribute to the broader goals of precision medicine and sustainable healthcare.
