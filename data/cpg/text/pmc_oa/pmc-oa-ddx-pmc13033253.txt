The Therapeutic Approach and Mechanism of Action of Insulin Eye Drops for the Treatment of Neurotrophic Keratitis
Abstract
Neurotrophic keratopathy or keratitis (NK) is a degenerative corneal disorder characterised by reduced corneal sensitivity and impaired epithelial repair, most often associated with trigeminal nerve dysfunction. Topical insulin eye drops have been proposed as a potential adjunctive therapy, but wider clinical adoption is constrained by variability in preparation methods, inconsistent dosing approaches, and the absence of consensus guidance or regulatory approval. Current clinical experience suggests that topical insulin may be well tolerated and could offer therapeutic value in selected NK settings, including difficult-to-manage cases. Nonetheless, the overall evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous, making it challenging to define optimal regimens or confidently position topical insulin within existing treatment pathways. This review includes clinical studies and case reports and highlights the role of TI as a promising, cost-effective and well-tolerated treatment option for neurotrophic keratopathy, especially in refractory cases. This work also illustrates the need for further research to standardise formulation and dosing, establish practical clinical guidance, and clarify the patient populations and disease stages most likely to benefit, as well as the role of topical insulin alongside established interventions.

Introduction
Neurotrophic keratopathy (NK) is a rare, degenerative disorder of the cornea characterised by partial or complete loss of corneal sensory innervation, leading to impaired epithelial maintenance and defective wound healing. The condition arises primarily from dysfunction of the trigeminal nerve and may result from a wide range of ocular and systemic insults, including herpetic keratitis, chemical or thermal injury, topical drug toxicity, contact lens (CL) misuse, radiotherapy, and traumatic or iatrogenic nerve damage. Systemic diseases such as multiple sclerosis and diabetes mellitus can be causative factors. Intracranial tumours, eg., acoustic neuroma, can also damage the trigeminal nerve and lead to NK. Strokes, aneurysms or congenital hypoplasia of the trigeminal nerve can also be causative factors.
Eye surgery can also lead to NK. In corneal surgery, refractive surgery and transplants can be causes: laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK), photorefractive keratectomy (PRK), corneal cross-linking, penetrating keratoplasty (PK) and deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) can result in reduced corneal sensitivity. Vitrectomy or retinal photocoagulation have been described in association with the development or aggravation of NK.
Due to the critical role of corneal innervation in maintaining epithelial integrity and trophic support, sensory loss predisposes the cornea to progressive epithelial breakdown, ulceration, and, in severe cases, stromal melting and perforation. The clinical presentation of NK is heterogeneous and often deceptively mild in early stages, contributing to delayed diagnosis and treatment. To standardise disease severity and guide therapy, NK is commonly classified according to the Mackie staging system, which stratifies the disease into three stages based on epithelial and stromal involvement. Management strategies are therefore stage-adapted and focus on protecting the ocular surface, promoting epithelial healing, and preventing sight-threatening complications. In stage 1, the focus is on intensive surface treatment with preservative-free lubricants (PF-L). All other topical eye drops should be discontinued. Stage 2 focuses treatment on protecting the corneal surface that is already significantly affected: therapeutic-CL, surgical tarsorrhaphy, injection of botulinum toxin A into the eyelid levator muscle or amniotic membrane grafting (AMG). In stage 3 therapy recommendations range from cyanoacrylate glue followed by therapeutic-CL, tarsorrhaphy with conjunctival flap, and lamellar or PK.
The emerging use of topical insulin in neurotrophic keratitis mirrors a broader trend in ophthalmology toward therapeutic repurposing, wherein agents developed for non-corneal or non-ophthalmic indications are subsequently applied to ocular surface disease. A well-recognised example is the translational trajectory of Rho kinase inhibitors, which were initially approved for intraocular pressure reduction in glaucoma, but later demonstrated biological effects relevant to ocular tissue remodeling and wound healing. Similar repurposing strategies have been observed with nerve growth factor, originally characterised for its neurotrophic properties, now approved for the treatment of neurotrophic keratitis, as well as with autologous serum eye drops, which leverage systemic growth factors to promote corneal epithelial repair. Within this evolving landscape, insulin—long established as a systemic antidiabetic therapy—has gained increasing attention for its growth-factor-like properties and potential to enhance corneal epithelial healing and neurotrophic support.
This review paper aims to provide an overview of the current state of knowledge regarding the use of topical insulin in the treatment of neurotrophic keratopathy. We will discuss the underlying mechanisms by which insulin promotes corneal healing and clinical studies along with case reports that have evaluated the efficacy and safety of insulin eye drops. Finally, we will highlight potential future directions for research, including optimization of dosing, formulation stability, and early-stage interventions, in order to better integrate topical insulin into standardised clinical management of corneal disease.

Staging of Neurotrophic Keratopathy
As a degenerative disease of the cornea, the neurotrophic keratopathy is caused by a partial loss or complete loss of cornea sensitivity and impairment of corneal healing as a result by a reduced innervation of the trigeminal nerve. Causes for the development of NK can be every ocular or systemic disease, which leads to a loss of the sensory innervation. Most common are herpetic keratitis, chemical and thermal burns, topical drug toxicity, misuse of contact lenses, corneal surgery, radiation of the eye or the region next to it and nerve injury traumatic or iatrogenic.
The clinical presentation is heterogenic, ranging from mild corneal surface irregularities to corneal epithelial defects and corneal stromal ulceration potentially leading to corneal perforation.

The use of insulin as a topical treatment for corneal ulcers dates back to 1945, when its therapeutic potential on the ocular surface was first described. Subsequent advances in ocular surface biology revealed the presence of insulin-like growth factor (IGF) receptors on the corneal epithelium, as well as measurable levels of insulin in human tear film, providing a biological rationale for its local application. Since these discoveries, multiple studies have demonstrated beneficial effects of topical insulin on corneal homeostasis and wound healing, although the precise mechanisms underlying its pro-epithelialization effects are not yet fully elucidated. Figure 2 presents serial slit-lamp images of a neurotrophic persistent epithelial defect cured by treatment with compounded insulin eye drops in isotonic saline solution.
In vitro studies have shown that topical insulin promotes corneal wound healing by stimulating epithelial cell and keratocyte proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and enhancing epithelialization through activation of key intracellular signaling pathways, including extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathways. In addition to its effects on epithelial repair, insulin has been shown to support keratocyte function in cultured corneal cells and to promote corneal nerve regeneration in diabetic animal models, contributing to the restoration of corneal sensitivity.

Mechanism of Action
Insulin eye drops are thought to promote corneal healing in neurotrophic keratitis through several interconnected pathways involving the insulin/IGF signaling system, which is richly expressed throughout the cornea. The corneal epithelium expresses insulin receptors (INSR), Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 Receptor (IGF-1R), Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 Receptor (IGF-2R), and INSR/IGF-1R hybrid receptors, allowing insulin to activate not only canonical insulin receptors but also partially cross-reactive IGF receptors. Activation of these receptors stimulates intracellular cascades such as PI3K–Akt, Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase MAPK/ERK, and mechanistic Target of Rapamycin (mTOR), which collectively enhance cell survival, proliferation, migration, and metabolic activity—all processes that are compromised in neurotrophic keratitis due to loss of corneal sensory innervation. Through these pathways, insulin mimics growth-factor–like behavior on the ocular surface, compensating for the deficiency of endogenous trophic support.
The IGF axis further amplifies insulin’s therapeutic potential. IGF-1, which is distributed throughout corneal layers but most concentrated near differentiating epithelial cell nuclei, interacts with E-cadherin complexes to promote tight cell–cell adhesion and epithelial integrity. By stimulating receptors that overlap with IGF-1R signaling, topical insulin can indirectly enhance epithelial cohesion and barrier restoration—critical functions in a disease characterised by non-healing epithelial defects. This augmentation of cell adhesion and migration helps re-establish the epithelial basement membrane and supports rapid resurfacing of the wounded cornea.
Additionally, IGF-2R, present in both corneal epithelial and stromal cells, undergoes marked upregulation after corneal injury, particularly in stromal myofibroblasts. While IGF-2R is traditionally considered a “scavenger receptor,” its increased expression during repair suggests roles in matrix remodeling, growth-factor trafficking, and regulation of fibroblast activity. Insulin’s interaction with the broader IGF signaling network may help modulate stromal repair, reduce pathologic fibrosis, and stabilise the epithelial–stromal interface. Together, these mechanisms explain why insulin eye drops can accelerate epithelial healing, improve cellular organization, and restore ocular surface homeostasis in neurotrophic keratitis.

Type of Insulin, Treatment Duration and Dosage Recommendations
Most published studies examining the use of insulin eye drops have reported a standard administration frequency of four times daily. However, a smaller subset of studies has also explored the efficacy and safety of twice-daily dosing regimens, suggesting some flexibility in treatment schedules depending on patient needs or study design. Over time, clinical investigations have shifted toward the use of lower insulin concentrations in ocular formulations, with 1 IU/mL emerging as the most frequently studied concentration in recent research. Despite this trend towards lower doses, studies have also evaluated much higher concentrations, with doses up to 100 IU/mL in saline solutions. These higher-dose formulations have been demonstrated to be safe and well tolerated by the human eye, even with prolonged and repeated administration, and no evidence of ocular toxicity has been observed.
Regarding the type of insulin used in the formulation of eye drops, most clinical studies employed rapid-acting human insulin analogs, primarily regular human insulin or insulin lispro, due to their stability in aqueous solution and predictable receptor-binding kinetics. Early exploratory work in corneal models showed that rapid-acting insulin retains biological activity when diluted into preservative-free saline or artificial tears, maintaining its ability to stimulate epithelial proliferation and migration. Subsequent clinical case series and pilot trials continued to rely on these rapid-acting formulations, citing their favorable pharmacokinetic profile, rapid receptor activation, and low risk of aggregation or precipitation at ophthalmic concentrations. For example, several clinical reports describing the successful closure of persistent epithelial defects used diluted regular insulin at concentrations ranging from 1 to 50 IU/mL, while others demonstrated effective healing with insulin lispro, which remains stable in solution even after repeated refrigeration cycles.

Clinical Studies and Case Reports
Recent clinical evidence highlights the efficacy of TI in promoting corneal epithelial healing in patients with NK of diverse etiologies. In a retrospective study of patients with NK following diabetic vitrectomy, Eleiwa et al (2024) demonstrated that treatment with insulin eye drops four times daily significantly accelerated epithelial closure compared with standard therapy, with mean re-epithelialization times of 10.9 ± 6.8 days versus 23.1 ± 11.6 days in the control group. Similarly, a prospective pilot study conducted in India by Jain et al (2025) reported a mean healing time of 21.5 ± 8.8 days across 22 eyes affected by NK from various causes, including chemical injury, neuroparalytic keratitis, and post-corneal transplantation. Notably, visual outcomes improved alongside epithelial repair, with mean best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) increasing from 1.1 ± 0.2 logMAR units at baseline to 0.8 ± 0.3 logMAR units at two months, indicating that topical insulin may contribute both to structural and functional corneal recovery.
Further evidence supports the utility of topical insulin across different disease severities and stages. Soares et al (2022) retrospectively analyzed 21 patients with stage II–III NK and observed epithelialization within 7 to 45 days in 90% of cases, with faster healing in stage II (18 ± 9 days) compared with stage III NK (29 ± 11 days). Padda et al included 29 eyes from 28 patients with various causes of NK. Interestingly, they did not focus exclusively on patients with NK II–III, but also included patients with mild NK I. 83.3% of NK I cases could be improved with IT. These findings suggest that earlier-stage disease may respond more rapidly, emphasizing the potential benefit of timely initiation of insulin therapy. Additionally, formulation and stability considerations have been addressed in a galenic study by Le Nguyen et al (2022), demonstrating that insulin eye drops in an artificial tear vehicle maintain microbiological and physicochemical stability suitable for clinical use. Collectively, these studies provide compelling evidence for the safety, tolerability, and effectiveness of topical insulin in accelerating epithelial repair in refractory NK, supporting its emerging role as a cost-effective adjunct or alternative to conventional therapies.
Across published case reports and clinical studies, TI has demonstrated consistent efficacy in the treatment of NK of heterogeneous etiologies, including herpetic keratitis, tumor- and radiotherapy-related cranial nerve injury, postoperative and surgery-induced NK, congenital neuropathies, autoimmune disease, chemical burns, and systemic conditions. In herpetic NK, multiple reports described rapid epithelial healing following TI in patients with persistent epithelial defects refractory to prior treatments such as scleral lenses, amniotic membrane grafting, tarsorrhaphy, autologous serum eye drops, and topical steroids, with complete re-epithelialization often achieved within weeks. In patients with NK secondary to tumor excision or radiotherapy involving trigeminal nerve damage, TI led to significant reduction and complete closure of epithelial defects within 10–21 days, despite failure of extensive prior conservative and surgical therapies, with stable outcomes during follow-up. Several cases of surgery-induced NK following vitrectomy, penetrating keratoplasty, deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty, neurosurgical procedures, and corneal transplantation demonstrated complete epithelial healing within 2–5 weeks after initiation of TI, often after months of unsuccessful conventional therapy. Paediatric cases, including NK associated with congenital glaucoma surgery, orbital tumors, and congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis syndrome, showed rapid epithelial closure within 13–14 days and long-term corneal stability under continued TI therapy. In autoimmune-associated NK, such as Sjögren’s syndrome, bilateral epithelial defects unresponsive to lubricants and cyclosporine A healed completely within three weeks of TI initiation, with sustained results at six months. Chronic and severe NK following chemical burns and post-infectious complications likewise responded to TI after years of failed medical and surgical interventions, achieving complete corneal re-epithelialization within weeks to months. Larger observational and comparative studies further confirmed these findings, demonstrating significantly faster healing times, higher epithelial closure rates, improved visual acuity, and partial recovery of corneal sensitivity in patients treated with TI compared with conventional therapy alone, without reported ocular or systemic adverse events. The results of the included studies show an efficacy of IT use in NK ranging from 77.2% to 94.7%.
An important limitation of the current evidence base is the possibility of publication bias, particularly given the substantial proportion of case reports and small case series. Successful or rapidly responsive cases are more likely to be reported than treatment failures, equivocal outcomes, or delayed responses, which may lead to an overestimation of the apparent efficacy and tolerability of topical insulin. In addition, adverse events may be underrepresented in such reports. These considerations further support the need for larger prospective and controlled studies to define the true therapeutic effect of topical insulin in neurotrophic keratopathy.

Evidence Comparing Insulin Eye Drops to Autologous Serum Eye Drops
Key studies comparing insulin eye drops with autologous serum for neurotrophic keratitis and persistent epithelial defects show consistently promising results for insulin. The comparative series by Diaz-Valle et al (2022) reported not only faster re-epithelialization in the insulin-treated group but also a higher proportion of complete epithelial closure within the observation period, along with fewer cases requiring amniotic membrane transplantation and lower rates of defect recurrence compared with autologous serum—though the study remained limited by its non-randomised, single-center design. Abdi et al (2024) expanded the evidence by examining insulin in patients with refractory and often long-standing epithelial defects, demonstrating marked reductions in defect area over the treatment course and noting that patients with smaller baseline defects healed more rapidly, highlighting a potential dose–response or severity–response relationship. Recent systematic and narrative reviews (2024–2025) integrate these findings with earlier case series, emphasizing insulin’s biological rationale—its trophic, pro-migratory, and pro-proliferative effects on corneal epithelium—and noting generally good tolerability. At the same time, they underscore persistent limitations such as small sample sizes, variability in insulin concentrations and compounding methods, and the absence of large randomised trials. In contrast, autologous serum continues to have a more established clinical foundation, supported by multiple observational studies and meta-analyses documenting benefits in epithelial healing and ocular surface rehabilitation. Importantly, a randomised controlled trial protocol directly comparing topical insulin with autologous serum has recently been published, indicating that more definitive comparative data are forthcoming.

Insulin Eye Drops versus Cenegermin
Cenegermin (OXERVATE, Dompé, Milan, Italy) is a recombinant human nerve growth factor (rhNGF) formulated as an ophthalmic solution for the treatment of stage 2 and 3 neurotrophic keratitis. By replacing deficient endogenous NGF at the ocular surface, cenegermin promotes corneal epithelial healing, enhances corneal innervation, and supports trophic maintenance of corneal tissues.
The therapy received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2018 following the positive clinical outcomes demonstrated in the pivotal REPARO trial, a randomized, double-masked, vehicle-controlled Phase II study. In this study, patients treated with cenegermin showed significantly higher rates of complete corneal healing compared with vehicles, along with sustained epithelial integrity during follow-up. These encouraging findings established rhNGF replacement therapy as the first pharmacologic treatment specifically targeting the underlying pathophysiology of neurotrophic keratitis rather than solely providing supportive care.
In terms of clinical efficacy, insulin eye drops have demonstrated comparable outcomes across a series of clinical studies, showing no inferiority to cenegermin. For example, in the cenegermin trials REPARO34 and NGF021435, efficacy rates of 72% and 65.2% were reported at 8 weeks, respectively. Studies investigating insulin eye drops have reported comparable ultimate healing rates (see Table 2). Padda et al emphasise, based on the final cure rate of 78.3% in their study, that IT is a strong alternative when patients have already exhausted all other treatment options but do not have access to Cenegermin.
Despite the demonstrated clinical efficacy of Cenegermin, its widespread use is limited by substantial cost, with an estimated price of approximately $100,000 for a standard 8-week treatment course. This high expense poses a considerable barrier to patient access. In addition, the medication is currently not available for clinical use in many countries, in contrast to insulin. As a more affordable alternative, topical insulin, estimated at roughly $150 for an equivalent 8-week course, has emerged as a potential therapeutic option. Moreover, cenegermin requires strict cold-chain handling (freezer storage at the pharmacy and refrigeration at home) and entails a relatively complex, multi-component daily administration process, which may pose practical challenges for some patients; additionally, long-term outcome data remain limited.

Cost Effectiveness and Future Directions
Cost considerations are increasingly relevant as topical insulin gains clinical traction for neurotrophic keratitis. Although no formal cost-effectiveness analyses specifically comparing insulin eye drops with autologous serum have been published, several practical economic advantages can be inferred from existing literature. Autologous serum therapy requires phlebotomy, sterile serum processing, controlled freezing, and frequent batch preparation, all of which elevate cost and limit accessibility - especially in centers without dedicated ocular surface laboratories. In contrast, insulin eye drops can be compounded from commercially available human insulin, which is inexpensive, widely accessible, and does not require blood donation or centrifugation. Reports describing the preparation of topical insulin consistently highlight its low material cost and suitability for compounding in standard sterile pharmacy settings. Because many patients with neurotrophic keratitis need prolonged therapy, the relatively low production cost of insulin drops may translate into meaningful savings compared with autologous serum—particularly in resource-limited environments or regions without serum-processing facilities. Insulin eye drops represent a potentially cost-effective therapeutic option for neurotrophic keratopathy, particularly when compared with existing advanced treatments such as recombinant human nerve growth factor (cenegermin) or amniotic membrane transplantation.
Looking ahead, future directions in this field focus on both refining formulations and strengthening clinical evidence. Several reviews emphasise the need for standardised insulin concentrations, improved stability studies, and validated compounding protocols to ensure consistent biological activity and sterility. Additional research is exploring sustained-release insulin delivery, such as nanocarriers, hydrogels, and polymer-based systems, which may further enhance corneal bioavailability and reduce dosing frequency. As mechanistic studies continue to elucidate insulin’s interactions with the IGF axis in the cornea, these innovations may produce next-generation biologically targeted therapies that combine affordability with advanced drug-delivery technology.

Treatment Recommendations
Early treatment of NK is considered crucial to prevent disease progression and irreversible corneal damage. Dana et al emphasised that NK should be treated as early as possible; however, early intervention remains challenging because initial symptoms are often mild and nonspecific. This leads to delayed diagnosis.
Therefore, the question arises how the use of TI would affect the development of the disease even in mild NK and whether TI could prevent the development of severe cases.
Eleiwa et al reported that corneal sensitivity could be restored in some cases through TI, which also gives pause for thought about the effect of TI in NK I, where there is no epithelial defect but sensation loss.
Across the assessed studies and case reports, no adverse events related to TI were reported. The favorable safety profile and tolerability of TI have been discussed in previous publications by Bartlett et al and Mancini et al; however, data regarding ocular penetration, ocular toxicity, and potential systemic side effects remain limited. This lack of comprehensive safety data may represent another reason for hesitation in prescribing TI for NK.
In addition to regulatory and evidentiary uncertainties, challenges related to the production and limited shelf life of TI may further complicate its clinical applicability. Although extended stability has been achieved in isolated experimental settings, additional research is required to establish reliable and standardised formulations suitable for routine clinical use.
The case reports and studies included in our analysis share several common characteristics. In nearly all cases, TI was prescribed as an off-label treatment after a prolonged history of therapy-refractory NK. Prior to the initiation of TI, many patients had already undergone surgical interventions (eg., AMG, tarsorrhaphy) and received high-cost therapies such as autologous serum eye drops. The duration of treatment before TI initiation often extended over several months. Notably, once TI was introduced, clinical improvement was frequently observed within weeks and, in some cases, within only a few days. These observations raise the question of why TI is not used earlier in the management of advanced NK, alongside established therapeutic options such as PF-L, AMG, temporary or permanent tarsorrhaphy, cyclosporine A eye drops, bandage contact lenses, autologous serum eye drops, punctal plugs, and vitamin A ointment. One likely explanation is the off-label status of TI. TI is not approved by either the European Medicines Agency (EMA) or the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for ophthalmic use and therefore must be prescribed off-label. Off-label prescribing requires extensive patient counseling, including discussion of potential adverse effects, which may represent an initial barrier to its broader adoption.
Another major contributing factor may be the absence of standardised treatment guidelines for NK. Our review of existing standards of care revealed that there are currently no national guidelines in Germany or Switzerland, nor any international European or global recommendations, that address NK as an isolated clinical entity. Dana et al conducted a pivotal study in which a panel of eleven experts used a validated methodology (modified RAND/UCLA Delphi method) to establish consensus recommendations for the screening, diagnosis, and treatment of NK. A total of 735 clinical scenarios were evaluated. For all patients with NK stages I–III, the panel recommended preservative-free lubricating drops, ointments and gels, punctal plugs, and autologous serum eye drops. For stage II NK, additional recommendations included prophylactic antibiotic eye drops, tetracyclines as matrix metalloproteinase inhibitors, bandage contact lenses, and AMG. For stage III NK, consensus recommendations consisted of AMG, synthetic tissue adhesives, tarsorrhaphy, and corneal neurotization. Notably, even the off-label-use of TI was not addressed in these recommendations.
Similarly, Sacchetti et al published recommendations focusing on accurate diagnosis, staging, and stage-adapted treatment of NK; however, TI was not mentioned despite its promising clinical results.

Conclusion
TI represents a promising, cost-effective, and well-tolerated therapeutic option for neurotrophic keratopathy, particularly in refractory cases where conventional treatments have failed to achieve sufficient corneal healing. Its regenerative potential and ease of administration make it an attractive candidate for broader clinical use. However, several critical gaps in knowledge and safety must be addressed before TI can be implemented routinely and with full confidence. Standardised preparation protocols, consensus-based clinical guidelines, and official regulatory approval are currently lacking, and their development is essential to ensure consistent quality and reproducibility. In addition, studies involving larger, well‐characterised patient cohorts are needed to validate current findings, while systematic assessments of possible adverse effects are required to better define the therapy’s safety profile. Interpretation of the available evidence should remain cautious, as the predominance of case reports and small case series may introduce publication bias, favouring the reporting of positive outcomes.
Further investigation is also necessary to refine therapeutic strategies and determine TI’s optimal placement within the NK treatment algorithm. Key areas of interest include establishing evidence-based dosing regimens, identifying the most effective duration and frequency of administration, and evaluating the relative efficacy of TI as a monotherapy compared with its use in combination with other established or emerging treatments. Moreover, exploring the potential benefits of TI in earlier stages of NK may reveal whether earlier intervention could delay disease progression or reduce long-term complications. Collectively, these research efforts will be crucial for translating TI from a promising experimental therapy into a standardised, evidence-based clinical tool.
