Protecting peritoneal dialysis patients: evaluation and prevention strategies for noninfectious risk factors
Abstract
Noninfectious risk factors in peritoneal dialysis (PD) are often overlooked, yet they significantly worsen patient outcomes. This review highlights nine key risk factors: advanced age and psychosocial vulnerability, malnutrition and sarcopenia, glucose and lipid metabolism disorders, hypoalbuminemia, chronic microinflammation, gut dysbiosis, kidney anemia, chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder, and volume overload with elevated intra-abdominal pressure. These factors interact to increase the risks of death, decline of residual kidney function, peritoneal fibrosis and cardiovascular events. We outline practical, evidence-based protective strategies that integrate nutritional and physical rehabilitation, biocompatible dialysis regimens, individualized fluid and dialysis prescription, targeted pharmacotherapy, psychosocial support, and regular monitoring of integrated inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers. The goal is to help clinicians improve long-term outcomes and quality of life for PD patients.

Introduction
Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive condition that may ultimately result in complete kidney failure, leading to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). Patients with ESKD require kidney replacement therapies, such as dialysis or kidney transplantation. Globally, the incidence of CKD is increasing, which is associated with factors such as aging population and increasing prevalence of chronic diseases like diabetes and hypertension.
Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is one of the kidney replacement therapies for patients with ESKD. It has gained widespread attention due to its advantages such as simple operation, preservation of residual kidney function (RKF), and low medical costs. Currently, PD patients account for approximately 9% of all kidney replacement therapy recipients globally, and this proportion is on an annual upward trend. Despite its advantages, PD is not without risks. Peritonitis, ultrafiltration failure, metabolic derangements, cardiovascular complications, and malnutrition can all compromise treatment efficacy and diminish patients’ quality of life. PD related infections have been studied extensively, generating a robust literature of reviews and evidence-based guidelines. By contrast, noninfectious risk factors remain fragmented across disparate reports, and no comprehensive synthesis has yet been attempted. Because these risks substantially influence treatment success and patient prognosis, a comprehensive understanding is essential for designing effective preventive strategies. This article offers a narrative review that integrates contemporary evidence from major clinical studies, consensus guidelines, and pathophysiological research to develop a comprehensive framework for understanding noninfectious risks associated with peritoneal dialysis.
To provide a coherent and clinically actionable framework, this review organizes noninfectious determinants into 4 interconnected domains: (1) Patient-centered factors, including demographics, lifestyle behaviors, and psychosocial health; (2) Nutritional, metabolic, and inflammatory dysregulation; (3) Systemic complications of CKD, such as Intestinal Dysbiosis, kidney anemia and Chronic Kidney Disease-Mineral and Bone Disorder (CKD-MBD); (4) Dialysis prescription and technical considerations, encompassing volume management, intra-abdominal pressure, catheter issues, and dialysate biocompatibility. This categorization is grounded in current clinical guidelines and pathophysiological understanding, aiming to highlight not only individual risks but also their synergistic interactions that collectively drive adverse outcomes in PD patients. This review draws on peer-reviewed literature identified through PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library, with a focus on publications from January 2010 to June 2025. Priority was given to randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, meta-analyses, and consensus guidelines – particularly those issued by the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis (ISPD). Landmark older studies were included when they provided foundational pathophysiological or clinical insights.

Patient characteristics: the influence of age, gender, and lifestyle
Age and cumulative time on peritoneal dialysis are independent predictors of mortality in PD patients, with risk increasing as either factor rises driven by chronic dialysate exposure, age-related physiological decline, accumulating comorbidities, and reduced tolerance to therapy. Notably, elderly PD patients often report poorer health-related quality of life compared to younger patients, likely due to higher burden of comorbidities (such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease), age-related physiological decline, and polypharmacy. These symptoms can limit physical activity, worsen sarcopenia, and impair psychosocial well-being – underscoring the need to prioritize symptom management and patient-centered goals alongside traditional clinical metrics.
A study conducted in the United States indicates that women have a 22% lower risk of experiencing failure in PD technology compared to men. A 25-year study found that women had a 30% lower risk of technical failure compared to men. The influence of sex on PD outcomes remains contentious. While U.S. data link female sex to a lower risk of technique failure, a Chinese cohort study identified female sex as an independent predictor of both withdrawal from PD and reduced survival. Premenopausal women have markedly higher estrogen levels than men, and estrogen confers significant cardioprotective and antioxidant benefits. However, women on PD appear to face a higher incidence of cognitive impairment, which in turn drives elevated hospitalization rates. Compared with women, men more frequently engage in unhealthy behaviors such as smoking and alcohol drinking. Consequently, when interpreting sex-related differences in PD outcomes, well-designed clinical trials that rigorously control for these lifestyle confounders are essential.
Unhealthy lifestyle behaviors substantially elevate the risk of complications in individuals undergoing PD. Compared to healthy individuals, PD patients have dialysate fluid in their abdominal cavity for most of the day, and this continuous dwell often causes abdominal fullness or discomfort, reducing their motivation to engage in physical exercise. Although smoking is recognized for increasing the incidence and mortality rates associated with cardiovascular disease, this effect is especially pronounced in dialysis patients. A meta-analysis indicates that smoking further exacerbates the risk of cardiovascular complications and heightens all-cause mortality in this population. The 2025 ISPD teaching guideline explicitly identifies depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment, frailty, and lack of social support as key domains requiring assessment prior to PD initiation. Depression, anxiety, low health literacy, and limited social support are prevalent among PD patients and have been consistently linked to higher rates of technique failure, peritonitis, and hospitalization. These factors can impair self-care capacity, reduce treatment adherence, and diminish motivation for daily exchanges. Routine screening for mood disorders and social vulnerability – ideally during initial assessment and follow-up – should be integrated into multidisciplinary PD care to enable early psychosocial intervention and support.

Nutritional, metabolic, and inflammatory dysregulation

Nutrition-related risk factors
Malnutrition is common in PD patients and impairs immunity, delays wound healing, and compromises overall health. A meta-analysis confirms that malnutrition is a robust predictor of mortality in the dialysis population. Early identification and proactive management of nutrition-related risk factors are integral to individualized PD care.

Body-mass index: obesity paradox
Body-mass index (BMI) is the standard anthropometric gauge of adiposity in humans. A global meta-analysis of PD cohorts revealed an early ‘obesity paradox’: during the first year of PD, a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 sharply increased short-term mortality, whereas overweight or obese patients experienced lower death rates. Yet when follow-up was extended to two years and again to 3–5 years, the mortality differences across BMI strata vanished. A meta-analysis confined to Asian PD patients uncovered a U-shaped association between BMI and long-term survival: both underweight and obesity were linked to progressively worse survival, whereas patients in the mid-range BMI category experienced the best outcomes. A 2023 Nature article emphasized that BMI, by accounting only for height and weight, overlooks critical variables such as age, ethnicity, body-fat percentage, and sex, rendering it an imperfect gauge of health. Fat is a significant source of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Clinical studies have shown that among individuals with a normal BMI, those whose body-fat percentage exceeds 30% have markedly higher circulating levels of Interleukin (lL)-6 and Tumor necrosis factor -α(TNF-α) than do their leaner counterparts. A prospective study of 223 PD patients revealed that those with sarcopenic obesity faced a significantly higher mortality risk than non-sarcopenic, non-obese patients. Yet the PD literature continues to rely almost exclusively on BMI, an approach that obscures crucial risk modifiers – most notably body-fat percentage. Many individuals classified as ‘overweight’ or ‘obese’ by BMI are in fact high-muscle, low-fat phenotypes, potentially inflating survival estimates in higher-BMI groups. We therefore urge future PD studies to adopt a multidimensional assessment of adiposity and body composition. Key metrics should include body-fat percentage, waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, visceral fat area, and composite indices such as the visceral adiposity index or obesity index, alongside BMI.

Sarcopenia: diverse etiological factors, digestive issues, and adverse prognosis
As RKF declines, dietary restrictions tighten, the continuous presence of dialysate creates persistent metabolic and mechanical stress, and complications accrue, PD patients experience reduced muscle-protein synthesis and dysregulation of the pathways governing muscle-cell growth, turnover, and apoptosis – culminating in sarcopenia. Sarcopenic patients consistently show elevated systemic inflammation. A large meta-analysis found that muscle strength is inversely correlated with circulating levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), IL-6, and TNF -α. Exposure of myotubes to elevated CRP reduces myofiber diameter and suppresses muscle-protein synthesis, an effect linked to CRP-mediated inhibition of AKT phosphorylation and concurrent enhancement of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that PD patients with sarcopenia face markedly worse long-term outcomes, including heightened cardiovascular-event incidence and increased mortality, compared with their non-sarcopenic counterparts. Sarcopenia also independently heightens the risk of falls and fractures in PD patient. In PD, sarcopenia is driven by uremic, nutritional, and dialytic stress; fuels systemic inflammation; and independently predicts cardiovascular events, mortality, and falls.

Abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism: a potential catalyst for cardiovascular complications
The majority of patients undergoing PD utilize glucose-based dialysate, which increases systemic glucose load. Empirical evidence indicates a direct correlation between peritoneal glucose absorption and dyslipidemia, with elevated absorption levels associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. Guo et al. identified diabetes as an independent predictor of PD technique failure. In a 1-year cohort of >10,000 incident ESKD patients with diabetes, Rhee et al. showed that HbA1c ≥8.0% predicted worse outcomes, whereas high high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was protective and elevated high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) increased risk. Currently, numerous novel composite parameters have been developed that enhance the prognostic accuracy for patients with PD and possess significant clinical utility. An elevated triglyceride-glucose–BMI index (TyG-BMI) independently predicts cardiovascular mortality in PD patients. Elevated serum triglyceride-glucose (TyG) levels have also been linked to a higher risk of PD–associated peritonitis. Remnant cholesterol (RC) outperforms LDL-C as a predictor of cardiovascular mortality in PD patients. In the analysis of the prognosis of PD patients, the incorporation of composite indicators can enhance the precision of patient condition assessments.

Low serum albumin: dual roles in nutrition and inflammation
ESKD accelerates nutrient catabolism and amplifies systemic inflammation. Serum albumin serves as both a barometer of nutritional status and an endogenous anti-inflammatory and antioxidant molecule. Clinically, hypoalbuminemia combined with hypergammaglobulinemia serves as a robust red flag: multiple studies associate this profile with higher risks of death, PD technique failure, and faster decline in RKF.
PD leads to a daily protein loss of about 5 g. Most patients compensate by boosting liver albumin production from 12–14 g/day to around 17 g/day or by reducing breakdown rates, keeping albumin levels normal or nearly normal. In PD patients, chronic inflammation can impair the liver’s ability to produce albumin, potentially leading to hypoalbuminemia. A substantial body of clinical evidence now attributes low serum albumin in this population primarily to inflammation-driven suppression of hepatic synthesis rather than to excessive albumin clearance into the dialysate. In the subsequent section on ‘Chronic microinflammation’, we will explore this mechanism. Consequently, hypoalbuminemia in PD patients is not merely a reflection of nutritional deficits or dialytic protein losses; it is a robust indicator of underlying chronic inflammation. Emerging evidence indicates that inflammatory markers derived from albumin outperform albumin alone in predicting survival. Tsai et al. demonstrated that the albumin-to-globulin ratio (AGR) is a more powerful predictor of cardiovascular events and long-term mortality than serum albumin by itself. Peng et al. likewise found that a low AGR is significantly linked to both all-cause mortality and heightened cardiovascular risk. Among PD patients, neutrophil-to-albumin percentage ratio (NPAR), platelet-to-albumin ratio (PAR), albumin-to-alkaline phosphatase ratio(AAPR), and the controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score all serve as valuable prognostic markers for long-term survival. Therefore, routine follow-up should incorporate a multidimensional panel – albumin, AGR, NPAR, PAR, AAPR, and the CONUT score – to monitor prognosis in real time and guide timely adjustments to both dialysis prescriptions and lifestyle interventions.

Chronic microinflammation: interaction with malnutrition
Chronic microinflammation refers to a persistent, low-grade systemic inflammatory state characterized by mildly elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP), without clinical signs of acute infection or overt inflammation. Unlike acute inflammation or autoimmune-mediated inflammation, microinflammation in PD patients is driven by continuous exposure to bioincompatible dialysate, uremic toxins, and gut-derived endotoxins, resulting in sustained low-level immune activation that gradually damages the peritoneum and vascular endothelium.
Chronic microinflammation in CKD accelerates vascular aging and shortens survival. Bioincompatible dialysate drives a progressive rise in systemic IL-6, TNF-α, and other inflammatory mediators in PD patients, exceeding levels seen in healthy individuals. This sustained inflammation inflicts vascular endothelial injury and accelerates peritoneal functional decline, even leading to peritoneal fibrosis and ultrafiltration failure. Meanwhile, chronic microinflammation serves as an independent risk factor for the deterioration of RKF in patients undergoing PD. Elevated concentrations of inflammatory markers, notably IL-6, TNF-α, and hs-CRP, are consistently correlated with an accelerated decline in RKF.
Inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α activate the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, accelerating muscle-protein catabolism and suppressing hepatic albumin production. This leads to reduced albumin synthesis in patients and the onset of sarcopenia, ultimately contributing to malnutrition and a diminished quality of life. Meanwhile, hypoalbuminemia and anemia may further activate inflammatory pathways, which impair the proliferation and function of immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and macrophages. Additionally, albumin deficiency decreases antibody production and reduces complement system activity, thereby compromising the body’s ability to clear pathogens.
Hypoalbuminemia impedes the proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells, diminishes the integrity of the mucus layer, downregulates the expression of tight-junction proteins such as occludin and claudin, and induces mucosal atrophy. These alterations compromise epithelial integrity, precipitate dysbiosis, and facilitate the translocation of endotoxins and bacterial metabolites into the systemic circulation.
High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is significantly associated with incident cardiovascular events in this population and constitutes an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in patients undergoing PD. Blood-cell-count-derived inflammatory indices – namely the aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI), the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) – are increasingly utilized because they are inexpensive and readily calculated from routine hematology panels. These composite biomarkers integrate neutrophil, monocyte, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, and have demonstrated robust prognostic value. A growing body of evidence indicates that elevated AISI, SII, and SIRI levels are independently and significantly associated with all-cause mortality in patients receiving PD. In routine care, PD clinicians frequently direct their attention exclusively toward malnutrition, instituting dietary or supplemental interventions while overlooking the concomitant microinflammation. Our appraisal of the literature demonstrates a robust and reciprocal relationship between malnutrition and persistent microinflammation in this population. Consequently, when malnutrition proves refractory to standard nutritional strategies, assessment and targeted modulation of the inflammatory milieu should be incorporated into the therapeutic plan. Prospective interventional trials are nevertheless required to establish the efficacy of such an integrated approach.

Intestinal dysbiosis: from digestive issues to systemic inflammation
Gastrointestinal disorders are highly prevalent among PD patients, with more than half experiencing a range of symptoms including gastroesophageal reflux disease, dyspepsia, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and constipation. Constipation is a particularly common and clinically significant bowel complication in this population. Although its reported prevalence varies widely due to differing definitions, systematic reviews indicate that constipation affects a substantial proportion of PD patients and is consistently among the top five gastrointestinal symptoms reported in randomized controlled trials. Nevertheless, when present, constipation in PD patients carries serious implications: it is associated with impaired quality of life, mechanical complications (e.g., catheter dysfunction), and an increased risk of enteric peritonitis, likely due to bacterial translocation across a compromised intestinal barrier.
PD patients exhibit a higher incidence of gastroesophageal reflux disease compared to those on hemodialysis, which may be attributed to delayed gastric emptying induced by PD effluent. The clinical management of gastroesophageal reflux disease commonly involves gastric acid suppressants (GASs). However, growing evidence suggests that long-term use of these medications may be associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events, peritonitis and all-cause mortality. Therefore, the use of GASs in the management of gastrointestinal dysfunction among PD patients should be approached with caution.
The gut microbiome plays an essential role in host health maintenance, and a healthy intestinal microbial ecosystem is generally characterized by high taxonomic diversity, considerable microbial gene richness, and overall community stability. In patients with PD, specific gut microbiota demonstrate potential associations with inflammatory markers in the PD effluent. Genera including Blautia, Faecalibacterium, Coprococcus, and Dorea exhibit negative correlations with pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. In contrast, Enterococcus, Streptococcus, and Klebsiella show positive correlations with these cytokines.
Disturbances of the gut microbiome are relatively common in patients undergoing PD. Studies have shown that, compared with healthy controls, PD patients exhibit reduced microbial α-diversity, a higher abundance of opportunistic pathogens, significantly elevated serum levels of toxins including lipopolysaccharide (LPS), D-lactate, and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), as well as markedly reduced concentrations of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Guo et al. demonstrated that reduced gut microbial diversity is independently associated with an increased risk of technical failure in PD. Long-term PD, high peritoneal glucose exposure, and loss of RKF are also associated with reduced intestinal microbial richness and decreased SCFAs concentrations. Zhang et al. reported that patients with elevated serum TMAO levels (≥50 μM) exhibited a significantly higher risk of new-onset peritonitis. Furthermore, TMAO promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, thereby exacerbating the systemic inflammatory response. Systemic complications of CKD

Kidney anemia: inflammation and iron metabolism disorder
Kidney anemia causes tissue hypoxia, prompting a compensatory rise in cardiac output that, over time, drives left-ventricular hypertrophy and precipitates cardiovascular events. Anemia is nearly universal in PD patients. It arises not just from erythropoietin deficiency but from a multifactorial, inflammation-driven process that amplifies malnutrition, cardiovascular disease, and technique failure. Uremic toxins such as indoxyl sulfate and methylguanidine trigger lipid peroxidation of the erythrocyte membrane; oxidative stress further weakens membrane skeletal proteins, markedly shortening red-cell survival. Elevated oxidative stress or inflammatory factors like IL-6 and TNF-α hinder the Hypoxia-Inducible Factor (HIF) pathway, a crucial mechanism in anemia development. Inflammation drives hepcidin up-regulation, which degrades ferroportin and traps iron within macrophages, causing functional iron deficiency. In PD patients, an additional contributor to iron deficiency is a marked reduction in intestinal mucosal uptake and transfer, leading to substantially lower whole-body iron retention. Building on the classic CKD–anemia pathway, PD patients face the added burden of dialysis-specific impairments in intestinal iron absorption and transport. This distinct mechanism heightens their need for effective hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase (HIFPH) inhibitors and intravenous iron supplementation.

Chronic kidney disease-mineral and Bone Disorder: Vitamin D loss from dialysate
CKD-MBD is a systemic syndrome that disrupts calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, and parathyroid hormone (PTH) metabolism. In patients undergoing PD, the depletion of active vitamin D differs from that observed in non-dialysis patients, with an average daily reduction of approximately 6–8% of the plasma pool attributable to dialysate loss. This reduction results in decreased serum concentrations of active vitamin D metabolites in PD patients, potentially playing a significant role in the development of kidney osteodystrophy. Evidence consistently indicates that abnormal calcium and phosphorus levels worsen prognosis in PD patients, while keeping them within the normal range reduces mortality. In 568 PD patients, lower baseline serum creatinine and higher baseline phosphorus at urgent PD initiation predicted subsequent technique failure. PTH and mortality follow a U-shaped curve in PD patients, with the nadir of risk at 300–500 pg/mL. The depletion of active vitamin D resulting from dialysis is clinically significant for patients, and prompt pharmacological supplementation is advisable. Concurrently, it is essential to monitor and maintain calcium, phosphorus, and PTH levels within safe parameters.

Dialysis prescription and technical considerations

Volume overload: heart failure and loss of residual kidney function
Due to the substantial reduction in RKF and the common insufficiency in timely adjustments of PD prescriptions, patients undergoing PD are susceptible to chronic volume overload. Volume overload is independently correlated with reduced survival rates and contributes to the onset of hypertension, which subsequently serves as a significant risk factor for the development of left ventricular hypertrophy and heart failure. In a prospective cohort study by Ng et al. comprising 311 peritoneal-dialysis patients, volume overload was found to be highly prevalent and emerged as a robust and independent predictor of both all-cause mortality and incident cardiovascular events. Multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (mf-BIA) is now the standard clinical tool for quantifying fluid status in peritoneal-dialysis patients. Overhydration (OH) and the extracellular water/total body water ratio (ECW/TBW) are validated mf-BIA parameters that reliably detect volume overload. Cader et al. demonstrated that OH is the principal determinant of left-ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) in patients with CKD. Emma et al. demonstrated that the ratio of OH to extracellular water (OH/ECW) is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in patients undergoing PD. RKF is pivotal for maintaining euvolaemia in peritoneal-dialysis patients, and its progressive decline constitutes an independent risk factor for technique failure. A retrospective study by Tian et al. (n = 190) found that patients with sustained volume overload (ECW/TBW ≥0.4) had an 8.9 times higher risk of RKF decline than those with normal hydration. A Canadian study indicated that hyponatremia was associated with reduced RKF and poorer nutritional status in patients. For PD patients, the prompt modification of their prescription to prevent volume overload is instrumental in mitigating cardiovascular risk and preserving RKF.

Intra-abdominal pressure: positioning and activity
In patients undergoing PD, intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) is determined by both intraperitoneal volume (IPV) and body position. Studies have demonstrated that IAP is highest in the sitting position, intermediate in the standing position, and lowest in the supine position. Excessively high IAP in PD patients may lead to serious complications, including hydrothorax, hernia formation, and dyspnea, which can adversely affect clinical outcomes. Evidence suggests that patients with elevated IAP experience a more rapid decline in RKF, particularly when IAP exceeds 15.65 cm H2O. It is recommended that PD patients aim to maintain IAP below this critical threshold, tailored to individual tolerance. We recommend that patients with elevated IAP avoid prolonged sitting and adopt an incremental PD regimen post-catheter insertion to gradually increase the dialysate volume. Additionally, patients should avoid heavy lifting and manage chronic cough through measures such as smoking cessation and treatment of underlying respiratory conditions.

PD technology: catheter insertion and peritoneal dialysate
No significant difference in patient prognosis was observed based on the shape of the PD catheter. Catheter selection should consider the patient’s body habitus, ideal exit-site location, and infection risk to reduce complications.
Laparoscopic-assisted placement demonstrates considerable advantages over open techniques by reducing surgical trauma, shortening operative time, and enhancing positioning accuracy. Specifically, laparoscopy-guided Tenckhoff catheter insertion is associated with reduced risks of catheter migration and perioperative infection. Ultrasound-guided catheter insertion is an effective approach for reducing complications associated with PD catheter placement. This technique enables real-time visualization of the catheter tip and adjacent anatomical structures, thereby minimizing the risk of mechanical and procedural complications.
When selecting PD solutions, it is crucial to avoid factors that may elevate IAP to minimize the risk of complications. Currently, the most widely used PD solution is glucose-based dialysate, which is known for its poor biocompatibility. This type of solution is characterized by low pH and leads to the formation of glucose degradation products (GDPs) and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) during sterilization, storage, and intraperitoneal dwell. These compounds contribute to peritoneal membrane injury, provoke local and systemic inflammatory responses, and accelerate the progression of peritoneal fibrosis and ultrafiltration failure. In its most severe form, persistent inflammation and fibrotic transformation can culminate in encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (EPS), a rare but life-threatening complication characterized by bowel encapsulation, intestinal obstruction, and high mortality. Although EPS remains uncommon, it is strongly associated with prolonged PD duration, recurrent or severe peritonitis, and cumulative exposure to bioincompatible dialysate. Biocompatible PD fluid formulations hold significant clinical value.

Comprehensive protection: key strategies for improving outcomes in PD

Intervention for the chronic microinflammation in PD patients
Chronic inflammation in PD patients significantly impacts their nutritional status, survival rates, and overall quality of life, and it constitutes a critical component of the risk factors associated with PD. Nevertheless, the clinical management of chronic inflammation in PD patients remains insufficiently emphasized in routine clinical practice. The following potential interventions are presented for reference. A prospective clinical study demonstrated that oral indobufen, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, can reduce microinflammation in PD patients and attenuate the progression of peritoneal fibrosis. Compared to the persistent peritoneal stimulation associated with continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD), nocturnal intermittent peritoneal dialysis (NIPD) provides periodic rest for the peritoneal membrane and reduces cumulative exposure to dialysate. Studies have shown that NIPD is associated with reduced serum concentrations of inflammatory markers, including CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α. In a murine model of PD, administration of Lactobacillus casei Zhang (LCZ) corrected gut microbiota dysbiosis, increased the abundance of short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria, and reduced macrophage infiltration, M1 polarization, and inflammatory cytokine levels in the PD effluent. Our previous investigations demonstrated that the traditional Chinese medicine compound Fushen granule increased the abundance of Bacteroides in the gut of PD patients, promoted SCFAs production, and reduced IL-6 levels, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects in this population. Although biocompatible dialysates, prebiotics, and exercise programs show potential in modulating microinflammation, it should be noted that existing data largely derive from pilot or single-center studies with limited sample sizes. Large-scale, randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm clinical benefit.

Dialysate innovation: biocompatibility and clinical benefits
Clinical monitoring and medication management for PD patients should incorporate multidimensional assessments – as highlighted in prior discussions – to facilitate early identification of potential risk factors and enable timely adjustments in pharmacological therapy. The selection of appropriate PD solutions and the optimization of catheter placement techniques remain critical to enhancing treatment efficacy, reducing complications, and improving long-term patient outcomes. Recent advances in medical technology have substantially improved both dialysis solutions and catheterization methods, providing new opportunities to refine PD protocols and elevate standards of care.
Clinical evidence indicates that low-glucose dialysis solutions not only prolong technique survival but also significantly reduce the incidence of peritonitis and mortality risk, while minimizing glucose-induced peritoneal membrane injury and accumulation of AGEs. These solutions contribute to improved dialysis efficacy, reduced complication rates, and enhanced long-term patient outcomes.
Furthermore, amino acid-based dialysate represents an innovative therapeutic strategy that provides essential amino acids to counteract protein catabolism and attenuate inflammation, offering particular benefit to malnourished PD patients, offering a comprehensive strategy for improving nutritional status and overall treatment success.
Biocompatible dialysis solutions, particularly low-GDPs formulations, demonstrate significant renoprotective effects by reducing peritoneal inflammation and fibrosis, thereby preserving RKF compared to conventional solutions. However, clinicians should monitor for metabolic acidosis, as earlier formulations were associated with this complication.
Bicarbonate-buffered dialysate, which features a physiological pH closer to that of the human body, demonstrates superior biocompatibility by reducing peritoneal irritation and cellular damage. Clinical studies have confirmed its association with lower peritonitis rates and improved dialysis efficacy. Icodextrin solution, a high-molecular-weight glucose polymer, provides distinct advantages for long-dwell exchanges owing to its slow absorption profile, low glucose load, and iso-osmolar characteristics. It significantly enhances ultrafiltration, mitigates volume overload, and reduces peritoneal irritation. Furthermore, it offers specific benefits in metabolic control, preservation of RKF, and improvement in quality of life – particularly among high-transport patients and those with diabetes . Furthermore, certain biocompatible PD solutions, such as neutral pH, bicarbonate/lactate-buffered solutions, are manufactured with very low endotoxin levels, which may further reduce peritoneal inflammation. Continuous technological progress is anticipated to facilitate more personalized applications of icodextrin, which may enhance patient survival rates and improve quality of life. Additionally, as highlighted in previous discussions, the optimal implantation of PD catheters employing biocompatible materials and ultrasound-guided minimally invasive techniques remains essential for achieving favorable patient outcomes.
The ISPD recommended in its 2020 guidelines that incident PD patients with preserved RKF should initiate therapy with a low-dose PD prescription, gradually increasing the dialysis dose as RKF declines – an approach consistent with the principles of incremental PD. incremental PD is a personalized treatment method that adapts dialysis dosage based on a patient’s RKF. It starts with a lower volume (2000–4000 mL/day) and increases as RKF decreases, eventually reaching a standard full dose (usually 8000 mL/day).
Incremental PD reduces daily exchanges to three or fewer sessions and cuts weekly treatment days to under seven, significantly shortening treatment time, lessening self-care and nursing demands, and enhancing quality of life. incremental PD enables individualized adjustment of the dialysis regimen – such as reducing dialysate volume or treatment frequency – to avoid over-dialysis, maintain osmotic homeostasis, mitigate inflammatory responses, and significantly delay the decline of RKF. The preservation of RKF is essential for preventing volume overload and cardiovascular complications, as well as for facilitating incremental PD. Clinical strategies aimed at decelerating the decline of RKF include the utilization of biocompatible low GDPs dialysate, the avoidance of nephrotoxic agents such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and aminoglycosides when feasible, and the maintenance of optimal blood pressure control through the use of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors when appropriate. Additional strategies involve the prevention of dehydration and intra-abdominal hypertension, as well as the prompt treatment of peritonitis episodes. Regular monitoring of RKF is crucial for the timely adjustment of dialysis prescriptions, thereby supporting a personalized incremental approach that minimizes unnecessary glucose exposure while ensuring adequate clearance. However, a meta-analysis indicates that no significant differences exist between incremental PD and conventional PD in terms of patient survival, peritonitis incidence, or technique survival. Furthermore, discrepancies remain across clinical studies regarding these outcomes. We hypothesize that this may result from delayed adjustments to the dialysis prescription in certain patients. Some individuals maintain an inappropriately low dialysis dose for prolonged periods, leading to inadequate clearance of uremic toxins and a subsequent decline in technique survival. Excessive pursuit of a minimal dialysis dose contradicts the original intent of incremental PD. Clinicians should regularly assess dialysis adequacy and promptly adjust prescriptions based on individual patient response.

Precision training: enhancing patient operational skills and compliance
Education and training play an essential role in the management of PD patients, ensuring that both patients and their caregivers can accurately understand and perform PD-related procedures. As emphasized in the 2025 ISPD Position Paper on Teaching Peritoneal Dialysis, training should be person-centered, preceded by comprehensive assessments of the home environment, learning preferences, health literacy, sensory/motor function, and psychosocial status – including screening for depression, anxiety, and social support. Elderly patients, individuals with comorbid conditions, and those with lower levels of educational attainment frequently necessitate extended periods to acquire proficiency in peritoneal dialysis techniques and exhibit a heightened susceptibility to peritonitis. It is imperative to implement tailored educational strategies for these populations, which may include the use of simplified language, visual aids, and an increased frequency of hands-on demonstrations. Throughout the educational process, healthcare providers should closely monitor learner progress, promptly identify difficulties in comprehension or practical performance, and adapt instructional content and methods to individual needs. For learners requiring additional time, educators should slow the pace and provide extended one-on-one support. For those demonstrating strong comprehension, supplemental materials – such as the physiological principles of PD and strategies for preventing complications – may be introduced to enhance understanding and promote self-management.
Assisted PD is a method where trained medical personnel, family members, or other caregivers assist patients in performing PD. It is suitable for patients who are unable to independently complete PD due to physical, cognitive, or operational impairments. For patients experiencing physical weakness, mobility issues, and cognitive disorders, or elderly individuals with ESKD, opting for assisted PD can be a beneficial choice. Assisted PD can enhance the patients’ quality of life, increase their life expectancy, and decrease the occurrence of peritonitis.
The extensive adoption of digital technologies has facilitated the development of varied educational pathways for PD management. The efficacy of PD treatment is significantly reliant on individualized dialysis protocols. Conventional methods may face challenges such as delayed adjustments and imprecise modifications in some clinical scenarios. Although clinical guidelines exhibit caution regarding the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into routine practice, considerable evidence indicates that AI and machine learning algorithms may surpass nephrologists in forecasting dialysis-related complications, including volume overload, peritonitis, cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality. The dialysis center has the potential to employ AI technology to integrate extensive patient data, analyze treatment patterns, predict complications, and generate real-time clinical alerts. Furthermore, the implementation of an AI-driven chatbot for PD enhances patients’ self-care capabilities, yielding outcomes comparable to traditional face-to-face educational methods.
Telephone follow-ups allow clinicians to remotely monitor patients’ adherence to home PD, identify issues, answer questions, and provide personalized guidance. This ongoing support enhances safety, ensures correct practices, and helps prevent complications at home. Social media offers valuable opportunities for patient education and peer support in PD care. Healthcare providers can share instructional videos and expert insights, while patients exchange practical experiences and self-management tips. This interactive setting fosters a collaborative learning community, enhancing treatment adherence and outcomes.
PD patient education should be a continuous, lifelong process, not just a one-time event. PD centers need to establish ongoing education programs where patients gradually enhance their skills under medical guidance, with regular updates to meet evolving treatment needs. This ongoing support, including workshops, competency-based training, and personalized learning plans, ensures safe practices, adapts to changing patient needs, and improves long-term treatment outcomes.

Exercise and nutrition intervention: Improving physiological and psychological states
Comprehensive management of PD patients must address both physiological and psychological dimensions, with exercise and nutrition serving as two foundational pillars of non-pharmacological intervention. CKD and long-term PD lead to progressive cardiopulmonary deconditioning, muscle wasting, and functional decline. These changes are driven by a triad of factors: protein-energy wasting, chronic systemic inflammation, and physical inactivity. Structured exercise programs, which include aerobic training, resistance exercises, and flexibility activities, have demonstrated efficacy in counteracting these effects by enhancing myocardial contractility, improving pulmonary efficiency, preserving lean body mass, and maintaining bone mineral density. Beyond physical gains, exercise exerts significant psychosocial benefits.
Current evidence supports the safety of moderate-intensity exercise in stable PD patients, with minimal risk of adverse events when appropriately tailored. The 2022 ISPD Clinical Practice Recommendations provide practical guidance on modality selection: walking, cycling, or light jogging are preferred for aerobic conditioning due to their low impact on IAP, whereas resistance training using bands or light weights can safely build strength without exacerbating hernia risk. To optimize safety, exercise timing should be coordinated with the dialysis schedule. For instance, reducing dialysate dwell volume or performing an exchange prior to activity can minimize IAP-related discomfort. Wearable monitoring devices may be used to track heart rate, blood pressure, and glucose levels in real time, ensuring physiological parameters remain within safe limits.
Given that PD leads to daily protein losses of approximately 5 g and imposes significant glucose absorption from dialysate, patients often face a paradoxical state of energy surplus coupled with protein-energy wasting and systemic inflammation. α-keto acid supplementation shown to preserve residual kidney function and reduce inflammatory markers such as CRP and IL-6. Nutritional interventions address metabolic complications associated with glucose-based dialysates, such as insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. This can be achieved by reducing the intake of refined sugars, increasing dietary fiber consumption, and promoting a favorable fatty acid profile that is rich in monounsaturated fats and omega-3 fatty acids. A meta-analysis shows that a low-protein diet can protect the RKF of PD patients. Emerging evidence further supports integrating gut microbiome-modulating approaches – such as prebiotics, probiotics, and fermented foods – to strengthen intestinal barrier integrity, lower circulating endotoxins like lipopolysaccharide and trimethylamine N-oxide, and indirectly dampen systemic inflammation. Additionally, routine losses of water-soluble vitamins and active vitamin D into the dialysate necessitate monitored replacement, particularly for vitamin D due to its critical role in bone health and immune regulation. Effective nutritional care in PD requires a dynamic, multidimensional assessment that combines body composition analysis, inflammation-sensitive biomarkers, and dietary personalization within a holistic, multidisciplinary framework aimed not only at sustaining nutritional status but also at disrupting the vicious cycle linking malnutrition, inflammation, and poor clinical outcomes.

Conclusion
For patients with ESKD, PD remains a crucial kidney replacement therapy. Besides the traditionally concerned peritonitis, noninfectious risk factors also affect the prognosis of patients. The patient’s own characteristics, such as aging, hormonal changes before and after menopause in women, smoking and prolonged sitting, can increase the risk of death and technical failure. In terms of nutrition, a high BMI in the early stage of dialysis may be a protective factor for PD, while as the dialysis time increases, both a low and a high BMI led to an increased risk of death. Sarcopenia exacerbates cardiovascular events through the inflammatory proteolysis axis. Glycolipid disorders and hypoalbuminemia jointly promote atherosclerosis. Chronic microinflammation is triggered by GDPs and AGEs in dialysate. The continuous increase of IL-6 and TNF-α directly damages the peritoneum and vascular endothelium, and accelerates muscle consumption through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, forming a vicious cycle of inflammation and malnutrition. and leveraging emerging technologies to enhance the quality of life and long-term survival of PD patients. Addressing these noninfectious factors comprehensively will not only optimize clinical outcomes but also empower patients to achieve greater autonomy and well-being in their dialysis journey. The dysbiosis of intestinal microbiota is characterized by reduced alpha diversity and increased levels of TMAO, which not only heightens the risk of technical failure but also exacerbates systemic inflammation. PD patients are more prone to anemia due to decreased iron absorption in the intestines. In the context of CKD-MBD, the depletion of active vitamin D in dialysis fluid, coupled with calcium and phosphorus imbalances and secondary hyperparathyroidism, markedly elevates mortality risk. Volume overload contributes to the development of left ventricular hypertrophy and heart failure, while accelerating the deterioration of RKF. Elevated intra-abdominal pressure may result in hernias, pleural effusion, and further kidney function decline. Regarding technical and dialysate considerations, traditional dialysate formulations with high glucose concentrations and low pH can damage the peritoneum, and the technique of catheter implantation influences the incidence of complications.
In response to the previously identified risk factors, we propose a comprehensive management strategy for evaluating joint interventions. Addressing patient-centered factors necessitates the recognition that frailty, cognitive decline, depression, low health literacy, and social isolation function not merely as comorbidities but as active determinants of PD outcomes. A thorough pre-dialysis assessment utilizing validated instruments for evaluating frailty, mood, and cognition should be established as standard practice. Beyond initial screening, interventions must adopt a proactive approach: structured, culturally tailored educational programs that involve family caregivers can enhance understanding of treatment and adherence; the routine integration of mental health support and social work services into the PD care team can alleviate psychosocial vulnerabilities; and the prescription of individualized exercise regimens optimally combining resistance and aerobic training under the supervision of a physiotherapist can counteract physical deconditioning and improve functional status.
The dysregulation of nutritional, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways constitutes a significant axis in the progression of cardiovascular damage, characterized by the interplay of protein-energy wasting, sarcopenia, glucose toxicity from dialysate, and chronic microinflammation. Addressing these issues necessitates a comprehensive approach that includes regular consultations with dietitians to ensure sufficient intake of high-quality protein and caloric balance, with supplementation through oral nutritional supplements or keto acid analogs as required. Additionally, replacing traditional high GDPs dialysates with biocompatible alternatives (low GDPs, neutral pH) is crucial to mitigate systemic inflammation and maintain residual kidney function. Furthermore, periodic monitoring of body composition using bioimpedance spectroscopy is essential to inform adjustments in fluid and nutritional management.
Systemic complications associated with CKD, such as gut dysbiosis, kidney anemia, and CKD-MBD, are frequently exacerbated by the PD environment. Interventions should focus on restoring homeostasis. Enhancing gut barrier integrity through the administration of dietary fiber or specific prebiotics may help mitigate endotoxemia and inflammation. Anemia management should extend beyond the use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents to include timely intravenous iron supplementation to address functional iron deficiency. Additionally, where available and appropriate, hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase (HIF-PH) inhibitors, such as roxadustat, offer potential in overcoming inflammation-related erythropoiesis-stimulating agents resistance. Concurrently, the management of CKD-MBD necessitates vigilant monitoring of calcium, phosphate, PTH, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels, with routine vitamin D supplementation being crucial due to significant losses in dialysate.
Finally, optimizing dialysis prescription and technical execution is fundamental to preventing avoidable harm. Starting with fewer exchanges in patients who retain meaningful RKF not only preserves kidney clearance longer but also improves quality of life. Prescriptions should be personalized based on peritoneal membrane characteristics, body size, and volume status, with dry weight regularly reassessed using objective tools such as bioimpedance to avoid chronic volume overload. Achieving technical success is contingent upon precise catheter placement, ideally utilizing laparoscopic or ultrasound-guided methods to reduce the risk of early leaks and migration.
While this review outlines key noninfectious risk factors in peritoneal dialysis, translating these insights into practice requires pragmatic adaptation to individual and systemic contexts. Not all recommendations are equally feasible across settings: intensive monitoring or multidisciplinary support may be accessible in well-resourced centers but unrealistic for patients in rural or low-income regions, or for older adults with frailty, cognitive impairment, or limited caregiver availability. Therefore, risk mitigation strategies should be tailored through shared decision-making, prioritizing patient goals, functional capacity, and social circumstances over rigid adherence to idealized protocols. In high-resource settings, structured assessments (geriatric, psychosocial, nutritional) can guide personalized care. In resource-limited settings, practical strategies include providing simplified patient training, actively engaging family caregivers in dialysis care, and delegating appropriate tasks to community health workers. Ultimately, high-quality PD care is not defined by protocol complexity, but by its ability to integrate medical evidence with real-world feasibility, equity, and patient-centered values.
