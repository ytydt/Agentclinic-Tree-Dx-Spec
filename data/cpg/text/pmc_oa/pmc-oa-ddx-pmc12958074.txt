A stage-sensitive approach to evaluating social prescribing in complex and evolving health systems
Summary
This Viewpoint, commissioned by the World Health Organization Regional Office for the Western Pacific (WHO WPRO), proposes a stage-sensitive evaluation framework to guide the implementation and scaling of social prescribing (SP) in the Western Pacific Region. The framework aligns with Dean Fixsen’s four stages of implementation—exploration, installation, initial implementation, and full implementation—and integrates multiple methodologies, including Theory of Change (ToC), dynamic logic models, implementation science, complexity science, realist evaluation, and participatory action research (PAR). The framework is especially relevant for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where resource constraints, cultural diversity, and system fragmentation present unique challenges. It supports participatory exploration, context-sensitive adaptation during installation, and complexity-informed evaluation in early and full implementation phases. Rather than relying on a single method, it promotes a flexible, pluralistic approach that combines the strengths of different evaluation tools. Used in combination, these methods provide a richer understanding of how and why SP works in varied contexts, while allowing for responsiveness, equity, and scalability. The framework also aligns with WHO’s emphasis on addressing social determinants of health and achieving people-centred, equitable care. It offers policymakers and practitioners a practical roadmap to build credible, context-relevant evidence for SP across diverse regional settings.

Introduction
Social prescribing (SP) is gaining momentum as a community-integrated strategy for addressing the social determinants of health. At its core, SP is about connecting people to non-clinical services to improve wellbeing, often through link workers or wellbeing coordinators who facilitate referrals to community resources such as exercise groups, housing support, or arts programs. While the idea is simple and compelling, evaluating how social prescribing works—and whether it makes a meaningful difference—is anything but straightforward. Its strength lies in being person-centred and adaptable, but that very flexibility makes it difficult to measure using traditional evaluation tools.
Building on the work of Caitlin Muhl et al., we define social prescribing as a holistic, person-centred approach that enables trusted individuals in clinical and community settings to identify non-medical needs and connect individuals with community-based supports that foster health, well-being, and agency. This perspective reflects the relational, dynamic, and context-sensitive processes at the heart of social prescribing.
Theoretical frameworks provide structured explanations of how interventions are expected to produce change. Complexity science emphasises the dynamic, adaptive, and emergent properties of health and social systems. Implementation stages, as described by Fixsen and colleagues, provide a temporal lens for understanding how interventions mature from exploration to sustainability. Together, these concepts underscore why social prescribing evaluations benefit from a stage-sensitive approach: interventions evolve within complex systems, requiring theory-informed, context-sensitive, and temporally aligned methods. Existing reviews show that most evaluations of social prescribing have focused narrowly on health and well-being outcomes, often without a guiding framework or consideration of complexity and implementation science al,. This gap highlights the need for a more integrated, stage-sensitive approach.
This paper focuses on the Western Pacific region, where sociocultural diversity, community structures, and health system fragmentation present unique considerations for SP evaluation. To ensure evaluations are meaningful and contextually grounded, engagement of key stakeholders, including healthcare providers, community organizations, policymakers, and service users is essential at each stage of the SP implementation process.
This viewpoint proposes a comprehensive evaluation approach that aligns with the four stages of Dean Fixsen's implementation framework: exploration, installation, initial implementation, and full implementation. It integrates Theory of Change (ToC), dynamic logic models, implementation science, complexity science, realist evaluation, and participatory action research (PAR), offering a multi-theoretical and methodologically pluralistic lens suited for real-world complexity.

Evaluating across implementation stages

Exploration stage: participatory action research (PAR)
The exploration stage is marked by uncertainty and opportunity. Purpose: The exploration stage establishes readiness and shared purpose among stakeholders, setting the foundation for subsequent design and evaluation. During this stage, communities, healthcare providers, and potential users co-identify needs, assets, and goals. Participatory Action Research (PAR) is ideally suited here, as it fosters co-creation of knowledge with stakeholders, captures lived experience and enhances local relevance and trust. PAR utilises a range of participatory methods, from traditional focus groups and community forums to innovative approaches like photovoice and asset mapping exercises, enabling communities to document their experiences and actively contribute to program development through multiple channels of engagement.
PAR helps stakeholders collectively define what “success” means in context. Rather than imposing predefined metrics, PAR allows insights to emerge from dialogue, action, and reflection. It is especially valuable in identifying the informal systems of care already present in communities, such as peer networks and faith-based supports. Insights from this participatory process form the foundation for the next stage, where these findings are translated into program design and logic.
Though its findings may be less generalizable, PAR offers actionable, practical insight into feasibility and acceptability, which are critical inputs before formalising program structure. The co-creation process helps establish trust and buy-in early, addressing potential power imbalances and ensuring that subsequent program design reflects local realities rather than imposed solutions. Moreover, the deep community engagement characteristic of PAR helps identify potential barriers and enablers to implementation, providing crucial insights that inform later stages of program development and evaluation.

Installation stage: theory of change and dynamic logic models
Once the foundational understanding of local needs and assets is established, the installation stage focuses on program design and early implementation structures. Purpose: The installation stage translates shared understanding into concrete design elements, governance structures, and early implementation mechanisms. Here, evaluators and implementers collaboratively develop a Theory of Change (ToC) that articulates the pathways through which social prescribing is expected to produce change.
The ToC should make explicit the assumptions underlying causal links—for example, how addressing loneliness through social connection may reduce health service use. Developing a ToC creates a shared understanding among diverse stakeholders, supports alignment of resources and activities, and sets the stage for future evaluation. This process is particularly valuable in complex interventions like social prescribing, where multiple mechanisms may operate simultaneously across different contexts.
Dynamic logic models complement the ToC by mapping resources, activities, and outcomes while incorporating feedback loops and contextual influences, providing a structured yet flexible tool that captures the complex adaptive nature of social prescribing systems and supports iterative adaptation.
Limitations: If theories and models are not revisited regularly, they may fail to capture emergent dynamics in evolving systems. Lessons from this stage directly inform the next, where program theories are tested and refined through early implementation.

Initial implementation: realist and complexity-informed evaluation
As social prescribing programs begin operating, it becomes clear that outcomes vary across settings, populations, and delivery modalities. Evaluation at this stage must move beyond whether a program “works” to examine how, why, for whom, and under what conditions it works. Realist evaluation, grounded in complexity science, is well-suited to this task.
Purpose: The initial implementation stage tests how the designed intervention performs in practice, exploring how, why, for whom, and under what conditions it works. Realist evaluation uses Context-Mechanism-Outcome (CMO) configurations to identify how different components interact in specific contexts. For example, in one locality, a link worker's personal relationship with a community choir may be the mechanism that drives improved wellbeing, while in another, it may be the sense of agency from choosing their own referral that matters more.
This stage recognises that social prescribing operates in complex adaptive systems where outcomes emerge from dynamic interactions among agents. Evaluation must account for unintended consequences, adaptive behaviours, and local innovation. While resource-intensive, complexity-informed approaches yield critical insights that support iterative refinement.
This dual analytical approach moves social prescribing evaluation beyond traditional outcome measures to capture the dynamic nature of implementation. Understanding these complex interactions helps bridge the gap between theoretical frameworks and real-world application, particularly valuable when programs need to adapt across diverse healthcare settings and populations. Such insights enable evaluators to provide actionable recommendations that balance program fidelity with necessary local adaptations.
Limitations: These approaches generate rich insights but are resource-intensive and require specialist expertise, which may limit feasibility in some LMIC contexts. Findings from this stage provide critical feedback to refine program logic, directly informing evaluation and scaling decisions in the full implementation stage.

Once programs reach a steady state, with stable staffing, workflows, and partnerships, they are ready for more traditional forms of evaluation. Implementation science offers structured approaches to examine fidelity, sustainability, and scale-up potential. Fixsen et al. identify key implementation drivers.
Purpose: The full implementation stage focuses on assessing fidelity, sustainability, and system integration once programs have reached maturity. Understanding these drivers allows evaluators to determine whether poor outcomes stem from the intervention itself or from implementation failure.
At this stage, pragmatic Randomised Controlled Trials (RCTs) become appropriate. While RCTs are often critiqued for their limitations in complex settings, they can generate robust effectiveness data when built upon strong contextual understanding. Several efforts have been made to standardise RCT reporting through guidelines such as the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) and Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT). Importantly, trials must integrate fidelity data and consider contextual moderators to ensure meaningful interpretation.
Evaluations at this stage build directly on earlier program logic and implementation learning, ensuring metrics reflect both fidelity and adaptation. When randomisation is not practical in real-world settings, researchers can turn to quasi-experimental designs and other robust approaches. Stepped-wedge designs, for instance, allow programs to be rolled out gradually across different sites–rather like a wave moving across a community–while still maintaining scientific rigour. Another quasi-experimental approach compares changes between areas that receive the intervention and similar areas that don't, helping us understand the true impact.
A comprehensive measurement approach typically combines direct outcome measures (such as the EQ-5D for quality of life or the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale) with intermediate outcomes or proxy indicators of programme impact, including healthcare utilisation patterns, social service uptake, and community participation levels. To capture the full picture, these outcome and intermediate measures should be complemented by process measures (e.g., referral completion rates and service engagement) and implementation indicators (e.g., fidelity measures and system integration metrics).
Healthcare evaluation relies heavily on two types of measures: patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient experience measures (PREMs). PROMs serve as validated instruments for evaluating healthcare interventions through physical functioning assessment (SF-36, EQ-5D-5L) and mental health screening (PHQ-9, GAD-7). In the Singapore context, the SF-36 and its abbreviated version SF-12v2 have undergone continuous refinement to ensure cultural appropriateness and validity.
PREMs evaluate service delivery through structured assessment of access to care (waiting times, scheduling efficiency), provider communication quality, and care coordination effectiveness. This integrated framework enables systematic evaluation and evidence-based service improvement within complex adaptive systems.
At the full implementation stage, Social Return on Investment (SROI) offers a structured methodology for capturing the broader social, economic, and environmental value generated by social prescribing initiatives. SROI goes beyond traditional cost-effectiveness analysis by incorporating stakeholders' perspectives on value, translating both tangible and intangible outcomes into monetary terms. This is particularly useful in the context of social prescribing, where benefits such as improved social cohesion, enhanced wellbeing, or reduced caregiver burden may not be fully reflected in clinical or service-use metrics.
SROI involves mapping inputs, outputs, outcomes, and impacts in close consultation with stakeholders and applying financial proxies to calculate a ratio of return. While resource-intensive, SROI complements PROMs, PREMs, and implementation metrics by offering policymakers a more complete picture of impact, helping justify investment and guiding scale-up efforts in settings where cost-benefit justification is essential.
The combination of implementation science frameworks and pragmatic trials provides a comprehensive evaluation strategy that satisfies both operational and evidence needs. This mature approach aids in identifying the organizational conditions necessary for sustainable implementation and scale-up.
Even at full implementation, evaluation approaches must remain adaptable in LMICs and the Western Pacific region, balancing rigorous evidence generation with resource limitations, local governance, and cultural priorities.
Limitations: While these methods provide robust evidence and economic justification, they may underrepresent emergent community-level benefits and intangible social value.

A synthesis: pluralistic evaluation in practice
Learning from earlier stages, such as insights from Stage 2 program logic, informs the selection of evaluation metrics and guides adaptation in later stages. This stage-sensitive approach ensures that each stage builds on evidence and learning from prior stages, supporting iterative and responsive evaluation.

This pluralistic ethos supports evaluators in moving beyond the binary of “did it work?” to address deeper questions: How did it work? For whom? In what context? With what adaptations?
Moreover, this approach supports learning-oriented evaluation, an essential stance for scaling and sustaining interventions in complex, diverse health systems. Programs can be continuously adapted and refined based on real-time feedback, while still generating credible evidence for decision-makers.

Implications for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) in the Western Pacific region
While this framework is broadly applicable across regions, its strength lies in how context-specific factors in the Western Pacific shape each stage's priorities and expression. The framework is universally relevant, but the details that populate and give meaning to each stage; such as sociocultural norms, governance structures, and resource availability; are highly context-specific, particularly in LMICs.
In the Western Pacific region, diverse sociocultural contexts shape both health-seeking behaviours and the success of social prescribing interventions. For example, in urbanized and developed Singapore, a highly integrated public healthcare system can effectively screen and flow patients along social prescribing. In Pacific Island nations, village chiefs, elders, and faith-based community networks often serve as both provider and gatekeepers to community resources, and evaluations must capture how social capital influences participation. In the Philippines, collectivist values and reliance on extended family caregiving shape the acceptability of social prescribing, necessitating evaluation metrics that reflect family-level outcomes in addition to individual measures. Embedding these cultural considerations into evaluation tools enhances contextual relevance and ensures that findings remain meaningful to local stakeholders.
The framework emphasizes participatory approaches and context-sensitive evaluation to ensure social prescribing models are culturally grounded and feasible. Flexibility in implementation and evaluation allows programs to adapt to pluralistic health systems, resource constraints, and local governance structures, generating evidence that is both scientifically credible and contextually relevant. Feasibility assessments or staged implementation pilots can provide actionable data without requiring full-scale, resource-intensive evaluation upfront, supporting sustainable, locally grounded program development and evaluation. Together with the context-specific examples above, this approach demonstrates how the framework can be applied across diverse LMIC settings in the Western Pacific while retaining stage-sensitive adaptability.

Limitations
As a viewpoint paper, this framework reflects the authors’ synthesis of existing literature and expert perspectives, rather than a consensus built through formal stakeholder consultation. The proposed approach may not fully capture the diversity of social prescribing programs across different contexts in the Western Pacific region, and certain nuances of local implementation challenges might be underrepresented. Additionally, the lack of empirical testing means that recommendations remain conceptual rather than validated in practice.
Stage-specific limitations should also be acknowledged. During the Exploration stage, gaps in local data and variability in program definitions may limit generalizability. In the Installation stage, stakeholder engagement may be uneven, and resource constraints could hinder comprehensive planning. During Initial Implementation, variability in staff capacity, fidelity of delivery, and contextual adaptations may affect outcomes. Finally, in the Full implementation stage, long-term monitoring and evaluation may be constrained by funding cycles and institutional support.
Despite these limitations, employing multiple approaches that draw on theory, implementation science, and participatory methods can help mitigate individual challenges and provide a more holistic understanding of social prescribing programs.

Conclusion
Social prescribing represents a bold shift toward integrated, relational, and personalised care. Its evaluation must similarly evolve. This stage-sensitive, pluralistic framework helps reconcile the need for rigour with the realities of complexity. It provides tools for understanding not just whether social prescribing “works,” but how, why, and for whom.
For policymakers and practitioners in the Western Pacific and beyond, especially in LMICs, this approach offers a roadmap for building evidence that is both scientifically credible and socially meaningful. It supports WHO's vision for equitable, people-centred health systems.
