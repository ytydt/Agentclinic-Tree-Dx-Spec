Ten tips for a safe approach in airway management
Airway management remains a critical aspect of anesthesia, intensive care and emergency medicine, particularly when facing difficult airways. Traditional methods, such as endotracheal intubation via direct laryngoscopy, have evolved with the introduction of advanced tools designed to enhance oxygenation and facilitate airway control. This narrative review presents 10 essential tips for safe up-to-date airway management, emphasizing the importance of a patient-centered, oxygen-focused and strategic approach rather than a purely device-oriented one. By incorporating strategies for assessment, prediction and timely interventions and transition, healthcare professionals can improve patient outcomes and minimize complications during airway management.

Background
The existing literature has consistently focused on developing strategic frameworks to improve the safety and efficacy of airway management. Airway complications account for approximately 24% of all complications during non-obstetric anesthetic practice in the UK. Traditionally, endotracheal intubation (ETI) via direct laryngoscopy has been the standard technique for airway management. However, videolaryngoscopy (VL) emerged as a safer and more effective alternative for both routine use and challenging airway scenarios. Among the established airway techniques, VL and flexible endoscopic intubation have become widely adopted for both routine and difficult airway management. While these devices do not directly prevent oxygenation failure, they facilitate safer airway management by preserving continuous visualization and reducing procedural delays and airway trauma. Consistent with recent guidelines and expert consensus, the maintenance of adequate patient oxygenation is now considered the central objective throughout the airway management process. Hence, the role of devices becomes secondary to a pre-planned strategy that identified the best available device within a structured decisional process.
The aim of this manuscript is to provide an overview of the main changes and pitfalls in airway management, suggesting some tips for an increasingly safer conduct and to maximize chances of success.

Anticipated difficult airways management

Tip 1—prediction and assessment of a difficult airway
Airway assessment is essential before every procedure requiring loco-regional, deep sedation or general anesthesia to identify potential challenges and plan for maintaining oxygenation. Despite its limitations in terms of specificity and sensitivity preprocedural airway evaluation remains fundamental to define a strategy and minimize the risks of airway management related hypoxia, airway trauma, brain damage and death. Key variables in airway evaluation include (1) anatomical characteristics that may predict difficult oxygenation/ventilation and/or ETI, although recent studies show that conventional bedside predictors have low specificity in case of difficult VL or awake tracheal intubation (ATI). Advanced tools such as nasal endoscopy and even more airway ultrasound use can improve anatomical visualization and assessment, while computer-based decision models can help to integrate multiple risk domains and guide individualized airway planning; (2) pathophysiological factors that could impact adequate oxygen delivery, consumption, and maintenance; (3) assessment and management of pulmonary aspiration risk; (4) setting factors (operating room, intensive care unit, non-operating room, emergency room, ward); (5) equipment and monitoring; (6) expertise of healthcare professionals, team competence and organization including non-technical skills as communication, planning or training integration.

Tip 2—ultrasound and airway safety
Integrating point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) into preoperative airway assessment enhances clinicians’ ability to anticipate and manage airway-related challenges, including difficult mask ventilation and laryngoscopy, risk of gastric content aspiration, difficulty in identifying and performing front-of-neck airway (FONA) access, and confirmation of correct tracheal tube placement. POCUS provides additional information that may not be apparent on physical examination alone and can guide planning of appropriate equipment, interventions, and precautionary strategies. (1) For the prediction of difficult intubation and ventilation, ultrasound can be used to identify airway edema, tumors, or cysts that may indicate upper airway obstruction, thereby influencing the decision to perform ATI or to administer preoperative corticosteroids. (2) Although not directly a part of airway anatomy, gastric ultrasound assessment of content and volume can influence airway management strategies, especially in emergency surgery and in patients at increased risk of aspiration. Ultrasound examination should be performed with the patient in the semi-recumbent position (approximately 45° head-up) and in the right lateral decubitus position, using a 2.5-MHz curvilinear probe. The probe should be placed in a cephalocaudal orientation in the sagittal plane over the epigastric region. The gastric antrum typically appears as a circular hypoechoic structure located between the left lobe of the liver and the superior mesenteric artery. Gastric ultrasound assessment may be performed qualitatively, by distinguishing between solid and fluid contents, or quantitatively, by estimating gastric volume through measurement of the antral cross-sectional area derived from the anteroposterior and craniocaudal diameters. A gastric fluid volume greater than 1.5 mL·kg⁻1 is considered to confer a high risk of aspiration, whereas an empty stomach or a gastric fluid volume below 0.8 mL·kg⁻1 is classified as low risk. (3) Cricothyroidotomy is a life-saving procedure performed in cannot-intubate, cannot-oxygenate (CICO) situations. One of the main causes of failure is the inability to accurately identify the cricothyroid membrane, a task that can be particularly challenging in specific patient populations, including females, patients living with obesity, and pregnant patients. Preprocedural identification of the cricothyroid membrane using ultrasound is superior to palpation, and also allows assessment of the depth of the membrane relative to the skin surface. To identify the cricothyroid membrane, a linear probe is placed sagittally along the midline of the neck to visualize the “strings of pearl” sign. In this view, the cricoid cartilage appears as the largest hyperechoic “pearl,” with the thyroid cartilage located cranially, and the cricothyroid membrane visualized between them. Before the procedure, it is advisable to explore the area using color Doppler to check for any vascular structures. For greater precision, the thyroid–airline–cricoid–airline (TACA) approach may also be used as a second-line technique for cricothyroid membrane identification. (4) Another important clinical application of airway POCUS is confirmation of endotracheal tube placement. When end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) monitoring is unavailable or unreliable, POCUS can assist clinicians in confirming correct ETI using both direct and indirect methods. The direct method consists in dynamic visualization of the endotracheal tube passing through the vocal cords during the intubation attempt and confirming its position in the trachea after the procedure, reducing the risk of unrecognized esophageal intubations. In the case of esophageal intubation, the ultrasound will show the characteristic two parallel hyperechoic lines representing the air-mucosa interface of the tracheal walls, known as the "double tract" or "double-line" sign with the probe positioned transversely at the suprasternal notch. The indirect method involves assessing for the presence of lung sliding and the characteristic “seashore sign” on thoracic ultrasound. This approach can also be useful for detecting inadvertent selective bronchial (endobronchial) intubation. Combining both techniques increases the sensitivity and specificity of the examination. The POCUS approach is not recommended in emergency situations; when EtCO2 monitoring is unreliable, the principle “no trace, wrong place” remains the most prudent approach.

Tip 3—maintain spontaneous breathing
When there is a high suspicion of an anticipated difficult airway involving difficult mask ventilation, rescue supraglottic airway ventilation, and ETI—conditions that also contraindicate locoregional anesthesia as an alternative strategy—spontaneous breathing should be preserved, and ATI should be considered. In this context, obtaining informed consent from the patient and providing a thorough description of the procedural steps and potential risks are essential. ATI should be performed by an experienced operator in the following situations: (1) anticipated difficult airway; (2) high risk of aspiration, as an alternative to rapid sequence induction and intubation; (3) physiologically difficult airway with reduced tolerance to hypoxia; and (4) anticipated difficulty with rescue airway techniques. Anatomical difficulty includes markedly limited mouth opening (< 2.5 cm), supraglottic or base-of-tongue tumors, prior head and neck surgery or radiotherapy, airway trauma, or cervical spine instability. Physiological difficulty refers to patients with minimal tolerance for apnea, such as those with severe hypoxemia, acidosis, hemodynamic instability, or raised intracranial pressure. In these contexts, ATI should be reserved for cases where these combined risks make maintaining spontaneous breathing the safest option.
Maintaining spontaneous breathing is fundamental to preserve airway patency and oxygenation. This can be achieved through meticulous topical or sometimes regional anesthesia techniques (such as the glossopharyngeal, superior laryngeal, and transtracheal blocks) to assure comfort and to suppress reflexes. When appropriate, carefully titrated and closely monitored procedural sedation may be administered to reduce the patient’s anxiety and discomfort. If light sedation is required, meticulous selection and dosing of sedative agents are essential to avoid apnea and hemodynamic instability. Evidence indicates that this approach reduces the risk of desaturation and airway obstruction compared with deep sedation or induction of general anesthesia. Continuous oxygen delivery via nasal cannula or high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) should be maintained throughout the procedure. HFNO has a lower incidence of desaturation (approximately 1.5%) than low-flow devices. To optimize oxygen delivery, particularly in case of a physiologically difficult airway, the head-up position of 25–30° is advised.
Initially, ATI was performed primarily using flexible intubation scopes; however, VLs have become widely adopted owing to their easier handling, broader clinician familiarity, and greater ease of use. Video stylets also serve as a viable alternative, particularly in cases of limited interincisal distance. Nevertheless, both VL and flexible endoscopy are appropriate techniques for ATI, as no significant difference in desaturation rates has been observed between the two techniques. In patients requiring deeper levels of sedation—typically due to lack of cooperation—it may be appropriate to use airway devices with a dedicated intubation conduit (such as masks or cannulae designed for endoscopic intubation) or to insert a second-generation supraglottic airway device (SAD) as a dedicated airway in the sedated patient. Second-generation SADs are preferred for their superior sealing capabilities, the option for a gastric route, and especially for their dedicated pathway to introduce an endotracheal tube under direct endoscopic vision. In certain predicted difficult cases, elective surgical or percutaneous tracheotomy through SAD or cricothyroidotomy under local anesthesia and ultrasound guide may become necessary to ensure patient safety.
The availability of EtCO2 monitoring to confirm tracheal tube placement and proper ventilation with SADs is now considered a standard recommendation and should always be available in all airway management settings. ATI should always be considered as a planned approach, never a rescue maneuver.

Tip 4—the universal videolaryngoscopy: widespread and routine use
Multiple manuscripts, consensus, and guidelines recommend using a VL across various settings and procedures. Current evidence supports VL as the preferred first-line device for ETI across all clinical settings and patient populations in which airway management is performed. VLs enhance glottic visualization, increase first-pass success, and reduce the incidence of complications related to ETI, such as esophageal intubation, hypoxic events, airway trauma, and aspiration of gastric content. Industry has developed a wide variety of VLs featuring different blade types that vary in design and intubation technique. Hyperangulated VL blades require specific training and a dedicated approach, as the intubation technique differs from that used with direct laryngoscopy or VL employing a Macintosh-type blade. The use of a stylet or bougie to facilitate advancement of the endotracheal tube through the vocal cords is generally considered mandatory with hyperangulated VL, although some debate on this point remains. The Macintosh-type blade used in VL is particularly useful for teaching direct laryngoscopy, as it allows the trainer to coach the novice intubator in real time, thereby minimizing patient harm and optimizing the maneuver. The universal use of VLs also offers benefits in non-technical skills, including improved communication, a shared airway and mental model, enhanced teamwork, and greater situational awareness, particularly when a separate external monitor is used.

Unexpected difficult airways
Unexpected difficulties in airway management can arise at any point during the procedure. Predictive criteria are not infallible, and anatomical and pathophysiological profiles may change throughout the airway management process. Furthermore, in emergency settings, comprehensive preoperative evaluations may not always be feasible.

Tip 5—peri-procedural oxygenation: the more oxygen, the better
Oxygen-focused safe airway management. This infographic provides a visual cognitive and conceptual framework aid outlining the key elements of an oxygen-focused strategic approach to safer airway management
Perioxygenation represents a contemporary approach to peri-procedural oxygen delivery, whereby oxygen is administered before, during, and immediately after airway instrumentation to prolong safe apnea time and prevent oxygen desaturation. Preoxygenation assumes a central role in peri-procedural oxygenation strategies, targeting an end-tidal oxygen concentration (EtO₂ ≥ 90%), reflecting near-complete denitrogenation of the functional residual capacity. Although a threshold EtO₂ ≥ 90% is widely cited, recent data suggest this may not be achievable in certain clinical settings (e.g., critically ill, elderly, or obesity). During the apneic phase of airway instrumentation, especially in high-risk patients, apneic oxygenation should be maintained via low-flow oxygen or HFNO during ETI. This technique has been named nasal oxygenation during efforts securing a tube (NODESAT). Even in the absence of active ventilation, oxygen continues to diffuse into the alveoli because carbon dioxide is eliminated from blood at a slower rate than oxygen is absorbed. The resulting fall in alveolar pressure generates a negative gradient (“mass flow” effect) that continuously draws oxygen from the pharynx into the alveoli. This physiological mechanism effectively prolongs the time to critical desaturation and helps maintain arterial oxygenation during periods of apnea. Applying these principles—by achieving optimal preoxygenation and maintaining continuous apneic oxygen delivery—maximizes oxygen reserves and enhances patient safety throughout airway management (Fig. 2).
Preoxygenation should be considered mandatory before any procedure that abolishes the patient's spontaneous breathing. If feasible, employing continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) of 5–10 cmH2O or CPAP combined with pressure support (non-invasive ventilation) is encouraged.
To facilitate airway management, induction in high-risk patients and in patients living with obesity should be performed in the ramped position.

Tip 6—induction of anesthesia: critically ill patients and rapid sequence induction and intubation
During the induction phase of anesthesia, maintaining hemodynamic stability is crucial to optimize oxygen transport. The INTUBE study demonstrated that the most frequent complication during the peri-intubation period is cardiovascular instability. This is particularly relevant in critically ill patients and during rapid sequence induction and intubation (RSII), where adequate volume optimization and the use of induction agents with minimal hemodynamic impact are essential. To avoid peri-intubation hypotension, early administration of vasoactive agents may be necessary. Team training in the planning and execution of RSII is essential. As emphasized in recent international guidelines, the use of a neuromuscular blocking agent is recommended to facilitate ETI. Current evidence suggests that administration of neuromuscular blockade should not be delayed to assess ease of face-mask ventilation. It is also recommended the use of a fast-acting muscle relaxant for RSII such as succinylcholine 1 mg kg−1 or rocuronium 0.9 to 1.2 mg kg−1 and the use of ulnar nerve stimulation and quantitative neuromuscular monitoring at the adductor pollicis muscle both to confirm the muscle paralysis depth . Management of the physiologically difficult airway in critically ill patients requires a structured and organized approach, including (1) team preparation, with experienced operators and use of checklists; (2) patient preparation, incorporating the ramped position, peri-oxygenation (preferably with HFNO), and early availability of vasopressors; (3) ETI using induction agents with minimal hemodynamic impact, such as ketamine or etomidate, while avoiding propofol; (4) use of VL as the first-line intubation technique; and (5) post-intubation measures, including confirmation of correct tube placement and proactive management of cardiovascular instability.

Tip 7—access to the airways: planning the next step, transitioning
Unexpected difficulties can occur at any stage of airway management, from face-mask ventilation and SADs placement to direct or indirect laryngoscopy. The human factor significantly influences the success of these maneuvers alongside technical skills. Non-technical skills are of paramount importance. After each failed attempt, it is vital to “stop” and reassess all possible and optimal options (Stop and Think) while administering oxygen to the patient. Adjusting the technique or strategy between various attempts can help avoid “fixation errors,” which occur when the problem is not identified, or when alternative solutions are overlooked. The absence of a contingency plan, known as "failure to plan for failure," often leads to negative outcomes in managing unexpected difficult airways.
Every attempt to apply an airway control technique should be performed under optimal conditions (e.g., head position, appropriate device size and bed height) to maximize first pass success of endotracheal tube. Swiftly transitioning to another device when the initial choice proves unsuitable is essential. Airway management may begin with any of the available devices, with the flexibility to transition to alternative devices during subsequent attempts as deemed appropriate by the operator and clinical context. In modern anesthesia, unexpected difficult airways can arise even when a SAD is chosen over intubation; further attempts can be made by changing the type or size of the SAD, the operator, or patient head position. In cases of an unanticipated difficult airway, if adequate oxygenation can be maintained and the procedure is elective, safely awakening the patient—using appropriate reversal agents when necessary—should always be considered. Differently, in emergency unexpected difficult airways this option is not possible, leading to a “forward strategy” towards definitive airway control.
Evidence advocates for an early consideration of emergency FONA (eFONA, named cricothyroidotomy) when oxygenation cannot be reliably maintained during airway management. This approach aligns with contemporary difficult airway guidelines, which identify eFONA as the definitive rescue strategy in the CICO scenario.
Early communication of a “difficult airway” to the team is crucial, triggering an internal protocol that ensures the availability of experienced professionals and all necessary emergency equipment.

Tip 8—rescue techniques: do not delay consideration of FONA
Whenever maintaining adequate oxygenation becomes challenging at any stage of the procedure, it is crucial to promptly implement “rescue” techniques for oxygenation and ventilation. This may involve the use of SADs in the event of failure or significant difficulty with face-mask ventilation and to perform FONA via cricothyroidotomy if SADs also fail.
Timely recognition of the exhaustion of all oxygenation options is essential. The objective is not to encourage more frequent performance of FONA, but rather to ensure that the airway team remains continuously prepared to proceed promptly and decisively once all other oxygenation strategies have failed (“priming for FONA”). This readiness-based approach aims to prevent the prolonged hypoxia that frequently results from delayed transition to FONA. Thus, FONA remains the definitive final step in the oxygenation pathway, to be considered promptly once a lack of ventilation or oxygenation is confirmed.
It is imperative to declare a CICO scenario to the entire team, followed by the activation of a dedicated internal protocol that outlines regulated procedures and required skills. The selection of the FONA technique should be based on the available kit and the operator's experience. The most familiar technique is often the most effective. Given the difficulty in identifying the cricothyroidotomy landmarks—particularly in patients with obesity, female anatomy, pregnancy, or significant neck pathologies—it is advisable to locate the cricothyroid membrane prior to initiating invasive airway management, using both clinical palpation and ultrasound guidance. Preprocedural identification of a difficult FONA may then become a trigger for an ATI. Notably, FONA should not be viewed as a failure of airway management; rather, it should be regarded as a valuable resource in the oxygen-centered algorithm. If the operator recognizes an inability to safely manage the airway using conventional strategies—such as flexible intubation scopes, indirect laryngoscopy with VL, SADs, or ETI—early performance of FONA, either as a primary approach or following failed extubation, should be considered in any CICO scenario.
FONA is a key element of difficult airway management and highlights the role of cognitive aids and human factors in high-stress airway emergencies. Placing “FONA at the center” does not mean performing it earlier but rather keeping it constantly in mind as part of situational awareness. This approach is oxygen-centered: decisions are driven by the ability to maintain or restore oxygenation, with the option of FONA considered continuously rather than only after other steps fail. In this way, it differs from traditional linear algorithms by focusing on readiness, clear team roles, and early recognition of failing oxygenation before a CICO scenario develops.

Tip 9—safe extubation strategies in patients with known or unknown difficult airways
Extubation should occur in a monitored, safe environment, guided by a well-defined strategy, and should be an integral part of the preplanned or unplanned difficult airway management process. In any case in which an expected or unexpected difficulty has been encountered, at the end of the airway management procedure it is recommended to consider extubation as a “risky” procedure. This maneuver must be performed with great care and according to well-defined steps to avoid situations of emergency. Therefore, it is recommended to continuously administer high inspiratory fraction of oxygen in a head-up position with adequate reversal of neuromuscular blocking agents, confirmed using quantitative neuromuscular monitoring and to proceed with extubation using a catheter or dedicated extubation set. Oxygen may be connected to the extubation catheter, but care must be provided: depth of insertion of the airway catheter should not exceed 28–30 cm at maximum and oxygen flow should not be above 1 L. Pre-extubation HFNO can be a safer alternative. Even when an extubation strategy is in place and the risk of failure is considered, an airway emergency trolley should always be readily available.
Evaluation of the post-procedural level of care (ward or post-anesthesia care unit) is a critical component of any extubation strategy, tailoring monitoring options, granting availability of a rescue trolley based on local organization, equipment and skills, and with an adequate handover process.

Tip 10—airway team and airway leads: organizational aspects
Communication, checklists, planning, predefined strategies are the cornerstone of human factors and non-technical skills in airway management, helping to reduce the paradox of choice and to minimize decision making at crucial points.
From an organizational perspective, an airway lead is an anesthetist responsible for promoting safe airway management within the hospital, including implementation of practice guidelines, standardization and availability of airway equipment, education and training, auditing of practice, and debriefing after critical airway events. Developing dedicated and shared protocols within each anesthesia department, with an airway leader who is responsible for its correct implementation, can help mitigate risks, reduce barriers, and design changes to improve human factors and ergonomics. Adherence to protocols can support operators and their teams in maintaining composure during critical situations.

Conclusions
The ongoing evolution in airway management necessitates a paradigm shift towards a more comprehensive patient-centered approach. By prioritizing oxygenation and employing a range of advanced tools and techniques, clinicians can better face the complexities of difficult airway management. The 10 tips outlined in this review serve as a framework for enhancing airway management strategies, ultimately aiming to improve patient safety and outcomes. Continuous training, teamwork, and adherence to established national and local protocols are vital for effectively addressing the challenges posed by difficult airways in various clinical settings.
