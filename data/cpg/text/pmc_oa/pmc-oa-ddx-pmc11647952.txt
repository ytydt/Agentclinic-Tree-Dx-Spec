Cancer model and its possible control—A Z-type control approach
Review Highlights
A cancer model is proposed and analysed in the presence of radiotherapy and immunotherapy.
Indirect Z-type control has been introduced for proper administration of immunotherapy.
Application of indirect Z-type control mechanism in cancer model is validated in diverse scenarios, confirming its efficacy and robustness.
Graphical abstract
This paper investigates the dynamics of a three-dimensional nonlinear cancer model involving interactions among cancer cells, normal cells, and immune cells. By performing a linear stability analysis of the equilibria and investigating the Hopf bifurcation in relation to the immune cell growth rate, we reveal the possibility of chaotic behavior when radiation is absent. However, with the appropriate implementation of radiotherapy, the cancer model demonstrates stable solutions, transitioning from chaotic oscillations through period-halving bifurcation. Additionally, we propose and examine an indirect Z-control mechanism within the cancer model. Our findings indicate that using the indirect Z-controller on the immune population successfully manages chaos and adjusts the cancer cell density to a desired level. Through extensive investigation, we demonstrate the robustness of the Z-controller in managing oscillations and provide insights into determining the minimum number of immune cells needed to achieve a predetermined cancer cell density. This study underscores the importance of control mechanisms in mitigating cancer progression and highlights the potential of Z-control for therapeutic intervention strategies.

Background
Cancer, being one of the leading illnesses, significantly reduces human lifespan, and its treatment typically involves surgical procedures, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and immunotherapy. In 2008, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) estimated a global incidence of 12.7 million new cancer cases. In 2008, the total number of cancer deaths reached 7.6 million, averaging approximately 21,000 deaths per day. Projections suggest that by 2030, the global cancer burden will increase to 21.4 million new cases and 13.2 million deaths. This growth is primarily due to population growth and aging, along with improvements in childhood mortality rates and reductions in deaths from infectious diseases in developing countries.
For many years, primary cancer treatments have combined chemotherapeutic drugs and ionizing radiation to target and reduce the bulk of tumor masses. While these treatments have provided significant benefits and sometimes resulted in cures, the issue of tumor relapse remains substantial due to the development of drug resistance in some tumor cells. Therefore, it is crucial to develop additional therapeutic strategies to eliminate these resistant tumor cells.
An appealing alternative approach involves therapy aimed at activating the immune system to elicit a robust anti-tumor response. In the past, the pioneering use of immunotherapy for cancer treatment was initiated by William Coley in the 1890s, who employed toxins derived from Streptococcus erysipelatis and Bacillus prodigious . In recent years, vaccinations against the hepatitis B virus and papillomavirus, both of which may cause cancer, have made great success in preventing cancer in a large section of the population. With a significant improvement in our understanding of basic immune processes, many immune pathways have emerged as viable targets for improving anti-tumor responses in cancer patients.
After Folkman introduced anti-angiogenic cancer therapy in 1971, several drugs have been developed to prevent tumor cells growth. However, there is ongoing debate regarding the efficacy of these anti-angiogenic therapies in treating the majority of cancers. Among the numerous mediators of angiogenesis, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is recognized as the most prominent regulator. Consequently, inhibiting VEGF represents a promising therapeutic strategy, as demonstrated by the first successful anti-angiogenic drug, bevacizumab. Currently, this anti-angiogenic medication is frequently administered alongside cytotoxic chemotherapy, aiming to eradicate tumor cells. Similar to any chemotherapy regimen, healthy host cells may also be affected by the treatment. However, combining chemotherapy with an anti-angiogenic drug significantly enhances the effectiveness of the treatment without introducing additional side effects. Multiple mathematical models have been developed to explore the response of treatment to anti-angiogenic drugs. However, these models focus solely on the interactions between tumor and endothelial cells, overlooking the involvement of the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, two other models concentrate exclusively on angiogenesis without accounting for treatment effects. The potential advantages of combined therapy have not been explored using a cancer model that includes interactions with the microenvironment (host cells).
Typically, the focus lies on interactions between tumor and immune cells, occasionally incorporating specific therapies. While a cancer typically begins with the transformation of normal cells into tumor cells, cells from the surrounding healthy tissue within the tumor microenvironment can significantly contribute to the advancement of the tumor. This can be explained by examining the interactions between tumor cells and host cells during tumor growth.
From the standpoint of a dynamical system, the challenge of achieving a predetermined infected population can be addressed by employing the Z-type dynamic control method proposed by Zhang et al.,. In this approach, an external control is added to the system being managed, with the control mechanism designed to ensure that the difference between the actual system output and the desired output decreases to zero. Although initially applied to neural networks, recent studies have extended the Z-type control method to a variety of ecological, epidemiological, and eco-epidemiological models to explore their dynamics. In the domain of infectious disease modeling, Z-type dynamic control was applied within a basic SI model, and the system dynamics were examined. The author explored the use of migration to isolate susceptible individuals as a control measure and showed that the migration rate could be adjusted using the Z-control method to manage infectious diseases. On the other hand, Lacitignola et al. recently studied the control of backward bifurcation phenomena by applying the Z-type control mechanism within an SIR disease model. They investigated the isolation of a portion of the susceptible population through preventive measures such as masks, hand-washing, gargling, and vaccination.
In this study, we examine a cancer model incorporating radiotherapy and a Z-type controller. This is the first novel attempt to apply a Z-type control mechanism in a cancer model. Our objectives are twofold: to manage cancer through radiotherapy and to achieve a specific cancer cell density using an indirect Z-control approach on the immune cells. We employ an indirect Z controller to regulate cancer progression. After the Z-controller is implemented (with updated parameters), immune cells are introduced or activated in response to cancer cells in the body. By leveraging the immune response and/or anti-tumor activity on cancer cells, we can regulate the cancer cell density to a predetermined target. Moreover, the Z-control mechanism provides valuable insights into determining the minimum number of immune cells required to effectively implement the Z-control scheme, aiming to achieve the predetermined level of cancer disease. This work represents the initial step towards integrating the Z-control mechanism into the cancer model. Its goal is to explore the potential benefits of eliminating chaos and achieving the predetermined cancer cell density.

Conclusion
Mathematical oncology is an emerging research field where various cancer models are developed to mathematically explain and functionally correlate the main physical and biological factors influencing cancer development, invasion, metastasis, treatment response, and therapeutic resistance. On an individual level, researchers have utilized mathematical modeling to adapt the mechanisms through which our immune system combats cellular diseases and proposed potential interventions that benefit us. On a broader spectrum, mathematical biologists have suggested strategies for controlling the development and spread of cancer. In this current study, we consider a competition model to capture the complex interactions among normal cells, cancer cells, and immune cells. Our focus has been on examining a deterministic cancer model incorporating a Z-type control mechanism in the presence of radiotherapy.
In the present investigation, we first explore the global dynamics of a cancer model depicting the interaction among cancer cells, normal cells, and immune cells, which are activated in the presence of cancer cells. We observe that initially, for lower values of the activation rate () of effector cells/immune cells, both normal cells and cancer cells coexist around the interior equilibrium. As we gradually increase the value of the effector cell parameter (), the system exhibits limit cycle oscillations and ultimately enters a chaotic regime.
Then we study the impact of radiation therapy , assuming that radiotherapy damages cancer cells and normal cells equally (). Initially, for lower doses of radiotherapy, the system displayed chaotic oscillations. As we increased the value of radiotherapy , the system transitioned from chaotic oscillations to limit cycle oscillations. Further increases in  stabilized the system around the coexisting equilibrium, forming a stable focus. We observe that if radiotherapy affects cancer cells and normal cells equally, then with increasing radiotherapy, the system may become stable, but effective cancer control is not viable.
In contrast, if radiotherapy can be administered in a manner that targets cancer cells more precisely, where the death rate of cancer cells  is higher than the death rate of normal cells () due to radiotherapy, then cancer can be controlled. From the bifurcation diagrams concerning the efficacy parameter  of targeting cancer cells during radiotherapy, it becomes apparent that for low, medium, and high values of radiotherapy, whenever  surpasses a threshold value, a cancer-free stable equilibrium can be achieved, leading to the elimination of cancer cells from the body. As a result, normal cells remain stable at their carrying capacity. Therefore, with precise and controlled administration of radiotherapy, it is possible to eliminate cancer cells from the system. This ensures the eradication of cancer cells while allowing normal cells to reach their carrying capacity. Consequently, this implies that with the precise application of radiotherapy, patients can achieve full recovery, evidenced by the eradication of cancer cells and the survival of normal cells. This intricate interplay marks a significant stride toward patient recovery, underscoring the pivotal role of this therapeutic approach in securing a comprehensive and lasting cure. The main challenge of such a treatment mechanism is that targeting only cancer cells i.e., separating cancer cells from normal cells during radiotherapy is not always feasible.
In such situations, we have theoretically proposed an alternative treatment strategy by combining radiotherapy and immunotherapy. In this work, we consider that immunotherapy is applied following an indirect Z-type control mechanism. We have found that a predetermined cancer cell density can be achieved using an indirect Z-control method applied to the immune cell population. Specifically, we employ an indirect Z-controller on the immune cell population to manage the progression of cancer. After implementing the Z-controller (update parameter), immune cells can be introduced into the body according to the update parameter, which depends on the cancer cell density present in the patient. Through the immune response exerted on cancer cells, these cells can be regulated to the predetermined targeted density. Hence, the Z-control mechanism provides insights into the minimum number of immune cells required during the Z-control scheme to attain the predetermined cancer cell density. In summary, the Z-type control mechanism could be very useful for controlling cancer in the presence and/or absence of other treatments, such as radiotherapy. In our future endeavours, we will study the cancer model by constructing a two-scale population model that takes into account the varying lifespans of normal cells, cancer cells, and immune cells.
