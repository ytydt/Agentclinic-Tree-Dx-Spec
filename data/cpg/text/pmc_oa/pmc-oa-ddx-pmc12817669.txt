Systematic review of health economic evaluation of robot-assisted hip and knee arthroplasty
Background
The effectiveness of robot-assisted arthroplasty has been paid more attention, however, the question whether robotic technology could serve as a cost-effectiveness tool dealing with the ever-increasing knee and hip arthroplasty demand is still unclear. Health economic evaluations (HEEs) are comparative analyses of alternative interventions in terms of their costs and consequences. With increasing publications on the HEE of robot-assisted arthroplasty, there is a need to systematically clarify their framework and methodology. This review aimed to identify recent economic evaluations studies and to discuss their methods, cost and outcome, and reported conclusions.
Methods
A systematic review of English language literature was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, and the Web of Science and Cochrane Library from studies published up to February 2025. Two reviewers independently performed screening, full-text inclusion, and data extraction. The study quality of all included studies was assessed by the latest version Consolidated Health Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS2022).
Results
The search identified 478 publications, of which twenty-five studies met the inclusion criteria. Sixteen studies were conducted in the United States. Among these, thirteen evaluated total knee arthroplasty, six examined unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, and five focused on total hip arthroplasty. Economic modelling was the predominant study design, applied in thirteen studies, while ten were observational in nature and two were randomized controlled trials. A total of twenty-three studies were conducted from the healthcare system or payer perspective. Data were derived from various sources, including existing databases, clinical registries, single-center studies, and previously published literature. Cost-utility analysis was the most frequently employed economic evaluation method, used in eleven studies, followed by cost-consequence analysis in eight studies and cost-effectiveness analysis in six studies. The time horizons applied across studies ranged from 90 days to lifetime. Although considerable heterogeneity was observed in the reporting of costs and outcomes, the collective evidence indicated that robotic-assisted arthroplasty was generally cost-effective, with cost-effectiveness influenced by surgical volume, patient characteristics, and length of follow-up. The methodological quality of the included studies varied, with CHEERS 2022 reporting guideline adherence ranging from 57.9% to 96.0%.
Conclusions
Current economic evaluations of robot-assisted arthroplasty, including cost-utility, cost-effectiveness analysis, and cost-consequence analyses, are highly heterogeneous in methodology, cost and outcome measures, and reported results. In addition, there is an urgent need to standardize the definition and measurement of both short-term and long-term cost and outcome indicators for robotic assist arthroplasty. Moreover, early-stage economic modeling and robust trial-based evaluations should be prioritized to better determine the long-term cost-effectiveness of robot-assisted arthroplasty.

Introduction
Knee and hip arthroplasty are well-established procedures for treating end-stage joint diseases, offering significant pain relief and functional improvement. Despite their overall success, patient satisfaction and patient-reported outcomes remain variable after joint replacement. Moreover, the long-term need for revision surgery, driven by complication such as periprosthetic infection, implant loosening, and dislocation, poses a substantial burden in terms of quality of life and healthcare costs.
In response to the challenges of implant alignment and surgical accuracy, robotic-assisted arthroplasty has emerged as a promising technological innovation. Since the introduction of ROBODOC system in 1992, the use of robotics in joint surgery has increased rapidly, with a 601% rise in robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasties between 2015 and 2020 alone. A growing body of evidence suggests that robotic systems may improve surgical precision, reduce complication rates, and enhance early functional recovery. However, high-quality evidence on their economic impact remains scarce, highlighting the need for more robust health economic evaluations (HEE) of robotic-assisted arthroplasty.
Economic evaluation, a basic tool for optimizing resource allocation, has the potential to improve the accessibility and equity of innovative healthcare technology worldwide. Several well-established methodologies are available for health economic evaluations, each with its distinct focus and application, including cost-effectiveness analysis, cost-utility analysis, cost-minimization analysis, cost-benefit analysis, and cost-consequence analysis. A growing number of studies have examined the economic value of robotic-assisted arthroplasty, yet their findings are heterogeneous due to differences in methodology, study design, perspective, time horizon, and clinical context. In addition, device-specific features, including incremental innovation, dynamic pricing, learning curve effects, and organizational impact, are seldom incorporated, despite their potential influence on economic outcomes. Despite the increasing adoption of robotic-assisted hip and knee arthroplasty, the economic implications of these technologies remain incompletely understood. Existing evidence remains limited in scope, as most studies focus on specific robotic systems, short time horizons, or narrow methodological frameworks, without adequately addressing variations in analytical approaches or long-term economic consequences across different healthcare settings. For instance, one study assessed the cost-utility of CT-based robotic-arm assisted unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, total knee arthroplasty, and total hip arthroplasty compared with manual techniques, reporting shorter lengths of stay and lower 90-day episode costs for robotic-assisted procedures. To address these limitations, we conducted a systematic review of the health economic evidence for robotic-assisted hip and knee arthroplasty. We mapped methodological approaches, summarized reported cost and outcome measures across different devices and procedures, and assessed the methodological quality of included studies. This approach highlights areas where evidence is limited and may inform the design of future economic evaluations in this field.

Methods
The systematic review of cost-effectiveness of robotic- assisted knee and hip arthroplasty was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and meta-analysis (registration number CRD42023453924).

Literature search strategy
The systematic literature search retrieved articles between January 2000 to February 2025. Electronic databases including the PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE and Cochrane Library were searched using the search terms related to “robot”, “arthroplasty”, “economic evaluation”, “cost-effectiveness analysis”, “cost-utility analysis”, “cost-consequence analysis”, and additional articles were identified on references of relevant articles. Details of the search strategy are provided in Appendix (Detailed Search Strategy).

Eligibility criteria
Four types of full economic evaluation were included, respectively cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), cost-utility analysis (CUA), cost-benefit analysis (CBA), and cost-minimization analysis (CMA). CEA offer a valuable framework for reducing variability in surgical decision-making by systematically evaluating both the economic costs and clinical outcome improvements associated with different procedures. CUA, a form of CEA, employs quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) to account for quantity and quality of life, enabling broader comparisons across healthcare interventions. CBA differs from CEA in that both costs and health benefits are expressed in monetary terms, allowing a direct comparison of net gains across interventions. CMA compares the costs of interventions that are assumed to produce equivalent outcomes, identifying the least costly option. In addition, we also included cost-consequence analysis (CCA), which is regarded as a special form of full economic evaluation within the literature. CCA reports both the costs and a range of disaggregated outcomes without aggregating them into a single summary measure, allowing decision-makers to consider multiple dimensions of value.
Population: Patients undergoing primary robot-assisted total hip or total knee arthroplasty. No restrictions were placed on age, gender, or geographic location.
Intervention: Robot-assisted primary total hip or total knee arthroplasty.
Comparison: Conventional manual primary total hip or total knee arthroplasty without robotic assistance, or arthroplasty performed with other non-robotic assistance technologies. Revision procedures, trauma surgeries, and other non-arthroplasty orthopedic operations were excluded.
Outcome: The primary outcome was the economic evaluation results of robot-assisted arthroplasty, expressed as cost-effectiveness, cost-utility, or cost-consequence depending on the type of analysis. Secondary outcomes included individual cost components, resource utilization, and health-related outcomes reported in the included studies.
Study type: Original research articles reporting an economic evaluation of robot-assisted primary arthroplasty. Editorials, letters, conference abstracts, comments, and systematic reviews were excluded.
A PICO framework was used for study selection:
Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records after duplicate removal. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were then reviewed. Disagreements or uncertainties were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by a third independent reviewer when necessary.

Data extraction
The data of the eligible studies were extracted independently by the two reviewers. Data extraction included author, publication year, surgery type, sample size, study design, data sources, and funding. The health economic analysis included evaluation type, perspective, time horizon, cost, and outcomes indicators, device-specific features, and main finding. Meta-analysis was not performed as there was high heterogeneity from different countries and health care systems.

Quality assessment
The quality assessment of all included full-text articles was performed using the latest version Consolidated Health Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS2022) and four. Furthermore, we evaluated four key dimensions specific to medical devices: learning curve, organizational impact, incremental innovation, and dynamic pricing.
CHEERS 2022 version included 28 checklists subdividing into seven main categories: title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion and other relevant information. Each item was evaluated using four classifications: “Yes” (fully meets reporting criteria), “Part” (partially meets criteria), “No” (does not meet criteria), and “NA” (not applicable). Scores were assigned as 1, 0.5, or 0 accordingly. Studies were classified as high quality (≥ 75%), moderate quality (50–75%), or low quality (< 50%) based on their overall evaluation. Two independent reviewers conducted the assessments, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the corresponding author.

Results

Summary of literature search and included study characteristics
The search yielded 478 publications, after the title and abstract screening, seventy-three potentially relevant studies were assessed in full text for eligibility. Based on inclusion criteria, twenty-five studies from six countries were selected for the systematic review (see flow chart in Fig. 1).
PRISMA diagram
The characteristics of the included studies are outlined in supplementary S1 table. The economic evaluation of robotic-assisted arthroplasty remains an evolving field, with most studies published between 2020 and 2023. Most studies were conducted in the United States (n = 16), followed by England (n = 5), while only two studies were from Asia. The most frequently assessed procedure was total knee arthroplasty (TKA, n = 13), followed by unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA, n = 6) and total hip arthroplasty (THA, n = 5); one studies included both TKA and THA.
Regarding study design, economic modelling was the most common approach (n = 13), with the remainder being comparative observational studies (n = 10) and two randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The evidence base for robot-assisted arthroplasty remains predominantly composed of observational and modeling studies. Data sources varied and included hospital or national registries, existing databases (e.g., Medicare or commercial claims), routine clinical data, and published literature. Among these, existing databases were the most frequently used source (n = 18), followed by single primary studies (n = 8), broader literature (n = 4), and routine clinical data (n = 2). Several studies integrated multiple sources to complement and validate their findings.
Funding sources for the included studies varied. Industry funding was the most common, reported in seven studies, followed by studies supported by government agencies (n = 5). Six studies reported no funding, and one study was supported by an academic institution. One study received joint funding from industry and a charitable organization. Funding information was not reported in six studies.

Economic characteristics of the included studies
The economic characteristics of the included studies are described in supplementary table S2-S3 and Fig. 2. The majority of the included studies were conducted from a healthcare/payer perspective (n = 23), while only two studies focused on a hospital perspective. The economic impact of robotic-assisted arthroplasty was assessed using diverse methodologies. CUA was the most frequently employed method (n = 11), focusing on both costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Cost consequence analysis (CCA, n = 8) and cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA, n = 6) were also commonly applied. The time horizons of the included studies covered short-, mid-, and long-term periods, ranging from 90 days to a lifetime. Short-term horizons of 90 days were most common (n = 8), followed by mid-term assessments of one year (n = 2), five years (n = 5), and ten years (n = 4). Long-term evaluations extended to twenty years (n = 2) or lifetime projections (n = 5). One study reported both a 10-year follow-up and a lifetime horizon.

Cost and outcome measures
Cost components reported across the included studies were highly heterogeneous, encompassing up to thirty-four distinct items. These could be broadly grouped into diagnostic and imaging costs, surgical and device-related costs, perioperative and rehabilitation costs, revision-related costs, and aggregated categories such as total direct costs, index costs, and episode-of-care costs. Surgical and device-related costs were the most frequently reported (n = 19), encompassing expenditures such as robotic equipment purchase, consumables, implants, and surgical procedure fees. Aggregated cost categories (n = 12) included total direct costs, index costs, and episode-of-care costs over varying follow-up durations. Perioperative and rehabilitation costs (n = 11) covered preoperative workup, anaesthesia, inpatient hospitalization, postoperative care, and rehabilitation. Revision-related costs (n = 10) referred to aseptic and septic revision surgery or re-revision costs. Diagnostic and imaging costs (n = 9) comprised modalities such as CT, MRI.
Outcome measures varied depending on the type of economic evaluation. CUA most often reported benefits in terms of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), derived from health-related quality of life instruments such as the EQ-5D. CEA commonly assessed clinical outcomes, primarily revision rates and mortality (n = 6). Among the eight studies employing CCA, outcomes primarily focused on resource utilization and clinical process measures rather than aggregated summary metrics. Length of stay (LOS) was the most frequently reported outcome, appearing in nine studies, followed by readmissions or post-acute care utilization in six studies. Post-discharge rehabilitation and broader resource utilization were reported in four studies, while postoperative complications or adverse events appeared in three studies. Operating or procedural time was included in two studies. These findings highlight that CCA studies generally present multiple, disaggregated outcomes to capture the multidimensional impact of robotic-assisted arthroplasty on both hospital resource use and patient care processes.

Summary of economic evaluation outcomes
Across the included studies, CUA, CEA, and CCA generally yielded favorable economic profiles for robot-assisted arthroplasty.
Overall, CUA most often found robot-assisted arthroplasty to be cost-effective (n = 6), with four studies reporting cost-effectiveness only in selected settings and one reporting it was not cost-effective. Notably, Blyth et al., Clement et al., and Moshetti et al. demonstrated that robot aid UKA achieved dominance or cost-effectiveness relative to manual UKA or TKA, particularly in high-volume centers. However, Zhang et al. indicated that robot assisted TKA may not be cost-effective under a pay-per-use robot contract, highlighting the influence of organizational cost structures and purchasing strategies.
CEA reached similar mixed conclusions, with one study finding the procedure cost-effective, four identifying cost-effectiveness only in selected settings, and one concluding it was not cost-effective. Hickey et al. emphasized that cost-effectiveness is context-dependent, likely restricted to specific clinical circumstances rather than all populations.
CCA most frequently reported lower costs with better outcomes (n = 5), while one study found cost-neutrality with better outcomes, and two found higher costs but better outcomes; one study reported lower costs and shorter length of stay but higher readmission rates and blood transfusions. Barsoum et al., Shah et al., and Pierce et al. reported reductions in length of stay, post-discharge resource utilization, and overall episode costs. In contrast, Tompkins et al. observed that r-TKA could be longer and more expensive than manual TKA without clinically meaningful differences in outcomes, emphasizing variability across centers and surgeon experience.

The scores of twenty studies were over 50%, and low quality in reporting were primarily due to the absence of health economic analyses and engagement with patient and others affected by the study, and characterizing distributional effects.

Discussion
This systematic review provides an updated synthesis of economic evaluations of robot-assisted arthroplasty, focusing on methodological rigor, economic evaluation outcomes, and reporting quality. Our findings reveal substantial heterogeneity in study designs, cost and outcome measures as well as reported outcomes. Economic evaluation outcomes of robotic-assisted arthroplasty are generally favorable, though their magnitude varies across settings due to factors such as surgical volume, patient characteristics, and follow-up duration.
The literature revealed significant inconsistencies in economic evaluations of robot-assisted arthroplasty. The CUA, CEA, and CCA were commonly employed in terms of payer and hospital perspective, however, these studies displayed substantial methodological heterogeneity. Overall, CUA and CEA have substantially contributed to the economic evaluation of robotic-assisted arthroplasty, with CUA quantifying its value in terms of cost per QALY gained, and CEA linking economic costs with clinically meaningful outcomes such as revision rates. While these approaches facilitate more evidence-based surgical decision-making and provide policymakers with metrics to weigh clinical benefits against resource allocation, their reliance on QALY-based, ICER-focused frameworks may overlook other important dimensions of value. This recognition has prompted increasing interest in multi-criteria approaches, which integrate clinical, economic, and patient-centered outcomes to provide a more comprehensive framework for healthcare decision-making.
CCA was utilized to evaluate the cost and effectiveness of robotic assisted joint arthroplasty, offering a means to capture a broader spectrum of outcomes over traditional CUA and CEA. Existing literature demonstrates that CCA’s disaggregated results enable healthcare decision-makers to identify and prioritize specific outcome measures for targeted economic assessment and resource allocation decisions.
Additionally, our finding contributed to the standardization of data collection processes through proposed core datasets. To improve transparency and comparability, we propose standardized reporting guided by the CHEERS 2022 framework and supplemented by core outcome datasets. Within the existing literature, length of stay and readmission emerged as the most frequently reported short-term outcome metrics, likely reflecting their substantial impact on hospital resource utilization. In contrast, revision rate was most often examined as a longer-term outcome, as revisions demand greater hospital resources and costs, posing a considerable burden on healthcare systems and society.In terms of cost metrics, our review indicates that surgical and device-related costs were the most consistently reported across studies, reflecting the central role of robotic equipment, consumables, implants, and procedure-related fees in the economic assessment of robot-assisted arthroplasty. Aggregated cost categories, such as total direct costs, index costs, and episode-of-care costs, were also frequently captured, highlighting the interest in estimating overall economic burden across different follow-up durations. Perioperative and rehabilitation costs, including preoperative workup, anaesthesia, inpatient hospitalization, postoperative care, and rehabilitation, were reported in approximately half of the studies, indicating some recognition of broader resource utilization beyond the surgical episode. However, substantial heterogeneity in the types of costs reported and their level of detail limits cross-study comparability and the ability to draw generalized conclusions about the cost drivers of robotic-assisted arthroplasty. Future economic evaluations should aim for standardized cost categorization and comprehensive inclusion of both direct surgical costs and perioperative resource use to improve comparability and inform decision-making. Meanwhile, societal costs, such as productivity loss and broader public health implications, were not identified in the current evaluations, highlighting an important gap that should be addressed in future research to better inform policy decisions. Furthermore, our findings indicate that existing economic evaluations of robotic-assisted arthroplasty rarely address key robot-specific factors, including the learning curve, organizational impact, incremental innovation, and dynamic pricing.
These gaps may limit the comparability of findings across settings and hinder accurate estimation of cost-effectiveness in the context of rapidly evolving surgical robotics. Future research should integrate these dimensions into study design and analytical modelling to ensure that assessments capture both the unique resource demands and the long-term value trajectories of robotic technologies. Addressing these gaps will require prospective studies that combine standardized economic metrics with evolving surgical techniques and implant innovations to establish robust implementation criteria.
With regard to study design, our analysis showed that economic modeling approaches predominated markedly over both randomized controlled trials and observational studies in the robotic arthroplasty literature. Economic modeling approaches, focusing on QALYs, revision rates and mortality, have filled this evidentiary gap by enabling preliminary cost-effectiveness assessments during technology adoption. However, their reliance on assumptions in the absence of robust long-term data introduces significant uncertainty. Such heterogeneity in study designs creates substantial methodological challenges for assessing fast-developing surgical innovations. While modeling provides necessary flexibility for early assessment, the field urgently requires more RCTs like Kayani et al.‘s trial, which incorporated both economic and clinical outcomes over a 2-year follow-up period- to validate simulated outcomes.
Economic evaluation outcomes of robotic-assisted arthroplasty generally indicate favorable results, with potential to improve patient outcomes and optimize resource utilization. However, the magnitude of these benefits varies across settings, reflecting the influence of contextual factors such as surgical volume, patient characteristics, and follow-up duration on the overall economic value. High-volume centers demonstrate greater cost-efficiency through optimized implementation, while the technology’s viability hinges on balancing initial investments against long-term gains. Innovative procurement strategies, such as volume-based pricing agreements, may mitigate upfront costs without compromising quality. Patient- and clinical-level heterogeneity also plays a critical role in determining the economic value of robotic-assisted arthroplasty. Different patient populations, such as older adults, those with multiple comorbidities, or patients undergoing complex procedures, may experience varying clinical benefits and associated costs. Therefore, future economic evaluations should incorporate a broader range of patient populations to enable more generalizable and context-specific assessments of cost-effectiveness.
Our review has several limitations. First, despite employing a comprehensive search strategy, non-English studies were excluded, potentially omitting relevant evidence. Second, most included studies did not adopt a patient-level perspective and failed to incorporate patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), limiting the assessment of outcomes meaningful to patients. Third, heterogeneity in study designs, model structures, and assumptions hindered direct comparison of cost-effectiveness results. Finally, the sensitivity of ICERs to institutional factors such as surgical volume and follow-up duration may reduce the generalizability of current findings, emphasizing the need to account for these variables in future evaluations.

Conclusions
Current economic evaluations of robot-assisted arthroplasty, including cost-utility, cost-effectiveness analysis, and cost-consequence analyses, are highly heterogeneous in methodology, cost and outcome measures, and reported results. In addition, there is an urgent need to standardize the definition and measurement of both short-term and long-term cost and outcome indicators for robotic assist arthroplasty. Moreover, early-stage economic modeling and robust trial-based evaluations should be prioritized to better determine the long-term cost-effectiveness of robot-assisted arthroplasty.
