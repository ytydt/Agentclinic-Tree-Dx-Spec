Economic evaluation of newborn screening for congenital cytomegalovirus infection: A systematic review
Purpose
This systematic review analyzes economic evaluations of newborn screening for congenital cytomegalovirus (cCMV) infection to identify key factors influencing cost-effectiveness and differences in methodological approaches.
Methods
Following a pre-registered PROSPERO protocol (CRD42023441587), we conducted a comprehensive literature search across multiple databases on July 4, 2024. The review included both full economic evaluations (cost and outcomes) and partial economic evaluations (cost only). Two reviewers independently performed data extraction and synthesis. Quality assessment used the Consensus Health Economic Criteria extended checklist and the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 Statement.
Results
From 543 records, nine studies met inclusion criteria: four full and five partial economic evaluations. Two full economic evaluations provided the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio per quality-adjusted life year. Full economic evaluations using decision trees and Markov models generally found universal screening more cost-effective than targeted screening, despite higher incremental costs. Partial evaluations focused on direct costs, with varying inclusion of long-term care costs. Most studies adopted a healthcare system perspective, excluding indirect costs. Quality assessment of full economic evaluations revealed high methodological standards but identified common limitations in outcome measurement and reporting.
Conclusions
Available evidence suggests potential cost-effectiveness of newborn cCMV screening, particularly with early intervention strategies. However, limited full economic evaluations and heterogeneous methodological approaches preclude definitive conclusions, highlighting the need for additional research across diverse healthcare contexts.

Introduction
Congenital cytomegalovirus (cCMV) infection occurs through placental transmission from mother to fetus. Of infected infants, 10%−15% are symptomatic while 85%−90% are asymptomatic. The main clinical manifestation is sensorineural hearing loss, affecting 40%−60% of symptomatic infants. Neurodevelopmental outcomes vary, with a large cohort study showing 70.4% of children developing normally, while 29.5% experienced mild to severe disabilities. Other symptoms include growth restriction, microcephaly, and various neurological abnormalities. The infection frequency ranges from 0.2%−6.1% globally, with a 1%−3% mortality rate among symptomatic infants. De Cuyper et al.'s review of 682 children found that valganciclovir treatment improved hearing outcomes in cCMV-related hearing loss, increasing recovery rates from 9.4% to 44.5% and reducing deterioration from 28.2% to 6.3%. Early detection and intervention are crucial for better outcomes in cCMV-related hearing impairment and subsequent reduction of high-cost treatments. While Leber et al. emphasize detection within 3 weeks of birth, studies show treatment benefits may extend beyond this window—Pasternak et al. and Morioka et al. found valganciclovir effective when initiated up to 2 months after birth, though earlier treatment generally yields better outcomes. These findings suggest that while earlier treatment generally leads to better outcomes, the window for effective intervention may extend beyond the first month. However, the exact cut-off point after which treatment efficacy significantly diminishes is yet to be determined.
A diagnosis of cCMV infection is confirmed by the detection of CMV or CMV DNA in a urine, saliva, or blood sample taken in the first three weeks after birth. For the detection of cCMV infection, the sensitivity of real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using urine is nearly 100%, while the sensitivity and specificity of saliva RT-PCR are 97% and 99%, respectively; this indicates that these screening tests are highly accurate in detecting cCMV.
There are two candidate populations for cCMV screening: universal and targeted. Targeted screening focuses on high-risk newborns, primarily those failing hearing screening tests (which identifies about 75% of cCMV cases with hearing loss). Additional risk factors include maternal CMV history, suspicious prenatal ultrasound findings, clinical signs at birth such as petechiae or hepatosplenomegaly, and preterm birth or low birth weight.
At present, universal newborn screening for cCMV infection is neither widely recommended nor implemented in most countries. Policy decisions in this area require the consideration of factors such as cost-effectiveness, clinical effectiveness, and practical implementation challenges. This systematic review aims to analyze economic evaluations of newborn screening for cCMV infection to identify key factors influencing cost-effectiveness and differences in methodological approaches.

Methods
The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023441587).

Data sources and searches
The following databases were searched up to July 4, 2024: the Cochrane Central Trial Registry of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Embase, the Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE) via ProQuest, EconLit with Full Text, the Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA) Registry, the International Health Technology Assessment (HTA) Database, and the National Health Service Economic Evaluation Database (NHS EED). We manually searched the references in the included studies and consulted experts in relevant fields to uncover any studies that may have been overlooked. Specific search strategies are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection
We included studies that reported complete or partial economic evaluations of screenings for cCMV infection in infants. Full economic evaluations were defined as those that compare the costs of the intervention with one or more alternative interventions and relate them to the outcomes measured. Full economic evaluations include CEA, cost–utility analysis, cost–benefit analysis, cost-minimization analysis, and cost–consequence analysis. Partial economic evaluations were defined as evaluations that reported the costs of an intervention but did not compare the costs with an alternative intervention or with the outcomes of the intervention. Costs reported from either a patient, hospital, taxpayer, or societal perspective were included in the review. We included any study design except case reports and reviews. We excluded studies that were not published in a peer-reviewed journal. Additionally, unpublished documents and grey literature, including conference papers, dissertations, and patents, were excluded.

Outcomes of interest
Our primary outcome was the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) of full economic evaluations that compared universal and targeted screening with no screening. Secondary outcomes included all other results of the economic assessment. Monetary terms were expressed in their original currency and were USD-adjusted according to the exchange rate of October 2024.

Data extraction and synthesis
Two reviewers (K.S. and H.S.) screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts independently and in duplicate to find studies that met the inclusion criteria, with disagreements resolved by discussion or by consulting the third reviewer (E.H.). These two reviewers meticulously scrutinized each data extraction to ensure comprehensiveness and accuracy. The data were organized into tables and described narratively because of methodological heterogeneity. Using ICER, screening strategies were evaluated as cost-effective based on each study’s willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold.

Quality assessment
We used the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022 Statement and the Consensus Health Economic Criteria (CHEC) extended checklist to assess the risk of bias in included studies. The CHEERS 2022 Statement assesses the quality of reporting for conducted studies, while the CHEC checklist outlines elements to consider in research design. Therefore, we utilized both evaluation tools to evaluate the risk of bias.

Table 2 presents the full economic evaluations of cCMV screening by Chen et al. and Aoki et al., both of which compared the cost-effectiveness of universal-, targeted-, and no-screening strategies and included QALYs as an outcome measure. Among the full economic analyses, only these two studies included QALYs as an outcome measure allowing comparisons across different health interventions and conditions. The other two full economic analyses used different outcome measures, such as cases detected or hearing loss prevented, which, while valuable, do not capture the full spectrum of health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) impacts associated with cCMV screening and treatment. Chen et al.'s study concluded that universal screening in the Chinese population would achieve the best health outcomes, with an ICER of 2,963 USD per QALY gained versus targeted screening, well below their 9,900 USD/QALY threshold. Similarly, Aoki et al. found universal screening cost-effective in Japan, with an ICER of 19,769 USD (2,966,296 JPY) per QALY gained versus targeted screening, below their 26,667 USD (4,000,000 JPY) threshold. Both studies concluded universal screening was more cost-effective than targeted screening, despite higher initial costs.
While Chen et al. included only direct costs, Aoki et al. included both direct and indirect costs. Direct medical costs covered screening, treatments, follow-ups, and interventions like cochlear implants. Indirect costs included educational expenses for children with cCMV sequelae and parental productivity losses, while patient productivity losses were excluded to avoid double counting. Notably, the sensitivity analysis showed that indirect costs didn’t significantly impact ICER. Sensitivity analyses revealed key parameters affecting cost-effectiveness: urine PCR test costs and discount rate in Aoki et al.'s study, and cCMV prevalence and PCR test costs in Chen et al.'s analysis. While Gantt et al. found results ranging from modest costs to net savings based on antiviral treatment assumptions, Phillips et al. identified twelve significant variables affecting outcomes, though specific details weren't provided. The differing approaches to sensitivity analyses highlights the need for standardized approaches to uncertainty analysis in economic evaluations of cCMV screening programs to ensure comparability and reliability of results across studies.

Studies varied in screening types, scope, with some focusing only on screening costs while others included treatment and follow-up care as a program. All studies reported direct costs: Williams et al. documented 24 USD per infant for saliva CMV PCR testing, while Beswick et al. reported 265 USD per newborn including confirmation testing and 18-year follow-up costs associated with caring for children with hearing loss. All full economic evaluations were assessed for risk of bias. Using the CHEERS 2022 Statement and CHEC extended checklists, the study designs and the reliability of the results were evaluated. Table 4 presents the results from the CHEERS checklist. Gantt et al., Chen et al., Aoki et al., and Phillips et al. each achieved 18 “Yes” responses out of 28 items. All four studies received “Suboptimal” ratings for their reporting of perspective, time horizon, and measurement of outcomes. Moreover, these studies uniformly failed to address three key areas: the health economic analysis plan, characterization of distributional effects, and approaches to patient engagement. Table 5 shows the results from the CHEC extended checklist. Gantt et al. received 16 “Yes” responses, Chen et al. and Aoki et al. each achieved 18 “Yes” responses, and Phillips obtained 17 “Yes” responses out of 20 items. Notably, no study received any “No” ratings, indicating at least partial compliance with all applicable criteria. Common areas for improvement were identified in the measurement of outcomes, with all four studies receiving “Suboptimal” ratings for this item. These results suggest that while these economic evaluations generally meet high methodological standards, there is consistent room for improvement in outcome measurement and valuation across all studies in this field.

Discussion

Cost-effectiveness of cCMV screening
Our review identified four full economic evaluations, of which two provided ICER expressed in cost per QALY. The two full economic evaluations by Chen et al. and Aoki et al. suggest that universal screening offers the highest cost-effectiveness, followed by targeted screening and no screening. However, it is important to draw conclusions from these findings with caution, given that they represent only two analyses in specific cultural contexts, which limits the robustness and generalizability of these results. The other two full economic evaluations in our review used different outcome measures, making direct comparisons challenging. The low number of full economic evaluations on cCMV screening primarily reflects uncertainty about clinical benefits, particularly the long-term outcomes in asymptomatic cases. Without clear evidence of screening effectiveness, researchers are hesitant to invest in comprehensive economic analyses. Additionally, rapidly evolving screening technologies make economic studies quickly outdated, further discouraging extensive evaluation efforts.

Variability in cCMV screening costs
According to the results from the partial economic evaluations, screening costs vary significantly across studies and countries, ranging from approximately 24 to 265 USD (19 GBP to 399 AUD) per infant. Aoki et al. and Chen et al. both offered cost breakdowns, although Chen et al. provided more detail; however, neither of them fully explored the differences in cost structures between universal and targeted screening approaches. Key cost differences could include screening, treatment, and follow-up care expenses, with universal screening generally leading to higher initial costs but potentially better outcomes. Neither paper deeply examined staff training costs or detailed follow-up care protocols. The other two full economic evaluations also did not explore the differences in cost structures by screening strategies. A more comprehensive analysis could explore initial setup costs, ongoing operational expenses, regional variations, long-term care costs, indirect cost impacts, and staff training expenses. This could reveal how universal and targeted screenings differ in terms of equipment, training, and infrastructure needs, as well as whether economies of scale in universal screening might offset higher initial costs. This level of detail would be particularly useful for policymakers considering the feasibility and long-term sustainability of different screening approaches in various healthcare contexts.

Methodological issues across the included studies
Our analysis identified several methodological issues across the studies that affect result interpretation and comparability. Our analysis revealed several methodological issues across the included studies that may impact the interpretation and comparability of results. These issues include variation in cost calculation methods, with some studies focusing solely on direct medical costs while others included broader societal costs; for instance, Aoki et al. included indirect costs such as educational expenses and parental productivity losses, while other studies focused primarily on direct costs. Some studies underestimated no-screening strategy costs by not accounting for later diagnosis and treatment costs of symptomatic children, biasing ICERs in favor of no-screening strategies. Studies used varying approaches, from decision trees to Markov models, potentially affecting long-term predictions. Most focused solely on hearing loss outcomes, overlooking other cCMV sequelae like developmental delays. Time horizons ranged from 1 year to lifetime, and inconsistent application of cost–benefit discounting across studies further complicated result comparability.
Aoki et al. and Chen et al. relied on secondary HRQoL data, neither specific to cCMV patients nor collected in their study countries (Japan and China). Chen et al. used adult population data, which may not accurately reflect infant and child experiences. The challenges in assessing HRQoL in young children who cannot self-report, combined with the use of data from different cultural contexts, limit these studies' accuracy. This highlights the need for age-appropriate, culturally sensitive HRQoL measurement methods for young children with cCMV.

Implementation barriers and healthcare system variations
While cCMV screening programs show favorable cost-effectiveness, implementation faces significant challenges beyond economics. Recent UK guidelines (2022) highlight uncertainties in identifying at-risk newborns, treatment protocols for asymptomatic cases, and optimal management approaches. Japan's 2023 guidelines did not recommend screening. Practical barriers include laboratory infrastructure needs, healthcare worker training, follow-up care pathways, and parental counseling protocols.
The applicability of these economic evaluations varies across healthcare systems. Single-payer systems like the UK and Japan may find results more directly applicable due to centralized decision-making. However, private healthcare systems face additional complexities due to fragmented payer systems and variable pricing. Cost-effectiveness thresholds and societal perspectives may also not translate well across different healthcare contexts.

Limitations of current economic evaluations on cCMV screening
This review analyzed four full and five partial economic evaluations. Their focus on hearing loss alone overlooks other significant cCMV sequelae like developmental delays, learning difficulties, and cerebral palsy. Although much remains unknown about the neurodevelopmental prognosis of cCMV, a large prospective cohort study reported that 70.4% demonstrated normal development while 16.9%, 7.4%, and 5.2% children presented with mild, moderate, and severe disabilities, respectively. Including these additional outcomes could significantly impact cost-effectiveness analyses, as lifetime costs of conditions like cerebral palsy and reduced earnings from cognitive impairments represent substantial societal costs. While broader outcome inclusion would provide more accurate evaluation of screening programs, the lack of robust long-term clinical evidence beyond hearing loss presents a challenge. This highlights the need for comprehensive longitudinal studies to fully assess cCMV's impact and screening benefits..
A limitation of this review is the absence of a quantitative synthesis of cost-effectiveness results across studies. While meta-analyses of ICERs are possible, they carry significant methodological challenges, particularly when studies exhibit high heterogeneity in design, outcome measures, and economic perspectives. In our review, the limited number of full economic evaluations (n = 4) and the substantial differences in healthcare contexts precluded a meaningful meta-analysis. Instead, we provided a narrative synthesis of the included economic evaluations.

Recommendations for future research
Future economic evaluations should prioritize investigating the broader impacts of universal cCMV screening beyond direct clinical outcomes. Key areas for exploration include improved early childhood development trajectories, enhanced parental quality of life through reduced uncertainty and anxiety, and earlier access to support services. These comprehensive benefits could be captured through expanded cost-utility analyses incorporating quality-of-life measures for both children and caregivers. To do this, long-term follow-up studies are needed to gather data on the extended outcomes of cCMV infection and the effects of early intervention. Additionally, studies should employ advanced modeling techniques like microsimulation to better represent these complex outcomes and adopt standardized lifetime horizons for improved comparability. Robust uncertainty analyses would help validate these broader benefit assessments, ultimately providing policymakers with more complete evidence for screening program decisions.

Conclusions
Overall results suggest that early cCMV detection and intervention, including via universal and targeted screening, demonstrate potential cost-effectiveness. Nevertheless, substantial variability in screening costs across studies, coupled with uncertainties in identifying at-risk neonates, establishing treatment protocols, and predicting long-term outcomes, presents significant challenges in comprehensively evaluating the efficacy of universal screening programs. Despite favorable full economic evaluations, the absence of universal cCMV screening in national newborn screening programs globally, as well as recent guidelines citing insufficient evidence, underscores the complexity of implementation. The narrow focus of existing studies on hearing loss and the exclusion of other critical sequelae, along with the potential lack of generalizability of model assumptions and variables to diverse healthcare contexts, further obfuscates the true cost-effectiveness of cCMV screening. Consequently, a holistic analytical approach, which encompasses both direct and indirect costs and considers a broader spectrum of long-term outcomes, is imperative for a robust assessment of the viability and impact of cCMV screening programs.
