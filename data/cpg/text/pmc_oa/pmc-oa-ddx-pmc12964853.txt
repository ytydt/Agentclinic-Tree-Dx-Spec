Gut microbiota, a new approach to management of polycystic ovary syndrome: a systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-evidence of 26 randomized controlled trials
Background
Evidence regarding the beneficial effects of biotics supplementation in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) remains inconsistent. To address this discrepancy, this study aimed to investigate the impact of biotics on health outcomes in women with PCOS by conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis.
Methods
Web of Science ISI, Scopus, PubMed, Cochrane, and Embase databases were comprehensively searched up to October 2025 to identify RCTs on prebiotics, probiotics and synbiotics on health outcomes.
Results
Twenty-six RCTs with participants aged 25 to 29 were included in the current meta-analysis. All participants were followed up for 8 weeks to 24 weeks. Results showed that prebiotics or probiotics and synbiotics are effective in reducing weight (SMD: -0.57, 95% CI: -1.05, -0.08) and body mass index (BMI) (SMD: -0.42, 95% CI: -0.75, -0.09), waist circumference (WC) (SMD: -0.78, 95% CI: -1.28, -0.28), insulin (SMD: -0.60, 95%CI: -1.12, -0.08), HOMA-IR (SMD: -0.79, 95%CI: -1.48, -0.11), malondialdehyde (MDA) (SMD: -0.77, 95% CI: -1.17, -0.37), and triglyceride (TG) (SMD: -0.62, 95% CI: -1.08, -0.16), low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) (SMD:-0.71, 95%CI: -1.21, -0.20), and total testosterone (SMD: -1.47, 95% CI: -2.32, -0.62), and led to a significant increase for total antioxidant capacity (TAC) (SMD: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.14, 1.01), and high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) (SMD: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.15, 0.61).
Conclusions
This meta-analysis suggests that prebiotic, probiotic, or synbiotic supplementation may have beneficial effects on several health outcomes in women with PCOS.

Introduction
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal disorder affecting approximately 8–13% of women of reproductive age and has been identified as a potential risk factor for cardiometabolic syndrome (CMS), representing a significant global public health concern. This condition is marked by numerous clinical and laboratory irregularities, such as menstrual dysfunction, insulin resistance, and obesity. PCOS patients also have reduced follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), thus increasing the luteinizing hormone (LH)/FSH ratio, elevating androgen synthesis from theca cells in the ovarium, and finally causing hyperandrogenism. Currently, there is no definitive cure for PCOS. Management includes lifestyle interventions and medications such as oral contraceptives, spironolactone, and metformin, which can alleviate symptoms but rarely resolve them completely. Thus, there is a pressing need for novel treatments that target the underlying pathophysiology and prevent long-term complications.
Alterations in gut microbiota composition have been observed in individuals with PCOS, potentially contributing to chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and metabolic disturbances. Women with PCOS exhibit reduced alpha diversity and lower abundances of beneficial short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria. Additionally, they show elevated serum levels of pro-inflammatory bacterial products, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), which may translocate into the bloodstream due to impaired intestinal barrier function. Additionally, LPS increases serum tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which are linked to insulin resistance.
Prebiotics are defined as “substrates that are selectively utilized by host microorganisms, conferring health benefits to the host”. Probiotics, as defined by FAO/WHO, are “live microorganisms which when administered in adequate amounts confer a health benefit on the host”. Prebiotics, probiotics and synbiotics primarily act by enhancing the composition of gut microbiota, reinforcing the integrity of the intestinal barrier, and reducing systemic endotoxemia through decreased translocation of LPS. This leads to a reduction in the activation of inflammatory pathways and boosts antioxidant defenses. Additionally, they promote the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which help modulate immune responses and improve glucose metabolism. While interest in prebiotics, probiotics and synbiotics is increasing, current research shows mixed results regarding their impact on health outcomes for PCOS patients.
The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of probiotics, synbiotics, and prebiotics on women diagnosed with PCOS. We hypothesized that the probiotics, synbiotics, and prebiotics significantly improves health outcomes in women with PCOS, leading to reductions in obesity, insulin resistance, and hormonal functions, as well as improvements in lipid profiles.

Methods
This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.

Search strategy
Web of Science ISI, Scopus, PubMed, Cochrane, and Embase databases were comprehensively searched up to October 2025. The keywords were selected based on the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) database and other pertinent non-MeSH terms. The detailed search strategies are reported in Supplementary File. This search included any time or language restrictions. The reference lists of eligible studies were completely searched to avoid missing related research.

Eligibility and exclusion criteria
Papers for inclusion to this review should meet the following criteria: (a) Intervention with prebiotics, probiotics or synbiotics, (b) Presence of appropriate control group, (c) Intervention on Women with PCOS (d) Reporting mean changes and SDs of weight, BMI, waist circumference (WC), hip circumference (HC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR)), lipid profile, blood pressure, glycemic indices, hormonal function including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), total testosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS), inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers followed by prebiotics, probiotics or synbiotics supplementation (or the outcomes were reported in the baseline and the end of intervention). Animal studies, observational studies, short communications, protocol papers, letters to the editor, case reports, case series, uncontrolled trials, and trials that combined the main intervention with biotic supplementation were excluded from this review.

Data extraction
Relevant data were independently extracted from the included trials by two researchers. The extracted information included the first author’s name, year of publication, country of origin, study design, total sample size, number of participants in each study group, characteristics of the study population (sex distribution, mean age, and mean body mass index), details of the intervention (type of biotic, dosage, and study duration), and mean changes with corresponding standard deviations (SDs) of outcomes in both intervention and control groups. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved.

Quality assessment and meta-evidence
The risk of bias in the included trials was assessed independently by two reviewers using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. The certainty of the evidence was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.

Statistical analyses
All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Overall effect sizes were calculated as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup analyses were performed to explore possible sources of heterogeneity. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I² statistic. Sensitivity analyses were carried out by sequentially removing each study to examine the stability of the results. Publication bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots and through Egger’s regression and Begg’s rank correlation tests. All tests were two-tailed, and a P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Gut microbiota and obesity

Weight
Prebiotics or synbiotics supplementation significantly reduced body weight levels in patients with PCOS (SMD: −0.37, 95%CI: −0.73, −0.02; P = 0.039; I2 = 0.0%, P = 872; two RCTs that included 124 participants) and (SMD: −0.71, 95%CI: −1.35, −0.06; P = 0.033; I2 = 89.7%, P < 0.001; seven RCTs that included 303 participants) respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1).

BMI
Pebiotics were associated with significant reduction in BMI level in two RCTs among 124 women with PCOS (SMD: −0.48, 95%CI: −0.83, −0.12; P = 0.009; I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.882) (Supplementary Fig. 2). While, synbiotics failed to show significant effects on the BMI level (SMD: −0.42, 95%CI: −0.85, 0.0; P = 0.053; I2 = 78.1%, P < 0.001).

WC
Reduced in WC was revealed among women with PCOS following synbiotic (SMD: −0.84, 95%CI: −1.57, −0.12; P = 0.023; I2 = 88.4%, P < 0.001) or prebiotic (SMD: −0.73, 95%CI: −1.10, −0.37; P = 0.044; I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.966) supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 3).

HC
Overally no changes in HC was shown after synbiotic administration among 317 women with PCOS (SMD: −0.22, 95%CI: −0.69, 0.25; P = 0.351; I2 = 74.7%, P = 0.003) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

WHR
Five studies involving 317 women with PCOS evaluating the effect synbiotics demonstrated that synbiotics are able to decrease the WHR level significantly (SMD: −0.84, 95%CI: −1.52, −0.153; P = 0.017; I2 = 87.1%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Subgroup analyses
Subgroup analysis revealed that prebiotic or probiotic or symbiotic could reduce weight, WC in lower BMI (< 30 kg/m2). In addition, both weight and WC could be improved in short-term (≤ 8 weeks) and long-term (> 8 weeks) supplementation (Supplementary Table 2).

Gut microbiota and inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers

Hs-CRP
No significant reduction in hs-CRP was demonstrated in 7 RCTs that involved 423 participants (SMD: −0.15, 95%CI: −0.49, 0.20; P = 0.409; I2 = 67.8%, P = 0.243) following either prebiotics or synbiotics supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Oxidative biomarkers
Effect on TAC was noted in two RCTs that included 102 participants (SMD: 0.58, 95%CI: 0.14, 1.01; P = 0.009; I2 = 15.1%, P = 0.278). Change with MDA was noted in two RCTs that included 102 participants (SMD: −0.77, 95%CI: −1.17, −0.37; P < 0.001; I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.710). No change in GSH was noted in one RCT that included 60 participants (SMD: −0.03, 95%CI: −0.53, 0.48; P = 0.914) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Gut microbiota and lipid profile

TG
A reduction in TG was demonstrated in eight studies that included 463 participants after symbiotic or prebiotic supplementation. Subgroup analyses showed that synbiotics are associated with significant reduction in TG (SMD: −0.59, 95%CI: −1.11, −0.07; P = 0.025; I2 = 83.6%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 8). In addition, it has been shown that the beneficial effects of symbiotic and prebiotic supplementation is more evident in lower BMI (< 30 kg/m2) and long-term (> 8 weeks) supplementation (Supplementary Table 3).

LDL-C
Reduction in LDL-C was demonstrated in eight studies with 463 participants following symbiotic or prebiotic supplementation. Based on subgroup analyses it has been shown that symbiotic were more potent to decrease LDL-C level (SMD: −0.62, 95%CI: −1.16, −0.09; P = 0.023; I2 = 84.5%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Subgroup analyses revealed consistent reductions with short- and long-term intervention duration (Supplementary Table 3).

TC
Overally symbiotics and prebiotic caused significant reduction in TC level involving eight studies with 463 participants. Using subgroup analyses synbiotics showed more potent reduction in the TC level (SMD: −0.65, 95%CI: −1.27, −0.03; P = 0.038; I2 = 88.0%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 10). Moreover, synbiotics and prebiotic were ore successful to reduce TC level in patients with short or long-term intervention as well as patients with lower BMI (< 30 kg/m2) (Supplementary Table 3).

HDL-C
An increasing effect on HDL-C was seen in eight RCTs that included 463 participants (SMD: 0.38, 95%CI: 0.15, 0.61; P = 0.001; I2 = 35.1%, P = 0.148) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Subgroup analyses showed that synbiotics and prebiotic could exert favorable effects in either short or long duration and in individuals with lower BMI (< 30 kg/m2) (Supplementary Table 3).

Gut microbiota and glycemic control

FPG
Pooled analysis of synbiotics, and prebiotics revealed significant reduction in FPG level. However, synbiotics supplementation didn’t have significant reduction in FPG level in seven RCTs with 445 participants (SMD: −0.44, 95%CI: −0.85, −0.03; P = 0.035; I2 = 76.8%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 12). Moreover, subgroup analysis revealed that synbiotics, and prebiotic are associated with significant reduction in FPG in short, long-term supplementation and in patients with lower BMI (Supplementary Table 4).

Insulin
Overall pooled effect of synbiotics showed significant reduction in the insulin level (SMD: −0.60, 95%CI: −1.12, −0.08; P = 0.023; I2 = 85.1%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 13). While, subgroup analyses based on the BMI and intervention duration couldn’t show any significant effect (Supplementary Table 4).

HOMA-IR
Combined effect of four RCTs encompassing 269 participants illustrated that synbiotics are able to decrease HOMA-IR level in women with PCOS (SMD: −0.79, 95%CI: −1.48, −0.11; P = 0.022; I2 = 85.7%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 14). This effect was significant in patients with lower BMI (< 30 kg/m2) and in short (< 8 weeks) intervention (Supplementary Table 4).

QUICKI
Overall effects showed that synbiotics were not associated with QUICKI (SMD: 0.70, 95%CI: −0.08, 1.48; P = 0.078; I2 = 85.0%, P = 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 15). The notable elevated level was seen in patients with lower BMI (Supplementary Table 4).

Gut microbiota and blood pressure
No change in SBP (SMD: −0.21, 95%CI: −0.56, 0.14; P = 0.248; I2 = 43.8%, P = 0.149), and DBP (SMD: 0.04, 95%CI: −0.39, 0.48; P = 0.845; I2 = 63.4%, P = 0.042) (Supplementary Fig. 16) was noted in four RCTs with 241 participants.

Gut microbiota and hormonal functions
Combined effect of RCTs demonstrated a significant reduction on testosterone level level (SMD: −1.47, 95%CI: −2.32, −0.62; P = 0.001; I2 = 92.1%, P < 0.001). While, no meaningful changes in DHEAS (SMD: −0.06, 95%CI: −0.31, 0.20; P = 0.662; I2 = 35.9%, P = 0.168), and SHBG (SMD: −0.37, 95%CI: −1.12, 0.39; P = 0.341; I2 = 88.0%, P < 0.001) level was shown (Supplementary Fig. 17).
In addition, pooled effect showed no significant changes on FSH (SMD: 0.39, 95%CI: −0.35, 1.13; P = 0.303; I2 = 83.5%, p < 0.001), LH (SMD: −0.63, 95%CI: −1.50, 0.25; P = 0.159; I2 = 90.0%, p < 0.001), and LH/FSH ratio (SMD: −0.68, 95%CI: −1.52, 0.16; P = 0.115; I2 = 91.1%, p < 0.001) was noted (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses
Egger’s and Begg’s tests were applied to assess potential publication bias in the effects of synbiotics, prebiotics, and probiotics on the evaluated health outcomes. The results indicated no evidence of publication bias for HC, WHR, FPG, HOMA-IR, QUICKI, LDL-C, or HDL-C values using Egger’s and Begg’s tests (P > 0.05). Whereas, there was evidence of publication bias for weight, insulin, TG, CRP values using Egger’s and Begg’s tests (P < 0.05). However, the results of Egger’s and Begg’s test were in consistent for the following values BMI (Egger’s test = 0.023, Begg’s test = 0.076), WC (Egger’s test = 0.001, Begg’s test = 0.072), and TC (Egger’s test = 0.003, Begg’s test = 0.108). The funnel plots for the are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 19.A-G.
Sensitivity analysis indicated that leave-one-out approach could not change the overall results for weight, BMI, WC, HC, FPG, TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, and hs-CRP values. While, the overall effect of synbiotics, and probiotics on WHR was altered to non-significant by excluding Esmaeilinezhad et al. and Chudzicka‑Strugała et al. studies. Similarly, the overall effect of synbiotics, prebiotics and probiotics on insulin, HOMA-IR, and QUICKI were changed by exclusion of some studies.

Discussion
This systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs thoroughly assessed the effects of prebiotics, probiotics or synbiotics on health outcomes in women diagnosed with PCOS. Our study demonstrated the potential effectiveness of this intervention in enhancing aspects related to PCOS outcomes. The findings highlighted that prebiotics or probiotics or synbiotics could have beneficial effects on the obesity indices (weight, BMI, WC, and WHR), lipid profile (TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C), glycemic markers (FPG, insulin, and HOMA-IR), testosterone, MDA, and TAC. However, some outcomes exhibited high heterogeneity irrespective of subgroup analysis; therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, a previous meta-analysis showed that probiotic supplementation could reduce blood glucose in PCOS patients, while synbiotics did not significantly affect FPG. Furthermore, prebiotics have a greater impact on obesity indices and lipid profiles, including TC, TG, HDL-c, and LDL-c, than synbiotics PCOS. Prebiotics are non-digestible food components, specifically polysaccharides, that can enhance the growth and activity of microbiota, particularly Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria. These components confer health-promoting effects on the energy status of the host balance. They also increase satiety by reducing ghrelin secretion, thereby reducing food intake. In a study of overweight adults with a BMI > 25, it had been shown that 21 g per day of oligofructose for 12 weeks led to significant weight loss, which was associated with a reduction in ghrelin expression.
Organizing studies into various subgroups reveals distinct aspects of how prebiotics and synbiotics supplementation influence health outcomes. This approach may assist in drawing definitive conclusions. Treatment duration may influence findings. Both short (≤ 8 weeks) and long-term (> 8 weeks) prebiotic, probiotic, and synbiotic use were accompanied with favorable effects on weight, WC, FPG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C. While, short-term intervention was more successful in reducing HOMA-IR level and long-term supplementation was potent to reduce TG level. The different strains of synbiotics operate through various mechanisms, demonstrating their potential for reducing weight and improving lipid profiles strain-specific. Conversely, a different study indicated that administering L. acidophilus, L. casei, and B. bifidum significantly changed LDL-C and HDL-C levels. Additionally, evidence suggests that L. casei is more effective at improving obesity-related markers than the B. animalis VKB and VKL strains. A cohort study reported a 5 cm reduction in WC with a 9% reduction in mortality over 6.7 years, regardless of BMI in healthy subjects. Given our findings of WC reduction after prebiotics and synbiotics intervention in PCOS patients over 12 weeks, there is potential for significant clinical impact if these effects persist over a more extended period. Synbiotics or prebiotics reduce gluconeogenesis by modulating lipid absorption and excretion, activate the FXR receptor, and reduce hunger by increasing the levels of glucagon-like peptides (GLP-1 and GLP-2), and increase fatty acid oxidation by modulating the expression of PPAR-a, ACAT, FAS, and SREBP-1.
We also evaluated the effects of prebiotic or synbiotics hs-CRP intake in PCOS. However, we could not find a statistical difference between the intervention and control groups. Our conclusions regarding the effects on hs-CRP levels were consistent with the findings of previous meta-analyses. The meta-analysis results on the impact of synbiotics on oxidative stress showed that these compounds reduced MDA and increased TAC; however, this increase was not statistically significant on GSH. Additionally, the meta-analyses revealed elevated plasma levels of TAC and GSH; however, the changes in GSH were not statistically significant. There is a correlation between oxidative stress and obesity hyperandrogenism. Synbiotics may reduce hydroperoxides, ultimately raising plasma nitric oxide levels. They also can reduce MDA by lowering blood lipids and inhibiting lipid peroxidase. Prebiotics and synbiotics supplements stimulate the growth of beneficial bacteria in the colon, and some bacteria, such as Lactobacillus species, indirectly regulate inflammation by maintaining and repairing epithelial barriers, which reduces the impact of pro-inflammatory stimuli such as LPS. Commensal bacteria may enhance the production of antimicrobial peptides that play a role in inflammation pathways. In addition, certain bacterial species and their metabolic products directly affect pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (e.g., nuclear factor-κB) by acting as ligands for innate immune system receptors (such as Toll-like receptors). Gut bacteria may also influence inflammation by altering the differentiation and function of immune cells, like dendritic cells, producing cytokines such as IL-10. Prebiotics reduce Firmicutes, modulate the gut microbiota composition, and regulate immune responses. These actions may help reduce systemic inflammation and enhance antioxidant factors.
The results of synbiotics on blood pressure showed that these compounds could not improve SBP and DBP level significantly. The previous meta-analysis revealed a significant reduction in serum testosterone SHBG, DEAS, and FAI in women with probiotic or synbiotic supplementation. Nevertheless, our results confirmed only a significant decrease in testosterone. Synbiotic supplements can positively impact total testosterone by reducing harmful bacteria, improving gut function, and reducing metabolic endotoxemia, which supports normal ovarian function and reduces androgen production. The positive impact of synbiotics on triglyceride levels could be attributed to the probiotics present in synbiotics, which integrate cholesterol into the cell membranes of gut bacteria and inhibit its absorption.
The present study’s limitations should be noted. Subgroup analysis could not be conducted on prebiotic type and dose due to variations in the interventions implemented. Therefore, the effects of specific prebiotic strains on each outcome were not examined. Due to variations in prebiotic strains and administered dosages, subgroup analysis based on probiotic strains, species, and dosage was not performed. Also, because of the limited number of studies for each synbiotic or prebiotic intervention, subgroup analyses based on intervention duration were not performed. However, overall, subgroup analyses were performed based on the duration of the included studies. Furthermore, future studies should focus on the impact of specific prebiotic strains on each health outcome. This approach will provide more detailed insight into how different prebiotic strains contribute to the modulation of markers, ultimately enhancing therapeutic strategies for PCOS. In addition, most of the included studies were conducted in Iran which may limit the generalizability of findings due to geographical differences, variations in the genetic and dietary habits. Also, given that several analyses were performed, the possibility of inflated type I error cannot be ignored, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. The main strength of our study was the first meta-analysis that used the GRADE assessment in this field, which was suggestive of the obtained evidence inclusiveness. Furthermore, we utilized the GRADE tool to assess the certainty of evidence, enhancing the clarity and validity of our findings, as it is a recognized and reliable method for this purpose.

Conclusion
The prebiotics or synbiotics were associated with reduction in weight, BMI, WC, WHR, TG, TC, LDL-C, FPG, HOA-IR, insulin, MDA, and total testosterone and increase in HDL-C and TAC. However, due to high heterogeneity, and the predominance of studies from a limited geographic region, these findings should be interpreted with caution. While the results are promising, large, well-designed RCTs with varied types and doses of prebiotics, probiotics, and synbiotics are needed to confirm these effects and guide more definitive clinical recommendations for PCOS management.
