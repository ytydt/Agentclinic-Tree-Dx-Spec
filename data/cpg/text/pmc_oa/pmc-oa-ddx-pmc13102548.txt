Mapping and evaluation of global and country-specific cardiovascular disease risk prediction models
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, requiring precise risk prediction models for effective prevention and management. This review maps and evaluates globally utilized and country-specific CVD risk prediction models, including the Framingham Risk Score, Pooled Cohort Equations, PREVENT, WHO/ISH Risk Charts, INTERHEART, and SCORE2. A structured literature search was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar, from which 30 relevant studies were selected. Most of the models integrate traditional risk factors such as age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol, and smoking status to estimate CVD risk. While these models demonstrate moderate to good discrimination (C-statistics ranging from 0.66 to 0.80) and validation, their applicability varies across populations, with concerns about overestimation or underestimation in non-original cohorts. Notably, the WHO/ISH and Globorisk models address global diversity by incorporating regional calibrations, making them suitable for low- and middle-income countries. Similarly, the country-specific risk scores outperform global models due to their incorporation of local sociodemographics. Limitations persist across existing models, including the underrepresentation of younger individuals, ethnic minorities, and the exclusion of emerging risk factors. Future efforts must prioritize the development of locally validated, population-specific models to support equitable and effective CVD risk assessment and prevention.
PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY
Heart diseases and stroke are among the leading causes of illness and death worldwide. Doctors and health care providers use “risk prediction models” to estimate a person’s chance of developing a heart disease or/and stroke (cardiovascular diseases: CVD) in future typically within the next 10 years. These models usually consider factors like age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, and smoking. In this review, we looked at widely available CVD risk prediction models, including the Framingham Risk Score, Pooled Cohort Equations, WHO/ISH charts, INTERHEART, SCORE2, and country-specific tools. We found that while these models work reasonably well overall, they do not always perform equally well in all populations. For example, some models may overestimate or underestimate risk when applied outside the country where they were developed. Models that are adapted for specific regions like WHO/ISH and Globorisk or developed within a country tend to provide more accurate predictions, especially in low- and middle-income countries. However, many models still miss certain groups, such as younger people or ethnic minorities, and do not include emerging risk factors. Future research needs to focus on creating and testing models that are locally relevant and fair, so that people everywhere can benefit from accurate CVD risk prediction and better prevention strategies.

Background
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of global mortality, responsible for an estimated 17.9 million deaths annually. Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), encompassing coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke, accounts for the majority of these deaths. Early identification of high-risk individuals through robust risk prediction models is critical for implementing preventive strategies and reducing disease burden. These predictions are made using equations that incorporate a range of risk factors, including age, sex, tobacco use, body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus (DM), raised blood pressure, and various biochemical indicators such as cholesterol levels.
For over four decades, research has focused on identifying individuals at the high risk of CVD to target preventive and treatment strategies effectively. Numerous predictive models have been developed that mathematically combine multiple predictors to estimate the risk of developing CVD over a 5- to 10-year period. These tools are primarily designed for individuals without established CVD and emphasize primary prevention. The key features of CVD risk calculators include the variables used, calibration (how closely predicted outcomes agree with actual outcomes), discrimination index (the ability to distinguish between cases (events) and non-cases (without events)), applicability, understandability, and cost-effectiveness).
Given the variety of available models, there is an ongoing question about which CVD risk prediction models are most effective in accurately assessing the risk of developing cardiovascular events in diverse populations. While prior reviews have discussed the CVD risk prediction models, this review fills a gap by mapping and discussing the global as well as country-specific CVD risk models, with the goal of guiding researchers, health care providers, and policymakers toward evidence-based best practices in cardiovascular risk assessment and prevention.

Methods
We conducted a literature search using PubMed and Google Scholar in January 2025. The search term “CVD risk prediction models” was used with appropriate Boolean operators. No publication year restrictions were applied. We also screened the reference lists of included articles and conducted a manual internet search to identify additional relevant literature not indexed in the databases. From Google Scholar, we screened the top 500 results based on relevance to identify studies reporting the development and validation of CVD risk prediction models. This approach was selected because Google Scholar ranks results based on relevance and user engagement, often reflecting current public discourse and high-visibility literature.
We included peer-reviewed primary studies of adult populations without known CVD that developed or validated risk prediction models for cardiovascular outcomes (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke, CHD). Both global and country-derived models were considered eligible. We excluded studies that focused on predicting recurrent CVD events, comorbidity-specific CVD outcomes (e.g., CVD risk prediction among HIV patients), or those that used CVD risk models to estimate population-level CVD burden.
In total, 812 articles were screened, and 30 studies met our inclusion criteria were included in the final analysis, as shown in Figure 1. For data extraction and reporting, we used a narrative synthesis approach rather than meta-analysis, because the included studies varied widely in population demographics, and outcomes. Due to this heterogeneity, we grouped them by type (global vs country models) and summarized findings qualitatively. Each model was described within its original context.

CVD risk prediction models
This review presents a discussion on the CVD risk prediction models. A summary of the key characteristics of globally used models is provided in Table 1, while country-specific models are provided in supplementary material.

Framingham Risk Score (FRS)
The FRS was the first popular CVD risk assessment tool, based on the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) conducted between 1971 and 1974. This included 5345 participants aged 30 to 74 years with no prior history of CHD, either from the original Framingham Study or the Framingham Offspring Study. Over time, several versions of the FRS have been developed. The most recent version, published in 2008, is commonly referred to as the Framingham General CVD Risk Score, which predicts the 10-year risk of any major CVD event.

Validation:
The FRS has been validated extensively in diverse cohorts but generally requires recalibration outside the original Framingham population. In the Framingham derivation sample, FRS models demonstrated good discriminatory power and were well calibrated.

Strength and limitations:
It was one of the first risk scores and based on epidemiological data from the well-designed FHS. These risk predictors are practical, clinically relevant, and modestly accurate. It is appropriate for the United States, Australia, and New Zealand populations. It is one of the few tools that allows for risk assessment in younger adults aged 30–39 years and includes a broader range of outcomes, such as peripheral arterial disease and HF. Many risk prediction tools (e.g., QRISK, ASCVD Pooled Cohort Equations) are built upon FRS principles.
Studies have shown concerns regarding the applicability and validity of FRS in different populations due to the homogenous demographics of the Framingham cohort. For instance, in Germany, and the UK, these models overestimated the CVD risk. Furthermore, evidence from South Asian and African populations remains inconclusive, with some studies suggesting that the FRS may underestimate risk in these groups.

Pooled cohort risk assessment equations (PCE)
In 2013 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) Guidelines on the assessment of cardiovascular risk advised using the PCE to predict 10-year risk of a first ASCVD event. In contrast to FRS, PCE was developed from several long-standing population-based cohort studies including the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study, Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS), and the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study, combined with applicable data from the Framingham Original and Offspring Study cohorts with at least 12 years of follow-up.

Validation:
In a large contemporary US cohort (REGARDS), Muntner et al. found that PCE discrimination was modest, but predicted risks generally exceeded observed event rates. For example, across all participants the PCE significantly overpredicted 5-year ASCVD incidence.

Strengths and limitations:
Inclusion of stroke as an end point and race as a characteristic allows better risk prediction especially in African American individuals; however, the inclusion of race for CVD risk prediction in US-based risk scores has fallen out of favor to eliminate propagation of race-based risk algorithms and clinical care as recommended. The ACC/AHA guidelines recommend using the PCE to guide statin therapy decisions in adults aged 40–79 years without clinical ASCVD or diabetes, and with LDL-C levels between 70 and 189 mg/dL. Additionally, the PCE is recommended to assess cardiovascular risk in adults with systolic blood pressure (SBP) 130–139 mmHg. In individuals with a 10-year ASCVD risk of ≥10%, initiation of antihypertensive therapy is advised as part of a comprehensive risk-reduction strategy.
In external validations, the PCE often overestimates absolute risk in contemporary cohorts especially at higher risk levels. However, it tends to underestimate the risk in South Asian populations, and its accuracy in other ethnic groups remains limited.
Recognizing miscalibration with newer data, Yadlowsky et al. proposed a revised PCE. They re-derived the risk equations in 26,689 US adults (age 40–79) pooled from six more contemporary cohorts including ARIC, CHS, CARDIA, Framingham offspring, the Jackson Heart Study (African Americans), and Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA). Using the same ASCVD endpoints, the revised models yielded systematically lower risk estimates. On average, the 2013 PCE overpredicted 10-year ASCVD risk by about 20% across risk strata, with an especially large overestimation in Black adults. The updated equations improved calibration in all sex/race groups. Notably, about 12 million US adults who were deemed high-risk (≥7.5% 10-year risk) by the old equations would be reclassified to lower risk by the revised equations.

PREVENT (PREDICTING risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs)
The AHA recently (2023) introduced the PREVENT equations to estimate cardiovascular risk in adults aged 30–79. These equations provide both 10-year and 30-year risk estimates for total CVD, defined as a composite of ASCVD and HF. The PREVENT equations were derived from pooled, individual-level data from 25 US cohort studies (N ≈ 3.28 million) collected between 1992 and 2017. The combined derivation sample included 6,612,004 adults (age mean±SD, 53 ± 12 years, 56% women) without known CVD, among whom 211,515 incident CVD events occurred. Separate models predict ASCVD alone and HF alone, and enhanced models incorporate optional measures (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, hemoglobin A1c, and a social deprivation index) when available.

Validation:
The PREVENT equations were internally and externally validated in the original derivation study. In pooled external cohorts, PREVENT yielded median C-statistics for total CVD, with calibration slopes of 1.03 and 0.94, respectively. Independent analyses have confirmed these findings. In NHANES (1999–2010), the PREVENT score achieved a C-statistic of 0.890 (95% CI 0.881–0.898) for predicting 10-year fatal CVD events, with a calibration slope ≈1.13 (slight underfitting). PREVENT significantly outperformed the PCE in this cohort: the net reclassification improvement was ≈0.093 in favor of PREVENT. Similarly, in the MESA (N = 6098), PREVENT more accurately predicted 10-year ASCVD than the PCE.

Strengths and limitations:
PREVENT differs from earlier risk equations by extending traditional risk factors and including HF as the outcome. It is race-free, aligning with growing recommendations to eliminate race-based clinical algorithms due to concerns about perpetuating bias and inequity in care. The model also caters to younger populations and provides both 10-year and 30-year risk estimates for total CVD, offering a broader temporal perspective.
However, the PREVENT equations require multiple laboratory inputs such as lipid profiles, estimated glomerular filtration rate, albuminuria, and HbA1c which may limit their feasibility in some clinical settings. Additionally, PREVENT tends to classify fewer individuals as high-risk compared to the PCE, which may affect treatment decisions such as statin initiation. Finally, PREVENT was developed using US data, and its performance in international populations remains uncertain, necessitating further external validation.

World Health Organization/International Society of Hypertension (WHO/ISH) charts
The 2019 WHO cardiovascular risk charts were developed to replace the older WHO/ISH charts (2007) with more up-to-date, global data. The new models were derived from individual-level data pooled from 85 long-term, population-based cohort studies (the Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration), comprising 376,177 participants with no prior CVD (aged 40–80 years) and 19,333 incident events over 10 years. To tailor predictions to different regions, WHO used contemporary risk factor and incidence data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study and the NCD Risk Factor Collaboration (NCD-RisC) to recalibrate the models for 21 GBD regions.
Two parallel versions of the risk model were created. The laboratory-based charts include classic Framingham-type predictors. The non-laboratory charts replace cholesterol with BMI. To use the charts, clinicians first select the GBD region corresponding to the country (e.g., South Asia: Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal, Pakistan), followed by the appropriate chart based on sex and diabetes status. The color-coded chart displays risk cells based on the risk factors. Unlike the 2007 charts, the new WHO charts categorize 10-year CVD risk into five categories. These categories emphasize earlier intervention at lower risk levels.

Validation:
The WHO 2019 CVD risk charts were both internally and externally validated. Internal validation, using the Emerging Risk Factors cohorts in an internal-external cross-validation design, showed moderate discrimination, with C-indices ranging from 0.66 to 0.76 (lowest for men on the non-lab model, highest for women on the lab model) and generally good calibration within cohorts. External validation in 19 independent studies (>1.09 million people, 25,950 events) confirmed that the models also discriminated well outside the derivation samples.
Regional calibration was performed using GBD incidence data and NCD-RisC risk factor distributions. The 21 geographic regions (e.g., East Asia, South Asia, Middle East, subregions of Africa and Latin America, Europe, North America, Pacific) were defined to capture broad epidemiological patterns. Applying the charts to WHO STEPwise survey data from 79 countries further supported regional calibration: the proportion of adults classified into moderate- and high-risk categories differed across regions in line with known epidemiological patterns. For example, a 60-year-old male smoker with SBP 140 mmHg and cholesterol 5 mmol/L had a 10-year CVD risk of ~11% in Andean Latin America versus ~30% in Central Asia, and the proportion of 40–64 year-olds at >20% 10-year risk ranged from <1% (Uganda) to >16% (Egypt). These results show that the WHO charts stratify risk well within regions and that regional recalibration is important to capture differences in absolute risk.

Strengths and limitations:
The 2019 WHO charts are built on large, global datasets and are systematically recalibrated to modern, region-specific CVD incidence and risk factor levels. These charts are available for 21 global regions, allowing for population-specific risk estimation, which enhances their applicability across diverse settings. They assess a combined outcome of fatal and non-fatal events. The BMI-based chart is intended for resource-limited settings where cholesterol testing may not be available. WHO has incorporated these models into its HEARTS package, facilitating their use by ministries of health.
However, the model is limited to traditional risk factors, it does not include lifestyle, diet, or psychosocial variables, nor family history or newer biomarkers, which may also influence risk. Also, the derivation cohorts were mostly from high-income countries (Europe, North America, Australia), as a result, the underlying risk associations may not perfectly reflect all populations. Finally, the charts apply to ages 40–80, reducing their applicability to younger adults. Nevertheless, the WHO 2019 charts address many limitations of older models and provide a transparent framework that can be updated as new data (including local surveys) become available.

Globorisk
In 2015, Hajifathalian et al. developed Globorisk, a set of country-specific CVD risk prediction equations, based on pooled data from multiple prospective cohorts involving adults aged 40–80 years. The primary aim was to address the limitations of traditional risk scores which were largely calibrated for high-income or specific populations. Globorisk employs Cox proportional hazards models incorporating age, sex, and major risk factors to estimate the probability of a first CVD event (fatal or nonfatal). In 2017, they released risk charts and online calculators for 182 countries, making it the first CVD risk tool designed for global applicability.

Validation:
Globorisk has demonstrated good statistical performance in both internal and external validation. The laboratory model showed C-statistics of approximately 0.70–0.72 and acceptable calibration for both men and women. Internal-external validation was performed by iteratively withholding individual cohorts, and external validation was conducted in independent cohorts not used in derivation (e.g., the Scottish Heart Health Study, Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study, and Australian AusDiab cohort).
Calibration was further addressed at the country level, as Globorisk is explicitly designed for national recalibration. This involves incorporating country-specific CVD incidence and risk factor distributions, and performance has been evaluated through split-sample internal validation, external validation, and comparison with observed national CVD incidence or mortality rates. Leave-one-cohort-out validation in the development study demonstrated calibration slopes close to 1.0 and minimal systematic miscalibration across diverse cohorts, while country-specific recalibrations in multiple settings (e.g., Iran, Scotland, Australia) improved agreement between predicted and observed 10-year risk. Comparative analyses with WHO risk charts showed strong correlation in an Iranian cohort, though Globorisk classified slightly more individuals as high-risk. Similarly, a Dutch study comparing SCORE and Globorisk found all models tended to underestimate 10-year risk, emphasizing the importance of local recalibration. Globorisk maintains meaningful discrimination and is well-calibrated across diverse populations, with empirically supported national recalibration.

Strengths and limitations:
The primary strength of Globorisk lies in its global applicability. By design, it can be implemented in any country with access to basic epidemiological data. It offers two versions, accommodating both clinical and low-resource settings. Additionally, the availability of online calculators and color-coded risk charts for 182 countries highlights its practical utility. As a tool aligned with global CVD prevention efforts, Globorisk enables estimation of how many people exceed treatment thresholds, which is critical for monitoring WHO targets of treating ≥ 50% of high-risk adults.
However, a notable limitation is that the original Globorisk coefficients were derived from a limited set of cohorts, all based in the USA, albeit with diverse ethnic representation. This raises concerns about the generalizability of the baseline hazard function and risk factor coefficients, even though recalibration mitigates some differences.

INTERHEART risk score
Building on the findings of the INTERHEART study, McGorrian et al. derived the INTERHEART Modifiable Risk Score (IHMRS) using data from 19,470 participants across 52 countries. The score was developed as a simple, globally applicable tool to quantify an individual’s myocardial infarction (MI) risk burden, relying primarily on non-laboratory-based data. The risk score was developed using logistic regression and provides a composite score from 0 to 48. It has since been applied in several community-based studies.

Validation:
In internal (split-sample) validation, the IHMRS showed good discrimination for incident MI. In external cohorts it also performed well, a 12% increase in MI risk per one-point score increment and a C-statistic ≈0.69 in a prospective validation population. More recent prospective validation in the PURE cohort (156,000 participants across 17 countries) assessed both the non-laboratory (NL-IHRS) and fasting-cholesterol (FC) scores for predicting composite CVD outcomes. The NL-IHRS achieved an overall C-statistic ≈0.69 (95% CI 0.68–0.70) for major CVD, with region-specific values ranging ~0.64–0.74. Calibration plots indicated systematic overestimation of risk (slope ≈0.77), so the model was recalibrated to local event rates, improving the slope to ≈1.0 and modestly boosting C-statistics to ≈0.72. The lab-based FC-IHRS yielded very similar results (overall C ≈ 0.71 raw, 0.74 after recalibration). Head-to-head, the two versions classified risk groups almost identically: FC-IHRS had only a trivial edge in discrimination (C ≈ 0.74 vs 0.73, p < 0.001) and a Cohen’s κ≈0.52 agreement. In short, both models show moderate to good ability to estimate risk across income and world regions.

Strengths and limitations:
The INTERHEART score’s main strengths are its simplicity and global derivation. It draws on a multinational sample (including many women and LMIC populations) and requires only history and basic exams, making it practical in settings without laboratories. It uniquely incorporates modifiable factors aligning with WHO NCD action plans that target lifestyle risk. Finally, the INTERHEART score’s predictive accuracy is comparable to other well-known charts (Framingham, SCORE, WHO/ISH) even without lab data.
However, the score was derived from a case – control MI study, not a prospective cohort, so it does not directly model time-to-event or 10-year risk, and it was designed for MI only (not total CVD). In practice this means it yields a “risk burden” score rather than an absolute risk estimate over a specific period and WHO guidelines emphasize total CVD risk assessment in all countries.

Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE)
The initial SCORE algorithm was developed in 2013 based on data from 12 European cohort studies (n = 205178) to predict fatal CVD outcomes. Most cohorts were population-based, though some occupational cohorts were recruited before 1986. In 2021, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) revised SCORE to address the contemporary European population risk. SCORE2 estimate 10-year fatal and non-fatal CVD risk in healthy individuals in Europe and was based on data from 45 prospective cohorts (677684 participants) in 13 countries included in the Emerging Risk Factor Collaboration and the UK Biobank. The algorithm comprises three versions, low, high and very high-risk regions. Two parallel score charts were produced (one using total cholesterol, the other the total-to-HDL cholesterol ratio), facilitating clinical use.
Risk classifications vary by age group, for individuals under 50, a risk of less than 2.5% is considered low, 2.5–7.5% is high, and above 7.5% is very high. For those aged 50–69, risk is classified as low if below 5%, high between 5% and 10%, and very high if greater than 10%.
A parallel extension (SCORE2-OP) was simultaneously developed for older persons (age 70–89) using similar methods.

Validation:
The SCORE2 models were tested on data from 25 separate cohort studies (≈1.13 million individuals, ~43,500 CVD events) spanning Europe. This validation found modest-to-good discrimination). SCORE2 has shown close agreement between predicted and observed risk: for example, in the EPIC-Norfolk study (n ≈ 19,560, age 40–69), the mean predicted 10-year CVD risk (3.7%) matched the observed incidence (3.8%), with AUC ≈0.75. However, the SCORE2-OP model for ages ≥ 70 has underperformed in validation: it tends to underestimate risk and has poor discrimination in octogenarians.

Strengths and limitations:
The SCORE system and its SCORE2 successor is specifically tailored to European populations and uses only routine clinical variables, making it straightforward to implement. ESC guidelines endorse the use of SCORE2 for apparently healthy adults. SCORE2 charts are calibrated to four European risk regions, which captures broad geographic differences in CVD incidence. Further, to facilitate clinical use, SCORE2 models have been incorporated into digital tools. The ESC’s HeartScore® platform implements the SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP algorithms with guideline recommendations and risk communications for physicians and patients. Numerous third-party calculators (e.g., MDCalc) and electronic health record tools also include SCORE/SCORE2.
However, diabetes is not included as a risk predictor in SCORE2 because individuals with diabetes are generally considered to be at high risk for CVD. SCORE2 also assumes that relative risk associations are consistent across all European populations, an assumption that may not hold in underrepresented subgroups. In practice, SCORE2-OP’s poor performance in those ≥ 70 highlights that even the updated models may not be optimal for all age groups.

SCORE2 Asia-Pacific models
The SCORE2 Asia-Pacific models are recalibrated versions of the original SCORE2 algorithms, specifically tailored for the Asia – Pacific region. Recognizing the region’s diverse and distinct CVD epidemiology, these models adjust the baseline risk using local incidence data and country-specific risk factor distributions. Four regional strata; low, moderate, high, and very high risk were defined based on WHO-standardized CVD mortality rates, ensuring that risk predictions reflect local disease burden more accurately. While the core structure and predictors remain the same as in SCORE2, the recalibration ensures that individuals in Asia and Oceania receive more accurate and context-specific risk assessments. This enables better identification of high-risk individuals, guiding preventive strategies and clinical decision-making across the region.

Country-specific risk prediction models
While global risk assessment tools such as the FRS have been widely adopted, their applicability across diverse populations is limited due to geographic, ethnic, and socioeconomic variations in CVD epidemiology and risk factor profiles. Recognizing these limitations, there has been a paradigm shift toward developing country-specific CVD risk prediction models. A summary of these models is provided in supplementary material.

Discussion
This review identified six global cardiovascular risk prediction models alongside several country-specific models. Their adoption varies significantly across continents, shaped by epidemiological patterns, guideline endorsements, and validation in local populations. In Europe, the SCORE model remains the cornerstone of risk assessment, embedded in the ESC guidelines. In America, the PCE dominate clinical practice due to their integration into US preventive cardiology guidelines. The WHO/ISH charts and Globorisk models appear most appropriate for low-resource settings. The INTERHEART Risk Score is also applicable for global use to assess cardiovascular risk burden.
The development of these scores has laid the foundation for evidence-based guidelines and prevention strategies in developed countries. However, all current risk models have notable limitations. Most global CVD risk models are validated for individuals aged ≥ 40, leaving younger adults underserved despite the rising prevalence of obesity, HTN, and diabetes in this population. Some updated models like FRC and PREVENT have incorporated the concept of vascular age to communicate lifetime risk in younger adults, translating their risk factor burden into an equivalent age metric to encourage early interventions. Moreover, lifetime CVD risk estimation has emerged as a valuable tool for younger populations who may have low short-term risk but high long-term risk due to persistent exposure to modifiable risk factors. Lifetime risk assessment can help guide early lifestyle and pharmacological interventions in this age group.
Cardiovascular risk may be significantly under/overestimated in ethnic minorities and populations with inherently higher susceptibility such as South Asians, if prediction models are not calibrated to local data. This limitation is especially relevant in LMICs, which bear more than 75% of global cardiovascular mortality, face unique challenges in adopting and implementing CVD risk prediction models due to heterogeneous population risk profiles. Western populations and may not adequately capture the elevated CVD burden in LMICs. The WHO/ISH CVD risk prediction charts were developed to address these gaps by offering an accessible, non-laboratory-based tool for community-level risk assessment. They remain widely used in resource-constrained settings for their simplicity and ease of implementation. However, their accuracy may be limited due to the lack of local calibration.
Another notable tool relevant to LMICs is the INTERHEART risk score, developed from a large international case-control study, which includes South Asian participants and incorporates behavioral and modifiable risk factors without requiring laboratory testing. This makes it particularly useful in community-based screening in LMICs. However, as a case-control-derived tool, it estimates relative rather than absolute risk and may not be suitable for long-term risk stratification or treatment thresholds. In comparison, the Globorisk model attempts to overcome some of these limitations by adapting the PCE framework to local mortality rates and risk factor distributions. For example, a study in South India found that Globorisk identified twice as many high-risk individuals compared to WHO/ISH (15% vs. 7%). For South Asia, where CVD events often occur at younger ages and at lower BMI and cholesterol thresholds, regionally adapted models are required to improve predictive accuracy and equity in care.
Although limited data are available directly comparing different models, evidence indicates that regional and country-specific risk scores often outperform global tools by incorporating locally relevant sociodemographic, genetic, and environmental factors. For example, in a multiethnic Brazilian cohort, the PREVENT risk score achieved an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI 0.72–0.80) compared with 0.75 (0.71–0.78) for the WHO charts, but PREVENT demonstrated substantially better calibration (predicted-to-observed risk ratio 1.21 vs 1.57) and a positive net reclassification improvement (NRI = +0.19). Similarly, reviews report that the Framingham Risk Score performs well in US cohorts but exhibits “absolute risk prediction problems” when applied to populations with different epidemiological profiles. Tailored models such as QRISK3, CUORE, and China-PAR illustrate how local adaptations better align with population-specific risk profiles. Such country- or region-specific models are notably lacking in South Asian countries, despite their disproportionately high burden of CVD, highlights the need for locally calibrated tools.
Most of the models rely on demographics and classical risk factors largely because of data availability and cost issues, however, some models include newer markers. For example, the Reynolds Risk Score incorporates hsCRP. which has been shown to significantly improve prediction accuracy. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) are actively being investigated: recent studies have combined multi-ancestry PRS with clinical factors and demonstrated better risk stratification.
The removal of race from new models reflects concerns that race, as a social construct, may encode structural inequities rather than biological risk. However, sex differs conceptually as a biological and hormonal determinant of ASCVD risk across the life course. Persistently lower risk estimates in women at identical risk factor levels may contribute to undertreatment of modifiable risk factors.
The quality of source data varied substantially across the studies included. Many models were derived or validated using retrospective cohorts or registry datasets, which are prone to missing covariates, and incomplete follow-up. Follow-up duration also varies widely. Differences were noted in how exposure and outcome variables were measured and validated. Approaches to handling missing data were heterogeneous, with some studies employing imputation, others relying on complete case analysis, and some not reporting. These inconsistencies limit the comparability of results across studies.
An important and often under-emphasized issue is the impact of switching between risk algorithms or updating them over time. Such transitions can have substantial effects on treatment eligibility, risk classification, and population-level statin allocation. For example, comparative analyses have shown that the PCE often classifies more adults as high-risk relative to the PREVENT equations, leading to divergent statin treatment recommendations in contemporary US cohorts. Conversely, the updated PREVENT models tend to classify fewer individuals as high-risk, which may reduce overtreatment but also raises concerns about missed preventive opportunities and underestimation of risk in certain subgroups. Beyond PCE and PREVENT, similar effects have been described when SCORE was replaced by SCORE2 in Europe, where reclassification significantly altered eligibility for lipid-lowering therapy. These transitions highlight that the performance of a model is not only a statistical but also a policy and implementation concern, with potential unintended clinical consequences.
This review itself has limitations. First, although we conducted a structured literature search and applied predefined eligibility criteria, a systematic review and metanalysis were not feasible due to substantial heterogeneity in populations, outcomes, calibration approaches, and validation metrics across risk models. Instead, we adopted a narrative scoping framework to map the landscape of CVD risk prediction tools. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the absence of systematic review procedures may introduce selection and publication bias and may not fully capture model-specific limitations across all settings.
Second, we focused on widely published global and country-specific CVD risk models, which means we may have overlooked local or emerging tools, particularly those that are unpublished or available only in non-English sources. Additionally, we did not review studies that have applied these tools to assess CVD risk in different populations. Despite these limitations, our review provides a broad overview of commonly used models, highlights key challenges in their application, and identifies important directions for future development.
To conclude, while several global CVD risk prediction models exist, their utility varies across regions. In high-income settings, models such as the PCE and SCORE2 remain widely endorsed. Models like the FRS and PREVENT incorporate younger age groups and provide lifetime risk estimates. Models such as the 2019 WHO CVD Risk Charts, Globorisk, and INTERHEART have made progress by offering simplified, non-laboratory-based formats suitable for resource-constrained settings though they still require recalibration to better reflect local epidemiology. Integrating ML-based approaches may help address gaps in access, personalization, and clinical utility, particularly if supported by robust local datasets. Ultimately, bridging the global gap in CVD prevention will require equitable, validated, and context-specific risk prediction tools that can be feasibly integrated into diverse health systems.

Future perspective
There is a need to develop or adapt models specifically for high-risk regions. For instance, South Asian populations have unique risk profiles, hence require region-/country-specific models. Innovative machine learning (ML) based models or hybrid approaches combining ML with traditional risk models or integrating them into mobile health (mHealth) platforms may increase accessibility and usability in low-resource settings. Governments and funding agencies should invest in the establishment or expansion of longitudinal cohort studies and national health registries to generate local data on CVD incidence and risk factors. This evidence base is crucial for developing or recalibrating risk prediction models to suit regional contexts.
Future research should explore approaches such as sex-specific thresholds, lifetime risk estimation, and incorporation of female-specific (e.g., hypertensive pregnancy disorders, early menopause) risk enhancers to mitigate unintended biases without ignoring clinically relevant biological differences.
The effects of model switching and guideline-driven reclassification should be rigorously studied, including the role of emerging risk factors (e.g., CAC score, genetics, inflammatory markers) that allow clinicians to reclassify patients upward or downward from algorithm-derived categories. Understanding how these adjustments influence treatment decisions and outcomes is essential for responsible model adoption.
Finally, risk assessment models should be integrated into national health programs or primary care screening guidelines to ensure they are used in practice. Studies are required to evaluate whether real-world implementation of risk algorithms translates into improved cardiovascular outcomes at the population level.

Article highlights
Compares widely used cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) risk prediction models based on risk factors, outcomes, and population applicability.
Offers guidance on how to choose an appropriate risk prediction model based on local context, available resources, and population risk profiles.
Highlights the urgent need for localized data and model adaptation to ensure accuracy and relevance in underrepresented populations globally.
Emerging machine learning approaches show promise for improved risk prediction using high-dimensional data but require robust, region-specific datasets.
Future research should assess real-world impact, including health equity, model switching, and integration of genetic, biomarker, and sex-specific risk enhancers.
