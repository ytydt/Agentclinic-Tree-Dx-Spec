Animal models of inflammatory bowel disease: category and evaluation indexes
Abstract
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) research often relies on animal models to study the etiology, pathophysiology, and management of IBD. Among these models, rats and mice are frequently employed due to their practicality and genetic manipulability. However, for studies aiming to closely mimic human pathology, non-human primates such as monkeys and dogs offer valuable physiological parallels. Guinea pigs, while less commonly used, present unique advantages for investigating the intricate interplay between neurological and immunological factors in IBD. Additionally, New Zealand rabbits excel in endoscopic biopsy techniques, providing insights into mucosal inflammation and healing processes. Pigs, with their physiological similarities to humans, serve as ideal models for exploring the complex relationships between nutrition, metabolism, and immunity in IBD. Beyond mammals, non-mammalian organisms including zebrafish, Drosophila melanogaster, and nematodes offer specialized insights into specific aspects of IBD pathology, highlighting the diverse array of model systems available for advancing our understanding of this multifaceted disease. In this review, we conduct a thorough analysis of various animal models employed in IBD research, detailing their applications and essential experimental parameters. These include clinical observation, Disease Activity Index score, pathological assessment, intestinal barrier integrity, fibrosis, inflammatory markers, intestinal microbiome, and other critical parameters that are crucial for evaluating modeling success and drug efficacy in experimental mammalian studies. Overall, this review will serve as a valuable resource for researchers in the field of IBD, offering insights into the diverse array of animal models available and their respective applications in studying IBD.

Introduction
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) manifests as a recurring chronic inflammatory condition of the intestines, prevalent in North America, Europe, Australia, and other developed regions. In China, the incidence of IBD has surged over the past two decades and is estimated to have reached 1.5 million cases by 2025. Both Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), the two main forms of IBD, present similar symptoms including abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss, rectal bleeding, and chronic intestinal inflammation. The pathogenesis of IBD is multifactorial, involving environmental factors, genetic susceptibility, alterations in intestinal flora, and aberrant colonic immune responses. Currently, a range of treatments are employed to mitigate clinical symptoms and manage complications of IBD; however, they often fall short of providing a cure and pose significant risks of adverse effects. Therefore, there is an urgent need to deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying this disease. The exploration of diverse animal models further enables researchers to simulate and investigate various facets of IBD pathophysiology, thereby yielding invaluable insights into disease progression and treatment responses.
Currently, animal models play a pivotal role in studying the pathogenesis, drug efficacy, and pharmacological mechanisms of IBD, with a predominant focus on elucidating the interplay between mucosal epithelium, intestinal microorganisms, and immunity. Rodents, non-rodent mammals, and some non-mammals are used for IBD modeling, and rats and mice are the most commonly used. The modeling methods include chemical induction, gene editing, and intestinal flora intervention. Large animals, such as monkeys, frequently exhibit spontaneous colitis, bearing greater resemblance to human patients.
Pathophysiological changes following modeling can be assessed through laboratory indicators spanning from macroscopic to microscopic detection methods. Common macroscopic approaches include clinical observation, gross scoring, and histopathological scoring. Molecular detections, such as analyses of intestinal flora, intestinal content, transcriptome, and metabolome, are essential, depending on the research objectives. Despite the chronic nature of IBD, the acute phase, characterized by active inflammation, is notably more detrimental, prompting the prevalent use of acute-phase models. However, there may be inconsistency in applying laboratory indicators to evaluate acute or chronic phases in these models. Currently, establishing distinct animal models for UC or CD appears challenging due to induced similar pathological changes and the utilization of identical scoring methods across different models. Another concern is that some human indicators, especially some fecal-related indicators, are not used to evaluate animal models.
In this review, we conducted a thorough search of reviews and research articles spanning from 2000 to 2023. We employed specific search terms such as “animal,” “animal model,” “epithelial barrier,” “fibrosis,” “inflammatory,” and “microbiome,” combined with variations of “inflammatory bowel disease,” “Crohn’s/Crohn disease,” “IBD,” or “ulcerative colitis.” Eligible studies were then identified via structured keyword searches in National Institutes of Health PubMed (2000–present), Google Scholar (2000–present), and CNKI (2000–present). Overall, here we provide a thorough review of various IBD animal models and associated experimental indices (Figure 1), with objectives of delineating the distinct characteristics of different animal models in IBD (Table 1) and examining related experimental indicators such as clinical observations, Disease Activity Index scores, pathological findings, intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, fibrosis, inflammatory markers, intestinal microbiome composition, and other relevant parameters (Table 2). These insights facilitate the assessment of modeling success rates and the evaluation of drug efficacy in experimental animals, and offer guidance for the design of future experimental protocols in IBD research.

The animals used for inflammatory bowel disease modeling and related evaluation indexes.

Rodent models

Rats

Used strains and typical models
Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats and Wistar rats are the common strains for IBD modeling. SD rats are induced colitis by free drinking or enema treatment using chemical substances such as acetic acid, Trinitro-benzene-sulfonic acid (TNBS)-ethanol mixture, dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), and oxazolone, and similar induction methods are used in Wistar rats. The dinitrobenzene sulfonic acid model and dinitrochlorobenzene model require skin sensitization prior to enema treatment.

Particular models
In recent years, researchers have tried to establish Chinese traditional medicine symptom rat models, offering novel avenues for investigating the therapeutic potential of Chinese traditional medicine in IBD. Reported Chinese traditional medicine animal models of IBD encompass various presentations such as liver-spleen deficiency type, wetness-heat in the large intestine type, spleen deficiency dampness type, and spleen–kidney yang deficiency type. For example, the spleen deficiency dampness type model has been induced by senna leaf and TNBS. The liver-spleen deficiency type model were established by electric shock, dietary disjoints, and acetic acid enema. The wetness-heat in large intestine type model has been established by high-fat/high-sugar diet and TNBS. And the spleen-kidney yang deficiency type of model has been established by senna leaf decoction combined with hydrocortisone and TNBS. Moreover, some food additives, carrageenan and sodium carboxymethyl cellulose, have been found to promote the process of IBD.

Advantages and disadvantages
Until now, no study has mentioned the different sensitivities of SD and Wistar species when using the same modeling method. The rat model is widely used because of its clear damage characteristics and its stability. The limitation of rat IBD models comes from their differences from human patients.

Mice

Used strains and typical models
Two inbred mice, namely C57BL/6J and BALB/c strains, are most frequently used in chemical induction modeling. In the same model, different strains show different sensitivities. Through the treatment of BALB/c mice with antibiotics and DSS, a Pseudo Germ-Free Mice model was created for studying the pathogenic microbiota in IBD. Presently, DSS and 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS) are the most widely used chemicals for establishing animal models of IBD. In Alex’s research, C57BL/6J mice is resistant to TNBS and is applicable to the Th2-mediated DSS-induced UC model, while the Th1-mediated TNBS model is more suitable for BALB/c mice. Some researchers argue that the DSS murine model closely mirrors human UC rather than CD, whereas TNBS-induced colitis is believed to more closely resemble CD, based on discernible differences in immunopathological mechanisms. Interestingly, although pathologists are trying their best to distinguish UC and CD, the clinical treatment methods for these two diseases are similar, including symptom control, anti-inflammatory remedy, and inflammatory regulation. Clinically, CD and UC can be adequately diagnosed and distinguished based on clinical, endoscopic, and laboratory information. However, almost the same pathological changes, including epithelial damage, distortion of crypt, and inflammation, were characterized in different studies and the same scoring method was used in different animal models.

Particular models
Genetically engineered mice are widely utilized in IBD models—a topic extensively discussed in prior reviews. Briefly, gene-targeting strategies of IBD mice include conventional transgenic, conventional knockout, inducible knockout, cell-specific transgenic, cell-specific knockout, dominant negative, knock-in, mutagen-induced, and innate models, and the targeted cells include epithelial cells and immune cells involving T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and regulatory T cells, etc. Mdr1a–/–, N-Cadherin, and NF-κB essential modulator knockout models present epithelial dysfunction and are suitable for exploring intestinal epithelial cell-related pathogenesis. Furthermore, genetic modifications in mice can lead to immune dysfunction, ultimately resulting in the development of colitis. These models were basically utilized to clarify the relationships between specific immune-related genes and IBD. The genes involved often include interleukin-10, T-cell receptor α chain, caspase-1, etc.. Sometimes, gene editing and other induction methods are synergistically employed. For instance, severe combined immunodeficiency mice with naive T-cell recombination have been utilized to create T-cell transplantation models. Additionally, SJL/J mice have been developed for chemically induced Th1 inflammation, while sterile interleukin-10–/– mice spontaneously develop colitis due to intestinal flora disorder. Certain mouse strains, including C3H/HeJBir mice and SAMP1/YitFc mice, are predisposed to spontaneous colitis.

Advantages and disadvantages
Compared with rats, mice offer numerous advantages in studying pathogenesis and treatment mechanisms related to specific genes. However, limitations persist due to differences between mice and humans.

Guinea pigs

Typical models
Enteric nervous system injury is associated with the pathophysiology and disease progression of IBD. Guinea pigs are the most common species for the functional and morphological properties of the enteric nervous system research based on the similarities of neurochemical coding in gut neurons between colitis models of guinea pig and human IBD biopsies, Hence, TNBS-induced colitis in guinea pigs has been employed to investigate the correlation between intestinal nervous system damage and colitis. Among rodents, guinea pigs are the most sensitive to sensitizing substances, making them an ideal model for immunological research. The therapeutic effect associated with immunity has been explored in DSS-induced colitis of guinea pigs. Guinea pigs with DSS-induced colitis are also used for exploring drug effects on the intestinal epithelial barrier.

Advantages and disadvantages
The guinea pig offers distinct advantages for studying neurological and immunological aspects in IBD. Nonetheless, its utility is offset by high expenses and management costs associated with animal care.

Non-rodent models in mammals
Compared with rodents, non-rodents are less commonly used in IBD research and are typically used in some specific areas. Due to their relatively limited research data, the following is a brief overview.

New Zealand white rabbits
The New Zealand white rabbit, with its larger body size, facilitates endoscopic biopsy procedures. Colitis induction in New Zealand rabbits via DSS treatment enables the monitoring of disease progression, from acute inflammation to chronic fibrosis. Furthermore, studies have reported on the therapeutic effects and mechanisms of microorganisms and plant extracts in TNBS-induced rabbit colitis. If endoscopic biopsy is required, the New Zealand rabbit IBD model is the recommended choice.

Dogs
Dogs are frequently employed in drug discovery and safety assessment studies. Canine idiopathic IBD typically arises spontaneously and shares a multifactorial etiology akin to human IBD, involving immune system abnormalities, genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and interactions with the gut microbiota. Being similar to humans, canines with idiopathic IBD suffer from vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss, and pathological changes in inflammation. Currently, most studies on dog IBD are associated with spontaneous models, which are evaluated through serum markers and microbial changes. Hernandez et al. tried to answer whether IBD dogs can represent the early stages of IBD in humans but uncertain results were acquired on the changes of mucin and lymphocytes due to the individual differences of animals, which need to be further explored. Some chemically induced models of dogs, such as TNBS enema and 6% acetic acid enema, can present similar clinical symptoms and histopathological changes to those of IBD patients. The dog serves as an excellent animal model for studying IBD due to its similarity to the human condition. However, the high cost associated with using dogs may pose a potential disadvantage.

Pigs
Experimental colitis models in pigs have historically been underappreciated. However, recent research has shed light on the fact that humans and pigs share comparable nutritional requirements, similar immunological responses, analogous metabolic processes, and comparable anatomical and physiological features in the gastrointestinal tract. Therefore, the pig colitis model holds promise as a viable IBD model. For instance, DSS-induced colitis has been successfully replicated in piglets aged 3–15 days, facilitating investigations into the regulation of mucosal immunity through dietary supplementation of amino acids or fatty acids. In the porcine colon, widespread inflammation was induced because DSS activated the NF-κB pathway. Pantothenic acid deficiency can also lead to the production and development of porcine colitis and the characteristic of chronic foci distributed along the colon is very similar to human UC. Fecal calprotectin is primarily used in human medicine as a biomarker of non-invasive intestinal inflammation. However, Barbosa et al. found that there was no difference in the calprotectin concentration of fecal samples in porcine enteritis induced with Salmonella typhimurium. Altogether, pigs offer a suitable model for studying IBD related to nutrition, metabolism, and immunity, despite the high associated costs.

Monkeys
Non-human primates are a valuable resource for biomedical research due to their biological similarity to humans. Research indicates that captive rhesus macaques and cotton-top tamarins often exhibit spontaneous enteritis, mirroring the clinical features observed in human IBD. Wang et al. performed transcriptome sequencing of the blood of rhesus macaques with spontaneous enteritis by using RNA-seq technology and found that 30%–40% of macaque monkeys had the same differentially expressed genes as the IBD human and mouse. A study by Takahashi et al. showed that prednisolone and vedolizumab can alleviate 0.25% of DSS-induced colitis in macaques. Monkeys demonstrate clinical features, immune responses, and gene expression patterns resembling human IBD during enteritis episodes, rendering them optimal subjects for IBD research. However, their constrained availability confines their utilization primarily to select preclinical inquiries.

Non-mammals

Zebrafish
Zebrafish have 87% gene similarity to humans. In contrast to the aforementioned mammals, they have a smaller size, a faster rate of reproduction, and optical transparency during early development. As a low-cost experimental animal, the zebrafish has outstanding advantages in the genetic and environmental aspects of IBD pathogenesis, which can track the state of disease development directly and dynamically because of its optical transparency. Zebrafish intestinal inflammation induced by TNBS or DSS is characterized by intestinal microbiota disorders, immune responses, and neuronal changes, similarly to mammalian or human IBD.
Currently, zebrafish IBD models mostly induced with TNBS and DSS have widely been used in the study of genes, development, gut microbiota, host immunity, and endoplasmic reticulum-related mechanisms. Zebrafish are significantly different from humans, limiting the extent to which research findings using zebrafish can directly translate to human IBD. However, such studies can offer valuable insights into pathogenesis and drug-screening processes.

Drosophila melanogaster
Drosophila melanogaster can be used for studying the mechanisms of intestinal disease because its several anatomical features are like those of humans. The proliferation and differentiation of intestinal epithelial cells in this model are similar to vertebrate intestines, helping to provide insight into the mechanisms of intestinal epithelial injury and repair. This model has been reported to be beneficial in studying the innate immune regulation pathway related to IBD, such as influencing the balance between microbial-induced epithelial cell damage and stem cell repair through Toll signaling. Additionally, research has shown that artificial sweeteners can enhance the intestinal immune response in DSS-induced Drosophila melanogaster colitis. Moreover, this model has been utilized to investigate the impact of Epstein-Barr virus DNA on IBD.
In future, Drosophila melanogaster might hold promise for large-scale genetic screening aimed at elucidating processes related to intestinal cell proliferation, differentiation, and function. It is anticipated that novel insights into the mechanisms underlying intestinal diseases will emerge from research involving Drosophila melanogaster.

Worms
Genomes of Caenorhabditis elegans have been found to have significant homology with mammalian genomes. The worm is transparent during the developmental stage, so its cells can be observed using a microscope. Bacterial–intestinal cell interactions can be studied relatively easily with worms, but the overall applicability of worms in IBD studies is limited due to their lack of immune and circulatory systems. A review has reported that worms are a powerful tool that can help in understanding the mechanisms of inflammation in the human gut and screening new drugs for IBD treatment.

Characteristic evaluation index
IBD animal models exhibit specific physiological and pathological manifestations upon disease onset, and the severity of the condition can be assessed through various methods. In this review, we primarily focus on characteristic evaluation indices of IBD animal models, which are crucial for assessing modeling success rates and evaluating drug efficacy. While these indicators are widely applicable in experimental mammals, clinical observation cannot be applied to fish, fruit flies, and worms. The current limitation of preclinical colitis models lies in disparities between disease etiology, diagnostic markers, and treatment assessment compared with clinical practices in humans. Nonetheless, their advantage lies in their suitability for exploring underlying mechanisms.

Clinical observation and Disease Activity Index score
The disease development status of model animals can be preliminarily diagnosed through observing clinical signs such as the disease activity, skin condition, and stool characteristics. Animals model used in IBD research have similar symptoms to human IBD such as weight loss, diarrhea, hematochezia, and depression. However, one notable difference is the occurrence of a dull coat color in animals. The Disease Activity Index is a quantitative clinical index that is calculated using the formula: Disease Activity Index = (body mass index + stool viscidity + degree of bloody stools)/3. In general, an increase in the Disease Activity Index score is an important manifestation of modeling success because weight loss, symptoms of diarrhea, and bloody stools can reflect the injury severity of the digestive tract.

Pathological observation
In human IBD examinations, endoscopy and colonic mucosal biopsy are commonly performed. Endoscopy allows the observation of abnormal mucosa, inflammation, strictures, and ulcers in IBD patients. Researchers using colonoscopy in animal models have identified thickened intestinal walls and the presence of fibrin, bleeding, or redness of the intestinal wall. In animal models, detailed examination of gross changes and histological lesions of the intestine is feasible due to the advantages of rapidity, reproducibility, and availability of anatomical methods. Following sampling, anatomical specimens of the intestine can be directly observed and the colon mucosal damage score can be determined based on the severity of ulcers, inflammation, and mucosal edema. In IBD models, the colons often become shorter, accompanied by splenomegaly. Optical micropathological observation is used to note pathological features such as mucosal epithelial injury, loss of crypts and goblet cells, infiltration of submucosal inflammatory cells, etc.. The extent of the pathological injury can be scored by evaluating the degree of mucosal inflammation infiltration, crypt loss, and mucosal edema. Another effective method is observing the ultrastructure of the colon by using electron microscopy and the reported ultrastructural changes of the colon include mitochondrial edema and endoplasmic reticulum dilation of epithelia, villi reduction, and interstitial collagen proliferation.

Intestinal epithelial barrier and fibrosis
Intestinal epithelial barrier integrity is a key focus in IBD studies, investigated through various techniques including chemical staining, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and Western blot analysis. These methods are employed to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the homeostasis of proliferation and apoptosis, tight junctions of intestinal epithelia, and mucus secretion.
Detailed reviews have been conducted on intestinal epithelial injuries associated with IBD. The homeostasis of proliferation and apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cell will affect the integrity of intestinal epithelial cells. A study found a decrease in Ki67 positive cells and an increase in apoptotic cells in the colons of IBD mice. Pyruvate kinase M2 plays a key role in promoting cell survival and proliferation. It is found that intestinal epithelial pyruvate kinase M2 levels were significantly reduced in IBD patients and IBD mice.
Defects in the mucosal barrier may lead to increased intestinal permeability. Research by Liu et al. highlighted increased intestinal permeability and the destruction of intestinal tight junctions in IBD patients. This research also provided insights into the molecular structure and regulation of intestinal tight junctions, as well as their involvement in IBD. Similar findings were also observed in IBD mouse and rat models.
Reduced mucus secretion has been reported in IBD. A review concluded that serum microRNAs (miR-19, miR-29) regulate the homeostasis of the intestinal barrier, inflammatory response, and autophagy of the intestinal epithelial by targeting certain signaling molecules. Specific miRNAs may become biomarkers in the diagnosis of IBD. As biomarkers for intestinal barrier disorders of IBD, the levels of D-lactate, diamine oxidase, and endotoxins in IBD patients were positively correlated with the disease activity. Similarly, D-lactate, diamine oxidase, and endotoxins in colitis rats and mouse were increased.
Moreover, IBD animals experience extensive tissue fibrosis. The single nucleotide polymorphisms associated with CD fibrostenosis are NOD2, R675W, G881R, and 3020insC, in which NOD2 mutations are also related to bacterial invasion and inflammation activation. The serum extracellular matrix reflects tissue remodeling. Increased matrix metalloproteinase activity in IBD mice and patients leads to an increase in the extracellular matrix in serum. Basic fibroblast growth factor is also closely related to IBD severity. To date, there are no highly specific markers for fibrosis, with most markers primarily serving to predict the prognosis of the disease.

Inflammatory markers
Various indicators associated with acute and chronic inflammation have been used to evaluate the severity of intestinal inflammatory response. As IBD is difficult to treat, the focus of immunotherapy is to control the severity of inflammatory reactions during the activation phase. The inflammation-related indexes can be divided into two aspects. The first is the balance of immune cell subsets and the second is immune cell function related to associated cytokines and proteins.
The result of routine blood tests shows that increased neutrophils in serum and colon tissue, as a characteristic of acute-phase inflammation, often occur in active IBD patients and IBD animals. By using the flow cytometer method, the distribution of macrophages, T cells, and dendritic cells has been detected in IBD animals. Lu et al. confirmed an increase in F4/80 macrophages in DSS-induced mouse colitis. A review has detailed the diffuse aggregation of T-lymphocyte subsets in the intestinal mucosa. Helper/effector and regulatory T cells and activated dendritic cells activate mucosal proteases and lead to the development of intestinal lesions.
So far, many studies have focused on the relationship between the cell products of immune cells, immunity, and IBD. These products consist of cytokines and inflammation-related proteins, including some antibodies and enzymes. Interleukin-1 beta, interleukin-6, interleukin-17A, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, and other pro-inflammatory factors are widely used as biomarkers in IBD studies, in which involved molecular signaling pathways are explored too. In order to evaluate the activation of neutrophils and macrophages in intestines of patients with IBD, it is necessary to detect some reactive proteins and enzymes, including lysosomal protein myeloperoxidase, C-reactive protein, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies 45, calprotectin, and chitinase-3-like protein 1 in serum or plasma, and calprotectin, neopterin, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, and chitinase-3-like protein 1 in feces or colon tissue. In these indicators, it has been reported that the activity of myeloperoxidase, the content of anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, C-reactive protein, and calprotectin were elevated in the serum, plasma, feces, or colonic mucosa of IBD animals. The C-reactive protein/albumin ratio is elevated in active IBD patients, so it has been recognized as a new indicator for evaluating inflammation. Anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae antibody—a kind of serum antimicrobial antibody—is detectable in the serum of IBD patients and in the serum and colonic epithelial in colitis mice. Besides, S100A12 is elevated in the serum and tissue of IBD patients, but S100A12 concentrations have a weak correlation with clinical and histological severity scores.

Intestinal microbiome
Alterations in gut microbiota abundance are one of the contributing factors in IBD. The review by Li et al. elaborates on changes in the composition of species-specific bacteria in IBD, including the decrease in microbial diversity and beneficial bacteria species, and the increase in potential pathogens. Because of the loss of normal anaerobic bacteria such as Bacteroides, Eubacterium, and Lactobacillus species, reductions in the diversity of gut bacteria have been observed, manifesting as decreased values across multiple alpha diversity indices including Shannon diversity and Pielou’s evenness and richness. Fusobacterium nucleatum are increased in IBD patients and it has been found that Fusobacterium nucleatum could exacerbate colitis in mice. Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes phyla are increased in IBD patients and mice. In addition, short-chain fatty acids produced by bacterial fermentation in the gut have a variety of effects on host metabolism and immunity. Studies have shown that IBD patients and mice have reduced levels of short-chain fatty acids in the colon. Acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid are the main short-chain fatty acids in the gut, which is closely related to the gut microbiota homeostasis. Parada et al. reported the sources of short-chain fatty acids and their relevance to IBD in detail. Additionally, fecal samples provide a non-invasive means of obtaining intestinal microbial samples, allowing the reflection of metabolic changes in the flora across different states. This method offers the most direct information on metabolic alterations between the host and the microbiota.

Other indexes
Oxidative stress plays an important role in the pathogenesis of IBD. Malondialdehyde, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione peroxidase activities were significantly elevated in IBD rats and active IBD patients. In addition, nitric oxide synthase is regarded as a marker of the inflammatory response in the gut. The nitric oxide produced by nitric oxide synthase may be implicated in initial cell damage, the formation of harmful peroxynitrite oxygen free radicals, and subsequent tissue healing in damage repair. Studies have demonstrated an increase in nitric oxide synthase levels in the colon of IBD mice. Reactive oxygen species formation can also cause direct damage to the inflamed intestinal mucosa and induce cell-signaling disturbance. Studies have shown that excessive production of reactive oxygen species in the colon of IBD patients and mice promotes the development of inflammation. Furthermore, stool marker studies of IBD have revealed a decrease in β-glucuronidase levels in both human and mice feces. A recent report suggests that fecal myeloperoxidase serves as an accurate biomarker of IBD endoscopic activity, indicating its potential utility as a characteristic marker in future animal IBD research.

Discussion and conclusions
In this study, our survey revealed the diverse array of animal models employed in IBD research, encompassing both mammals (rats, mice, rabbits, monkeys, and dogs) and non-mammals (zebrafish, fruit flies, and nematodes). Mammalian models, particularly rats and mice, are extensively utilized for investigating IBD pathogenesis and therapeutic mechanisms, while larger mammals such as monkeys and dogs often manifest spontaneous colitis, resembling human patients more closely. Gene-edited mice are predominantly utilized to elucidate the role of specific genes in IBD pathogenesis. Guinea pigs offer suitability for studying neurological and immunological aspects of IBD, whereas the New Zealand rabbit model excels in endoscopic biopsy procedures. Pigs provide a valuable model for studying IBD-related aspects of nutrition, metabolism, and immunity. Among non-mammals, zebrafish, Drosophila melanogaster, and nematodes serve as advantageous models in specific research areas. Despite the range of models available, none fully replicates human IBD. Despite numerous attempts by researchers to develop dedicated models specific to either UC or CD, success has remained elusive thus far. This area may warrant further exploration and focused efforts in future research endeavors. Additionally, the emergence of Chinese traditional medicine syndrome models underscores a burgeoning focus on Chinese traditional medicine research.
In this review, we have summarized several experimental indicators of IBD, encompassing clinical observation and Disease Activity Index, pathological examination, assessment of the intestinal epithelial barrier and fibrosis, evaluation of inflammatory markers, analysis of the intestinal microbiome, and other relevant indices. These indicators serve to assess the success rate of modeling and evaluate the efficacy of drugs in experimental mammalian models. Presently, a comprehensive research framework for mammalian models has been largely established, encompassing indicators spanning from macroscopic clinical evaluation to microscopic molecular mechanisms, including clinical assessment, postmortem and histological analysis, hematological examination, histopathological investigations, analysis of the intestinal microbiome, and elucidation of molecular pathways underlying intestinal epithelial barrier function and immune response. While these methodologies offer valuable insights, there remains room for improvement, such as the refinement of quantitative clinical indicators, optimization of histological scoring systems, and exploration of novel biomarkers. Furthermore, certain evaluation indices employed in human IBD assessment, such as neopterin, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin 2, chitinase-3-like protein 1, and fecal myeloperoxidase, have yet to be extensively utilized in animal models, warranting further investigation into their potential utility as valuable indicators in experimental animals.
