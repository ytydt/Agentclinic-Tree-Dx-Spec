Advancing One Health implementation and technological innovation in China: a technology assessment approach
One Health approaches integrating human, animal, and environmental health are critical for addressing complex public health challenges, yet effective implementation requires robust technological innovation and cross-sectoral collaboration. This paper delves into the implementation and technological innovation of One Health in China, highlighting its significance from a technology assessment perspective. China has made marked progress in applying One Health technologies across domains including emerging infectious disease management, food safety, and antimicrobial resistance control. However, hinderance remains in developing an integrated system for wider technology dissemination. We focus on four main categories of One Health technologies (molecular technologies, geospatial technologies, risk monitoring and surveillance, and artificial intelligence and big data) to offer insights into their benefits, drawbacks, factors for successful implementation, scaling and governance strategies that both facilitate and hinder technology integration. Despite China's accomplishments, challenges remain in cross-sectoral integration, data sharing, and governance. Hence, we propose policy enhancement on the adoption and scaling of One Health technologies in China, aiming to bridge gaps in data integration and collaboration and strengthen governance frameworks.

Introduction
One Health represents a multi-disciplinary framework that integrates human, animal, and environmental health through coordinated surveillance and response systems. In this context, technology plays a vital role in enabling surveillance, early warning, and coordinated response.
China has actively promoted One Health technologies and innovations in managing infectious diseases, food safety, food security, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and occupational health. However, significant gaps remain in developing an integrated system to better disseminate these technologies. Similar challenges exist globally, where countries emphasize policy formulation, resource investment, and technological advancement to promote harmony between humans and nature. While One Health principles are being embedded into national strategies for zoonotic disease control, AMR mitigation, and climate adaptation, practical application often remains limited.
The One Health framework operates across three critical stages: emerging infectious disease (EID) preparedness, prevention, and control. In recent years, One Health implementation has progressed, propelled by research methodologies, technological innovations, and cross-disciplinary collaboration. This model offers a structure for understanding how technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI), molecular diagnostics, Geographic Information System (GIS), and integrated surveillance systems, can support One Health goals.
Technology assessment evaluates the value of new or emerging technologies, both independently and compared to existing ones using criteria such as safety, efficacy, outcomes, risk management, financial implications, and ethical considerations, along with health priorities and cost-effectiveness. A comprehensive investigation on the current One Health technology landscape in China through evaluating cases of technology adoption and governance mechanisms would facilitate more effective implementation and scaling.
Therefore, this paper adopts a technology assessment perspective to analyze One Health technologies in China. We aim to map existing technologies supporting One Health objectives, evaluate their current status and scalability, and examine governance arrangements that enable or hinder their integration. Through this analysis, we contribute to global knowledge on how technology can strengthen One Health systems, particularly in resource-diverse settings such as China.

Methods

Study design
One Health framework for technology application and governance.
Fig. 1
This research adopted a narrative literature review approach, supplemented by reviews of grey literature and government reports. A literature search on databases, including MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Scopus for English sources, as well as China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Wanfang Data for Chinese sources, was collected. Besides, health technology assessment reports (e.g., from International Network of Agencies for Health Technology Assessment [INAHTA], National Institute for Health and Care Excellence [NICE], and Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health–Advisory Methodologies Committee [CDA-AMC]), international organizations' websites, governmental websites, and focused internet searches were also included. The search strategy used controlled vocabulary, such as the National Library of Medicine's medical subject headings (MESH) and keywords. Search concepts were developed based on research questions and preliminary search, focusing on the above-mentioned One Health areas in Fig. 1 (risk assessment, EID surveillance and early warning, AMR, wildlife health and natural environment, food safety, zoonotic diseases). The search was conducted between October 2024 and May 2025, covering publications from 2000 to 2025.

Selection criteria and methods
This study aimed at five areas of One Health technologies: risk assessment, EID surveillance and early warning, AMR, wildlife health, food safety, zoonotic diseases. Two reviewers independently and back-to-back screened the citations and selected studies. In the first screening stage, titles and abstracts were reviewed, and potentially relevant articles were retrieved and assessed based on the full text. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Specifically, we included studies focusing on at least one of the five domains of interest, in English or Chinese, and provided original or synthesized evidence. Commentaries, news articles, and conference abstracts without full text were excluded.

Inclusion criteria
Health technology assessment reports, systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials, nonrandomized studies, economic evaluations, case studies and evidence-based guidelines on the above-mentioned main search concepts were included. The inclusion was limited to English and Chinese resources.

Exclusion criteria
Non-relevant sources, conference abstracts, and letters were excluded from this review.

Results

Mapping One Health technologies
Based on Fig. 1, we illustrated the life cycle of One Health technologies from innovation and assessment to application and governance. Furthermore, we organized them according to their main application stages: EID preparedness (risk assessment, EID surveillance and early warning), prevention (AMR, wildlife health and natural environment), and control (food safety and zoonotic diseases). Details of the discussed One Health technologies, including their advantages, disadvantages, current level of use in China (widespread, limited use, and research use only), applicable sectors (wildlife health, animal health, and human health), are presented in Appendix 1, Mapping of One Health technologies in China. While technologies in certain fields (e.g., AMR, wildlife health, and food safety) can have multi-stage relevance across preparedness, prevention, and control, in this review, they were positioned within the phase where their primary implications are most evident.

EID preparedness
EIDs pose significant threats to human health. New emerging diseases are continually being identified, and they can spread rapidly, causing serious disruption to global health and economics. Preparedness relies on effective surveillance and early warning systems, which forecast risk exposures to prevent the potential larger-scale spread.

Risk assessment
Risk assessment encompasses three key aspects: risk identification, risk analysis, and risk evaluation. In the early stages of infectious disease outbreaks, it supports identification of transmission risks and prediction of epidemic trajectories, enabling timely interventions.
Current laboratory detection methods, including microbial culture and susceptibility testing, molecular biology techniques, and mass spectrometry (MS), are widely employed to identify pathogenic risks, contributing to early detection of causative factors of EIDs. While traditional diagnostic methods, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), offer high sensitivity and specificity, they rely on specialized equipment and trained personnel. In contrast, multiplex point-of-care testing technologies suits well for large-scale screening as it enables simultaneous detection of multiple targets within a single sample while reducing errors associated with single-marker testing. Advanced MS method named matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) also helps identify multiple biomarkers in a rapid, accurate, and cost-effective way. However, its sensitivity remains insufficient for direct analysis of complex samples, and microbial culture is still required.
Beyond risk identification, quantitative analysis of probability, consequences, and severity is essential for prioritizing risks. Commonly used methods (e.g., expert consultation, risk matrix, and analytical flowcharts) rely heavily on subjective judgment. Advances in statistical methods and computer modeling have introduced better evaluation tools. For example, an improved susceptible–exposed–infectious–recovered (SEIR) model integrates the entropy weighing and analytic hierarchy process (AHP) to weight indicators, and has shown promising data reliability. This model also introduces a contagion decay equation to account for time and interventions, thereby improving predictive accuracy. The consistency between the predicted results and real-world data demonstrates its reliability and practical value in EID risk forecasting.

EID surveillance and early warning
Building on the risk assessment, surveillance systems enable continuous tracking and early intervention of EID threats. In China, several systems support this shift. The National Notifiable Infectious Diseases Reporting Information System (NIDRIS) collects physician-submitted reports reviewed by health authorities, then the China Infectious Diseases Automated Alert and Response System (CIDARS) use the aggregated data to monitor 39 types of infectious diseases. However, CIDARS generates a large number of false signals primarily due to the limited sources of information, delayed reporting of EIDs, and the use of outdated early warning model techniques. To address these issues, various provinces attempted to adopt new AI-based warning threshold technologies and methods, which can establish automated and intelligent infectious disease monitoring and early warning systems to improve accuracy and efficiency.
Besides, the National Influenza Surveillance Network (NISN), comprising 408 provincial and municipal center for disease control and prevention (CDC)-affiliated network laboratories and 554 hospitals to gather influenza-like illness (ILI) case specimens from sentinel hospitals and conducts nucleic acid testing and virus isolation, enabling real-time monitoring of influenza trends. In recent years, early detection of H7N9, H10N8, and H5N6 has demonstrated that China's influenza network has achieved a high level of sensitivity and capability.
In China, routine surveillance is conducted for 55 known zoonotic diseases caused by 59 pathogens, such as highly pathogenic avian influenza and rabies. Additionally, the national vector surveillance system, which comprises 1097 monitoring sites across different vector groups, conducts real-time data analysis supported by an integrated reporting platform. Enhancing the sensitivity of detection technologies and establishing more proactive, forward-looking defense strategies represent key directions for the future development of prevention and monitoring of vector-borne infectious diseases.

Prevention

AMR
The “One Health” concept has been distinguished as a key element for AMR control in regional and global strategies. While antibiotic usage in clinical care contributes directly to resistance, the environmental dimension must also be addressed.
Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) lie at the heart of the environmental resistome, and their enrichment and spread through water, soil, and other compartments drive the emergence and persistence of the AMR environmental dimension. However, the emergence and dynamics of antibiotic resistance genes remain largely unknown. For the analysis of ARGs in the environment, current methods include isolation, PCR, and metagenomics. These three methods complement each other to help connect the environment to clinical sites. Nevertheless, metagenomic analysis is far less standardized compared with traditional isolation and PCR methods, making data comparison and synthesis difficult. Quantifying ARGs is also essential for risk assessment. Absolute quantification, expressed as ARG copies per cell, can be estimated via total cell counts using flow cytometry or nanopore sequencing. For site-specific risk assessments, a viability-resolved metagenomics approach can assist comprehensive microbial risk assessments and guide risk management for AMR control.
Moreover, a critical step for large-scale cost-effective AMR management is the identification of priority ARGs for control. One study proposed a framework that assessed human-associated enrichment factors, gene mobility and host pathogenicity, and identified 73 high-risk ARG groups, 35 of which are included in the World Health Organization (WHO) list of high clinical concern. Such tools facilitate targeted AMR control strategies by linking molecular surveillance to public health decision-making.

Wildlife health and natural environment
China has made significant progress in wildlife health and environmental conservation. Its monitoring system currently tracks 55 known zoonotic diseases caused by 59 pathogens across humans, domestic animals, and wildlife. This integrated approach enhances early detection and facilitates cross-sectoral coordination.
Advanced molecular techniques have been widely adopted in wildlife disease surveillance. For instance, next-generation sequencing (NGS) has revealed significant genetic diversity in astroviruses among wild animals in Yunnan, China, as well as potential interspecies transmission. Similarly, nanopore sequencing identified 27 mammalian virus families in South China, highlighting diverse viral communities and cross-species risks. MALDI-TOF MS has also been used for investigating multidrug-resistant Citrobacter spp. strains isolated from imported animals. Despite these advancements, the application of genetic editing techniques, such as clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR), in wildlife health monitoring remains relatively limited in China. Beyond pathogens, emerging technologies have supported environmental health monitoring. Biosensors and environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding have been used to monitor amphibians in China, improving the wildlife biodiversity assessment and understanding of vulnerable species. These tools enable real-time ecosystem evaluation and inform conservation strategies.
In addition, geospatial technologies have further enhanced surveillance capacity. Novel unmanned aerial vehicles equipped with hyperspectral sensors have been used to identify rat holes in desert grasslands, providing real-time data on rodent activity and helping to reduce plague risk. The integration of such innovative technologies with disease surveillance systems illustrates China's growing capacity to utilize multi-source tools for zoonotic disease prevention.

Control

Food safety
Food safety has also seen significant advances, as food is a common vector that transmits potential pathogens and substances to harm human health. China has demonstrated technological innovations to provide safe food to its people. Rapid detection methods such as enzyme inhibition assays, immunoassays, and sensor-based technologies for pesticide and veterinary drug residues have advanced to guarantee consumer safety. These methods are capable of providing rapid on-site results, making them highly practical for large-scale implementation in food supply chains. Also, genetic editing technologies, CRISPR-Cas system-based subtyping, was applied to improve the discrimination of pork-derived Listeria monocytogenes isolates, enabling comparisons at the genome level to provide deeper insight into genetic diversity and pathogen relatedness.
Additionally, the incorporation of AI has further advanced the present technologies used to enhance food safety. For instance, AI has been applied to food detection for both chemical and microbiological contamination. Tang et al. reported a model for detecting pesticide residues on apples using an electronic nose combined with three data recognition algorithms—principal component analysis, linear discriminant analysis, and support vector machines—achieving validation accuracy of over 90 %. Similarly, Han et al. built a hyperspectral imaging system integrated with a convolutional neural network for the detection of aflatoxins in peanuts, where the deep learning approach reached a recognition rate of more than 96 % at the pixel level, outperforming traditional models. The integration of rapid detection technologies with machine learning algorithms enables AI to further improve the efficiency and accuracy of food safety monitoring. Specifically, deep learning models have been applied to the quality assessment of fruits, meat products, aquatic products, tea, oils, mushrooms, nuts, and milk power, providing more accurate measurements. These approaches not only enhance detection accuracy and speed but also increase practicality for large-scale implementation in the food supply chain.

Zoonotic disease response and control
China's agricultural workforce, large domestic livestock population, and diverse ecology make it particularly vulnerable to zoonotic disease transmission. The One Health Zoonotic Disease Prioritization (OHZDP) workshop held in China in 2019 identified priority zoonotic diseases such as avian influenza, echinococcosis, rabies, plague, and brucellosis, highlighting the need for a multi-sectoral, One Health collaboration for disease prevention and control.
In China, DNA-based technologies such as PCR methods, meta-transcriptomic sequencing, nested PCR, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) are commonly employed to trace zoonotic diseases by identifying the specific species and strains of pathogens responsible for disease. PCR has been extensively employed for rapid detection of the rabies virus, enabling efficient case confirmation and source tracking. Meta-transcriptomic sequencing enables non-targeted, high-throughput detection of viral communities in vectors, providing valuable insights into viral diversity at the human–animal–environment interface. Nested PCR, when combined with genetic typing, has been employed to assess the prevalence of zoonotic pathogens in livestock and human populations. The widespread adoption of these DNA-based technologies in China allows for comprehensive detection of both known and novel pathogens, early identification of cross-species transmission risks, and support for evidence-based disease control strategies.
In addition to microscopic technologies, geospatial technologies also contribute to zoonotic diseases tracing through complementary macroscopic perspectives. By enabling real-time monitoring of human mobility, environmental changes, and infrastructure deployment, these tools support rapid public health response and informed decision-making during outbreaks. During the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) Omicron BA.2 outbreak in Shanghai, researchers combined passively collected mobile phone signaling data with traditional surveillance systems to map fine-grained mobility patterns. Moreover, China's domestically developed BeiDou Navigation Satellite System supported the rapid construction of the Huoshenshan and Leishenshan emergency hospitals in Wuhan with high-precision positioning, significantly accelerating the building process and enhancing emergency readiness. Additionally, AI has further enhanced zoonotic disease control by enabling integration of complex datasets, real-time risk assessments, strategy optimization, and predictive modelling. These tools facilitate outbreak monitoring and strategic planning, yet performance can be constrained by data quality and sample representativeness—especially in under-resourced regions.

Technology assessment

Fig. 2
Based on literature and the above mapping, we classify One Health technologies used in China into four categories: molecular technologies, geospatial technologies, risk monitoring and surveillance, and AI and big data (Fig. 2). The next section explores the technology assessments of these four groups of technologies.

Risk monitoring and surveillance
China has made important strides in building risk monitoring and surveillance systems, yet several challenges limit their timeliness and integration. A major issue is the weak linkage among monitoring institutions, hindering data exchange across health departments, hospitals, and social platforms. Hospital-based systems often suffer from delayed electronic medical record updates, compromising early warning accuracy. Future systems should integrate emerging technologies and social data for more responsive alerts.
In addition, systems such as CIDARS demonstrate high sensitivity but generate excessive false signals due to insufficient information sources, delayed reporting, and outdated modelling techniques. While high sensitivity ensures no actual outbreaks are missed, it simultaneously increases the likelihood of false-positive signals, creating operational inefficiencies. A pilot program in Tianjin achieved remarkable deployment rates, with 100 % software installation completion and sentinel hospital deployment, as well as a 98.9 % integration deployment in public medical institutions at or above the second level. However, this initiative face barriers including high costs, limited funding, and technical infrastructure gaps. These issues underscore the need for greater investment and capacity building. Another critical gap exists between customs, animal disease, and human health surveillance. Their fragmentation and lack of standardized protocols hinder coordinated outbreak response, especially during zoonotic and cross-border events. Harmonized evaluation methods are essential for effective One Health implementation.
Finally, leveraging big data analytics presents a transformative opportunity for advancing disease surveillance systems. In China, the integrated health big data platform (IHBDP) identified 240 immunization-deficient children, versus 20 by traditional methods. Yet deployment requires data quality assurance, secure sharing, and strong privacy protections. Despite the challenges, big data analytics continue to demonstrate exceptional promise for revolutionizing disease surveillance through enhanced real-time monitoring and predictive capabilities.

Molecular technologies
Molecular technologies form a critical foundation of One Health surveillance in China, offering high sensitivity, specificity, and resolution for detecting and monitoring pathogens. Common tools include PCR, mNGS, meta-transcriptomics, eDNA, and CRISPR-based diagnostics. These platforms offer significant benefits in diagnostic precision, enabling both qualitative and quantitative assessments. For instance, mNGS has been used to diagnose unknown pneumonias and detect co-infections, while CRISPR-based assays provide portable, low-cost alternatives for field diagnostics. Despite their potential, molecular technologies face significant challenges in implementation. Operational complexity and biosafety requirements restrict their use in field or primary-level settings. High costs and the need for advanced equipment and trained personnel hinder broader adoption. While simplified platforms such as loop-mediated isothermal amplification and eDNA analysis reduce technical barriers, variations in protocols limit comparability across regions. Also, complex outputs from sequencing platforms are difficult for non-experts to interpret. Moreover, scalability and integration remain key issues. Lack of standardized procedures hampers data interoperability and weakens surveillance consistency. To enhance utility, investment in regional laboratory networks, cross-disciplinary training, and integrated platforms that combine molecular diagnostics with epidemiological analytics would improve accessibility, data harmonization, and real-world impact across sectors.

Geospatial technologies
Geospatial technologies, including GIS, remote sensing, and global navigation satellite system—collectively known as “3S”, have become an essential component of China's One Health implementation, enabling spatially explicit risk assessment and real-time outbreak tracking. In terms of technological benefits, “3S” systems enhance surveillance scalability by covering wide geographic areas and integrating multi-source data. They also exhibit strong compatibility with other systems and technologies, expanding their functional responsiveness and enabling cross-sectoral collaboration. Furthermore, advancements in interaction capability have led to more intuitive user interfaces, enhancing usability and promoting wider adoption. Nevertheless, several challenges for “3S” technologies remain. First, ensuring reliability under diverse field conditions is essential for maintaining consistent data support. The collection and use of personal location data raise confidentiality concerns, which requires robust data governance. Additionally, managing data quality and integration from multiple heterogeneous sources poses persistent difficulties, potentially affecting analytical accuracy. Effective technical implementation often demands interdisciplinary expertise and interdepartmental coordination, which may be hindered by institutional barriers. Finally, long-term system maintenance (including timely updates, functional scalability, and error correction) is needed, as technologies and use cases continue to evolve. Looking ahead, geospatial technologies are expected to play a key role in building multi-point trigger intelligent infectious disease surveillance and early warning system. Although China's capabilities in this field rank among the most advanced globally, their application in healthcare remains limited. Continued innovation, together with supportive policies and cross-sector collaboration, is crucial for broader implementation.

AI
As mentioned above, AI-embedded technologies have demonstrated significant functional advantages in One Health applications through big data analysis, and intelligent surveillance systems, offering higher sensitivity, accuracy, and capability to process large-scale datasets. However, practical deployment remains limited by several challenges. First, compatibility issues persist due to fragmented data standards and difficulties in integrating multi-source information across departments, as One Health governance often requires cross-department collaboration. In addition, although some AI platforms feature simplified user interfaces suitable for roll-out in primary healthcare and environmental management settings, others still require advanced expertise, making them less accessible for frontline personnel with limited technical training. Furthermore, the reliability of AI-generated outputs varies, raising potential ethical concerns. In disease surveillance and food safety assessment scenarios, AI models with promising reliability in testing still showed instability when confronted with missing data. Frequent algorithmic updates may also introduce new uncertainties. Moreover, AI models’ high-quality performance requires large volumes of high-quality data as well as compatible hardware, which could be limited in grass-root settings. Finally, information security remains a significant concern. As EID surveillance systems and intelligent medical platforms handles personal and confidential data, AI-embedded systems may further increase the risk of data leakage. To prevent such issues, it is necessary to use secure communication channels, robust encryption, and appropriate authorization and authentication algorithms, alongside the establishment of relevant laws and regulatory frameworks to safeguard data security.

Discussion
Our review indicates that One Health technologies in China are advancing across the entire lifecycle, from innovation and assessment to application and governance. When categorized by application stage, these technologies contribute to preparedness (risk assessment, EID surveillance and early warning), prevention (AMR, wildlife health, and natural environment), and control (food safety and zoonotic disease response). The findings suggest clear progress in technological capacity but also reveal uneven development and persistent challenges related to cost, interoperability, and scalability. These observations highlight that technological advancement must be accompanied by effective governance to fully realize the potential of One Health technologies.

Governance of One Health technology implementation in China
China has been actively advancing One Health technologies through policy reform, targeted funding, and technological innovation. In the policy domain, the country has strengthened the Wildlife Protection Lawof the People's Republic of China and the Animal Epidemic Prevention Lawof the People's Republic of China, and established surveillance systems for livestock and wildlife to enhance animal health. To enhance food safety and security, the Food Safety Lawof the People's Republic of China and the National Agricultural Sustainable Development Plan (2015–2030) promote stricter regulation of food additives, the expansion of organic farming, and the reduction of food waste, including through the Anti-FoodWaste Lawof the People's Republic of China enacted in 2021. In addition, climate and digital strategies such as the "Data Element×" Three-Year Action Plan (2024–2026) emphasize cross-sector data integration and privacy-preserving computation to enable secure One Health data collaboration. International experiences also provide valuable models. For example, Pakistan's initiative to convert locusts into chicken feed during the 2019–2020 locust outbreak exemplifies innovative, low-cost response strategies. Global analyses have also highlighted the need to integrate genomic, epidemiological, and environmental data to enhance pandemic preparedness, while addressing ethical and standardization challenges.
Despite progress, China still faces governance gaps. Effective One Health implementation requires qualified personnel, adequate infrastructure, financial support, and political will. Regional disparities further limit capacity at the provincial level. A study of 16 provincial-level open-data platforms found that, while most provinces and provincial-level municipalities have initiated data openness, only Shandong Province and Tianjin Municipality provide comprehensive features like demand submission and feedback loops. Moreover, China's current legal frameworks, such as the Cybersecurity Lawof the People's Republic of China and the upcoming Regulations on the Sharing of Government Data, offer foundational principles but lack implementation mechanisms specific to One Health. Fragmented responsibilities and the absence of cross-sector integration continue to hinder coordinated governance.

Policy implications
To successfully adopt or scale these technologies in China, the central government should establish a unified policy framework that clearly defines the roles of various departments in health data collection, sharing, and application, while strengthening cross-departmental collaboration. Promoting data integration through the development of interconnected national and local health data platforms is also essential to address data silos. To ensure secure and equitable data sharing, the FAIRER principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable, Ethical, and Reproducible) should guide the formulation of an open data governance framework. Additionally, enhancing digital infrastructure, particularly in rural areas, is essential, including improving network coverage and access to digital devices to support widespread implementation of these technologies. Finally, public education and professional training should be prioritized to build trust in, and improve the proficiency of, citizens and healthcare workers in utilizing digital health tools.
The central government can facilitate the transfer, adoption, and scaling of One Health technologies in China through measures such as financial incentives, standardized regulatory frameworks, international cooperation, and the implementation of small-scale pilot projects. At the same time, private entities can play a complementary role by optimizing AI algorithm, developing data management platforms, and enhancing the adoption and usage of digital health devices through commercialization strategies.

Conclusion
This paper reviewed the development and implementation of One Health technologies in China from a technology assessment perspective. By mapping technologies across EID preparedness, prevention, and control, it examined the current landscape in four major domains: molecular diagnostics, geospatial tools, risk monitoring systems, and AI and big data platforms. Each technology was assessed in terms of its functional characteristics, scalability, integration capacity, and governance conditions.
While China has made significant strides in deploying these technologies to address zoonotic risks, food safety, AMR, and environmental health challenges, multiple barriers remain. These include fragmented institutional arrangements, limited cross-sectoral data sharing, uneven regional infrastructure, and the lack of unified governance frameworks tailored to One Health.
To enable effective implementation and scaling, this review identifies several priorities: enhancing interdepartmental coordination, investing in digital infrastructure, particularly in under-resourced regions, and strengthening technical capacity through training. The adoption of FAIRER principles and the development of interoperable national data platforms would support secure, ethical, and actionable data use. In addition, stronger collaboration among government, academic, and industry stakeholders is essential for optimizing technological solutions and promoting their practical uptake. Public awareness and trust in digital health tools must also be strengthened to ensure sustainable adoption.
Ultimately, advancing One Health technologies in China requires an integrated policy framework that aligns innovation with governance reform. This entails multi-sectoral cooperation, targeted investment, and institutional mechanisms that support transparent, secure, and equitable use of health-related data across human, animal, and environmental sectors.
