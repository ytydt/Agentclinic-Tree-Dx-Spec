Efficacy and safety of CT-guided 125I particle implantation for the treatment of elderly lung cancer: a system evaluation and meta-analysis
Background
To systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of 125I particle implantation in the treatment of elderly lung cancer.
Methods
Both randomized and non-randomized controlled studies on CT-guided 125I particle implantation therapy for the treatment of elderly lung cancer patients were retrieved from databases including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, VIP, and SinoMed from inception to October 31, 2022. Two researchers screened the retrieved studies, extracted data, and evaluated the study quality independently. Then, meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan5.3 and Stata15 software.
Results
A total of 15 studies were included, involving 886 subjects. The meta-analysis results showed that compared with the control group, the 125I particle implantation group had a higher objective response rate [RR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.50–1.96, P < 0.001] and disease control rate [RR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.19–1.43, P < 0.0001], longer survival [HR = 0.48, 95% CI 0.37–0.63, P = 0.0005], but an 8.96-fold increase in postoperative complications [RR = 8.96, 95% CI 4.75–16.92, P < 0.0001]. There was no significant difference in the incidence of adverse reactions between the two groups [RR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.91–1.14, P = 0.73].
Conclusions
The CT-guided 125I particle implantation therapy is able to significantly improve the short-term efficacy and survival of elderly patients with lung cancer. Although the risk of complications was significantly increased, none of the included studies reported serious complications. Our meta-analysis exhibited clinical heterogeneity due to non-uniform interventions, varied treatment protocols, inconsistent staging criteria, and study limitations such as small samples, few studies and short follow-up, potentially introducing bias. More high-quality research is needed to further validate the conclusions of this study.

Background
Lung cancer, the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, has become one of the most common malignant tumors in the global elderly population. Under the joint action of smoking, aging, environmental pollution and other factors, the incidence rate and mortality of lung cancer are exhibiting a rising trend. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for about 80–85% of all lung cancers. Due to its asymptomatic early course, NSCLC is typically detected at advanced stages, conferring a dismal prognosis (5-year survival < 20%). This situation seriously affects the quality of life of the elderly.
Following the aging process, the physiological reserves of elderly patients with lung cancer gradually decrease, accompanied by drug metabolism changes and various chronic diseases. Therefore, the treatment of elderly lung cancer is facing some special challenges. Standard radiotherapy and chemotherapy can easily cause adverse reactions. Common adverse reactions of standard radiotherapy include radiation pneumonitis, esophagitis, skin burns, and bone marrow suppression; chemotherapy mainly causes nausea and vomiting, hair loss, neurotoxicity, and liver and kidney function impairment. Customized treatment may help reduce toxic side effects, but the efficacy is limited. Targeted therapy and immunotherapy, as emerging treatment strategies, have insufficient application experience in elderly patients. Overall, it is urgent to explore and develop safer and more effective individualized treatment plans to improve the efficacy of elderly patients with lung cancer.
A number of studies have shown that the 125I particle implantation therapy can effectively control advanced NSCLC and prolong the survival of patients. In 247 advanced patients with lung cancer receiving 125I seed implantation, 3-year local control and survival rates were 49.3% and 17.9% respectively. The intervention demonstrated superior safety to controls, particularly in radiation pneumonitis incidence (11.54% vs. 34.62%; P < 0.01). Systematic evaluation in geriatric populations is clinically imperative due to age-related physiological constraints. Although current evidence supports the clinical value of I-125 seed implantation for elderly patients with NSCLC, ‌three critical issues should be addressed prior to widespread adoption‌, including prospective validation of long-term survival benefits, stratified efficacy analyses across different staging subgroups and exploration of dosage standardization for combination therapies.
Unfortunately, existing studies were mostly based on small samples and single-center research, presenting inconsistent quality. Besides, the included patients were subjected to a large age range, lacking particular attention to the elderly population. No globally consensus-based definition of ‘elderly’ exists in oncology. The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies older persons as those aged ≥ 60 years, contrasting with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Geriatric Oncology Guidelines which specify an age threshold of ≥ 65 years. This study aimed to conduct a meta-analysis on patients with NSCLC aged ≥ 60 years to clarify the efficacy and safety of the 125I particle implantation therapy in the elderly population. We hoped to provide evidence-based support for optimizing the individualized treatment of elderly lung cancer.

Data and methods

Inclusion criteria

Research type
Randomized and non-randomized controlled studies focusing on human subjects;
Language: Chinese or English;
Published studies.

Research subjects
Patients diagnosed with lung cancer through pathology or cytology, regardless of clinical stage, aged ≥ 60 years, and with an ECOG score ≤ 2.

Intervention measures
Intervention group: CT-guided 125I particle implantation through percutaneous puncture. Studies with a treatment plan that meets one of the following criteria were included (no restriction on the sequence of treatment methods):
125I particle implantation treatment only;
Combination of 125I particle implantation with other treatment options including various chemotherapy regimens, vascular intervention, and immunotherapy.
Control group: Conventional treatments such as simple radiotherapy, chemotherapy, vascular intervention, or supportive therapy.

Outcome measures
Objective response rate (ORR), i.e., effective rate;
Disease control rate (DCR);
Median survival time. The efficacy evaluation criteria refer to RECIST version 1.1.
Efficacy indicators:
Postoperative complications, such as pneumothorax, hemoptysis, particle loss or displacement, etc.;
Treatment-related adverse reactions, such as bone marrow suppression, radiation pneumonitis, etc. The toxicity evaluation criteria refer to CTCAE version 4.0.
Safety indicators:
At least one of the outcome measures must meet the requirements of this study.

Exclusion criteria
Inability to extract the required data or data incompleteness;
Repeatedly published studies;
Reviews, conference abstracts, case reports, and other non-original research articles.
Studies meeting any of the following criteria were ruled out:

Literature search
Both randomized and non-randomized controlled studies on CT-guided 125I particle implantation therapy for the treatment of elderly patients with lung cancer were retrieved from databases including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, VIP, and SinoMed from inception to October 31, 2022.

Chinese search terms
Lung cancer, lung tumor, iodine-125, seed implantation, brachytherapy, seeds; elderly, 60 years old and above.

English search terms
Lung neoplasm, lung carcinoma, carcinoma, iodine radioisotopes, 125-iodine, iodine seeds, 125I, particle implantation, older, the older adults, older people.

Literature screening, data extraction, and quality evaluation
Two researchers independently conducted literature screening, and data extraction and statistical analysis based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. If there was any disagreement, a third researcher would be consulted for resolution. The quality of randomized controlled studies was evaluated using the ROB-1 tool, which was based on the Cochrane Systematic Review Manual, while that of non-randomized controlled studies was evaluated using the ROBINS-I scale. Application of the GRADE framework yielded low-certainty evidence for critical outcomes, culminating in a weak recommendation.

Statistical analysis
According to the general requirements of meta-analysis, RevMan 5.3 and Stata 15 software were employed to conduct meta-analysis on the extracted data. The Q-test was used to assess the heterogeneity among included studies. The funnel plot, Begg’s and Egger’s tests were used to evaluate publication bias. If there was any publication bias, the robustness of the results would be further tested using the pruning method. Finally, sensitivity analysis was conducted by iteratively excluding each individual study.

Objective response rate
All the 15 studies were included for the subgroup analysis of ORR based on different intervention measures. The results showed that compared with chemotherapy alone or bronchial embolization combined with perfusion chemotherapy, the 125I particle implantation therapy had a better efficacy on elderly patients with NSCLC (66.22% vs. 37.59%) [RR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.50, 1.96, P < 0.00001], as shown in Fig. 6. There were 12 studies that compared the efficacy between intravenous chemotherapy and 125I particle implantation combined with intravenous chemotherapy or 125I particle implantation alone. The results showed that both 125I particle implantation combined with intravenous chemotherapy and 125I particle implantation alone had a better efficacy (61.77% vs. 35.06%) [RR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.46, 2.01, P < 0.0001], as shown in Fig. 7.

Disease control rate
A total of 12 studies were included for the subgroup analysis of disease control rate based on different treatment measures. The results showed that 125I particle implantation could significantly improve the disease control rate (85.15% vs. 64.07%) [RR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.19–1.43, P < 0.0001] (see Fig. 8). In addition, subgroup analysis was conducted based on different chemotherapy regimens, the efficacy of the 125I particle implantation group was significantly higher than the control group [RR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.20, 1.45, P < 0.0001], as shown in Fig. 9.

Postoperative complications
Major complications of 125I seed implantation include procedure-related pneu-mothorax/hemoptysis (acute phase), radiation pneumonitis (subacute phase), and rare but critical seed migration or needle tract metastasis (long-term monitoring required). A total of 10 studies were included for the analysis of postoperative complications. The results showed that the incidence of postoperative complications in the 125I particle implantation group was significantly higher than that in the control group (16.07% vs. 0.37%) [RR = 8.96, 95% CI 4.75, 16.92, P < 0.0001]. Then, subgroup analysis was conducted based on different complications. It was found that the incidence of pneumothorax [RR = 9.77, 95% CI 4.09, 23.37, P < 0.0001] or bleeding [RR = 9.57, 95% CI 2.93, 31.26, P = 0.0002] was significantly higher than that of the control group, as shown in Fig. 10. Notably, none of the included studies reported any risk of serious complications such as massive hemoptysis, severe pneumothorax or radiation pneumonitis, or death, so meta-analysis could not be conducted due to the lack of data.
125I treatment vs. control group: Postoperative complications

Adverse reactions
The assessment of adverse reactions focuses on whether combining 125I seed implantation with existing therapies exacerbates regimen-specific toxicities, including: myelosuppression (leukopenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia), gastrointestinal events (nausea/vomiting), and systemic effects (alopecia, skin rash, hepatorenal impairment). A total of 11 studies were included, and the main adverse reactions reported included bone marrow suppression, gastrointestinal reactions, etc. The results showed that there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of adverse reactions between the 125I particle implantation group and control group [RR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.91, 1.14, P = 0.73], as shown in Fig. 11.

Bias risk assessment
The funnel plots for the ORR, disease control rate, and median survival time are shown in Fig. 13. Figure 14 demonstrates the impact of each individual study on the overall combined effect. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of findings (all RR > 1; 95% CIs > 1). The Begg’s and Egger’s tests are illustrated in Fig. 15, while the pruning method is illustrated in Fig. 16. Concordant signals of publication bias were detected for ORR via Begg’s test (P = 0.020) and Egger’s test (P = 0.003), whereas DCR showed no bias (Egger’s P = 0.055). To assess the robustness of ORR estimates, Trim and fill analysis (Stata 15) was performed to evaluate robustness, calculating pooled RRs. Given non-significant heterogeneity (Q = 14.374, P = 0.422), a fixed-effect model yielded RR = 0.488 (95% CI: 0.356–0.619). Trim and fill analysis imputed 7 missing studies, yet the pooled ORR estimate remained non-significant (fixed-effect RR: 0.365, 95% CI: 0.252–0.479; P > 0.05) with consistent directionality. This indicates minimal bias impact, supporting result robustness.

Discussion
In this study, the efficacy and safety of CT-guided 125I particle implantation in the treatment of elderly patients with NSCLC were evaluated through meta-analysis. The results showed that compared with chemotherapy alone or bronchial intervention therapy alone, the 125I particle implantation therapy was able to significantly improve the short-term efficacy and survival of patients, without increasing the risk of adverse reactions such as bone marrow suppression, gastrointestinal reactions (e.g., nausea, vomiting), hair loss, rash, and liver and kidney damage. Although complications like pneumothorax, bleeding, and seed displacement might occur after implantation, most of them were mild and could be well controlled after symptomatic treatment. This suggests that 125I particle implantation is potentially a safe and effective treatment for elderly patients with NSCLC.
It has been reported that the 125I particles implanted into tumors have good killing and inhibitory effects on advanced non-small cell carcinoma solid tumors. The results of our meta-analysis showed that the ORR (66.22% vs. 37.59%) and disease control rate (85.15% vs. 64.07%) were both significantly higher in the 125I particle implantation group than in the control group. This is consistent with previous research findings. In addition, we also found that compared with chemotherapy alone, all 125I particle implantation involved treatments could significantly improve the efficacy of elderly patients with NSCLC. In subgroup analyses stratified by chemotherapy regimens, no statistically significant intergroup differences were observed (all p > 0.05, see Figs. 7 and 9). This indicates that the efficacy remains consistent regardless of the specific chemotherapy protocol when integrated with 125I brachytherapy.
In terms of the median survival time, 5 studies were included, and the results suggest that the 125I particle implantation therapy is able to effectively prolong the survival of elderly patients with NSCLC. This is consistent with the research outcomes of Yue TH et al.. Unfortunately, due to the limited follow-up time and number of included studies, the long-term survival rate of patients with NSCLC treated with 125I particle implantation should be further confirmed, and longer follow-up studies are needed to verify the long-term therapeutic effects of this therapy. In addition, 125I particle implantation can not only improve the quality of life of locally advanced patients with NSCLC, but also alleviate tumor related symptoms such as cough, chest pain, chest tightness, and hoarseness, as well as pain caused by malignant tumor bone metastasis. For elderly patients, this therapy exhibits certain advantages in improving their quality of life and prolonging survival. However, the number of studies included was relatively small, and the evaluation tools used varied in different studies, making it necessary to further validate the reliability of the results. In future research, we plan to conduct prospective controlled studies by adopting a unified quality of life assessment scale, in order to provide stronger evidence for the 125I particle implantation therapy in improving the quality of life of elderly patients with NSCLC.
In terms of safety, the studies included showed that complications like pneumothorax, bleeding, radiation pneumonitis, and seed displacement might occur after the 125I particle implantation surgery, but most of them were mild and could be well controlled after symptomatic treatment. None of the included studies reported any serious complications such as respiratory distress, shock, or death.
The main consideration with respect to adverse reactions was the impact of 125I particle implantation on the incidence of adverse reactions when it was adopted in combination with other treatments. In the included studies, 125I particle implantation was usually combined with various intravenous chemotherapy or bronchial artery infusion chemotherapy drugs, so the adverse reactions reported were mainly related to chemotherapy, including gastrointestinal reactions, bone marrow suppression, liver and kidney toxicity, etc. All these adverse events were significantly improved after symptomatic treatment, and no serious adverse events were observed. Our meta-analysis showed that after 125I particle implantation, the incidence of adverse reactions like leukopenia and thrombocytopenia was similar to that of the control group [RR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.91,– 1.14, P = 0.73]. Moreover, chemotherapy is often associated with toxic side effects, including bone marrow suppression and gastrointestinal reactions, suggesting that radioactive particle implantation may be a less toxic local treatment option. Considering the limited number of studies included, publication bias, and limitations of each study itself, the studies included in the meta-analysis of side effects were not sufficient to influence the results, therefore, multi-center clinical studies with larger sample sizes and diverse populations are needed in the future to provide stronger evidence.
When interpreting our research results, there were two points worth noting. Firstly, in the evaluation of therapeutic efficacy, we employed the Egger’s method and Begg’s method to quantify the ORR, and the pruning method for testing. The results indicated that there was no publication bias or only minor publication bias (P = 0.422 vs. P = 0.074, both > 0.05), and the combined results were robust. Overall, the publication bias had an insignificant impact on the stability of the ORR of 125I particle implantation for elderly patients with NSCLC. Secondly, the subjects included were aged 60–85 years old. The original texts only reported results based on intervention measures, lacking further grouping results such as tumor staging and age groups. Therefore, there might be related biases.
Some limitations to this study should be highlighted. Firstly, conflict between treatment individualization and randomization implementation: Considering the specificity of treatment, the specific treatment plan often needs to be customized based on the patient’s individual condition. Thus, there are still significant challenges in implementing blinding or concealing random allocation schemes. This operational bias may have led to imbalanced baseline characteristics between groups, potentially resulting in overestimation or underestimation of the true efficacy of iodine-125 seeds. Secondly, standardization deficiencies in the interventions across included studies: the intervention measures included in our meta-analysis lacked uniformity, and different treatment plans and inconsistent pathological staging and classification standards were adopted in different studies. Coupled with problems like small sample size, limited number of studies included, and short follow-up, there was a certain degree of clinical heterogeneity, which potentially compromising the reliability of pooled effect estimates. Third, geographical and publication Biases: All studies originated from Chinese institutions, with English publications predominantly authored by Chinese scholars. This may introduce:
Treatment preference bias (e.g., overrepresentation of platinum-based chemotherapy).
Begg’s test indicated funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.020), suggesting inadequate publication of negative results. Fourth, limitations in study design and control groups: most of the included studies were retrospective studies. Due to limited research on the 125I particle implantation surgery in elderly patients with lung cancer, there was a lack of data on the comparison with radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, such selection bias limited generalizability to other treatment modalities. The limitations may result in effect size inflation with potential overestimation of OR 95% CI lower limit by 0.15–0.3 units, reduced external validity restricting conclusions exclusively to elderly Chinese lung adenocarcinoma populations, and clinical decision-making risks due to an inability to determine optimal sequencing of iodine-125 seeds in combination therapies.

Conclusions
The 125I particle implantation therapy is able to significantly improve the short-term efficacy and ORR of elderly patients with NSCLC, and has been proven safe and effective, making it an alternative treatment option. In future research, more large-scale, high-quality, multi-center randomized controlled trials are needed to validate our results.
