Prospective Evaluation of Screening Performance of First-Trimester Prediction Models for Preterm Preeclampsia
ABSTRACT
Preterm preeclampsia (PPE) is a serious pregnancy complication with significant risks for maternal and fetal health. This review aims to evaluate the prospective performance of first-trimester prediction models for PPE, highlighting their effectiveness, limitations, and clinical applicability. A comprehensive narrative review of studies published from 2020 to 2024 was conducted. Studies focusing on first-trimester prediction models for PPE were included, with an emphasis on their sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and performance across different populations. The review revealed that multifactorial models combining biomarkers (e.g., pregnancy-associated plasma protein-A (PAPP-A), placental growth factor (PlGF), soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1)), clinical risk factors, and ultrasound markers (e.g., uterine artery Doppler, mean arterial pressure (MAP)) show promising results with high sensitivity and specificity in predicting PPE. It is concluded that first-trimester prediction models for PPE are effective tools for early risk assessment but require further refinement and validation in diverse populations. Continued research and technological advancements, including machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI), are necessary to enhance the models’ accuracy and generalizability for widespread clinical use.

INTRODUCTION
Preterm preeclampsia (PPE) is a major pregnancy complication that presents both immediate and long-term risks to maternal and fetal health. PPE develops with hypertension and features of organ dysfunction: renal and liver in pregnancy-related hypertension and that occurs before 34 weeks of gestation with high rates of preterm labor, neonatal morbidity, and maternal mortality. Due to the life-threatening effect of PPE in particular, and the absence of early intervention approaches, identification of high-risk women forms an important component in enhancing maternal outcomes. Preeclampsia occurs in the second half of pregnancy, while PPE is more challenging to hypothesize and control. Antepartum monitoring is critical in the first trimester aiming at early identification of mothers at risk hence formulation of curative intervention strategies including aspirin. Although preeclampsia has become more common, screening for PPE remains a hardship because the condition is polygenic and polyetiologic. The pathophysiology of preeclampsia is complex and multifactorial. It is associated with maternal and clinical and biochemical characteristics, such as the presence of hypertension, diabetes, advanced maternal age and high body mass index (BMI), inheritance factors, and markers of the placenta. Any at-risk pregnancies if properly evaluated through screening methods may likely be controlled early before they develop PPE. However, as this study shows current screening practices are inadequate in providing a definitive identification of those individuals who are likely to develop the condition during the first trimester. It has become possible to identify several first-trimester predictants of PPE in recent years. These models usually incorporate maternal characteristics with several biochemical markers (such as placenta growth factor, angiogenic factor, and uterine artery Doppler flow) assessed in the first trimester.
This review explains the screening performance of first-trimester prediction models for PPE.

METHODOLOGY
This review article aims to evaluate and synthesize the available evidence on the screening performance of first-trimester prediction models for PPE from 2020 to 2024. Data extraction was conducted using a predesigned form that captured key information from each selected study. This included details such as the study’s author(s), publication year, country of origin, sample size, and study design. For each study, information was also extracted regarding the prediction models evaluated, including the variables or biomarkers used, whether clinical factors were incorporated, and the statistical methods employed. The primary outcome measures, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and area under the curve (AUC), were recorded to assess the performance of the models.

Review of literature
The early prediction of PPE has been a significant focus of research in maternal-fetal medicine due to its association with poor maternal and neonatal outcomes. Preeclampsia, which can lead to severe complications like eclampsia, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets (HELLP) syndrome, and fetal growth restriction, is traditionally diagnosed after the 20th week of pregnancy. However, PPE, defined as the development of preeclampsia before 37 weeks of gestation, presents a greater challenge due to its earlier onset and higher associated risks. Early prediction, ideally in the first trimester, has the potential to reduce these risks through timely interventions.

Biomarkers in preterm preeclampsia
A significant body of research has focused on the role of biomarkers in early prediction. Some of the key biomarkers identified include:

Placental growth factor (PlGF)
This angiogenic factor is essential for placental development. Low levels of PlGF in early pregnancy have been associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia, particularly in women who develop the condition before 34 weeks of gestation. Biomarkers play a crucial role in the early detection, prediction, and management of PPE, a serious pregnancy complication characterized by high blood pressure and proteinuria before 37 weeks of gestation.

Soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1)
An anti-angiogenic factor that competes with PlGF for binding to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptors. Elevated levels of sFlt-1 are linked with endothelial dysfunction and are a hallmark of preeclampsia. The sFlt-1/PlGF ratio is commonly used in predicting preeclampsia risk, with a higher ratio indicating a greater likelihood of developing the disease. sFlt-1 is a protein that plays a significant role in the pathophysiology of preeclampsia, particularly in its preterm form.

Pregnancy-associated plasma protein-A (PAPP-A)
This protein is involved in regulating fetal growth. Reduced levels of PAPP-A during the first trimester have been associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia. PAPP-A is a glycoprotein that plays a key role in pregnancy, particularly in the early stages. The placenta primarily produces it and circulates in the maternal blood throughout pregnancy.

First-trimester screening models
Several comprehensive prediction models have been developed and validated in first-trimester screening. The most popular model reported is the Fetal Medicine Foundation (FMF) screening model that uses maternal factors, biochemical markers (PlGF, sFlt-1, PAPP-A), and fetal structural imaging. The FMF model proved to have a high detection rate for PPE with relatively low false positivity, but it validated only in a selected population and is not suitable for use in all. Other models based on screening and identifying high-risk pregnancies include the ASPIRIN trial model, which analyzes the use of low-dose aspirin in combating preeclampsia. This model applies simplified clinical and biomarker characteristics to define patients who might benefit from aspirin. Although aspirin is useful in preventing preeclampsia among high-risk women, its usage is fraught with some complications especially when the screening model misses some of the most vulnerable women. First-trimester screening models are issued to quantify the risk of pregnancy complications in the 11–13 weeks gestation.

Multifactorial models
Multifactorial models that combine clinical risk factors (age, medical history, etc.), biomarkers, and ultrasound measurements have been increasingly explored for improving the prediction of PPE. These models are intended to yield a sounder risk appraisal. Carvalho et al. (2021) assessed the performance of a model with five parameters that include maternal age, obesity status, history of preeclampsia, level of PAPP-A, and uterine artery examination. For the multifactorial model, the values for sensitivity, specificity, and AUC were 87%, 85%, 0.91, respectively, proving the effectiveness of the developed screening model with respect to the conventional monofactorial model. The authors believed that this model might be used to identify women at high risk during the early pregnancy and would enable the initiation of preventive measures such as aspirin administration. Jain et al. (2023) employed machine learning as a multifactorial model including demographic data, clinical history, biomarkers, and uterine artery Doppler. The experimental model applied to a selected population of patients yielded high sensitivity of 88%; specificity of 90%; and the AUC of 0.92. Campbell and colleagues highlighted how this approach of using machine learning algorithms in risk-stratification of patients could help to reduce clinician error. A study done in Asian population by Nguyen et al. (2024) compared the with a multifactorial model with maternal age, previous preeclampsia, PAPP-A, and uterine artery pulsatility index (UTAPI). They have provided the sensitivity percentage of 83 percent; specificity of 82 percent; and the estimated AUC at 0.87. However, the study also showed that the model’s accuracy was not the same across all ethnicities; the model needs to be tested more in other ethnic groups. Hughes et al. (2023) chose a biomarker model, which includes PAPP-A, PlGF, sFlt-1, and clinical risk factors such as age, obesity, and hypertension for first-trimester PPE screening. Incorporating the model, the highest accuracy reached a sensitivity of 85%, specificity of 86%, and AUC of 0.90. The authors pointed out that although the model worked, issues of cost with testing as well as complicated ultrasound techniques hindered additional use, especially in the developing world.

Challenges and limitations
Despite the progress in developing first-trimester prediction models, some several challenges and limitations need to be addressed. One of the key limitations is the generalizability of these models across different populations. Many early studies have been conducted in high-resource settings, and their applicability to low- and middle-income countries with differing healthcare access and maternal risk profiles remains unclear. Furthermore, while models that combine clinical, biochemical, and ultrasound data have shown improved predictive accuracy, the implementation of these models in routine clinical practice remains complicated due to the cost and accessibility of some of the required tests, particularly in resource-constrained environments.

CONCLUSION
It is concluded that first-trimester prediction models for PPE show significant promise in early identification and risk stratification, potentially improving maternal and fetal outcomes. However, their performance varies across populations, and challenges remain in terms of accuracy, generalizability, and clinical implementation.
