A universal strategy for real-time discrimination and evaluation of food freshness using natural colorants as indicators: from detection to antioxidant and antibacterial properties
In recent years, natural pigments have made significant contributions to food preservation, antioxidant, and antibacterial fields due to a wide range of sources, high safety, and no obvious side effects, and are increasingly used as sensors and indicators for judging food freshness because of their unique chemical structures and physicochemical properties. This paper takes the latest research progress on natural pigments as indicators in the food industry as its starting point, providing a comprehensive review of the structure, types, freshness indicators, and functional characteristics of natural pigments as antioxidants and antimicrobial agents. The paper also explores the synergistic and antagonistic effects between different natural pigments, as well as the combined use of natural pigments with nanomaterials to enhance the stability of natural pigments in food industry applications. In addition, in order to actively respond to sustainable development, this review also describes the extraction of natural pigments from food waste and their application in the food industry. Finally, we summarize the advantages and challenges of natural pigments and look forward to the potential and future research prospects of pigments in interdisciplinary fields such as food and biomedicine, including the development of novel drug delivery systems.

Introduction

Food color is an important sensory indicator that influences consumers’ perceptions of freshness, quality, and flavor. Existing research shows that consumers base 62–90% of their product evaluations on color. In recent years, natural pigments derived from plants, animals, and microorganisms have gained widespread application in the food industry due to their advantages of abundant sources, high biocompatibility, and low toxicity and side effects. It is worth noting that natural pigments such as anthocyanins, curcumins, and carotenoids are highly sensitive to pH, temperature, humidity, and specific gases (such as volatile amines and hydrogen sulfide), causing visible and reversible color changes. This makes them ideal materials for developing smart packaging. At the same time, natural pigments also have antioxidant and antibacterial properties, which can be used in food to delay lipid oxidation, inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria, and thereby reduce the use of preservatives Natural pigments also have a variety of active functions. For example, anthocyanins derived from plants have been widely studied for their significant medicinal value, including anti-cancer and cardiovascular disease prevention effects. Faced with the waste of approximately 1.3 billion tons of edible by-products each year, researchers have extracted natural pigments from them, providing new ideas for sustainable development. Although natural pigments have demonstrated significant potential in food preservation, smart packaging, and biomedical applications, their poor stability and low extraction efficiency remain major barriers to industrialization. Additionally, as global food supply chains become increasingly complex, ensuring comprehensive quality control throughout the entire production, distribution, and consumption process has emerged as a critical challenge for food science and packaging technology. Therefore, this paper systematically reviews the classification, structural characteristics, color-changing mechanisms, and latest advancements of natural pigments in smart packaging, antioxidant properties, antimicrobial effects, and byproduct recovery. At the same time, interdisciplinary collaboration strategies and future research directions were explored, with the aim of not only providing a theoretical foundation and technical support for the construction of a safe, green, and sustainable food system, but also bringing significant environmental and health benefits to society (Scheme 1).

Natural pigments are diverse and come from a wide range of sources, and a scientific and rational classification of them can help to better understand their properties, application scope, and development potential. In this paper, the common classifications of natural pigments will be elaborated in detail, to provide references for research and application in related fields. Natural pigments are classified according to their chemical structure (Scheme 2) into flavonoid derivatives, such as anthocyanins; isoprenoid derivatives, such as carotenoids; pyrrole derivatives, such as chlorophyll; Azetidin derivatives, such as belatains and other pigments, such as carminic acid. Natural pigments are classified according to their source into plant sources (e.g., chlorophyll in green leaves, carotene in carrots, lycopene in tomatoes, etc.), animal sources (e.g., hemoglobin in muscle, astaxanthin in shrimp shells, etc.), and microbial sources (e.g., erythritol on the surface of soybean curd, etc.). It also includes mineral sources, such as chromium green. Moreover, in order to make the natural coloring more widely used in food processing, its solubility is also worthy of attention. According to their solubility, they can be classified into water-soluble pigments, such as anthocyanins (anthocyanidins); and fat-soluble pigments, such as carotenoid compounds (Table 1). During food processing, water-soluble natural pigments have broad applications in aqueous systems, like drinks and dairy items, due to their good solubility and dispersibility. In the manufacturing of lipid-based products, for instance, vegetable oils, oil-soluble natural pigments are favored for their compatibility with fat systems.

Main natural pigments

Carotenoids
Carotenoids belong to a category of liposoluble and oil-soluble pigments, primarily consisting of eight isoprene units forming a 40-carbon atom framework. Generally speaking, their basic structure consists of a polyene chain containing nine conjugated double bonds, with specific end groups attached to both ends of the polyene chain. Based on their chemical structure, carotenoids are categorized into carotenoids and luteinoids, with those containing carbon and hydrogen atoms being carotenoids and those containing oxygen atoms in addition being luteinoids.
Due to the long-conjugated system of carotenoids, their colors range from yellow to orange and finally to red. Carotenoids with more conjugated Carbon-carbon double bonds absorb longer wavelengths of light. For instance, α-carotene, featuring 10 conjugated double bonds, has absorption wavelengths of 422, 445, and 473 nanometers and appears orange. In contrast, lycopene, which possesses 11 conjugated double bonds, one more than α-carotene, has maximum absorption wavelengths of 444, 470, and 502 nanometers and appears red.

Anthocyanins
Anthocyanins are part of flavonoid polyphenolic compounds, the core skeleton mainly made up of a C6-C3-C6 chain, containing eight conjugated bonds and an oxygenated three-carbon ring connecting two benzene rings. This structure endows them with excellent coloring properties, and they are widely found in grape seeds, blueberries, purple cabbage, hawthorn, and tea leaves.

Anthocyanins often have hydroxyl substitutions at carbon positions 3, 5, and 7. and tend to exist as glycosides owing to the existence of multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups, which often bind to monosaccharides, such as glucose, galactose or xylose, enhancing their stability and solubility. In addition to this, anthocyanins can also bind to aromatic acids in a chemical process known as acylation, which primarily takes place at the third position of the C ring, and this diversity of structural modifications has led to more than 500 known anthocyanin derivatives with a diverse range of physicochemical properties. Among the more than 20 different anthocyanin types that have been identified, pelargonidin, cyanidin, delphinidin, peonidin, petunidin, and malvidin are the most common, their structures are shown in Fig. 1a.
Anthocyanins show different colors depending on the cytoplasm’s pH level. The color of anthocyanins changes from red to orange at pH 1–3, where they are more stable; at a pH value of 7, it shows purple; in a pH range of 8 to 10, anthocyanin shows blue. at pH>11, it shows cyan. Apart from this, the structural characteristics and co-pigmentation effects of anthocyanins also play a role in their color variation. Anthocyanins are widely used in food freshness detection and antioxidant effects (Fig. 1b, c).

Curcumin
Curcumin is a naturally occurring hydrophobic polyphenol derived from turmeric, which appears yellow in color and is a symmetric molecule consisting of two o-methoxyphenol moieties linked by a heptane chain and an enol form of β-diketone.
Curcumin undergoes reversible structural transformations at different pH conditions, causing curcumin to display different colors at different pH conditions. For example, the color of curcumin is yellow when pH <8, light yellow when pH = 8, and reddish brown when pH is 9–11. Curcumin has a variety of biological activities, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, etc. However, it is poorly stable in vivo and easily metabolized, and when higher doses of curcumin are ingested, the concentration it reaches in plasma is relatively low. Curcumin is used as a flavoring and coloring agent in the traditional food industry due to its distinctive odor and yellow color, and also plays a large role as a pH indicator and biosensor in smart packaging.

Betalains
Batalains is a water-soluble nitrogenous pigments derived from tyrosine, consisting of β-alanine [4-(2-oxoethylidene)-1,2,3,4-tetrahydropyridine-2,6-dicarboxylic acid. Based on their condensation reactions with betulinic acid, they are divided into two main groups: those that proceed with cyclic 3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (i.e., cyclic DOPA) or the products of its glycosylated form are betulinic acid. The condensation with amino-containing compounds (e.g., amino acids, amines, and their derivatives) is betaflavin.
The coloring ability of betalain derives from the fact that the betamido acid in its molecular structure contains three conjugated double bonds, which form a conjugated system that resonates electrons and absorbs visible light at a wavelength of about 406 nm, thus giving the beet a specific color. Its stability is affected not only by internal influences, such as chemical structure and enzymes, but also by external influences, such as oxygen, light, and pH. Betailain is purplish-red between pH 3–7, changes to orange at pH 8–9, and changes to yellow when the pH continues to rise to 10–12. In addition, temperature is the main factor affecting betalain degradation, and it has been confirmed in numerous studies that the absolute value of the primary degradation rate increases positively with increasing temperature, indicating a faster degradation at higher temperatures. Studies have shown that betalain possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antitumor biological activities.

Chlorophyll
Chlorophyll is a fat- and oil-soluble pigment made up of four pyrrole subunits linked by methane bridges to form a porphyrin ring. The side chain structure of chlorophyll is diverse, forming several types, such as chlorophylls a, b, and c, chlorophyllogen, and bacterial chlorophylls. Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b are widely studied and exist in nature, and are distinguished by their C7 substituents, with a being methyl and b being a formyl group.
The porphyrin ring contains multiple conjugated double bonds, which leads to the delocalization effect of electrons and endows it with light absorption properties. Different structures of chlorophyll also present different colors, for instance, chlorophyll a appears bluish-green, and chlorophyll b appears yellowish-green. The stability of chlorophyll is influenced by enzyme activity, light, temperature, and pH value. For example, under acidic conditions, chlorophyll will lose its central metal cation and thus present an olive-green color.

Other pigments
Aspergillus erythrophilus pigments are secondary metabolites of Monascus Purpures and M. Anka yeast. Aspergillus erythrophilus pigments consist of at least six pigment types, of which the red pigments include rubropunctamin and monascurubramin, the yellow pigments include monascin and ankaflavin, and orange pigments include rubropunctatin and monascorubrin. It belongs to the class of polyketides, and its chemical structure contains functional groups such as hydroxyl, ether, and carbon groups, which endow it with a variety of biological activities such as antioxidant. When pH is lower than 4, erythrochrome is insoluble in water, while it is soluble in water at pH 5–10. Aflatoxins are a class of secondary metabolites produced by certain molds, such as Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus, which are mycotoxins. Based on their fluorescence properties under ultraviolet light, they are categorized into four types: B1, B2, G1, and G2 aflatoxins. Among these, research indicates that aflatoxin B1 exhibits substantial liver toxicity, carcinogenicity, and mutagenicity in humans and animals.

Natural pigment stabilization technology
Natural pigments are easily affected by factors such as pH, temperature, humidity, and light exposure. Additionally, different pigments possess distinct properties, such as lipophilicity or hydrophilicity, and their characteristics are suited for various applications. The primary reason for the coloration of natural pigments is often attributed to the presence of conjugated double bonds or Schiff base structures. As pH increases, the concentration of hydroxide ions in the solution rises, causing the conjugated system to dissociate, resulting in lighter or even absent coloration. For example, Lukitasari et al. (pH-dependent stability of major betalains in the encapsulated beetroot extracts) measured the stability of encapsulated beetroot pigments under different pH conditions. The results showed that under acidic conditions, the pigments were relatively stable with reversible color changes, stable under neutral conditions, and began to degrade irreversibly under alkaline conditions due to the hydrolysis of aldehyde imine bonds in betanin. Another example is that under high-temperature conditions, betalain decomposes into betalamic acid and cycloDOPA 5–0-glycosides. Due to the hydrolysis of N=C, it becomes colorless. To expand the application of natural pigments in the food industry, researchers have explored various methods. The following will introduce two approaches to enhance the stability of natural pigments.

Encapsulation
Encapsulation technology regulates the release of environmentally sensitive bioactive molecules by encapsulating them within solid particles or liquid vesicles. This method enhances the stability of natural pigments against changes in pH, light exposure, and temperature, effectively inhibiting their degradation process. Additionally, it improves amphiphilicity and antioxidant activity. Next, we will introduce examples of encapsulation using carriers such as liposomes, nanoparticles, and starch. Vergara et al. studied the stability of beetroot pigments using modified starch as a microencapsulation agent, and found that beetroot pigment loss was minimal. Compared to maltodextrin, modified corn starch has a higher glass transition temperature, which improves the hygroscopicity of the powder. Liposomes are ultramicroscopic spherical particles formed by phospholipid molecules through hydrophobic interactions, consisting of two layers: an inner hydrophilic core and an outer phospholipid bilayer that isolates it from the external environment, thereby significantly enhancing stability. Zhang et al. combined anthocyanin liposomes, carrageenan, and agar to construct a double-layer film for non-destructive evaluation of shrimp freshness. The results showed that it retained its response to pH but had reduced sensitivity.

Electrospinning
Electrospinning technology uses a high-voltage electric field between a needle and a collector to form nanofibers from a polymer solution. When the electrostatic repulsive force of the polymer droplet exceeds its surface tension and viscoelastic force, a conical jet is formed. As the solvent evaporates and the droplet undergoes continuous unstable stretching oscillations, the resulting polymer ultimately deposits as nanofibers on the collector. Nanofibers produced using this technology exhibit the advantages of a large specific surface area and high porosity. Indicator films prepared using this technology offer superior pigment encapsulation performance, small pore sizes, large specific surface areas, and high porosity, providing more active sites to achieve higher sensitivity and color changes. Additionally, electrospinning technology can enhance the stability of indicator films Zou et al. compared indicator films made using electrospinning technology with those made via casting. The results showed that indicator films produced using electrospinning technology retained pigments for a longer duration. This indicates that electrospinning technology can indeed enhance the stability of indicator films.

Application of natural pigments in foodstuffs
In today’s food industry, the use of natural pigments is gradually becoming a trend. One of the fundamental objectives of the food industry is to produce visually appealing food products. Adding pigments to food can restore colors lost due to changes in light, temperature, humidity, or storage conditions; enhance existing colors; or color colorless foods to attract consumers. Consumer demand for safe, healthy, and natural foods has driven the adoption of natural food pigments in food products. According to the Natural Food Pigments Market Size, Share, and Growth Report, the global natural food pigments market size was USD 1.32 billion in 2022. This market is expected to continue growing in the coming years, reaching USD 2.52 billion by 2030. During the period from 2023 to 2030, the compound annual growth rate is projected to be 8.3%. This is because producing visually appealing food is one of the fundamental objectives of the food industry. Due to their safety, environmental friendliness, and ease of acquisition, natural colorants have become an important choice for food processing companies. Not only do they enhance the color of food, but they also meet people’s expectations for healthy food. This article will explore the wide application of natural colorants in food and the multiple advantages they bring.

As modern food enterprises develop rapidly, food faces numerous challenges during production, processing, transportation and storage, among which freshness detection is particularly important. Traditional detection methods cannot achieve rapid and non-destructive testing, and are time-consuming and labor-intensive, making it impossible to monitor changes in the supply chain in real time. The principle of food freshness indicators is that metabolites produced by food during its shelf life and spoilage (such as volatile amines, sulfides, etc.) react specifically with the indicators, causing visible color changes (Fig. 2a–c, e). By comparing with the reaction color card, non-destructive detection of food freshness can be achieved.
On the one hand, in terms of safety, most previous experiments have used artificial pH indicators like bromothymol blue, methyl red, and phenol red as colorants. These colorants are sensitive to color, but if they come into contact with the indicator film during food storage, the phenomenon of indicator migration is likely to occur, and safety cannot be guaranteed. In addition, when using these edible packaging materials, it is necessary to review the regulatory frameworks of each country in advance. For example, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) primarily bases its regulatory framework for edible packaging materials on the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), and Compliance Policy Guides (CPG). The FDA classifies Food Contact Substances (FCS) as indirect food additives, and all FCS entering the market must undergo compliance evaluation. First, consult the positive list in 21 CFR Parts 174–178. If a substance is not listed or does not meet the requirements, you can refer to the Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) list, the list of substances approved before 1958, the TOR exemption list, or valid Food Contact Notification (FCN) notifications. If a substance not covered by the positive list requires an FCN submission, it is important to note that the FCN is only valid for the manufacturer or supplier that submitted it. In summary, before proceeding to industrialization, it is essential to identify relevant standards to ensure consumer safety.

Compared with traditional indicators, Chen et al. developed a nano-colorimetric sensor (NCS) based on anthocyanin, curcumin, alizarin, and malachite green and successfully applied it to the monitoring of the freshness of aquatic products. In this experiment, six porous organic frameworks were combined with natural pigments. Compared with other single natural pigments, four nano-pigments were screened out for their sensitivity and safety in the reaction with ammonia, and they were utilized for detecting the content of total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) in aquatic products. After nanotechnology modification, the detection sensitivity of the natural pigments was significantly improved, which could reflect the degree of spoilage of aquatic products more accurately and stability (Fig. 3a). Although this method enhances the stability and sensitivity of natural pigment determination, the high cost of nanomaterial preparation restricts its large-scale commercial application. Meanwhile, the biosafety of nanomaterials is a concern, potentially posing risks to Environmental and human health. This method shows promising development trends, but still requires continuous exploration and improvement in certain aspects.
Anthocyanins are common utilized in intelligent packaging for freshness detection of shrimp due to their sensitivity to a wide range of pH values. However, their chemical properties are easily affected by ultraviolet rays, light, and temperature, which often limits the application of natural pigments. Therefore, improving the stability of natural pigments is crucial. On the other hand, from the perspective of encapsulation and stability, electrospinning nanotechnology is particularly suitable for anthocyanins. Xu et al. utilized this technology to combine anthocyanin (ACN), polylactic acid (PLA), and chitosan ammonium chloride (HACC) to prepare a nanofiber membrane for blueberry preservation. Among them, PLA was used as a biopolymer matrix, HACC provided antibacterial properties, and ACN was used as a pH indicator. The experimental results showed that the 6% ACN/PLA/HACC membrane exhibited obvious color changes within the pH range of 3–11, with the color difference value (ΔE) decreasing from 29.22 to 23.63, a difference of 5.59, which could be wiped with the naked eye (generally, when the ΔE difference of the nanofiber membrane exceeds 5, obvious color changes can be observed). In addition, the nanofiber membrane had good antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, and enhanced the 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2’-azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical scavenging activities. However, excessive ACN content would weaken the color change of the membrane, so the addition amount of ACN needs to be controlled in practical applications. The implementation of electrospinning technology relies on specialized equipment such as electrospinning machines, and the purchase cost of these devices is relatively high. This, to some extent, leads to an increase in production costs and may pose a challenge to its large-scale promotion and application. However, with the continuous advancement of technology and the gradual maturation of the market, the cost of equipment is expected to gradually decrease, providing strong support for the wider application of electrospinning technology in actual production. Boonmahitthisud et al. mixed chitosan, chitin nanofibers, and red cabbage anthocyanins, then added citric acid at different concentrations to initiate a cross-linking reaction, resulting in a smart food packaging film(Fig. 2d). After adding citric acid, a new absorption peak appeared at 1720 cm-1 in the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), indicating that citric acid had cross-linked with chitosan, chitin nanofibers, and anthocyanins. When the CA content was 0 phr, the membrane’s water absorption rate was 242.1% ± 19.6%. At 5 phr CA, the water absorption rate was 178.3% ± 5.7%. As the CA content increased to 10 phr, the water absorption rate decreased to 149.7% ± 10.8%. This indicates that the cross-linking enhanced the membrane’s water stability. This phenomenon was also confirmed by Gocho et al. However, the CA 0 and CA 10 membranes exhibited similar color parameters in response to pH. The membrane could also detect NH3 concentrations as low as 0.1 M. Boonmahitthisud et al. also explored the application of this membrane in shrimp. According to the data, the a* value of the CA 0 membrane decreased from −6.21 ± 0.33 to −6.61 ± 0.65 from the start of storage to 6 h later, and further decreased to −8.95 ± 1.04 after another 6 h of storage. The B* value increased from −1.55 ± 0.56 to 1.83 ± 0.33 and 5.91 ± 0.63, with the color gradually shifting toward more yellow and green. The CA 10 membrane also exhibited similar color changes.
Zhou et al. developed a double-layer indicator film (DIF), with the indicator layer composed of κ-carrageenan (CAR), anthocyanin (ATH), and/or curcumin (CUR), and the outer layer composed of konjac glucomannan (KGM) and camellia oil (KC layer), which was stored at 25 °C for four days to detect the free radical activity of meat. Compared with the single layer, DIF had lower water content, better thermal stability, and also had UV resistance and light resistance. The results showed that the freshness of chicken decreased after four days of storage, volatile nitrogen-containing compounds were produced, the pH value increased, and the color of the film changed. The color changes of CAR-ATH-KC and CAR-CUR-ATH-KC were more obvious than that of CAR-CUR-KC, and the ΔE change was significant (P < 0.05). Since meat and meat products are relatively sensitive to light, the transmittance of the films with ATH and CUR added was lower than that of CAR-KC. This change indicates that the combined use of ATH, ATH and CUR has higher sensitivity than the sole use of CUR. This phenomenon suggests that in future research and applications, we can consider the coordinated combination of different pigments to further enhance the detection sensitivity. Moreover, DIF not only performs well in stability and sensitivity, providing new ideas for meat preservation and quality inspection, but also has a relatively low production cost. This gives the technology a significant advantage and potential in commercialization, and it is expected to be widely applied in the fields of meat preservation and quality inspection, injecting new vitality into the development of related industries. Zhou et al.  also confirmed in another experiment that camellia oil has good UV resistance, indicating that the DIF film can effectively block UV rays and prevent meat from deteriorating. Meanwhile, Yang et al. prepared a green indicator film by combining polysaccharides extracted from black fungus with chitosan and composite natural pigments (curcumin and betalain), and the results showed that it also possesses UV-blocking capabilities. The UV-blocking capability of the indicator film may be attributed to the following mechanisms: first, the polysaccharides from black fungus and chitosan form a dense network structure through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, causing light to reflect or scatter at the surface. Second, the composite natural pigments absorb UV light. Finally, this achieves the UV-blocking effect. UV exposure accelerates oxidative reactions in food. By blocking UV exposure, the oxidative reactions in food are not affected, thereby delaying spoilage.
It is important to note that all experiments were conducted in PBS buffer or simulated meat juice, without testing for interference in real-world environments such as high-iron or high-salt ion conditions. Before moving into industrial-scale production, different experimental conditions must be established for validation. Additionally, existing research on sensitivity enhancement is largely confined to laboratory conditions and has not simulated real-world applications under conditions such as temperature and lighting in cold chain logistics. Only by addressing these issues can we achieve the leap from laboratory to table and advance the commercialization of smart packaging.
According to the World Health Organization (WHO), about 600 million people worldwide are infected with pathogenic bacteria and become ill, and about 420,000 of them die as a result. These infections are closely related to the consumption of contaminated food, which suggests that food preservation is a crucial issue and if not handled correctly, food will face serious challenges affecting its safety and quality. The growth and metabolic activities of microorganisms are common causes of food spoilage, especially the presence of foodborne pathogenic bacteria, which are responsible for foodborne diseases. Therefore, the development of effective detection and early warning technologies is of vital importance for the prevention of pathogenic bacteria. Currently, Sun et al. developed a colorimetric sensor using polyvinyl alcohol as the substrate and anthocyanin as the indicator by electrospinning technology with glutaraldehyde and hydrochloric acid to enhance its stability in detecting Staphylococcus aureus (Fig. 3b). The detection limit of Staphylococcus aureus is 2×102 cfu/mL. The total color difference ΔE = 7.6 > 3.3, and the color change can be observed with the naked eye. As the bacterial concentration increases, the color of the membrane changes from light pink to grayish black and finally to grayish yellow. Therefore, the colorimetric sensor can visually and non-destructively detect the concentration of S. aureus (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, approximately 150 million people worldwide suffer from urinary tract infections (UTIs) each year due to bacterial invasion, causing inflammation of the urothelium. Bacterial cultures in urinary tract infections involve clinically important microorganisms such as Escherichia coli, S. aureus, and Streptococcus, with E. coli being the predominant bacterium causing the infection. Failure to detect, identify early and diagnose pathogens in a timely manner can lead to life-threatening situations once the pathogen enters the kidneys and invades the circulation. Razackd et al. conjugated anthocyanins onto silver nanoparticles for the detection of pathogens. The results showed that when the concentration of AgNP-AC was 50 μL, the color change was most significant, with a cumulative ΔE of 244.86 ± 12.97, and the detection limit was 105 CFU. In addition, the sensor was able to detect not only single pathogens, but also differentiate the color change of multiple pathogens in mixed culture. The sensor is not only easy and inexpensive to operate, but also has a rapid response and is expected to be used for the rapid diagnosis of urinary tract infections.
In conclusion, although natural pigments have shown great contribution and potential for development as sensors in food preservation and biomedicine, the ability of natural pigments to cope with complex environments still needs to be improved, as mentioned above by Razackd et al. Urine tests use artificial urine rather than real urine samples, which are much more complex. Whether the performance of natural colors will be affected, we need to further explore the use of natural colors in more complex environments and improve them in the future.

Natural pigments as antioxidants in foods
In recent years, the issue of food preservation has received increasing attention with the rising global fisheries and aquaculture production and the widespread consumption of highly nutritious foods such as meat. According to the State of World Fisheries and Aquaculture Report (SOFIA) 2022 published by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), global fisheries and aquaculture production reached a record high in 2022, with a total production of 223.2 million tons. Among them, the output of aquaculture alone reached a record high of 130.9 million tons. Aquaculture is not only a source of protein, but also a unique and extremely diverse supplier of essential Omega-3 fatty acids and available micronutrients. Aquaculture and meat are not only important sources of high-quality protein, but are also rich in essential Omega-3 fatty acids and a wide range of micronutrients. However, due to their high nutrient and moisture content, these foods are susceptible to deterioration due to protein and lipid oxidation during transportation, distribution and storage, which can affect the color, taste, and texture of the product.
In order to slow down the oxidization process of food products, conventional preservation measures include the addition of synthetic antioxidants, refrigeration, and proper packaging. Among them, synthetic antioxidants such as butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) and butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) were once Universally utilized. However, in recent years, these compounds have been suspected to be a major cause of liver damage and cancer, and are now restricted by law. In addition, butylhydroxyanisole (BHA), tert-butylhydroquinone (TBHQ), and propylene glycol (PG) have been reported to have the potential to interact with the nucleic acid structure and form molecular complexes that can cause damage to the double helix structure of DNA. Growing consumer concerns about the safety of synthetic antioxidants have led to increasing interest in the study of natural colors as natural antioxidants.
Natural pigments have gradually become a research hotspot in the field of food preservation due to their wide sources, high safety, and no obvious side effects. Natural pigments exert their antioxidant effects by reducing the accumulation of reactive oxygen species and scavenging free radicals. Curcumin is one of the important natural pigment antioxidants. The hydroxyl and methoxy groups in the curcumin molecule provide protons, which can block free radical reactions and thereby eliminate reactive oxygen species. In addition, curcumin also exerts its effects through multiple molecular mechanisms, such as chelating heavy metals or altering the activities of various enzymes, thereby reducing oxidative stress levels and further enhancing antioxidant capacity. Carotenoids exert their antioxidant effects by quenching singlet oxygen. When a carotenoid molecule is excited, it dissipates the newly acquired energy through a series of rotational and vibrational interactions with solvent molecules, returning to the ground state and being able to quench more free radicals. This process usually occurs when the carotenoid molecule contains conjugated double bonds. Although carotenoids themselves are relatively stable and do not readily react with free radicals, in the presence of specific free radicals (such as peroxyl radicals), they may decompose to form non-radical compounds, thereby terminating the chain reaction initiated by free radicals; Unlike other natural pigments, the antioxidant mechanism of chlorophyll is believed to be achieved by inhibiting the decomposition of hydrogen peroxides. To date, many studies have applied natural pigments in food as antioxidants. For example, Bellucci et al. added dragon fruit extract rich in polyphenols and β-vanthanin glycosides as a natural antioxidant to meat patties, and the experimental results showed that its addition significantly enhanced the antioxidant performance of the meat patty membrane; In addition, Karoui et al. added tomato pomace rich in lycopene to chicken, and found that the hue angle (H0) (90: yellow, 180: green, 0: red) of the chicken patty remained relatively stable during storage, indicating that both the color and antioxidant properties of the chicken patty were effectively protected. Astaxanthin also has a strong ability to scavenge free radicals due to its unique chemical structure (such as hydroxyl and ketone groups, etc.). El-Bialy et al. added astaxanthin to tilapia surimi, significantly reducing the pH value of the surimi, delaying lipid oxidation, and thereby extending the freshness period of the surimi. In addition, astaxanthin has also been used in whole wheat biscuits, black tiger shrimp, and lamb, etc., not only enhancing antioxidant performance but also reducing lipid oxidation. The above results indicate that it is feasible to add natural pigments to food as antioxidants. However, the acceptance of consumers towards food with added natural pigments has not been taken into account. However, natural pigments are costly to extract and relatively unstable. Synthetic antioxidants are less expensive and more stable than natural pigments, so it is necessary to consider how to balance costs with food prices. In addition, although natural pigments have antioxidant properties and are used in a wide variety of foods, can they exert their antioxidant properties in all types of food? Therefore, when adding natural pigments as antioxidants to food, it is necessary to comprehensively consider various factors such as consumer acceptance, cost, and safety.
In summary, natural pigments have strong antioxidant ability and have a broad application prospect in the field of food preservation. In the future, with the innovation of extraction technology and the reduction of cost, natural pigments are expected to replace synthetic antioxidants in more food products to satisfy consumers’ demand for healthy and natural food. In addition, the antioxidant properties of natural pigments are not only limited to the food industry, but also play an important role in the prevention of cardiovascular diseases in the fields of medicine and cosmetics.

Natural pigments as antimicrobial agents in foods
Natural pigments can serve as excellent natural antibacterial agents. The antibacterial mechanism of natural pigments mainly manifests in the following aspects. Firstly, natural pigments can reduce the stability of the cytoplasmic membrane and increase the permeability of the cell membrane, leading to the leakage of intracellular contents and ultimately causing bacterial death. Secondly, natural pigments can also inhibit the normal physiological functions of bacteria by interfering with their metabolism, preventing them from growing and reproducing normally. Additionally, certain natural pigments (such as curcumin) not only interact with cells but also inhibit the function of the filamentous temperature-sensitive mutant (FtsZ) protein necessary for cell division, further enhancing their antibacterial capabilities.
Recently, Qin et al.incorporated anthocyanins into starch/polyvinyl alcohol and nano-complex-based smart packaging films and examined the total viable counts (TVCs) under different film conditions, which showed that the total viable counts (TVCs) of seabass fillets packaged with polyethylene (PE) and starch/polyvinyl alcohol (starch/PVA, SP) films (Total Viable Count, TVC) increased rapidly and exceeded the limit of 6 lg CFU/g specified for aquatic products on days 5 and 6, respectively. In contrast, the starch/PVA film containing free anthocyanins (the film containing free anthocyanins, SP-FA) and the starch/PVA film containing anthocyanins-loaded nanocomplexes (starch/PVA film containing anthocyanins-loaded nanocomplexes, SP-AN) films packed with sea bass fillets did not exceed this limit until day 8. There was no significant difference between the SP-FA film treatment group and the SP-AN film treatment group at a storage time of less than 8 days. The better freshness preservation of SP-FA and SP-AN films can be explained by the antimicrobial activity of anthocyanins. Moreover, in another study, the composite chitosan membrane with curcumin-grafted cellulose nanofibers also showed significant antimicrobial and UV-blocking properties. Zhang et al. in their experiments, it was found that the number of E. coli in the samples treated with the membrane was significantly decreased within 24 h by comparing it to the control, while the number of E. coli in the control was increased by about 4.61 log CFU/mL. This difference in antimicrobial effect may be related to the different antimicrobial mechanisms of chitosan and curcumin. Curcumin also has some inhibitory effects on bacteria. Zhang et al. also synthesized silver nanoparticles using red curcumin under simulated sunlight, and the results showed that silver nanoparticles exhibited good antibacterial activity against three pathogens, namely, E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and S. aureus, with silver nanoparticles on S. aureus. The results showed that silver nanoparticles exhibited good antimicrobial activity against the three pathogens, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus, with silver nanoparticles showing the strongest inhibition against Staphylococcus aureus, with the minimum inhibitory concentration of 7.8125 μg/mL and the minimum sterilizing concentration of 31.25 μg/mL.
As the antimicrobial mechanisms of natural pigments are gradually being deepened, their potential for use in biomedical applications is gradually being demonstrated. Antimicrobial therapy is crucial in clinical infection management, but in recent years evolutionary pressures for survival have led to the emergence of drug resistance, and traditional antibiotic therapy has suffered a tremendous impact, increasing in the cost of treatment and a significant reduction in therapeutic efficacy. In checkerboard experiments, curcumin has been recognized as a potential cracking agent for antibiotic resistance due to its ability to restore the antimicrobial efficacy of antibiotics that have failed due to resistance by reducing the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of antibiotics by 4–128-fold. The combination of curcumin and antibiotics in the treatment of drug-resistant Aspergillus abscesses. The results showed that when curcumin and amikacin were combined at low concentrations, it disrupted the biofilm and when curcumin concentration was higher than amikacin, it had a significant disruptive effect on the biomass of the biofilm formed by Mycobacterium abscessus at the highest of 4 and 8 days. In addition to this, it also reduced the number of viable cells in the biofilm. The introduction of natural pigments provides a new strategy for the treatment of drug-resistant infections.

In summary, natural pigments demonstrates extensive potential for application in the fields of food preservation of their unique antimicrobial mechanism and safety. Future research can further explore the application of natural pigments in food packaging, optimize their antimicrobial properties, and develop more efficient and environmentally friendly natural pigment extraction technologies to meet consumer demand for healthy food. In addition, the antimicrobial properties of natural pigments not only show great potential in the field of food preservation, but also their synergistic applications in the biomedical field have attracted much attention. With the increasing prominence of bacterial infections in clinical treatments and the challenge of the difficulty of eliminating germs on the surface of medical devices, the development of low-toxicity and highly biocompatible antimicrobial strategies has become an urgent issue. Natural pigments provide new ideas and solutions in this field due to their natural origin, low toxicity, and strong antimicrobial ability. By combining natural pigments with existing biomedical therapeutics, not only can the antimicrobial effect be enhanced, but also the side effects associated with traditional antimicrobial agents can be reduced, thus providing a safer and more effective strategy for avoid and managing bacterial infections in clinical settings treatment of bacterial infections as well as the elimination of germs from medical devices. This cross-field synergistic application is expected to bring breakthroughs in the biomedical field and promote the development of antimicrobial therapeutics.

Extraction of natural colors from food scraps
About 12% of the global population still does not have access to adequate food, with 9% of the Asian population facing food shortages occupying the first place globally, and one in four people in sub-Saharan Africa being deficient in food and vital nutrients. However, the global food production industry is facing serious challenges, producing about 1.6 billion tons of food waste annually, of which the edible portion is about 1.3 billion tons, with a commercial value of up to $750 million. In the absence of effective countermeasures, food waste will increase by 40% in the future, equivalent to 126 million tons per year. In this context, how to achieve in-utilization of food waste has to be solved. It can not only effectively reduce food waste and alleviate food shortage, but also realize the reuse of resources and sustainable development. The traditional treatment of food waste includes incineration and transportation to landfills, and the incineration of food waste produces harmful substances such as carbon dioxide and methane, which can exacerbate climate change. In addition, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are released, including dioxins, furans, etc., which contribute to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases.
Food waste, including peels, shells, pulp, and seeds, typically accounts for 30–40% of food mass. For example, food waste generated during mango processing accounts for 10–60% of the total weight, while apple waste accounts for 25% of the total weight. Extracting natural pigments from these waste materials not only successfully transforms waste into useful resources, reducing resource waste, but also lowers the carbon footprint. In reports, carbon footprint types are typically categorized as follows: the climate footprint of production systems (calculated in kilograms of carbon dioxide per kilogram of food), supply chain footprint, percentage reduction in carbon footprint, and zero-carbon footprint targets.
To address global challenges and achieve sustainable development by 2030, the United Nations has established 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), forming a comprehensive and universal political agenda. SDG Goal 12 emphasizes the importance of ensuring sustainable consumption and reducing waste, and our research is guided by this goal to explore how innovative technologies can transform waste into valuable resources. Additionally, this practice is closely related to SDG Goal 13 on climate action. Climate change is one of the global challenges we face. The reuse of food waste reduces the incineration of waste, thereby decreasing the release of greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide produced by incineration, mitigating the greenhouse effect, and making a positive contribution to the SDGs. Furthermore, it reduces reliance on traditional chemical fuels, thereby decreasing carbon emissions in industrial production processes. Therefore, extracting high-value natural pigments from food waste offers an environmentally friendly and sustainable alternative to traditional waste disposal methods.
To address these challenges, current solutions include converting food waste into high value-added products, including bioethanol, biodiesel, and bioelectricity. It is noteworthy that many of these wastes are It is noteworthy that many of these wastes are rich in biologically active natural pigments, which are important resources for the development of novel natural pigments. Currently, Ordóñez-Santos et al. innovatively used ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) to extract carotenoids extracts from mandarin epicarp as natural colorants and applied them in breads and cakes, while breads and cakes to which lemon yellow was added were used as a control group. The results of the study showed that the addition of the extracts significantly increased the carotenoid content of the baked products, and the content was higher in bread than in cake. This phenomenon may be due to the thermal decomposition of carotenoids at higher baking temperatures of cakes. Although carotenoid extracts from citrus peels can be used as colorants for baked goods, baking conditions need to be determined to achieve optimal coloration. Meanwhile, Kawasoe et al. analyzed the natural colorant extracted from the pericarp of passion fruit and examined its thermal, light, and storage stability, which showed that, compared to the solution, the better stability of the samples in powder form or covered with light shielding of colored commercial plastic bags suggests that, with proper handling and packaging, passion fruit peels are no longer discarded as food residues in the food industry and can be used as natural colors in the food industry. These studies not only demonstrate the reuse of food scraps such as citrus peel and passion fruit peel, but also provide new ideas for the reuse of other food scraps, provide a sustainable source of natural colors for use in the food industry, and help to protect the environment and reduce food waste. Although existing research has demonstrated the feasibility of extracting natural pigments from orange peel and passion fruit peel, it remains largely confined to the laboratory setting. Given the enormous volume of by-products involved, further experimentation is needed to determine whether low-cost, high-yield production can be achieved on an industrial scale. In addition, we need to consider whether transporting by-products from various locations to construction sites could lead to mold growth, causing fluctuations in pigment recovery rates.
The extraction of natural pigments from food residues not only achieves resource utilization of food residues and effectively reduces environmental pollution, but also meets the consumers’ pursuit of more natural and healthier food. This method opens up a new way for the extraction of natural pigments, and also lays the foundation for the extraction of natural pigments from agricultural by-products such as sorghum skins and peanut inner skins. However, this method also has certain limitations. Current extraction technologies generally have low extraction efficiency and high extraction costs. In addition, not all food waste contains natural pigments suitable for extraction, and specific pigments must be recovered from specific sources.

Conclusion and outlook
Color is one of the key points for consumers to judge the quality of food, and synthetic colors are widely used in food due to their stable nature and low price. However, as researchers gradually studied synthetic colors, they found that they have side effects on the human body. Consumers are concerned about them, and natural colors are gradually replacing synthetic colors in food products. Natural pigments are generally derived from plants, animals, and microorganisms, with natural ingredients, and no obvious side effects on the human body have been found in current studies. Their colors are usually similar to those in nature and there is a wide variety of them, covering a wide range of colors, which can give food a variety of colors more realistic. Natural pigments can not only be used as food coloring agents, but also have antioxidant and antibacterial properties. Therefore, the addition of natural pigments to food not only has the advantage of improving its color but also extends the shelf life of the food. Due to the sensitivity of natural pigments to pH changes, they are used as pH indicators in smart packaging to detect the freshness of food, allowing consumers to directly judge the freshness of food through color changes. However, the application of natural pigments is limited by the extraction technology, low purity of extraction, instability, and susceptibility to environmental influences.
There have been researchers using microwave and high-pressure assisted extraction methods to extract natural pigments from food waste, but the equipment is expensive and not always suitable for industrial production. In addition, the stability of natural pigments is a key factor limiting their application scope. Therefore, we need to continue to develop new technologies or improve existing ones. This will not only help meet consumer demand for healthy foods but also promote sustainable development in the food industry and other sectors, holding significant economic and social implications. For example, by improving extraction techniques, we can enhance the extraction yield and purity of natural pigments, reduce production costs, and make them more suitable for large-scale industrial production. Furthermore, research and application of natural pigments can also promote interdisciplinary collaboration across fields such as chemistry, biology, materials science, and engineering. Such interdisciplinary research not only helps address technical challenges in the application of natural pigments but also provides insights for the development and utilization of other natural resources. In the long term, the widespread application of natural pigments will help reduce the use of synthetic pigments, minimize environmental pollution, and drive the green transformation of the entire food industry. However, the application of natural pigments in the food industry currently faces certain challenges and limitations. First, natural pigments extracted from plants, animals, and food waste generally have low concentrations, and the colors obtained are less vibrant than those of synthetic pigments. Second, there may be color variations between different batches of natural pigments, which could affect consumers’ acceptance of food appearance. Perhaps the most critical consideration is that regulations governing food contact materials vary by country. To establish a global industry, different formulations must be developed to meet these varying requirements.
In our literature review, we were unable to find any studies specifically addressing the long-term stability of dye-rich products, their response stability during storage, or batch-to-batch variability. However, we provide some insights into this issue in the section on future directions. We believe these areas hold significant research potential. Exploring them could lay the groundwork for the future industrialization of natural pigment production and help us better understand the relationship between natural pigments and dosage. This is also an area that requires our attention, such as whether the addition of natural pigments alters the flavor and structure of food, and whether the formulation of food products needs to be modified after adding natural pigments. These challenges and limitations highlight that while natural pigments hold significant potential for widespread application in the food industry, ongoing improvements to existing technologies are necessary to overcome these obstacles and achieve their full potential in the sector.

Natural pigments have made their mark in the dyestuffs and cosmetics industry with their excellent coloring properties, making them an ideal choice for coloring. The wide variety of pigments and the wide range of colors they present provide the dyestuff and cosmetic industries with a wealth of color materials. Lipsticks have a high demand for color richness, and natural pigments are a perfect match for this demand. Nowadays, artificial intelligence is developing rapidly, bringing a lot of convenience to people’s lives. When people need to mix a specific color, with the help of AI’s precise calculations, they can quickly determine the right proportion, without having to go through the tedious and lengthy process of repeated trials (Fig. 5a). The natural pigments’ antioxidant and antibacterial properties have applications beyond the food industry and show great potential in medicine. In addition to being used in sensors for detecting pathogenic bacteria and sterilizing medical devices, their antioxidant capacity is also very strong, and in the future, natural pigments can be added to anti-aging medicines, as well as protecting the cardiovascular system and preventing diseases such as atherosclerosis(Fig. 5c).
