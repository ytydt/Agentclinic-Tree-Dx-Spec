Analysis of the causes of redo pull-through for recurrent constipation and the risk factors affecting the prognosis of the Hirschsprung’s disease: a single-center retrospective study and systematic review
Background
Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR) is a congenital gastrointestinal disorder. Pull-through (PT) surgery, the primary treatment, often leads to recurrent constipation. The causes of redo pull-through (redo-PT) for recurrent constipation remain unclear, with limited research on follow-up outcomes and prognostic factors.
Methods
We searched “PubMed” and “Web of Science” for references using the title/abstract terms “Hirschsprung” and “Redo pull-through”. We enrolled patients from the literature review group and our retrospective group who underwent redo-PT for recurrent constipation. A comparative analysis was conducted between two groups based on the clinical information for redo-PT.
Results
A total of 360 cases were collected from the references (327 patients) and our center (33 patients). Recurrent aganglionosis (RA) and Mechanical Obstruction (MO) were the primary reasons for redo-PT, accounting for 52.50% and 36.67%, respectively. When comparing the causes of redo-PT between the literature group and our center group, RA accounted for 49.85% and 75.76%, while MO accounted for 49.85% and 21.21%, respectively. Prognostic outcomes of 31 patients from our center were analyzed, revealing no significant associations between postoperative complications and gender, age, causes of redo pull-through, or surgical approach (P > 0.05).
Conclusions
Recurrent constipation requiring redo-PT are predominantly caused by RA and MO. Gender, age, causes of redo pull-through, or surgical approach do not affect the prognosis of redo-PT. The laparoscopic approach demonstrated superior cosmetic outcomes compared to the laparotomy approach.

Background
Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR) is a rare congenital gastrointestinal disorder characterized by the absence of enteric neurons in the distal colon, affecting about 1 in 5000 births, with a male-to-female ratio of 4:1. The condition results from disruptions in the proliferation or migration of enteric neural crest cells (ENCCs) during embryogenesis, influenced by genetic and environmental factors. These disruptions cause a range of clinical manifestations, including constipation, abdominal distension and developmental delays.
Surgery is the primary treatment for HSCR. The treatment strategy involves resection of the affected intestinal segment and reconstruction of gastrointestinal continuity. Classic surgical techniques include the Swenson, Duhamel, Soave and Rehbein procedures. With the widespread use of minimally invasive techniques, first-line surgical options now include one-stage laparoscopic-assisted colon pull-through (PT) and pure transanal PT procedures.
The most common complication after PT for HSCR is the recurrence of postoperative constipation, with an incidence ranging from 8 to 30%. Most of these patients with constipation can alleviate their symptoms through non-surgical interventions, such as anal dilation and enemas. Approximately 2–3% of these patients develop refractory severe constipation unresponsive to conservative management, requiring secondary surgical procedures. Some studies provided diagnostic and therapeutic suggestions for redo-PT, and a few investigations described the classification of causes for redo-PT. However, the causes of redo-PT for recurrent constipation and the risk factors influencing HSCR prognosis are still not well understood.
This study collected a comprehensive dataset of clinical information and postoperative follow-up data from individuals who underwent PT surgery for HSCR and later required redo-PT due to recurrent constipation. The objective of this study was to identify the factors leading to redo-PT in cases of persistent constipation and to determine the prognostic risk factors affecting clinical outcomes in HSCR patients.

Materials and methods

Study subjects
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Ethics Approval Number: TJ-IRB20230739). As this is a retrospective study, informed consent has been exempted. Cases were included based on the following criteria (last accessed December 2021). Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients admitted to the Pediatric Surgery Department of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, between January 2005 and December 2021. (2) HSCR patients presenting with “constipation recurrence” and requiring redo-PT. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients undergoing staged surgeries or internal sphincterotomy. (2) Patients with severe congenital anomalies, such as Down syndrome. (3) Patients with incomplete postoperative follow-up data after redo-PT.
Case screening flow chart of HSCR patients who underwent redo-PT for constipation recurrence in our center and references
We searched “PubMed” and “Web of Science” for references published between 2007 and 2023 using the title/abstract terms “Hirschsprung” and “Redo pull-through” (last searched June 2023). Duplicate and incomplete data were removed, and clinical information was compiled from the remaining references. The complete PubMed search strategy is provided in Fig. 1.

Data collection of general clinical information
Two independent reviewers screened titles/abstracts using EndNote X9 to remove duplicates. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed against inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through consensus with a third investigator. Clinical data from patients at our center and referenced studies were collected and analyzed by one reviewer and verified by another. Primary outcomes included postoperative complication rates stratified by time intervals and scar evaluation (SCAR scale). Secondary outcomes comprised patient demographics, pathological classification of the initial PT, complications after PT, reasons for redo-PT, surgical approach, intraoperative blood loss, patient age, surgical duration and postoperative hospitalization duration of redo-PT. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was adapted to assess observational studies, evaluating selection (representativeness, ascertainment), comparability (confounder adjustment), and outcome (assessment, follow-up) domains. Two reviewers independently scored studies, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.
We conducted a systematic review of the literature and summarized the factors associated with redo-PT in cases of recurrent constipation. Based on the causes presented in these studies, we classified the causes of redo-PT for recurrent constipation into three distinct categories that integrates both pathological evaluation of intestinal ganglion cells and mechanical obstruction factors: 1. Residual Aganglionosis (RA), which includes incomplete resection of the proximal or distal non-nerve-segmented bowel; 2. Acquired Aganglionosis (AA), which is neuronal atrophy or loss due to complications such as postoperative enteritis. 3. Mechanical Obstruction (MO), including all patients with unclear or normal pathological findings who also present with postoperative complications that cannot be resolved through non-surgical treatments, such as strictures, or twisted pulled-through bowel.
Postoperative follow-up data for selected patients were collected using discharge records and telephone/WeChat follow-ups. The Scar Cosmesis Assessment and Rating (SCAR) scale was used to quantitatively evaluate the cosmetic outcomes of surgical incisions. Data were grouped by gender, age, and surgical approaches. The number of cases with and without postoperative complications was calculated for the following periods: < 1 year, 1–5 years, 5–10 years, and > 10 years.

Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 26.0. Quantitative data were reported as frequencies, medians (interquartile range, Q1–Q3), or means ± standard deviations (M ± SD). Frequency distribution histograms and statistical tables were generated for subsequent analysis. Categorical data were reported as ratios (relative ratios), proportions (structural relative numbers), and rates (intensity relative numbers). Prespecified subgroup analyses examined effects by surgical approaches and patient age groups. Sensitivity analyses excluded studies with high risk of bias or small sample sizes (n < 5). Gray literature was searched to mitigate reporting bias.
Shapiro–Wilk test (n < 50), Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (n > 50) and Q-Q plots were utilized to evaluate the normality of quantitative data. Parametric tests (Student's t-test) were applied for normally distributed data. The Wilcoxon (Mann–Whitney) rank-sum tests were applied to evaluate the significance of non-normally distributed quantitative data. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were conducted using 2 × C contingency tables to determine significant differences in rates among subgroups. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
This study adhered to PRISMA and AMSTAR guidelines for reporting.

Results

Basic clinical information
A total of 33 HSCR patients in our center who underwent redo-PT for recurrent constipation were included in this study (Fig. 1, Table S1). The age at redo-PT and the surgical duration (in minutes) approximately followed a normal distribution (Fig. S1A-B).
Proportional analysis of reasons for redo-PT in different groups. A. Literature group; B. Our center group; C. In total. RA, Recurrent Aganglionosis; AA, Acquired Aganglionosis; MO, Mechanical Obstruction
Among the HSCR patients undergoing redo-PT for recurrent constipation, 25 were male (75.76%) and 8 were female (24.24%), yielding a male-to-female ratio of 3.13:1. The mean age at redo-PT was 40.27 ± 25.63 months. The mean surgical duration of redo-PT was 281.73 ± 24.47 min, with a median of 283.00 min and an interquartile range of 260.50–302.50 min. A laparotomic approach was used in 23 cases (69.70%), while 10 cases (30.30%) were performed laparoscopically. All 33 cases underwent the Soave procedure for redo-PT (Table S2). Postoperative pathology from the initial PT revealed an absence of ganglion cells in the narrowed intermuscular and submucosal plexuses, confirming the HSCR diagnosis in all 33 cases. According to our center’s categorization of redo-PT reasons, 25 cases (75.76%) were due to RA, 1 case (3.03%) to AA, and 7 cases (21.21%) to MO (Fig. 2B).

We initially included 47 referred articles about HSCR patients who underwent redo-PT for constipation. After excluding articles with irrelevant or incomplete data on redo-PT for constipation, 11 English articles were selected, comprising 327 cases of HSCR with recurrent constipation requiring redo-PT (Fig. 1). The 11 selected articles in Table S3 showed the publication year, authors, countries, time range and cases number of different redo-PT reasons.

A total of 360 cases were collected from referenced studies and our institution, focusing primarily on the causes of redo-PT. The cases were categorized into the “Literature Group” and the “Our Center Group”. Fisher’s exact test revealed a significant difference in the proportions of redo-PT causes between the two groups (P = 0.017, Table 1). When comparing the causes of redo-PT between the literature group and our center group, RA accounted for 49.85% and 75.76%, while MO accounted for 49.85% and 21.21%. AA was about four times less frequent in the literature group (11.62% vs. 3.03%) (Fig. 2A-B). RA and MO were the predominant causes of redo-PT, accounting for 52.50% and 36.67% of cases, respectively (Fig. 2C).

The prognostic outcomes of 31 patients were summarized in Table S4. All patients underwent redo-PT, with no deaths or need for permanent stoma during follow-up. Two cases of 33 patients had missing follow-up data. Postoperative complications of redo-PT included anastomotic leakage, anal incontinence, recurrent constipation, and enteritis. In this study, postoperative complications were recorded as binary variables (presence or absence). Thirty-three HSCR patients were categorized into a “laparotomy group” (n = 23) and a “laparoscopy group” (n = 10) for further perioperative analysis. The mean duration of redo-PT was 274.30 ± 24.97 min in the laparoscopy group and 284.96 ± 24.08 min in the laparotomy group. An independent t-test showed no statistically significant difference in surgical duration between the laparotomy and laparoscopy groups (t = 1.156, P = 0.257; Table S5). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test revealed no significant difference in intraoperative blood loss between the laparotomy group and the laparoscopy group (z = 1.051, P = 0.324; Table S5, Fig. 3A). The mean hospital stay was 20.00 ± 5.19 days for the laparoscopy group and 24.45 ± 7.56 days for the laparotomy group. A t-test confirmed no significant difference in hospital stay duration between the two groups (P = 0.107; Fig. 3B). SCAR scale was used to compare scar cosmesis between the laparoscopy group (n = 10) and the laparotomy group (n = 23). The analysis revealed significantly lower mean SCAR scores (P < 0.001, Table 3) in the laparoscopy group (4.20 ± 2.62) compared to the laparotomy group (8.74 ± 2.00). These findings suggest that patients in the laparoscopy group had better cosmetic outcomes regarding scar appearance than those in the laparotomy group post-surgery. However, there was no significant difference in unplanned readmission rates between the two groups (P = 0.306, Table 3).

Discussion
Patients of HSCR who undergo PT surgery often experience short- or long-term postoperative complications, necessitating redo-PT procedures. This study offers a comprehensive analysis of the causes and prognostic factors associated with redo-PT for recurrent constipation. A dataset of 360 cases was compiled from referenced sources and our institution. The analysis identified RA (52.50%) and MO (32.67%) related issues as the primary causes of redo-PT for recurrent constipation. Gender, age, causes of redo pull-through, or surgical approach showed no significant correlation to the prognosis of redo-PT patients in our center. The laparoscopic approach demonstrated superior cosmetic outcomes compared to laparotomy, as evidenced by significantly lower SCAR scores, without compromising surgical efficiency. This study provides valuable insights into the causes and prognostic factors of redo-PT in HSCR patients with recurrent constipation. These findings hold potential to improve the clinical management and therapeutic outcomes for these patients.
Several studies on redo-PT were identified that did not meet our selection criteria. The exclusion of these articles was primarily based on their specific research focus and methodological characteristics. A few studies focused on technical aspects of re-operative surgical approaches or postoperative rehabilitation outcomes. These specific research orientations, while valuable in their respective contexts, did not contribute directly to our investigation of etiological factors and classification framework development, thus warranting their exclusion from our primary analysis. By systematically categorizing the reasons for redo-PT into RA, AA, and MO, our findings showed that RA (52.50%) and MO (32.67%) were the predominant causes of redo-PT, aligning with previous studies.
RA was the leading cause of recurrent constipation requiring redo-PT, primarily due to incomplete resection of aganglionic bowel segments. Despite efforts to ensure adequate removal of aganglionic segments and proper anastomosis with ganglionated segments during the initial pull-through, residual aganglionosis and transition-zone pull-throughs can still occur. While preserving as much normal bowel as possible benefits function, it also increases the risk of RA. The incomplete preoperative assessments of HSCR may contribute to inadequate intraoperative resection of affected intestinal segments. This highlights the need for careful consideration of biopsy and resection lengths during PT surgeries in HSCR treatment centers. Addressing these factors could improve intraoperative resection precision and reduce the risk of postoperative recurrent constipation.
MO was the second primary cause of recurrent constipation requiring redo-PT. MO encompass complex conditions leading to recurrent constipation that cannot be alleviated by non-surgical treatments. MO leads to food retention, constipation, bloating, and nutrient absorption issues, hindering recovery. Additionally, perioperative complications in MO, such as narrowing, infection, fistulas, or ulcers, can cause intestinal obstruction, compromised blood supply, and pain, further hindering recovery. In cases of recurrent constipation following PT surgery, the underlying cause may be related to the internal anal sphincter's inability to relax, often due to pelvic floor dysfunction or anal sphincter spasm. Anorectal manometry, demonstrating elevated resting pressure, can confirm this diagnosis. Initial management should focus on bowel regulation through laxatives and other conservative measures. For patients unresponsive to these interventions, temporary sphincter-relaxing therapies, such as botulinum toxin injections or nitroglycerine paste, may be attempted. Should these approaches prove ineffective, and after excluding residual aganglionosis, anal sphincterectomy could be considered. Ultimately, if recurrent constipation persists despite these measures, a comprehensive assessment integrating pathological and functional evaluations may be necessary to determine the appropriateness of redo-PT.
For patients experiencing postoperative complications after redo-PT, initial management often includes bowel regulation through stool softeners and enemas to alleviate constipation. Pelvic floor training may be recommended for patients with sphincter-related dysfunction. In cases where these measures are insufficient, temporary interventions such as botulinum toxin injections or nitroglycerine paste may be considered. If non-surgical approaches fail to resolve symptoms, a thorough evaluation is conducted to determine the need for further surgical intervention, such as adhesiolysis or redo operation. These strategies are consistent with established clinical practices and are tailored to individual patient needs. We will consider incorporating more detailed data on readmissions and non-surgical interventions in future studies to provide a more comprehensive analysis.
This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the retrospective nature of the analysis introduces potential selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the proposed classification system for redo-PT causes is preliminary and requires validation through prospective multicenter studies with standardized diagnostic and surgical protocols. Third, the small number of follow-up cases and limited granularity in follow-up intervals (e.g., lack of data at one, three, and six months postoperatively) restrict the ability to conduct detailed prognostic evaluations. Increasing the sample size and refining follow-up protocols could enhance the identification of additional prognostic indicators and improve the monitoring of postoperative bowel recovery.
