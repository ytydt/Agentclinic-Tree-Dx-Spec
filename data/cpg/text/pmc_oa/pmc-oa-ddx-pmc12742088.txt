Evaluation of universal health coverage for maternal health outcomes in low- and middle-income countries: a scoping review of the context, reforms and progress
Abstract
Objectives
This scoping review explores the global context, policies and programmes aimed at achieving universal health coverage (UHC) for maternal health services, focusing on progress within low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Additionally, it maps methodological approaches and indicators used to evaluate UHC progress for maternal health.
Design
Scoping review methodology.
Data sources
ProQuest, Scopus, Web of Science and Ovid MEDLINE were searched.
Eligibility criteria for selecting studies
Included were peer-reviewed studies that evaluated UHC policies, programmes and progress towards achieving UHC of maternal health services in LMICs, reported primary research and conducted in all healthcare settings.
Data extraction and synthesis
Two independent reviewers screened and appraised eligible studies using preset criteria based on a ‘population-concept-context’ framework.
Results
The initial search resulted in 959 studies of 61 articles selected in the final review. Most studies provided international (eg, Sustainable Development Goals, n=35), and national context on health system reforms related to healthcare financing and budgets (n=36). While progress was made on the coverage of maternal health services, the equity in maternal health service access was low for most studies. Both studies evaluating interventions and those not evaluating interventions reported high coverage in maternal health indicators, with antenatal care showing low coverage (n=7, 41%), skilled birth attendance (n=6, 55%) and facility delivery (n=6, 60%), while postnatal care coverage remained variable across studies. Two studies reported high coverage of caesarean section births (n=2, 100%). Studies evaluating intervention found equity improved in access and utilisation of maternal health services; conversely, evaluation without intervention for most studies identified low equity in access and utilisation of maternal health services. High inequity in determinants of maternal health outcomes was reported across studies measuring equity domains.
Conclusions
Countries have many approaches to reaching UHC and tackling the problem of achieving UHC for maternal healthcare. Therefore, policymakers in LMICs must decide how to allocate limited resources to prioritise coverage, equity or quality of maternal healthcare.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY
Conducted a scoping review using established frameworks (Arksey and O’Malley, JBI, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses-extension for Scoping Reviews) to map methodological approaches and indicators used to evaluate progress in maternal health research in low- and middle-income countries.

Introduction
Universal health coverage (UHC) is a global health priority and a core element of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) adopted by the United Nations (UN) in September 2015. In this review, we define UHC following the WHO framework: ensuring that all individuals and communities receive the service they need, ranging from prevention to treatment and rehabilitation, without financial hardship. Its goal is to achieve health for all, regardless of the ability to pay. The ambitious aim to expand health service coverage to everyone and provide financial protection has a longstanding history tracing back to the 20th century. Achieving UHC requires not only financial resources but also political commitment and comprehensive social policies that address the root causes of health inequities. In its 1948 Constitution, the WHO described the fundamental right to health of every human being. Universal coverage is perceived as the best way to attain that right and is fundamental to the principle of ‘Health for All’ set out in the Declaration of Alma-Ata in 1978. In line with the international calls to attain the right to health, corresponding efforts were made to improve maternal health. In 1987, the Safe Motherhood Initiative (SMI) set the goal to reduce maternal mortality by 50% by the year 2000. Subsequent international initiatives like the Cairo International Conference on Population and Development in 1994 and the Fourth World Conference in Beijing on Women in 1995 broadened the concept of maternal healthcare services to include efforts addressing both the direct and indirect causes of maternal mortality and morbidity. In 2000, the adoption of the UN’s Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) further reinforced maternal health as a global priority and in 2005, WHO’s Commission on Social Determinants of Health was established. Notably, UHC has increasingly gained attention as a global health goal since the 58th WHO Assembly resolution in 2005. UHC gained even more political attention following the WHO Health Report on Health Financing Reforms in 2010 and with the recognition of widening inequalities in health outcomes between population groups and geographical areas that emerged post-MDG era.
Despite progress, inequities in maternal health remain both between and within countries. Inequities in health are defined as differences in health or access to health services that originate from socioeconomic disparities, which can be corrected by changes in policies, programmes or practices. These health inequities, including those in maternal health, have become a long-standing policy debate worldwide and a global health priority. Given the social determinants of health, maternal mortality is not only an indicator of health but also reflects inequities in underlying social factors that influence access to affordable health services and health outcomes. The WHO Commission on the Social Determinants of Health raised awareness that global health challenges are shaped by the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age. Tackling these inequities is a priority of the UN Development Programme’s ‘human development’ approach, which seeks to address the broader range of inequities beyond economic inequality, including key issues such as health, education, environmental inequality, respect and dignity for all.
Barriers to accessing and using maternal healthcare services include socio-cultural factors, health system barriers, geographical challenges, delay or inability to access care, lack of decision-making powers, lack of transport and insufficient supplies at the facilities. Studies from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) also show that payment for health services presents a major barrier to accessing and using maternal healthcare services. The impact of user fees on maternal health outcomes is widely reported in low-middle-income settings. Populations in low socioeconomic quintiles or living in rural and remote areas are often unable to pay for essential services or experience catastrophic effects of health expenditure.
UHC can therefore be a vehicle for enhancing equity, improving health, promoting the financial well-being of households and driving development. The Lancet Commission on Investing in Health, in its 2013 report Global Health 2035: A World Converging within a Generation (Global Health 2035, 2013) argued that countries should adopt progressive (pro-poor) pathways towards UHC that prioritise the needs of the poor from the beginning. Progressive universalism is regarded as an effective strategy for achieving better health outcomes and increased financial protection. Following is a list of steps for countries proceeding to: (1) set and expand guaranteed services, 2) develop health financing systems to fund guaranteed services and ensure financial protection, 3) ensure high-quality service availability, (4) improve governance and management of the health sector and (5) strengthen other aspects of health systems to move closer to UHC.
Several reviews relevant to this study have been conducted. For example, a review examining the influence of social determinants of health on maternal health services and maternal mortality in India mapped the evidence of social determinants and explored their mechanisms of influence using the SDH framework. Another review examined health system reforms and global health interventions that facilitate the achievement of UHC, identifying successful interventions such as financial protection, service coverage, population coverage and quality health services. Further studies include summaries of indicators and domains used in studies examining causal explanation of social determinants of health using equity-oriented analysis of linkages between health and other sectors. Two studies conducted by Ansu-Mensah and colleagues examining the perception of mothers (2020) and managers and providers (2021) regarding the quality of care during the era of free maternal healthcare in sub-Saharan Africa, both reviews found poor perceptions of the quality of maternal health services. Additionally, a review of intervention designs aimed at supporting and improving the performance of primary healthcare workers in LMICs to strengthen the health system and achieve UHC found a lack of comparative intervention trials to establish clear standards for such initiatives. Finally, a review of maternal health literacy promotion interventions identified various strategies, including both individual-based and group-based interventions, each with distinct strengths and limitations.
Although progress towards achieving UHC is tracked by the UN agencies, empirical case studies are rarely documented, especially in developing countries and even more so in post-war contexts like Somalia. Best-practice strategies that countries can take to address the most difficult and context-specific implementation challenges to achieve UHC are less well understood. Limited information is available on the ‘how’ questions, that is, those related to maximising the chances of implementation success. For example, how have some countries managed to push UHC to the top of the political agenda? How did they build and sustain political commitment for UHC over time? In addition, the path to UHC differs between all countries based on variations in demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, among other factors. Hence, there is a need for more country case studies to measure progress and to understand both facilitating and limiting factors in making progress towards UHC.
This scoping review is the first phase in a larger doctoral study examining progress towards achieving UHC of maternal healthcare in Somalia and mechanisms through which social determinants contribute to inequities in maternal health outcomes. The review maps international evidence to identify indicators, frameworks and methodological approaches which are then applied and adapted in the Somali context. In doing so, it ensures that the Somali study is informed by global lessons while generating context-specific evidence.

Methodology

Scoping review framework
This scoping review follows the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley and Levac et al, as outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines. Reporting was conducted in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews. A scoping review was selected as the appropriate form of evidence synthesis to provide a comprehensive overview of existing literature on this topic.

Inclusion criteria
The review included studies evaluating policies and programmes aimed at achieving UHC in LMICs. Inclusion criteria were based on the population, concept and context criteria proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute, where the population of interest was on UHC policies and programmes. The key concept was the evaluation of the progress towards achieving UHC, which included exploring conceptual frameworks adopted for evaluation, mapping methodological approaches, identifying indicators used to measure outcomes and reviewing evidence on progress towards UHC. The study context was LMICs (as defined by the World Bank, including low-, lower-middle- and upper-middle-income economies). High-income countries were excluded.
The review considered peer-reviewed studies published in English, covering primary health research conducted across all healthcare settings, with no restrictions on study design, between 1 January 2010 and 20 October 2025. A preliminary search was conducted in May 2021, and the search was subsequently updated and re-screened through October 2025 to ensure inclusion of the most recent studies. The starting point was 2010 because maternal health indicators became more consistently measured and reported in the later MDG period, and because this coincided with the increasing global emphasis on UHC, which was further reinforced by the SDGs from 2015 onwards. Somalia adopted UHC commitments later than many other countries, in 2019, due to the collapse of governance and resulting institutional fragility following decades of conflict, compounded by limited, donor-dependent resource; therefore, focusing on more recent global developments provides particularly relevant insights for informing the Somali case study within the doctoral project. Earlier initiatives (2000–2009) were important in shaping maternal health reforms but were excluded to maintain focus on the more standardised and policy-relevant evidence base from 2010 onward. Review articles (systematic or scoping), conference abstracts and studies evaluating multiple countries simultaneously were excluded. While this exclusion may limit comprehensiveness, the broader doctoral project includes a separate document analysis of grey literature in the Somali context. Future reviews could combine peer-reviewed and grey literature sources to provide a more comprehensive evidence base.

Study selection
A single author (JAA) conducted the initial literature search. Two coauthors (JAA and DH) screened titles and abstracts during the final literature search. Articles presenting unclear data in the abstract were reviewed in full. A full-text review was conducted for eligible articles (JAA and DH), and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third coauthor (JD). Reference lists of included studies were screened to identify additional articles for inclusion (figure 1).

Extraction of results
Data extraction was conducted using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (online supplemental table S2), recording details such as standard bibliographic information (first author, title, journal and year of publication), study objectives, country and setting(s), methodology (approach, design, population, data source, time period), intervention evaluated, outcomes measured, global and national context, theoretical or conceptual framework (if any) findings, conclusions and limitations.

Presentation of results
Information extracted from the selected articles was analysed using a descriptive quantitative analysis and qualitative thematic analysis following guidelines proposed by Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework for thematic analysis. Two reviewers independently coded the data, identified and refined themes and resolved differences through discussion. This iterative process ensured consistency and rigour in themes development. Results were presented in line with the scoping review objectives, identifying knowledge gaps in the topic area.

Ethics
This scoping review sourced previously published and publicly available studies; therefore, ethical approval was not required.

Patient and public involvement
None.

Results
The database searches yielded 2634 results, with 959 remaining after duplicate papers were removed. These studies were screened based on title and abstract and 93 articles were eligible for full-text review. 38 articles were excluded after the full-text review, while an additional 6 articles were identified from reference lists of included articles, resulting in 61 articles for the final review. Full-text articles were excluded due to inadequate addressing of the population of interest or the concept of evaluating progress towards UHC in maternal health services (online supplemental table S3) shows details of included articles).

Coverage of studies by country and income group
The 61 studies spanned 33 countries across 5 WHO regions, excluding only the European region (table 2). The majority of studies (n=32) were from the African region, with the following countries represented in two to six studies (Ghana, n=6; Burkina Faso, n=3; Ethiopia, n=3; Mauritania, n=2; Tanzania, n=2; Gabon, n=2; Nigeria, n=2; Kenya, n=2; Madagascar, n=2) and one study in other countries in the region (Benin, Uganda, Senegal, Rwanda, Guinea, Zambia, Mali, Cameroon and Zimbabwe). 14 studies were from the Southeast Asian region (Indonesia, n=5; Nepal, n=4; India, n=3; Bangladesh, n=2). The Western Pacific region was represented by seven studies (China, n=3; Vietnam, n=1; Philippines, n=1; Cambodia, n=1; Papua New Guinea, n=1). Five studies were from the Eastern Mediterranean region (Morocco, n=1; Afghanistan, n=2; Iraq, n=1; Somalia, n=1) and two studies were from the Region of Americas (Mexico, n=1; Brazil, n=1).

Study rationales
Most studies provided both an international and national context (table 2). International contexts frequently centred on international declarations or agendas, citing SDGs (n=38), MDGs (n=27), the SMI (n=5) and human rights and women empowerment frameworks (n=3). 12 studies did not provide an international context.
National contexts often addressed health system reforms related to healthcare financing and budgeting (financial risk protection) (n=35) and health policies to improve equitable access or utilisation of healthcare services (n=19). Additionally, some studies discussed health system strengthening (n=11) to ensure the provision of health services and the quality of health services (n=7), specifically focusing on the quality domain of UHC, such as the perceived responsiveness of services. Financial risk protection reforms were applied either broadly across all healthcare services, such as through a universal healthcare system (n=15) or were specifically targeted at maternal health (n=23), often through the introduction of a maternal health voucher scheme or increased maternal health investment. Similarly, policies to address equity in access or utilisation of healthcare services were relevant to all healthcare services (n=8) or maternal healthcare specifically (n=10).
In studies that provided ‘other’ national contexts (n=3), the focus was on the prioritisation and policymaking processes shaping maternal health services, influenced by government ministries, UN partners, the private sector and Non-Governmental Organisation (NGO) actors. Studies lacking a national context (n=11), primarily aimed to assess progress (or lack of progress) on coverage and equity in maternal health services. Furthermore, slightly fewer than half of the studies evaluated an intervention (n=29).
Methodological characteristics: Almost all studies were quantitative (n=54), with five qualitative studies and two studies using mixed methods (table 3). Most quantitative studies adopted cross-sectional (n=22) or repeated cross-sectional study designs (n=24), while other study designs included a longitudinal cohort study (n=3), time series analysis (n=2) and interrupted time series (n=3). All qualitative studies, and the qualitative component of the mixed methods studies, were case studies; the quantitative component used time series analyses. The majority of studies conducted their analyses at the national (n=38), with an additional four studies encompassing both national and regional levels. Other studies were based on subnational analyses: regional (n=8), district (n=7) or facility-based (n=4).

Maternal health indicators and outcomes
In reporting on progress towards achieving UHC of maternal healthcare, a distinction was made between studies that evaluated intervention and those not evaluating an intervention. Both groups of studies examined progress relating to service delivery and equity in utilisation. The majority of intervention-evaluating studies (n=29) reported significant improvement in coverage of maternal health indicators like ANC (n=10, 53%), skilled birth attendance (n=12, 80%) and facility delivery (n=14, 78%) with moderate improvement in PNC (n=4, 57%) (table 4). Several studies reported improvement in coverage, but changes were not statistically significant. Non-intervention studies (n=32) reported mixed coverage of maternal health indicators, with high coverage for skilled birth attendance (n=6, 55%), facility delivery (n=6, 60%), while ANC (n=10, 59%) and PNC (n=3, 43%) showed low coverage and caesarean section births (n=2, 100%) (online supplemental table S4).

Discussion
This study explored measures for achieving UHC of maternal healthcare in LMICs, mapped methodological approaches used to evaluate the success of these measures, and examined progress towards achieving UHC of maternal healthcare.

Context of studies
Most studies referenced one or more of the global political declarations and agendas such as MDGs, SSDGs and the SMI as the international context for their studies.
National contexts provided the rationale for studies that centred on one or more of the UHC components, most commonly health financing reforms and equity in access and utilisation of health services, either generally or specifically targeting maternal healthcare. Studies indicated that countries with stable political environments and strong economic foundations are more successful in implementing UHC policies. Social policies that address inequalities also play a key role. The WHO’s report on inequality in reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health (RMNCH) emphasises the need for equity-oriented health information systems and policies to address and monitor these inequalities.
This study did not examine how global declarations influenced national contexts or how ministries and stakeholders use these declarations to influence UHC reforms. However, as outlined in a study by Odoch et al, global declarations tend to shape public policy priorities at a national level by energising governmental processes and providing reference points to guide national policies toward priority development issues. Therefore, global and regional declarations can be powerful tools for shaping policy and programme responses to public health concerns.

Methodological approaches
Most studies used quantitative methods with secondary data to either describe the current status of universal access to maternal health services or evaluate a health system reform aimed at improving access to maternal health services. Fewer studies used qualitative approaches, revealing a gap in the literature and an opportunity for more qualitative research. Various statistical analyses were applied depending on the specific research questions addressed: some studies analysed the improvement of universal access to maternal healthcare at an aggregated population level, while others examined inequities in access among socioeconomic groups, and regions, or assessed quality of care. Improving the quality of care within UHC requires stronger government commitment, better data collection and the development of standardised quality measures adaptable to local contexts.

Improvements in UHC of maternal health services
Coverage of indicators following interventions improved, many of which demonstrated statistically significant differences (table 4). For example, Brazil has made notable strides in achieving UHC of maternal health by adopting a comprehensive approach through its health system reform (Unified Health System), combining strategies to reduce poverty with expanded primary health services. As a result, Brazil has seen increased coverage across all indicators and a significant reduction in wealth inequalities for preventive interventions.
Despite overall improvements reported, challenges remain. Women may access services with insufficient frequency, timing, continuity and content. In Uganda, for instance, despite high coverage of ANC, initial contact with the health system is often delayed. Another study in Burkina Faso reported high coverage for facility delivery but low coverage for ANC, likely due to ANC requiring multiple contacts with health providers. Several studies from conflict-affected contexts, including Mali and Somalia (the latter forming part of the broader doctoral project informing this review), emphasise that insecurity and fragile governance continue to hinder equitable service provision. Such gaps may contribute to persistently high maternal mortality rates, and there is often a time lag between policy introduction and observable benefits.
While appreciable progress towards equity in access and utilisation of maternal healthcare has been made, the national averages often mask disparities across subgroups. Studies found that the progress made in reducing the equity gap in the use of maternal health services is uneven across regions. Moreover, substantial socioeconomic disparity in the use of maternal health services remains, particularly affecting disadvantaged, uneducated and ethnic minority groups.
A growing body of literature on the social determinants of health, alongside the Commission on Social Determinants of Health (CSDH) framework for action, supports addressing structural determinants like socioeconomic position, social class, education and income to reduce health inequities, which is the most effective and sustainable approach. However, it is challenging to address these upstream factors; hence, many interventions focus on modifying behaviours rather than tackling the root causes of health-seeking behaviours. A comprehensive approach addressing wider social and structural determinants of health such as income, education, gender disparities and residence is needed. To accommodate a broader developmental agenda and the development of human capital, policies beyond the health sector such as poverty reduction are shown to influence the health services effectiveness and impact on health equity. The health sector should track these determinants and associated policies to encourage action on social determinants of health; equity-focused progress towards UHC will remain limited without parallel action on health determinants. For example, China has improved maternal health outcomes by implementing financial subsidies to increase access to maternal health services like facility delivery and interventions beyond the health sector, reducing poverty, improving women’s literacy and building infrastructure.
A system-wide approach is required to improve maternal health services utilisation and adequately address maternal health needs. Demand-side financing alone is insufficient for achieving UHC in maternal health. While demand-side intervention, such as user fee exceptions, may increase the use of maternal health service usage but without supply-side investment the quality of care and provider motivation will suffer. Furthermore, ‘elite capture’ could ultimately prevent the poorest from accessing healthcare.
Therefore, strategies covering both the supply and demand factors are recommended for achieving UHC. While demand-side intervention may promote the use of facility deliveries, adequate supply-side resources are also necessary. Studies show different socioeconomic groups respond differently to supply-side and demand-side interventions. For example, Parmer and Banerjee found that supply-side intervention increased maternal health service use for higher socioeconomic groups whereas demand-side interventions were more impactful for lower socioeconomic status. Policies and programmes should thus be strengthened to enhance healthcare access and utilisation among the poor, less educated and rural residents.
This review has several limitations. The decision to begin the search from 2010 may have excluded earlier studies; however, this cut-off was chosen because maternal health indicators become more consistently measured during the later MDG period, coinciding with the growing global focus on UHC and the subsequent SDGs. The exclusion of grey literature and conference abstracts may also limit the comprehensiveness of the findings. This was intended to maintain methodological rigour, and a complementary document analysis of grey literature within the broader doctoral project helps to address this gap. Future reviews could integrate both sources for a fuller evidence base.

Conclusion
Achieving UHC is a central goal of the 2030 Agenda for SDGs, aiming to achieve health for all regardless of the ability to pay. Tailored investments addressing provincial barriers to the utilisation of maternal health services are urgently needed to achieve equitable distribution in the use of maternal health services. Supporting policies may be necessary to encourage behavioural change, especially among disadvantaged groups. Incentives for facility-based deliveries that cover expenses and user fee exemption are recommended. Our findings, in line with the CSDH framework for action on the social determinants of health, highlight that factors like income and education, both individually and communally cannot be ignored if we hope to achieve real progress towards equitable access to PNC services.
