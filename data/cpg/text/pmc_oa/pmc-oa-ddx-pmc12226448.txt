Early warning score and feasible complementary approach using artificial intelligence-based bio-signal monitoring system: a review
Early warning score (EWS) have become an essential component of patient safety strategies in healthcare environments worldwide. These systems aim to identify patients at risk of clinical deterioration by evaluating vital signs and other physiological parameters, enabling timely intervention by rapid response teams. Despite proven benefits and widespread adoption, conventional EWS have limitations that may affect their ability to effectively detect and respond to patient deterioration. There is growing interest in integrating continuous multimodal monitoring technologies and advanced analytics, particularly artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML)-based approaches, to address these limitations and enhance EWS performance. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the current state and potential future directions of AI-based bio-signal monitoring in early warning system. It examines emerging trends and techniques in AI and ML for bio-signal analysis, exploring the possibilities and potential applications of various bio-signals such as electroencephalography, electrocardiography, electromyography in early warning system. However, significant challenges exist in developing and implementing AI-based bio-signal monitoring systems in early warning system, including data acquisition strategies, data quality and standardization, interpretability and explainability, validation and regulatory approval, integration into clinical workflows, and ethical and legal considerations. Addressing these challenges requires a multidisciplinary approach involving close collaboration between healthcare professionals, data scientists, engineers, and other stakeholders. Future research should focus on developing advanced data fusion techniques, personalized adaptive models, real-time and continuous monitoring, explainable and reliable AI, and regulatory and ethical frameworks. By addressing these challenges and opportunities, the integration of AI and bio-signals into early warning systems can enhance patient monitoring and clinical decision support, ultimately improving healthcare quality and safety. In conclusion, integrating AI and bio-signals into the early warning system represents a promising approach to improve patient care outcomes and support clinical decision-making. As research in this field continues to evolve, it is crucial to develop safe, effective, and ethically responsible solutions that can be seamlessly integrated into clinical practice, harnessing the power of innovative technology to enhance patient care and improve individual and population health and well-being.

Introduction
According to 2021 statistics, coronary artery disease accounts for more than 300,000 deaths in the United States. This remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide. Many hospitals are implementing proactive interventions to prevent such clinical deterioration, as early recognition can contribute to reducing patient mortality. Multiple studies have demonstrated that patients typically experience physiological instability prior to clinical deterioration. Precursor warning signals, such as increased respiratory rate or decreased blood pressure, often precede severe illness, and early recognition of these signs presents a crucial opportunity to reduce mortality. Furthermore, both prospective and retrospective reviews, along with recent reports from the national confidential enquiry into patient outcome and death (NCEPOD), indicate that evidence of clinical deterioration appears several hours before serious adverse events (SAEs) such as cardiac arrest, death, and intensive care unit admission.
The concept of early warning score (EWS) was introduced in 1997 to minimize clinical deterioration. Subsequently, various EWS variants have been developed to support clinicians in recognizing early signs of physiological deterioration, enabling timely intervention and more intensive care provision. EWS enables healthcare professionals to simultaneously monitor and respond to multiple parameters, facilitating early detection and intervention of patient deterioration through vital signs changes. Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of EWS in reducing intensive care unit transfers and mortality rates, leading to its widespread international adoption across various healthcare settings.
Despite the proven effectiveness and extensive implementation of EWS, existing systems exhibit limitations in their capacity to effectively detect and respond to patient deterioration. Notable limitations include: (1) passive data collection and omission; (2) discontinuous monitoring; and (3) single-parameter systems that inadequately reflect multidimensional characteristics.
Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) has been leading the biomedical field in various disease detection applications. The integration of AI technology with bio-signals provides a significant breakthrough in medical innovation, transforming methodologies in medical diagnosis and treatment management. Bio-signals such as electroencephalography (EEG) and electrocardiography (ECG) play crucial roles in medical diagnosis. EEG is utilized in neurological studies to monitor brain activity and evaluate epilepsy and cognitive states, while ECG is essential for detecting cardiac abnormalities such as arrhythmias, which can be life-threatening if not immediately identified. The development of wearable and non-invasive biosensors has enabled the collection of various physiological signals, including EEG and ECG, with enhanced user-friendliness and high temporal resolution. Combining these bio-signals with AI and machine learning (ML) algorithms can provide valuable real-time information about patient health status, potentially preventing manual errors inherent in traditional EWS and facilitating early detection of subtle health deterioration indicators. Furthermore, the advancement of deep learning (DL) techniques, with their ability to automatically extract complex features from raw bio-signal data, has significantly enhanced predictive capabilities in EWS, enabling more accurate patient risk stratification and potentially reducing mortality rates through earlier interventions.
In 2012, researchers at the University of Chicago developed the electronic cardiac arrest risk triage (eCART), a ML-based scoring system. While eCART and its variants have demonstrated potential as complementary systems to EWS, eCART’s primary focus on cardiac arrest limits its applicability to diverse conditions and situations. Current systems warrant improvement to overcome these limitations and enable more precise and rapid monitoring.
This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current status and future directions of EWS and AI-based bio-signal monitoring for more effective early alert systems. First, we will discuss the role, types, and overview of EWS in current clinical workflows. Second, we will introduce AI-based bio-signal early prediction systems and their types, examining various clinical applications of AI-based bio-signal monitoring systems. Third, we will review strategies and possibilities for utilizing machine learning systems in EWS. Finally, we will discuss the challenges and opportunities for integrating AI-based bio-signal monitoring systems with EWS, as well as future research directions.

Overview of EWS
Early Warning Systems have been widely adopted in healthcare settings globally, with hospitals establishing Rapid Response Teams (RRT) to identify patients at risk of clinical deterioration and improve quality of care. EWS facilitates rapid identification of patients at risk of future deterioration by integrating objective measurements of clinical status. The EWS concept, initiated in the late 1990s, utilized five physiological parameters based on expert opinion: (1) pulse rate, (2) respiratory rate, (3) temperature, (4) systolic blood pressure, and (5) mental status. In 2001, the modified early warning score (MEWS) emerged as one of the most frequently cited models. As presented in Table 1,various other EWS exist, and these scoring systems have demonstrated accuracy in predicting cardiac arrest and mortality. One of the most prominent EWS is the national early warning score (NEWS), developed by the royal college of physicians, London. In 2017, NEWS2 was introduced as an upgrade to the original NEWS, improving accuracy based on a large validation database encompassing diverse patient types and enhancing recognition of potential sepsis.
Additional specialized EWS variants have been developed and implemented for specific purposes, including the pediatric early warning score (PEWS) for pediatric patients, the VitalPAC early warning score (VIEWS) integrated with electronic health records (EHR), the Hamilton early warning score (HEWS) focusing on admission risk assessment, the triage early warning score (TREWS) for risk assessment at emergency department triage, and the modified early obstetric warning score (MEOWS) reflecting the unique physiological changes during pregnancy and postpartum periods.

Types of representative early warning systems
Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS): Introduced in 2001, MEWS utilizes five physiological indicators: systolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, and consciousness level. Validation studies demonstrated that scores of 5 or higher were associated with a 5.4-fold increase in mortality risk.
National Early Warning Score (NEWS/NEWS2): Developed in 2012 by the Royal College of Physicians for standardization across the NHS, this system evaluates six physiological parameters and oxygen supplementation status. The 2017 revision, NEWS2, introduced new oxygen saturation criteria for patients with chronic respiratory failure.
Pediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS): Designed to account for the unique physiological characteristics of pediatric patients, this system evaluates age-specific vital sign criteria, capillary refill time, and respiratory effort. Multiple versions exist, including Brighton PEWS and Bedside PEWS.
Modified Early Obstetric Warning Score (MEOWS): Developed to reflect the distinct physiological changes during pregnancy and postpartum periods. Beyond standard vital signs, it incorporates obstetric indicators such as fetal heart rate and uterine contractions, demonstrating 89% sensitivity in identifying patients requiring urgent intervention.
Quick Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA): Developed during the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis, this simplified assessment tool rapidly evaluates sepsis risk using three criteria: altered mental status, systolic blood pressure ≤ 100 mmHg, and respiratory rate ≥ 22/min.
VitalPAC Early Warning Score (VIEWS): An electronic health record-integrated system that automatically collects and calculates indicators similar to NEWS. It demonstrates high accuracy in predicting 24-h mortality, cardiac arrest, and unplanned intensive care unit admission.
Electronic Cardiac Arrest Risk Triage (eCART): The ML-based prediction model utilizes comprehensive clinical data including vital signs, laboratory results, and medication information. It demonstrates superior performance in cardiac arrest prediction compared to traditional early warning scores..
Hamilton Early Warning Score (HEWS): Focusing on admission risk assessment, this system evaluates respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, and consciousness level. It demonstrates excellent performance with an AUROC of 0.81 in predicting composite adverse outcomes.
Triage Early Warning Score (TREWS): Developed for risk assessment at emergency department triage, this system distinctively includes age as a key predictor. It achieved exceptional accuracy with an AUROC of 0.95 in predicting in-hospital mortality.

Trends in AI-based bio-signal approach
AI is driving innovation across diverse fields. ML, as a subset of AI, employs algorithms to empirically learn patterns from data. ML can extend beyond traditional statistical approaches by identifying non-linear relationships and high-dimensional interactions between multiple variables that might be challenging to detect using conventional statistical methods. Currently, AI is leading the medical field in various disease detection applications. The outlook for medical AI has matured significantly and is poised to extensively restructure the medical field. Recent research has utilized diverse and rich data sources beyond image classification, including molecular information, natural language, and medical signals such as EEG data and multimodal data. Studies have demonstrated that AI applications can extract enhanced information from bio-signals.
Numerous studies have demonstrated the potential of AI and bio-signals to significantly improve the accuracy of early disease diagnosis and prognosis prediction. Machine learning algorithms can be utilized to detect subtle changes in bio-signals and link them to early disease indicators. As demonstrated in Table 2,research combining bio-signals such as EEG and ECG with machine learning has shown promising results in early diagnosis of various conditions, including epilepsy, stroke, myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, and sepsis.
The integration of AI technology and bio-signals demonstrates potential to overcome the limitations of existing EWS and enable more accurate and rapid prediction of patient conditions. AI-based bio-signal analysis can detect subtle physiological changes in patients that might be difficult to capture through vital signs alone, potentially aiding in the early prediction of clinical deterioration.

Feasible bio-signals and their clinical implementation using AI
Electroencephalography (EEG)
Electrocardiography (ECG)
EEG is a medical imaging technique that reads scalp electrical activity generated by brain structures, enabling real-time monitoring of brain function changes. Despite limited spatial resolution, EEG demonstrates considerable potential for both patients and healthy individuals due to its non-invasive nature and excellent temporal resolution. EEG is utilized in diagnosing and monitoring various cerebrovascular conditions, including epilepsy, stroke, and comatose states. Recent AI advancements, when combined with EEG signals, demonstrate potential for early prediction of various conditions. One study showed that EEG with DL-based models could provide faster and more reliable diagnosis for post-stroke assessment, enabling timely interventions. Another study proposed an efficient automatic seizure detection method using EEG and BiLSTM-based neural networks, achieving 98.09% sensitivity and 98.69% specificity, showing promising potential for clinical practice. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of using NEWS and MEWS for predicting outcomes in stroke patients. However, recent advances in AI technologies when combined with EEG demonstrate promise in capturing multidimensional characteristics and overcoming the limitations of discontinuous monitoring. This approach suggests that integrating EEG with conventional EWS could enable more effective early intervention for patients with stroke and epilepsy.
ECG is a physiological signal and standard examination measuring electrical activity that describes heart muscle movement, widely used in hospitals as a quality-oriented and cost-effective diagnostic method. These advantages make it particularly useful in diagnosing various cardiac conditions including arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, and sepsis. Its sensitivity to cardiac rhythm changes makes it valuable for early detection of patient condition changes. Recent AI advances have significantly impacted ECG analysis. According to Demilade A. et al., AI-based ECG analysis, including DL and machine learning, aids in detecting cardiovascular diseases in digital ECGs. Paper-based ECGs can be converted to digital models by AI for better analysis, helping physicians save time and provide better support. One study utilized ECG and convolutional neural network (CNN)-IGRU based models for detecting premature ventricular contractions (PVC), demonstrating high model performance with 98.3% and 97.9% accuracy on the MIT-BIH arrhythmia database and China Physiological Signal Challenge 2018 database, respectively. Another study successfully demonstrated myocardial infarction classification using CNN-BILSTM neural network models with ECG, showing potential for early intervention in cardiac deterioration or heart attacks.
Other potential bio-signals
ECG has also shown promising potential in sepsis diagnosis. Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by dysregulated host response to infection, is a major global medical concern. While sepsis screening and diagnosis require bio-signals and blood tests, bio-signals are measured at intervals by medical staff, and blood tests require infrastructure for collection and analysis, making real-time monitoring of sepsis onset challenging in hospitals. One study integrated ECG using 12-lead, 6-lead, and single leads with DL-based models to provide reasonable performance in sepsis screening. These various AI-based applications demonstrate ECG’s potential applicability not only to cardiac diseases but also to various conditions including sepsis.

Comparison between EWS models and AI based bio-signal monitoring
AI-based bio-signal monitoring systems and traditional EWS have distinct advantages in patient care. Traditional EWS are widely implemented in hospital settings, allowing healthcare providers to use them with familiarity, enabling rapid assessment with simple vital parameters without additional equipment, and featuring customized systems for specific patient populations (PEWS, MEOWS). In contrast, AI-based systems can detect complex bio-signal patterns, enable real-time monitoring, and possess the potential for continuous performance improvement through data learning. Quantitative comparisons demonstrate that AI-based models achieve high accuracy rates exceeding 90% in detecting epilepsy (98.69%), stroke (90.17–99.75%), arrhythmia (98.3–98.57%), and sepsis (95%), whereas traditional EWS generally show lower metrics (NEWS: AUROC 0.89, VIEWS: AUROC 0.888) with the exception of TREWS (AUROC 0.95). Regarding sensitivity, AI-based models (epilepsy 98.09%, stroke 89.86%, arrhythmia 98.4%) outperform traditional systems such as PEWS (78%) and MEOWS (89%). In conclusion, AI-based bio-signal monitoring demonstrates superior performance in accuracy, sensitivity, and early detection capabilities compared to traditional EWS, suggesting particular effectiveness in disease-specific detection.

Data acquisition strategies for AI implementation in future
The acquisition and storage of high-quality data are essential for AI-based bio-signal analysis. In current ward environments, nurses typically measure patients’ vital signs at a few hour intervals during their rounds. While these discrete measurements are useful for momentary status assessments, they present limitations for long-term trend analysis and AI model development.

However, this transformation must be implemented without significant disruption to existing clinical workflows. While continuous 24-h monitoring for all patients would be theoretically ideal, practical limitations exist regarding equipment costs, potential restrictions on patients’ daily activities, and associated discomfort. Therefore, a gradual and pragmatic approach is warranted. The future early warning system should maintain the current nurse rounds system while incorporating 5–10 min of automated continuous data collection during each visit. This approach maintains the current frequency of intermittent measurements while acquiring richer time-series data during each assessment period. Such a methodology offers the advantage of gradually accumulating necessary data for AI analysis without substantially disrupting existing workflows.
The collected data should be transmitted to and securely stored in the hospital’s central server for subsequent analysis. This approach represents a practical solution for obtaining the time-series data necessary for AI analysis while preserving current clinical practices. Through this implementation strategy, healthcare facilities can effectively balance the requirements for advanced AI-driven monitoring with the practical constraints of clinical operations.

Challenges and future directions
AI systems based on bio-signals demonstrate significant potential for advancing inpatient monitoring and early warning systems beyond the limitations of conventional EWS. However, several critical challenges must be addressed before implementing these systems in clinical settings.

Verification of the role and utility of additional bio-signals
A key consideration is to review the role of additional bio-signals within the EWS protocol and repeatedly validate their utility. If current EWS sufficiently predicts most cases of acute deterioration, measuring additional bio-signals, which requires significant cost and resources, may be unnecessary. However, despite widespread utilization, there remains a lack of consensus on what constitutes an ideal early warning score system and limited research on its clinical utility. Furthermore, some studies have found that most EWS have inadequate design and implementation, resulting in minimal clinical effect. To address these limitations of existing EWS systems, additional bio-signals could be integrated into current systems to contribute to higher prediction rates of acute deterioration. Particularly in specific conditions such as stroke, where early detection is difficult with conventional EWS, additional bio-signal monitoring such as EEG has shown potential to improve patient safety. Similarly, the added value of bio-signal-based early warning must be demonstrated through prospective clinical studies, especially evaluating the impact on early detection of acute deterioration, timing of interventions, and improvement of patient outcomes. Therefore, it is essential to establish clear guidelines on which bio-signals should be measured for specific diseases and patient populations, and to develop predictive models that integrate existing EWS indicators with bio-signals.

Development of user-friendly bio-signal measurement protocols and monitoring equipment
A significant challenge for continuous bio-signal monitoring in ward environments is improving the portability and wearability of monitoring equipment. Current bulky and wired measurement devices impede reliable data collection while maintaining patients’ daily activities. To address these issues, patient-friendly wearable/patch-type bio-signal measurement devices with wireless connectivity and miniaturization are necessary. The first consideration for patient-friendly bio-signal measurement devices is ensuring long-term reliability and durability of sensors attached to the skin. This can minimize signal noise due to adhesion and time delays in various environmental conditions and improve wearability by considering bio-compatibility and breathability. Second, bio-signal measurement protocols and equipment should be developed to match the characteristics and requirements of hospitalized patients. This is important for selecting optimal measurement locations and methods considering the patient’s condition, ward environment, and daily activities. Finally, personalizing monitoring parameters and alarm thresholds according to individual physiological baselines is also a key factor to consider in building an effective bio-signal monitoring system.

Real-time data acquisition and computational/hardware constraints
The transition of data acquisition is a significant challenge for real-time bio-signal monitoring. Continuous 24-h monitoring for all patients is burdensome in terms of cost and patient convenience, while computational and hardware constraints may delay implementation in actual clinical environments. In particular, recent advances in DL algorithms may increase computational complexity and consume substantial hardware resources. Accordingly, it is necessary to discuss computational/hardware constraints and examine strategies to overcome them.

Computational constraints
Continuous bio-signal monitoring systems face several computational constraints including real-time processing speed, data processing complexity, and memory limitations. Several studies have observed delays in algorithmic processing for real-time ECG, EEG, and PPG data. These issues demonstrate constraints in detecting key features of bio-signals and recognizing changes. Another study showed the feasibility of identifying cardiac rhythms through portable ECG technology but still revealed limitations in continuous arrhythmia monitoring. While the latest DL models have demonstrated innovation in various fields including healthcare, most DL models developed today are full-precision (FP) models that represent weights and activations as 32-bit parameters. However, FP operations are more costly than low-precision (LP) operations in terms of computational cost and memory consumption, making them difficult to deploy on resource-constrained devices such as mobile phones. Similarly, running efficient and stable DL architectures on mobile devices to process noisy and complex sensor data is likely to be highly resource-intensive. In particular, multimodal sensing based on multiple biosignals remains challenging because it includes limitations in power consumption, data transmission rates, and bandwidth.

Hardware constraints
Real-time bio-signal monitoring systems have hardware constraints such as sensor signal quality, energy efficiency, and wireless transmission bottlenecks. These hardware constraints can also delay the integration of real-time bio-signal collection. For example, the performance of QRS complex detection algorithms using ECG in dynamic environments decreased significantly, increasing errors in real-time arrhythmia diagnosis. Additionally, optimizing the size and weight of devices for long-term continuous monitoring in clinical settings is a critical issue, as there is generally a trade-off between form factor and battery capacity. Therefore, smaller batteries may reduce operating time, constraining continuous monitoring. Constraints due to wireless transmission bottlenecks also affect early detection of patient conditions. Some wireless communications such as ZigBee can extend communication range up to 1500 m in the absence of obstacles and maintain longer battery life, but may have limitations in simultaneous measurement and data buffering due to low data transmission rates.

Strategies to overcome computational and hardware limitations
Various technical strategies are being developed to overcome the constraints of bio-signal monitoring systems, and the introduction of real-time bio-signals can be considered in a gradual transition approach. Several recent studies show notable results for overcoming various computational and hardware limitations.
For real-time bio-signal analysis, lightweight AI model design is crucial, and one approach to overcome this is model quantization. Model quantization is the process of converting FP models into faster and lighter models by reducing the precision at which parameters are represented to a lower number of bits. For this purpose, various studies are being conducted to analyze 8-bit quantization, and have shown that in extreme cases, any parameter precision including 1-bit quantization can be used. These low-bit precision models can achieve results close to FP accuracy when considering large-scale networks.
Another method for lightweight AI model design is utilizing Knowledge Distillation (KD). The general idea with KD is that the student’s learning process is guided by the teacher, who acts as an external agent transferring knowledge about the subject to the student. This approach is frequently used in model compression, where a lightweight student model learns to mimic the behavior of a complex teacher model, providing a compact solution within acceptable performance parameters.
Pruning is also a good strategy for lightweight AI model design. The process of neuronal deactivation or synaptic pruning that occurs in the actual brain minimizes the memory and energy costs required to process external information. Pruning strategies in AI models work similarly, extracting redundant information after model training, allowing for a reduction in the total number of connections without significantly affecting final performance. Thus, pruning strategies compress the network by tearing away certain parts.
Additionally, one study for detecting EEG epileptic spikes proposed a DL architecture that could minimize computational resources, demonstrating suitability for identifying real-time epileptic spikes in medical environments. The proposed 1D-ResCNN model, when implemented on a C2000 microcontroller, showed performance metrics including inference time of 30 ms per sample, latency of 50 ms, power consumption of 5W, memory usage of 150 MB, and model size of 2 MB, demonstrating the ability to detect seizures in near real-time, support immediate intervention in emergency situations with fast latency, and enable continuous patient monitoring with minimal resources. In this manner, to implement real-time bio-signal monitoring systems that can overcome computational and hardware constraints, they should be designed considering energy efficiency, wireless communication speed, computational complexity, memory capacity, and patient wearability.
To accelerate the clinical application of real-time bio-signal monitoring systems, data collection strategies should be considered alongside technical improvements. This approach includes maintaining existing nurse rounds while continuously measuring bio-signals for specific periods during each visit. This method minimizes computational and hardware constraints and can be established as a system that complements the prediction of patient clinical deterioration through additional continuous data. Actual implementation should begin with pilot continuous monitoring programs for high-risk patient groups and gradually expand based on accumulated experience and lessons learned. In this process, exploring ways to minimize nursing workload while improving efficiency allows for the gradual accumulation of data necessary for AI without significantly disrupting nursing workflow.

AI acceptance in clinical settings and interpretability
The clinical interpretation and utilization of AI-based risk prediction results require careful consideration. While bio-signal-based AI models excel at detecting subtle pattern changes in vast datasets, medical professionals’ expertise remains essential for interpreting these results in a clinical context. The introduction of AI-based EWS systems in healthcare has the potential to improve patient monitoring and clinical decision-making, but successful implementation largely depends on acceptance and trust in clinical settings, as well as the interpretability of decisions made by AI systems. Therefore, it is necessary to establish guidelines and educational programs that enable healthcare providers to critically evaluate AI predictions and appropriately integrate them into clinical decision-making processes. Strategies that facilitate the acceptance of AI-based EWS systems in clinical settings through eXplainable AI (XAI) methodologies should be considered.

AI education programs for healthcare professionals should reflect AI literacy elements including technical understanding, critical evaluation, and practical application. Programs and workshops that provide practical opportunities to interpret and use AI-based EWS in real clinical situations can also be helpful. According to one study, customized educational programs should comprehensively cover the full spectrum from basic algorithm concepts to actual clinical cases, while another study reported that simulation-based training improves application capabilities in real situations. Another study emphasized that more effective results can be obtained when various healthcare professionals learn together through a multidisciplinary team approach, and a recent study suggested that as AI systems continuously evolve, medical staff should understand the changes and performance characteristics of the latest algorithms through regular update training.
Several key elements are needed to build clinicians’ trust in automated decision support systems. One study revealed that transparency of AI systems, particularly the ability to clearly explain the basis for specific recommendations, is essential for improving reliability, while another study reported that a collaborative design approach in which clinicians participate in AI system design from the early development stages reflects actual clinical needs and increases acceptability. Another study emphasized that demonstrable performance improvements in real clinical environments are a key factor in enhancing reliability, and an additional study argued that trust improves when AI systems leave final decision-making authority to medical staff and play a supportive role. Therefore, when these elements are implemented integratively, trust in AI-based decision support systems in clinical settings will be formed, enabling long-term acceptance.

Interpretability of decision-making
Local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME)
Shapley additive exPlanations (SHAP)
Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) is a model-independent explanation method developed to explain individual predictions of complex AI models, generating a simple interpretable model that approximates the original model around a specific prediction instance. This method derives explanations in a human-understandable form by generating samples in the input space and observing the predictions of the original model. Additionally, LIME is applicable to various data types including text, images, and tabular data, and has high computational efficiency, providing fast local explanations. However, due to its local focus, it may have limitations in consistency when applied to multiple datasets or larger systems.
Layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP)
Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is a method that calculates the contribution of each feature to a model’s output based on Shapley values from game theory, integrating various existing explanation techniques (LIME, DeepLIFT, etc.) to provide intuitive interpretations of complex machine learning models’ prediction results. It satisfies three properties: local accuracy, missingness, and consistency, and provides both local interpretations for individual predictions and global interpretations for overall model behavior. It also enhances reliability through mathematically consistent explanations and helps with feature selection, bias detection, and model fairness improvement. However, SHAP has the limitation that its high computational cost may cause difficulties in application in real-time environments.
Class activation map (CAM)
LRP (Layer-wise Relevance Propagation) is a method that visualizes the contribution of each input feature to prediction results by tracking layer-wise contributions in deep neural networks, operating by back-propagating relevance scores from the network’s output to the input layer. This method can visualize pixel-level relevance for complex inputs such as high-dimensional image data, making it particularly useful for understanding the decision-making processes of DL models in medical image analysis and providing more specific clinical explanations. While it can be expanded to apply to complex deep neural networks, computational costs may be an important consideration in architecture and large-scale datasets.
Hybrid approaches
Class Activation Map (CAM) is a method proposed to visualize the decision-making process of image classification tasks in CNNs. It provides visual explanations by highlighting important input regions considered by the model when making predictions in heatmap form, combining prediction scores for specific classes with feature maps from the last convolutional layer. This method is effectively utilized for tasks such as object location identification or lesion site identification in medical images, and is particularly important for verifying whether CNN models focus on the correct visual features. However, CAM requires specific network structures and has limitations in direct application to pre-trained models. Grad-CAM overcomes the limitations of existing CAM by utilizing gradient information without changing the model structure, but considerations for multiple object distinction and resolution limitations should be taken into account.
Integration in real clinical settings
In clinical environments, hybrid approaches combining multiple XAI techniques can be effective. In particular, hybrid approaches for real-time clinical application seek balance between computational efficiency and interpretative accuracy. As shown in several studies, including diabetic retinopathy diagnosis research, a strategy of flexibly applying appropriate interpretation methods depending on the situation is effective in clinical environments. For example, computationally efficient Grad-CAM can be used for initial screening requiring quick decision-making, while more sophisticated SHAP or integrated gradient methods can be applied to uncertain or complex cases. Additionally, hybrid approaches combining Grad-CAM and LRP can provide enhanced performance in terms of complexity through noise reduction and clearer visualization, so such approaches can also be considered. As such, the strategy of implementing interpretable decision support systems that naturally integrate into clinical workflows by combining various interpretation methods through interactive designs reflecting medical staff feedback and optimized algorithms capable of generating explanations at speeds suitable for real-time patient monitoring is very promising.
Multimodal bio-signal-based early warning systems in real clinical environments can improve the accuracy and reliability of patient deterioration predictions by integrating various interpretability models. One study utilized LRP to visualize important temporal patterns in ECG analysis, while another study showed that eXplainable AI Early Warning Score (XAI-EWS) demonstrated the transparency of the decision-making process of EHR data-based prediction systems through SHAP values. A study proposing a contextual explanation model reflecting clinicians’ requirements showed that the proposed hierarchical interpretation framework could identify bio-signals affecting overall predictions with SHAP values and identify important time intervals within each signal. Similarly, various studies have verified that XAI systems make medically valid decisions. These studies show that by integratively utilizing various interpretability models in clinical environments, the decision-making transparency of AI systems can be enhanced, medical staff trust can be built, and ultimately the quality of patient care can be improved.

Regulatory framework
The introduction of AI-based EWS systems in clinical settings requires a thorough approach to ethical considerations and regulatory compliance. Because these systems directly impact patient safety and medical decision-making, compliance with relevant regulatory agency guidelines is essential.

FDA regulatory framework
In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates AI/ML-based Early Warning Systems (EWS) as Software as Medical Device (SaMD). According to the Action Plan for AI/ML-based medical devices announced in 2021, the FDA emphasizes a risk-based approach, Good Machine Learning Practice, patient-centered approach, and performance evaluation in real situations. AI-based EWS must undergo 510(k) or De Novo approval processes as medical devices, with algorithm performance (sensitivity, specificity, etc.), validity of decision thresholds, representativeness of datasets, and explainability of algorithms being evaluated. Additionally, the FDA requires a comprehensive surveillance system for AI/ML-based medical devices, including post-marketing monitoring, performance change tracking, regular model update plans, and adverse event reporting mechanisms.

EU medical device regulatory framework
In the European Union (EU), the Medical Device Regulation (MDR), fully implemented since May 2021, serves as the main regulatory framework for AI-based EWS. According to the MDR, patient monitoring and alarm systems are generally classified as Class IIa or IIb based on risk level and require evaluation by a Notified Body. For AI-based systems, algorithm transparency, performance consistency, data quality, and cybersecurity are particularly important evaluation factors. Additionally, the EU AI Act, agreed upon in 2023, classifies AI systems in the medical field as “high-risk” categories, applying additional regulatory requirements including the establishment of risk management systems, ensuring human supervision of system decisions, technical documentation, and transparency requirements. The EU regulatory framework takes a comprehensive approach to ensure the safety and performance of medical AI systems throughout their entire lifecycle.

Strategies for regulatory compliance
AI-based clinical decision support tools such as multimodal digitized EWS systems are likely to fall within the scope of FDA and EU medical device regulations, with more stringent regulations applying particularly when real-time patient monitoring and early warning functions are included. If such systems are classified as medical devices, patient safety and regulatory decisions are made according to a risk-based approach. Therefore, developers should ensure transparency of regulatory review through systematic lifecycle management including development to continuous monitoring, demonstration of safety and effectiveness through thorough performance validation in various clinical environments, establishment of clear change management protocols for AI algorithm updates and improvements, and thorough documentation of development processes, algorithm design, data sources, validation methods, and results. For the successful implementation and clinical application of multimodal digitized EWS systems, it is essential to integrate these ethical considerations and regulatory requirements from the early development stages, thereby prioritizing patient safety while fully utilizing the benefits of innovative technology.

Data quality management and AI system decision support interface
Quality management and standardization of collected bio-signal data is an important challenge. Development of preprocessing techniques is necessary to minimize variability due to sensor characteristics and filter noise and anomalous data. Standardization of data formats and protocols should precede the integration of heterogeneous data generated in various hospital departments and institutions. On this foundation, more accurate early warning models can be developed through integrated analysis of accumulated bio-signal big data and clinical information. Multi-institutional collaborative research is essential to overcome the limitations of single-institution data and develop generalizable models, and emphasis should be placed on developing personalized risk prediction models that integrate various parameters including demographic characteristics, medical history, laboratory results, and medication information.
Development of interfaces and workflows that support collaborative decision-making between healthcare providers and AI systems is necessary. The system should be designed to intuitively visualize patient status and AI predictions while providing prompt alerts and response options when risk indicators appear. Additionally, it is essential to build a closed-loop learning system that continuously updates and improves model performance based on healthcare provider feedback.

Conclusion
This review has examined the current status of EWS and AI-based bio-signal monitoring, discussing integration strategies for ML-based multimodal bio-signals and their potential for future EWS to overcome the limitations of conventional early warning systems in predicting acute deterioration among hospitalized patients. Research utilizing bio-signals such as ECG and EEG, combined with machine learning techniques, has demonstrated promising early prediction performance across various acute conditions, including sepsis, cardiac arrest, and stroke. The value of additional bio-signal monitoring has been particularly evident in conditions such as stroke, where detection through conventional EWS has proven challenging.
However, successful implementation of AI-based early warning systems utilizing multimodal bio-signals requires concurrent consideration of several factors: (1) development of patient-friendly measurement devices; (2) construction of personalized prediction models; (3) gradual transition of data collection methods, (4) integration with existing medical workflows. Notably, considering patient comfort and cost implications, constraints exist in transitioning from current intermittent manual measurements to fully automated systems. Therefore, development should focus on integrating multimodal bio-signals and AI with existing EWS through semi-continuous measurements, thereby securing sufficient data for AI analysis while minimizing the burden on nursing staff.
The bio-signal-based early warning system is expected to significantly contribute to improving inpatient safety and healthcare quality through future large-scale clinical trials validating its effectiveness, development of generalizable models through multi-institutional collaboration, and establishment of healthcare provider-AI collaborative frameworks. Achieving this requires developing optimal bio-signal utilization strategies and institutional foundations through public discourse and consensus-building among healthcare providers, AI researchers, healthcare policy makers, and patients.
In conclusion, this review paper presents a future paradigm for integrating bio-signals and AI technology to advance existing EWS frameworks. However, for such innovation to successfully integrate into clinical practice and enhance patient safety, technological advancement must be supported by in-depth discussion of ethical and institutional challenges and social consensus. The authors anticipate that this review will serve as a guide for future research and policy development in this field.
