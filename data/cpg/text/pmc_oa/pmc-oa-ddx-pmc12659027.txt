Evaluation of the relative benefits of FFR-guided, angiography-guided CR or culprit-only revascularization in patients with multivessel coronary disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Background
At present, the benefits associated with fractional flow reserve (FFR)-guided complete revascularization (CR) for instances of non-culprit stenosis in patients of coronary artery disease (CAD) affected by multivessel disease (MVD) remain poorly understood. This systematic review and meta-analysis was undertaken to clarify the cardiovascular benefits associated with FFR-guided and angiography-guided CR in patients with CAD and MVD.
Methods
The PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases were searched to locate randomized control trials (RCTs) comparing FFR-guided CR with angiography-guided CR or culprit-only percutaneous intervention (PCI). The primary outcome is major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), as well as secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, recurrent myocardial infarction (MI) incidence. Primary and secondary outcomes were compared among groups using DerSimonian and Laird random-effects models.
Results
Ten RCTs enrolling 7249 participants were included. No significant differences were found between FFR- and angiography-guided CR use in patients with CAD and MVD in terms of MACE (RR: 0.88, 95%CI: 0.69–1.12, P = 0.302), cardiac mortality (RR: 0.90, 95%CI: 0.45–1.80. P = 1.000), recurrent MI (RR: 0.83, 95%CI: 0.55–1.25, P = 0.376), repeat revascularization (RR: 0.93, 95%CI: 0.71–1.21, P = 0.574), or target lesion revascularization (TLR; RR: 0.90, 95%CI: 0.33–2.48 P = 0.840). All-cause mortality was similar between the groups (RR: 1.00, 95%CI: 0.57–1.75, P = 1.000). Relative to culprit-only PCI, FFR-guided CR was associated with reduced risk of repeat revascularization (RR: 0.50, 95%CI: 0.37–0.68, P < 0.001) and TLR (RR: 0.31, 95%CI: 0.2–0.48, P < 0.001), while MACE incidence did not differ significantly between the two groups (RR: 0.72, 95%CI: 0.51–1.01, P = 0.006), nor did all-cause mortality (RR: 1.09, 95%CI: 0.84–1.42, P = 0.526), cardiac mortality (RR: 0.82, 95%CI: 0.55–1.21, P = 0.312), or recurrent MI (RR: 0.85, 95%CI: 0.73–1.24, P = 0.713).
Conclusion
FFR-guided CR was found to linked with reduced repeat revascularization and TLR in individuals with CAD and MVD in comparison with culprit-only PCI. While no significant difference was detected between FFR-guided and angiography-guided CR procedures for the analyzed cardiovascular outcomes, a lower absolute number of adverse events was noted with FFR-guided CR. These findings indicate that the FFR-guided CR of non-culprit vessels may help reduce the need for the implantation of stents in patients with CAD and MVD without any adverse impact on their prognostic outcomes.

Introduction
Multivessel disease (MVD) affects roughly half of all acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients and is associated with poor prognostic outcomes. In recent meta-analyses pooling data from multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs), complete revascularization (CR) was shown to be superior to culprit-only percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) approaches in MVD as a means of reducing major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) incidence and achieving a better prognosis. Current ESC guidelines for managing ACS recommend CR during the index PCI or a procedure performed in stages with 45 days in patients with ST- and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI and NSTEMI).
Fractional flow reserve (FFR)-guided coronary revascularization procedures targeting significant non-culprit stenoses have been linked to improvements in patient clinical outcomes relative to angiography-guided revascularization procedures. The actual benefits of FFR-guided CR for all non-culprit stenoses in coronary artery disease (CAD) and MVD patients, however, remain incompletely understood. In one prior meta-analysis comparing FFR- and angiography-guided multivessel PCI, both strategies were associated with comparable improvements in 1-year outcomes, but data pertaining to adverse event incidence was derived from just 3 RCTs enrolling patients with CAD and MVD. In addition, subgroup analyses included in the recent FRAME-AMI trial revealed inconsistencies when comparing STEMI and NSTEMI patients. The most optimal approach to guiding non-culprit lesion CR thus remains uncertain. As such, the present RCT-based meta-analysis was undertaken to assess the clinical benefits associated with FFR-guided versus angiography-guided CR for non-culprit lesions in CAD and MVD patients.

Methods

Search strategy
Searches were performed in the PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases, identifying publications as of 23 March 2024 with the search terms “angiography,” “fractional flow reserve,” “multivessel disease,” and “percutaneous coronary intervention” and no language restrictions. The goal of this systematic search was to identify RCTs that compared FFR-guided CR and angiography-guided CR or culprit-only PCI procedures in CAD and MVD patients. An updated search was performed on 13 June 2024 that led to the identification of an additional study. The references of publications were also manually searched, as was ClinicalTrials.gov, to facilitate the retrieval of any other relevant trials. The meta-analysis is reported follows the PRISMA guideline (Supplementary Material), and this study was registered in INPLASY (INPLASY record 202440042).

Study selection
RCTs comparing clinical benefits associated with culprit-only, FFR-guided CR, or angiography-guided CR in CAD and MVD patients were enrolled in this meta-analysis. No language restrictions were imposed. Trials that did not report FFR-guided CR or any relevant clinical outcome were excluded. If there were multiple studies based on the same trial, trials with the longest follow-ups were used.

Outcomes
the primary outcome in the present meta-analysis was the MACE incidence. MACE definitions in the included RCTs are presented in Table S1. The secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, recurrent myocardial infarction (MI) incidence (as per the Third Universal Definition of MI), the incidence of repeat revascularization (any repeated PCI or bypass surgery), and the incidence of target lesion revascularization (TLR; any PCI or bypass surgery-based revascularization performed after stent placement within the stent or 5 mm of the stent).

Data collection
Two researchers (Xuan-Yan Liu and Jing-Chao Sun) undertook independent extraction of data pertaining to clinical outcomes, study characteristics (CAD types, drug-eluting stent implantation, definition of CR, and duration of follow-up), and patient characteristics (FFR application conditions, interventional strategies, CR timing, the presence of three-vessel disease, procedural duration, and the contrast agent used) based on a data collection sheet that had been predesigned. A third researcher was consulted if disagreements arose.

Evaluation of the bias
The revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (ROB2) was utilized for bias assessment in the studies, based on the following items: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported outcome and selection of the reported result. Based on these criteria, two investigators (Xuan-Yan Liu and Xian Lin) independently assigned bias risks as low, high, or unclear, with any discrepant judgments being resolved through consensus or input from a third investigator (Bin-Hua Ye).

Statistical analyses
The DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model was used to separately comparing FFR-guided CR to either angiography-guided CR or culprit-only revascularization based on summary estimate risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Outcome assessments were performed with an intention-to-treat analysis, with a two-tailed P < 0.05 being considered significant. Skewed data given as medians and interquartile ranges were transformed into means with standard deviations. The I2 statistic was used to assess heterogeneity among studies, with low, moderate, and high heterogeneity levels defined by an I2 < 25%, 25–50%, and >50%, respectively. Funnel plot asymmetry related to publication bias was detected and corrected with the Trim-and-Fill method, whereas Egger’s tests were used to examine small-study effects. RCTs that had a disproportionate impact on the overall study results were detected through sensitivity analyses implemented with a leave-one-out approach. The benefits of FFR-guided CR in acute MI were also explored by separating studies into subgroups focused on MI and stable CAD patients, given that vasospasm has the potential to result in an overestimation of the severity of stenosis in the acute phase. As controversy persists regarding staged versus immediate CR procedures, subgroup analyses were also performed in which staged and immediate CR approaches were compared among patients who underwent FFR-guided CR. Subgroup analyses based on length of follow-up and FFR cutoff values were also performed. To investigate the influence of timing of FFR assessment, we also performed subgroup analysis based on whether FFR is applied during the index procedure (immediate FFR) and the index hospitalization (staged FFR). STATA v17 (STATA Corporation, TX, USA) was used to perform this meta-analysis.

Results

Search results
Initial searches returned 477 studies, with 97 of these excluded as duplicates. Examination of titles and abstracts led to the elimination of 344 studies, while 34 studies from the initial search and one manually retrieved trial underwent full-text review. Of these, 4 non-RCTs were excluded, as were 3 studies lacking outcomes of interest, 8 with ineligible participants or interventions, and 13 with duplicated data. The detailed search procedure is outlined in Figure S1.

Study characteristics
This study ultimately included 10 RCTs enrolling 7249 patients (12.5% for stable angina and 87.5% for ACS), with follow-up durations from 12 to 60 months. For details regarding the characteristics of the enrolled studies and patients, see Tables 1 and 2. Of these studies, six performed comparisons of FFR-guided CR and angiography-guided CR, while 4 compared FFR-guided CR and culprit-only PCI. Three studies (Nunen et al., Quintella et al., Rioufol et al.) enrolled stable CAD patients and compared FFR-guided with angiography-guided CR, while the remainder recruited MI patients. Most of these studies implanted stents in coronary arteries with an FFR ≤ 0.80, while two studies used an FFR threshold of < 0.75 for implantation. The degree of stenosis addressed via FFR varied markedly among studies. For instance, in the FAMOUS-NSTEMI trial, FFR was performed for all lesions with a stenosis diameter of ≥ 30%, whereas Quintella et al. employed FFR for all vessels with significant lesions but failed to clearly define the criteria used to identify these lesions, and all other RCTs performed FFR when visual inspection revealed ≥ 50% stenosis. FFR-guided CR was primarily conducted during the primary index procedure in four of these RCTs, while staged CR was performed in four RCTs, and two RCTs did not define the utilized CR strategy. The mean age of the analyzed individuals was >60 years, with most being male.

Risk of bias and publication bias analyses
The ROB2 tool was used to evaluate potential bias (Figure S2). 8 of these studies exhibited some risk of deviation from the intended interventions as a result of knowledge regarding participant allocation. The trial authored by Ghani et al. did not declare a randomization process, raising some concerns of bias. By imputing a study on the left, the Trim-and-Fill method did not show any significant difference between the result and the primary outcome, nor was there any apparent funnel plot asymmetry, indicating no evidence of publication bias was detected (Figure S3). Egger’s tests did not detect any small-study effects (P = 0.765).

Clinical outcomes
Relative to angiography-guided CR, FFR-guided CR was found to be associated with a lower but non-significant MACE incidence (15.7% vs. 17.8%, RR: 0.88, 95%CI: 0.69–1.12, P = 0.302, I2 = 49.11%, Fig. 1). No significant differences were found in terms of all-cause mortality (4.0% vs. 4.2%, RR: 1.00, 95%CI: 0.57–1.75, P = 1.000, I2 = 53.81%, Fig. 2), while there was a trend towards reduced rates of cardiac mortality (3.7% vs. 3.9%, RR: 0.90, 95%CI: 0.45–1.80, P = 0.766, I2 = 66.36%, Fig. 3), recurrent MI (5.6% vs. 6.7%, RR: 0.83, 95%CI: 0.55–1.25, P = 0.376, I2 = 57.05%, Fig. 3), repeat revascularization (9.6% vs. 10.4%, RR: 0.93, 95%CI: 0.71–1.21, P = 0.574, I2 = 36.58%, Fig. 3), and TLR (1.9% vs. 2.1%, RR: 0.90, 95%CI: 0.33–2.48, P = 0.840, I2 = 48.48%, Fig. 3) in patients receiving FFR-guided CR.

FFR-guided CR showed similar rates of MACE (17.8% vs. 23.3%, RR: 0.72, 95%CI: 0.51–1.01, P = 0.060, I2 = 78.02%, Fig. 1), all-cause mortality (7.2% vs. 6.0%, RR: 1.09, 95%CI: 0.84–1.42, P = 0.526, I2 = 0%, Fig. 2), cardiac mortality (3.1% vs. 3.4%, RR: 0.82, 95%CI: 0.55–1.21, P = 0.312, I2 = 0%, Fig. 3), and recurrent MI (7.0% vs. 7.6%, RR: 0.85, 95%CI: 0.73–1.24, P = 0.713, I2 = 0%, Fig. 3) to culprit-only PCI. Patients in the FFR-guided CR group did exhibit markedly reduced rates of repeat revascularization (9.7% vs. 19.6%, RR: 0.50, 95%CI: 0.37–0.68, P < 0.001, I2 = 52.59%, Fig. 3) and TLR (6.1% vs. 18.4%, RR: 0.31, 95%CI: 0.2–0.48, P < 0.001, I2 = 0%, Fig. 3).

Subgroup analyses
When subgroup analyses of different CAD types, the cutoff value, study duration, and staged CR were performed, results comparing FFR-guided and angiography-guided CR approaches were similar to the primary analysis. Relative to culprit-only PCI, immediate FFR-guided CR led to markedly reduced MACE incidence (15.6% vs. 39.2%, RR:0.52, 95%CI:0.39–0.69, Fig. 4), whereas a non-significant rate was observed for staged FFR-guided CR procedures (18.4% vs. 21.3%, RR:0.82, 95%CI:0.59–1.13, Fig. 4). The results of other subgroup analyses for studies in which culprit-only PCI served as the control treatment were not significant. The subgroup allocation of the timing of FFR measurement is consistent with the timing of CR, as well as the results are similar in the two subgroup analyses. The leave-one-out method of sensitivity analysis suggested a robustness in MACE between FFR-guided an angiography-guided CR (Figure S4). However, in the analysis comparing FFR-guided CR and culprit-only PCI, when the study by Ghani et al. or FULL REVASC trial were excluded in sequence, the outcomes changed to be positive, indicating the uncertain result (Figure S5).

Discussion
For this meta-analysis, 10 RCTs enrolling 7249 patients were included to explore outcomes associated with different approaches to CR among MVD patients. While the two approaches did not differ significantly in terms of MACE incidence or other secondary outcomes, the absolute numbers of these outcomes was lower among patients undergoing FFR-guided CR. Similar rates of all-cause mortality were seen in the FFR- and angiography-guided patient groups, while the FFR-guided CR group showed markedly reduced incidence of repeat revascularization and TLR, but no differences in MACE incidence, all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, or recurrent MI relative to culprit-only PCI.
In a prior network meta-analysis, FFR-guided CR was associated with increased incidence of all clinical endpoints other than in-stent thrombosis, although the differences were not significant. A Bayesian hierarchical network meta-analysis performed recently detected no marked reductions in all-cause or new MI when comparing patients undergoing FFR- or angiography-guided CR. While no significant differences were evident in the present study when directly comparing FFR- and angiography-guided CR, a tendency towards a reduction in MACE incidence was noted in the FFR-guided CR group. Another meta-analysis revealed that angiography-guided CR had a significant cardiovascular benefit for the composite endpoint, with this result being driven primarily by a significant reduction in MI incidence. Differences between these past studies and this study may be due to the limited number of studies focused on FFR-guided CR in these meta-analyses and the fact that just 1 RCT included direct comparisons of cardiovascular benefits between FFR- and angiography-guided CR.
Patients in three prior network meta-analyses focused exclusively on individuals with STEMI and MVD, whereas individuals with stable CAD and NSTEMI were included in the present analysis. While clinical data have confirmed that FFR-guided CR is superior in stable CAD, the efficacy of FFR in MI, which is associated with turbulence in microvascular coronary circulatory and plays a crucial role in coronary resistance regulation, is less certain. Extended transient global microcirculatory and hemodynamic dysfunction may lead to false elevations or reductions in FFR, and procedural outcomes in STEMI patients may thus be abnormal. Subgroup analyses were herein performed in which patients with different types of CAD were separated to assess the cardiovascular benefits associated with stable CAD and MI. In this analysis, stable CAD and MI were associated with similar outcomes. However, sensitivity analyses could not be conducted as only one relevant trial was included pertaining to FFR- and angiography-guided CR in STEMI patients.
STEMI patients are often regarded as high-risk individuals who exhibit an atherosclerotic plaque burden more significant than that for patients with stable CAD. In the PROSPECT trial, intravascular ultrasound imaging revealed that MACE-related non-culprit lesions were evident in cases of mild obstruction with enormous plaque burden, and complex plaque phenotypes were more likely to be detectable via optical coherence tomography in obstructive non-culprit lesions. Intravascular imaging analyses that can assess the plaque burden and fibroatheroma morphology free with being influenced by the coronary microcirculation have thus been proposed to be more relevant than FFR-based assessment in an acute care context. When data from the FLAVOUR trial were recently leveraged in a cohort study, however, similar outcomes were observed for FFR- and intravascular ultrasonography (IVUS)-guided CR among intermediate coronary stenosis patients. In another network meta-analysis, intravascular imaging-guided revascularization failed to outperform functionally guided revascularization for the analyzed clinical outcomes, with similar results in subgroup analyses of ACS, despite imaging-guided revascularization being ranked as the best approach in primary and subgroup analyses.
In a prior network meta-analysis, compared with culprit-only PCI, FFR-guided CR was found to be related to a reduction in MACE risk among STEMI patients with MVD, in contrast with the present results. The significant reduction in the article published by Elbadawi et al. was attributable to a reduction in revascularization rate, but without any additional benefits on other outcomes, including all-cause or cardiac mortality, or recurrent MI. In a Bayesian hierarchical network meta-analysis, a composite of all-cause mortality and MI recurrence was used to rank three CR strategies (FFR-guided CR vs. angiography-guided CR vs. culprit-only PCI), with FFR-guided CR having been ranked the lowest such that it may be associated with the lowest degree of cardiovascular benefit. This may be attributable to the improper estimation of lesion hemodynamics as a result of microvascular dysfunction in acute period. In addition, intravascular intervention performed with the FFR guidewire also has the potential to alter the stability of vulnerable plaques. However, when comparing FFR-guided CR vs. angiography-guided CR, in our subgroup analysis based on the timing of FFR, no significant difference was detected between immediate and staged FFR measurement. Moreover, in the comparison between FFR-guided CR and culprit-only PCI, immediate FFR showed a reduced risk of MACE than staged FFR. The subgroup analysis result of the timing of FFR measurement indicated that immediate FFR could be a safe strategy to guide CR although the robustness of conclusion may be weakened by limited included studies.

Study limitations
This study has multiple limitations. For one, the enrolled individuals consisted primarily of males over 60 years of age, such that the degree to which these findings can be generalized to other populations, including females, remains to be assessed. Secondly, because of the median follow-up duration is inconsistent in subgroups, the results of subgroup analysis may reflect the real-world situation with bias. Third, there were also differences among RCTs in terms of the non-culprit lesions evaluated via FFR, and heterogeneity was observed with respect to CR timing. While subgroup analyses were performed based on whether or not staged CR was performed, there were relatively few trials in the individual subgroups. Finally, MACE definitions also varied among studies such that a sensitivity analysis was conducted in which studies with consistent MACE definitions (i.e., MI recurrence, all-cause mortality, or repeat revascularization) were used to compare outcomes among the different study groups. As a result, the sensitivity analysis yielded results similar to those from the primary analysis.

Conclusion
The meta-analysis showed that FFR-guided CR was associated with lower TLR and repeat revascularization rates relative to culprit-only CR among patients with CAD and MVD. While no differences in cardiovascular outcomes were observed when comparing patients that received FFR-guided and angiography-guided CR, the former was linked with a reduced absolute number of adverse events relative to angiography-guided CR. Based on these results, FFR-guided CR for non-culprit vessels appears to be capable of reducing the need for stent implantation without any adverse prognostic effects in patients with CAD and MVD.
