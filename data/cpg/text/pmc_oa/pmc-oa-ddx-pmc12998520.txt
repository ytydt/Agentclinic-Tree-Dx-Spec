Seizures and electroencephalographic findings in inborn errors of metabolism: Clues to differential diagnosis in the neonatal period, infancy, childhood and adolescence, and review of the literature
Abstract
Although inborn errors of metabolism (IEM) are a rare cause of epilepsy, seizures are a common presentation in these disorders. Seizures in IEM are frequently refractory to conventional anti‐seizure medication and might warrant initiation of specific treatments based on vitamins or dietary modifications or provision of alternative substrates (to bypass a block). In most IEMs, seizures present with variable and non‐specific semiology and EEG findings. Nevertheless, certain distinctive electro‐clinical features might suggest an underlying IEM and even point towards the etiology. Even though biochemical and genetic tests will confirm the definitive diagnosis, EEG has the great advantage of being rapidly available, before the results of these tests. This article aims to describe through a literature review, illustrated by videos and EEGs from personal cases, seizures, and EEG patterns in IEM. We will focus on distinctive electro‐clinical features that may help the clinician to establish an appropriate diagnosis allowing a prompt and specific treatment in order to improve outcome.

Key points
Seizures are a common presentation in inborn errors of metabolism (IEM).
Seizures in IEM are frequently refractory to conventional anti‐seizure medication and may need specific treatments based on vitamins, dietary modifications or provision of alternative substrates.
Early diagnosis of IEM is important to initiate prompt and specific treatment in order to improve outcome.
This article describes through literature review illustrated by videos and EEG aspects distinctive electro‐clinical features that may guide differential diagnosis.

INTRODUCTION
Inborn errors of metabolism (IEM) are the consequence of genetic defects that lead to a metabolic block in a biochemical pathway critical to cellular function, targeting many organs including the brain. IEM include intoxication diseases by proteins or sugars, mitochondrial energy disorders, lysosomal diseases, and other complex diseases involving organelles such as peroxysomes, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, and neurotransmitter and vitamin metabolism disorders. Neurological manifestations are frequent and include coma, encephalopathy, seizures, movement disorders, ataxia, developmental delay, and neurological regression. Parental consanguinity, a positive family history, neonatal deaths in siblings, and abnormal pregnancy with possibly excessive fetal movements (intrauterine seizures) are a suggestive anamnestic context of IEM. Neonatal history of rapidly progressive coma occurring after a symptom‐free interval associated with excitability, abnormal movements such as pedaling, boxing (protein‐related intoxication diseases), unusual odors of urine (few protein‐related intoxication diseases or generalized fatty acid oxidation defects), severe metabolic acidosis (inconstant, depending of the disease), hiccups (fluttering or hiccoughs), myoclonia (glycine encephalopathy, vitamin‐dependent disorders), high‐amplitude tremors, or severe lethargy arise suspicion of the presence of IEM. EEG is frequently required in these patients for the evaluation of abnormal movements, encephalopathy, and/or seizures. In infants, children, and adolescents, progressive neurologic deficits, encephalopathy, abnormal movements, stroke‐like episodes, progressive myoclonic epilepsy, recurrent status epilepticus of unknown etiology, and epilepsia partialis continua (EPC) are suggestive of IEM. Though the definitive diagnosis is made by biochemical and/or genetic testing, electro‐clinical features may allow early suspicion of IEM and help to guide the differential diagnosis, allowing early specific treatment. For example, clinical hyperexcitability, intractable myoclonic jerks, and/or epileptic spasms associated with a discontinuous or burst suppression (BS) background activity could point towards pyridoxine dependency and therefore prompt specific treatment by vitamin B6 supplementation (Tables 1 and 2). A comb‐like EEG pattern in a neonate with unexplained rapidly progressive coma could be a pointer towards underlying maple syrup urine disease (MSUD). Moreover, video‐EEG allows differentiation between abnormal non‐epileptic movements (boxing and pedaling movements in MSUD) and epileptic seizures, and contributes to determine seizure type, and if possible, epilepsy syndrome, for example, early‐infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy or infantile spasms syndrome, which are common in IEM. In older children, progressive disorganization and slowing of background activity, increasing epileptic EEG anomalies, and EEG patterns such as lateralized periodic discharges or photosensitivity may point towards an underlying IEM (Table 2). EEG is also essential for differentiation between non‐convulsive status epilepticus and metabolic encephalopathy in the comatose child (e.g., hyperammonemic encephalopathy in urea cycle defects). Moreover, EEG allows quantifying seizure burden, especially if electrographic‐only seizures are frequent, helps to plan treatment endpoints, and contributes to neurodevelopmental and epilepsy outcomes.

In this publication, we review the literature on ictal and interictal EEG findings in IEM with emphasis on distinctive electro‐clinical features, which raise early suspicion of IEM. Moreover, we supplement the narrative review with seizure videos and EEG segments of representative clinical cases.
IEM can be categorized based on their onset, their predominant signs and symptoms, the main organs or systems affected, acuity or chronicity of presentation, or according to the biochemical process involved such as endogenous intoxication secondary to accumulation of metabolites, vitamin or cofactor deficiency, disorders of neurotransmitter metabolism, and disorders of energy deficiency. For a more pragmatic approach, here, we group IEM by age at onset, presenting in the neonatal period and early infancy, in late infancy and childhood, and in adolescence and adulthood. Nevertheless, it is prudent to emphasize that there can be considerable overlap in the age at presentation for many disorders, which could present at any age across an individual's life span. Selected disorders that have more specific electro‐clinical characteristics are described.

DISORDERS PRESENTING IN THE NEONATAL PERIOD OR IN EARLY INFANCY

Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6)‐dependent developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (PD‐DEE)
PD‐DEE is caused in most cases by pathogenic variants in the ALDH7A1 gene, resulting in the deficiency of alpha amino adipic semialdehyde dehydrogenase (antiquitin), which is involved in brain lysine metabolism. In some patients, PD‐DEE is associated with pathogenic variants in PLBP (earlier known as PROSC). Inheritance is autosomal recessive.

Epidemiology
The incidence of PD‐DEE caused by pathogenic variants in the ALDH7A1 gene has been estimated to be around 1 in 64 000 live births.

Clinical features
The clinical presentation is characterized by irritability, grimacing, hyperexcitability (see Video 1), abnormal eye movements, and seizures usually starting within 48 h after birth. In the majority of the patients, seizures begin in the neonatal period. However, seizures may start in 25%–30% of patients outside the neonatal period, and onset even at adolescence has been reported. Mothers of patients with early‐onset pyridoxine‐dependent seizures may also recall abnormal intrauterine movements such as episodic, recurrent hammering movements, suggestive of intrauterine onset of seizures. Other symptoms reported in infants with PD‐DEE are agitation, excessive crying, sleeplessness, apnea, cycling movements, hiccups, and startle reactions to touch and sound. Severe cognitive deficits and refractory epilepsy have been reported later in childhood.

Seizures
Seizures include focal, multifocal, and bilateral myoclonic jerks, clonic seizures, and epileptic spasms, often prolonged and possibly evolving to status epilepticus with a distinctive pattern, alternating between clonic seizures and epileptic spasms in clusters (see Figure 1 and Video 2). Also, atonic seizures, atypical absences, and myoclonic seizures provoked by intermittent photic stimulation have been reported. Seizures are resistant to conventional anti‐seizure medication (ASM) but respond to pyridoxine (vitamin B6).

EEG
Interictal EEG findings range from non‐specific background depression to a wide spectrum of abnormalities such as burst suppression (BS) pattern, bilaterally synchronous sharp and slow waves, intermittent or continuous slow waves, focal or multifocal spike‐and‐polyspike waves, hypsarrhythmia, and photoparoxysmal response. There is no particular pathognomonic EEG abnormality.

Treatment
To interrupt seizures, pyridoxine‐HCl is given 100 mg intravenously in case of status epilepticus in the intensive care setting, or orally/enterally, with a dose of 30 mg/kg/day. In the setting of intravenous pyridoxine treatment, a first administration might be accompanied by severe side effects such as apnea, profound hypotonia, and hemodynamic disturbances; therefore, treatment should be performed with support from respiratory management. EEG might transiently turn to severe diffuse depression. To make sure that late and masked response is not missed, treatment with oral/enteral pyridoxine should be continued until antiquitin deficiency is excluded by negative biochemical or genetic testing. Long‐term treatment dosages vary between 15 and 30 mg/kg/day in infants or up to 200 mg/day in children and 500 mg/day in adults. A transient electro‐clinical response to intravenous pyridoxine is not specific to vitamin B6 dependency and might also be seen in other IEMs like pyridox(am)ine phosphate oxidase deficiency and hyperprolinemia type 2. Apart from vitamin B6 substitution, a lysine‐restricted diet and competitive inhibition of lysine transport in the intestine, blood–brain barrier, and entry into the mitochondria through the use of pharmacologic doses of L‐arginine have been recommended as adjunct therapies. Though these adjunctive therapies have shown improved biochemical parameters and cognitive development, the benefit has been limited to case reports and case series and hence warrants further research.
Some patients with antiquitin deficiency have been shown to have additional benefit from folinic acid, mainly during the neonatal period or infancy; folinic acid (3–5 mg/kg/day) is recommended for PD‐DEE patients who fail to respond to pyridoxine alone. Diagnosis of this subset of patients is confirmed by the presence of antiquitin mutations and response to folinic acid along with pyridoxine. The role of folinic acid in PD‐DEE is not completely understood.

Pyridox(am)ine‐5′‐phosphate oxidase (PNPO) deficiency DEE
Pyridoxamine‐5‐phosphate oxidase enzyme converts pyridoxine and pyridoxamine into pyridox(am)ine‐5‐phosphate, which is a cofactor for many enzymes involved in the synthesis of dopamine, serotonin, glutamate, serine, gamma‐aminobutyric acid, and in the catabolism of gamma‐aminobutyric acid. The deficiency of this enzyme leads to a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (PNPO‐DEE). Inheritance is autosomal recessive.

Epidemiology
The incidence of PNPO‐DEE is unknown.

Clinical features and seizure phenotypes
Affected neonates are often born prematurely and present with multifocal myoclonic jerks, tongue thrusting, peculiar eye movements, orofacial dystonia (see Video 3), and epileptic seizures. Recently, PNPO deficiency has also been observed to cause hepatic dysfunction and cirrhosis in children.
24‐day‐old term newborn. Pyridox(am)ine‐5′‐phosphate oxidase (PNPO) deficiency developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (PNPO‐DEE). Video showing irritability, hyperexcitability, grimacing, multifocal myoclonic jerks. Video content can be viewed at https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/epd2.70065.

EEG
The EEG findings vary from intermittent diffuse slowing with irregular generalized epileptiform discharges to burst‐suppression pattern, similar to PD‐DEE.

Treatment
Traditionally, the clinical and electrophysiological response to pyridoxal‐5'‐phosphate treatment (30–50 mg/kg/day) and lack of response to pyridoxine treatment had generally been regarded as characteristic of PNPO deficiency. However, it is now being increasingly recognized that the clinical spectrum of PNPO deficiency is much broader, and some of the neonates, especially preterms, have shown to respond to the addition of pyridoxine to pyridoxal‐5' phosphate treatment. The possible phenomena behind this observation could be that the pyridoxine responsiveness in PNPO deficiency is affected by prematurity and age at the time of the therapeutic trial.

FOL1 ‐related cerebral folate transport deficiency
Cerebral folate deficiency is a treatable inherited metabolic disorder with seizures, which is responsive to folinic acid. Mutations in the FOLR1 gene have been shown to affect cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) folate transport, leading to cerebral folate deficiency. Diagnosis is suggested by very low 5‐methyltetrahydrofolate (5‐MTHF) in CSF (≤5 mmol/L) with no systemic folate deficiency and no characteristic peaks on neurotransmitter metabolite chromatogram and confirmed by genetic testing. Inheritance is autosomal recessive.

Epidemiology
The incidence of FOL1‐related cerebral folate transport deficiency is unknown.

Clinical features
Affected children present with global developmental delay or regression of milestones, ataxia, and refractory epilepsy. Movement disorders, including ocular (nystagmus, strabismus), hypotonia, abnormalities of gait, ataxia, tremors, and myoclonic jerks, have been reported.

Seizures
Seizures are reported in ≥90% of affected individuals. Myoclonic and myoclonic‐atonic seizures are the most common seizure types seen. Drop attacks provoked by specific movements (e.g., washing face or hands) have been reported.

EEG
Reported EEG findings include hypsarrhythmia, generalized spike– and polyspike–wave discharges, and multifocal spike–wave discharges.

Treatment
Treatment with oral or intravenous folinic acid is associated with improved clinical outcomes.

Disorders of amino acid metabolism
Among the disorders of amino acid metabolism, nonketotic hyperglycinemia (NKH) or glycine encephalopathy (GlyE), GABA transaminase deficiency, serine deficiency, and congenital glutamine deficiency can present with seizures in the neonatal period or in early infancy.

Glycine encephalopathy or nonketotic hyperglycinemia
Glycine encephalopathy (GlyE) is a defect of a glycine cleavage enzyme that is responsible for glycine degradation, leading to the accumulation of toxic levels of glycine in the body's organs and tissues. Diagnosis relies on the findings of hyperglycinemia and elevated CSF glycine levels in the absence of an organic acid disorder confirmed by molecular genetic testing (GLDC, AMT, GCSH). Inheritance is autosomal recessive.

Epidemiology
The estimated incidence of GlyE is 1 in 76 000 live births.

Clinical features
Clinical presentation is most often neonatal, with the neonatal severe (classic) form and the neonatal attenuated form, respectively, in 85% and 15% of patients. The neonatal severe form, frequently lethal, is characterized by severe hypotonia and progressive lethargy both leading to feeding problems starting immediately at birth, as well as abnormal jerking movements, seizures, refractory hiccups, and/or prolonged apneic episodes, and often early death. The infantile form can be severe (50%) or attenuated (50%) presenting with hypotonia, mild to moderate psychomotor delay, and seizures. The late‐onset form is less common and more heterogeneous. Symptoms start after the second year of life, even during adulthood, mainly with mild cognitive decline and behavioral problems. In few cases, a slowly progressive spinocerebellar syndrome including ataxia, upper‐motor neuron signs, optic atrophy, and peripheral neuropathy has been described.

Seizures
Myoclonic seizures are the most frequent presentation in the neonatal period. Seizures in the infantile presentation include epileptic spasms, focal or generalized tonic (see Video 4), or tonic–clonic seizures.

Treatment
There is no curative therapy available, and treatment aims for reduction of glycine burden and antagonism of neurotransmitter effect of glycine, apart from seizure control. Measures to reduce the glycine concentration include protein restriction, synthetic diet devoid of glycine, and promotion of renal excretion by benzoate, strychnine, and benzodiazepines. Sodium valproate must not be used as it further inhibits glycine metabolism. Serial EEGs may be helpful to assess the efficacy of treatment and to modify the therapeutic approach. The long‐term prognosis of this disorder remains poor.

Serine biosynthesis disorders
This autosomal recessive disease is caused by deficient functioning of any of the three enzymes involved in serine biosynthesis, 3‐phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (3‐PGDH), 3‐phosphoserine aminotransferase (PSAT), and phosphoserine phosphatase (PSP). Inheritance is autosomal recessive.

Epidemiology
The incidence of serine biosynthesis disorders is unknown.

Clinical features
Clinical presentation includes congenital or early acquired microcephaly, intrauterine growth retardation, congenital cataract, severe psychomotor retardation, spastic quadriplegia, hypogonadism, megaloblastic anemia, and refractory epilepsy.

Seizures
Seizure types include epileptic spasms (75% of the affected patients), tonic–clonic seizures, tonic seizures, atonic seizures, gelastic seizures, atypical absences (see Figure 4 and Video 6), and myoclonic seizures. Evolution towards Lennox–Gastaut syndrome has been described.

EEG
Multifocal spike–wave discharges, generalized slow spike‐and‐wave discharges (1.5–2 Hz) with paroxysms of fast activity and hypsarrhythmia have been reported.

Treatment
Treatment consists of supplementation with serine (up to 700 mg/kg/day) and glycine (up to 300 mg/kg/day). Early diagnosis and treatment are associated with seizure control and favorable outcomes in patients with 3‐PGDH deficiency. Normal neurodevelopmental outcomes have been reported in prenatally treated patients with 3‐PGDH and PSAT deficiency.

Molybdenum cofactor deficiency and sulfite oxidase deficiency
This group comprises rare autosomal recessive conditions of sulfur‐containing amino acid metabolism with overlapping clinical features. Both disorders can be differentiated only biochemically by the presence of low levels of uric acid in plasma only for molybdenum cofactor deficiency and by elevated urinary and plasma S‐sulfocysteine in both conditions. Inheritance is autosomal recessive for both conditions.

Incidence
The prevalence of this group of disorders is less than 1:100 000.

Clinical features
Affected newborns typically present with rapidly progressive encephalopathy, feeding difficulties, irritability, hypotonia, apnea, and seizures. The clinical phenotype often resembles hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, and this diagnosis may be re‐inforced by MRI findings of multicystic encephalomalacia. Some of these patients may also exhibit later onset epileptic encephalopathy. In these late‐onset patients, trivial trauma has been found to precipitate acute regression, movement disorders, and seizures. Lens dislocation is a common associated finding.

Seizures
Seizures usually start between day 2 and day 50 of life, and are often pharmacoresistant. Semiology is variable, including tonic–clonic and myoclonic seizures.

EEG
Interictal EEG shows low‐amplitude or disorganized background or burst suppression pattern and multifocal epileptiform discharges, similar to those observed in hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy. In sulfite oxidase deficiency, the presence of delta‐beta complexes (see Figure 5 and Video 7) might be a diagnostic marker. In molybdenum cofactor deficiency, the ictal EEG pattern is described to consist predominantly of rhythmic delta frequency with recruitment, arising independently from the central regions, often electrographic‐only.

Treatment
A novel treatment is available for a subset of patients affected with the molybdenum cofactor deficiency type A using purified intravenous cyclic pyranopterin monophosphate (cPMP). Its use has demonstrated reduction in seizure frequency and severity and improved developmental outcome, along with correction of biochemical abnormalities. However, this treatment appears to be helpful when started early in life, before permanent brain damage has set in. In the remaining one third of patients who have a distal defect, and for patients with sulfite oxidase deficiency, no definitive treatment is currently available.

Biotinidase deficiency (BTD) and holocarboxylase synthetase (HCS) deficiency
Both are autosomal recessive disorders affecting the vital coenzyme function of biotin. The diagnosis is usually clinical, supported by biochemical investigations and confirmed by measurement of enzyme levels or molecular findings. Biochemical abnormalities may include accumulation of one or more metabolites from enzymes from which biotin serves as a coenzyme: acetyl‐CoA carboxylases, propionyl‐CoA carboxylase, 3‐methylcrotonyl‐CoA carboxylase, and pyruvate carboxylase.

Incidence
The incidence of BTD varies from 1:40 000 to 1:60 000 births. The incidence of HCS deficiency is 1:200 000 live births.

Clinical features
Patients usually exhibit neurological and skin abnormalities such as alopecia and seborrheic dermatitis, and biotin treatment can dramatically ameliorate or prevent symptoms. Untreated patients develop neurodevelopmental sequelae such as optic atrophy, hearing loss, and global developmental delay.

Seizures
Seizures start within the first 3 or 4 months of life, and often as myoclonia, epileptic spasms, or tonic–clonic (see Video 8) or clonic seizures. They do not respond to anti‐seizure medication but respond promptly to small doses of biotin (5–20 mg/day). In HCS deficiency, clinical symptoms and response to biotin are very similar to BTD, though seizures are less frequent, occurring in 25%–50% of all children.

EEG
EEG findings in children with seizures vary from normal, mild slowing to markedly abnormal characterized by sharp, often multifocal spikes, and hypsarrhythmia.

Treatment
Treatment is lifelong biotin supplementation with a dose 5–20 mg/day.

Urea cycle disorders
Urea cycle disorders (UCD) represent a group of IEM resulting from single‐gene defects involved in the detoxification pathway of ammonia to urea. The enzymes in the urea cycle pathway include: carbamoyl phosphate synthase I (CPSI); ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC); argininosuccinic acid synthetase (ASS); argininosuccinic acid lyase (ASL); arginase (ARG); and the allosteric activator of CPSI, N‐acetyl glutamate synthetase (NAGS). The prototype OTC deficiency will be discussed below.

OTC deficiency
OTC deficiency is inherited in an X‐linked recessive mode of inheritance, with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern in the remainder. Biochemical investigations disclose high ammonia levels in the absence of metabolic acidosis or ketosis, high plasma glutamine levels, and abnormal plasma concentrations of amino acids depending on the deficiency. Increased urinary orotic acid level is specific to OTC deficiency. Diagnostic confirmation is obtained by molecular genetic investigation.

Incidence
The incidence of OTC deficiency is 1:14 000 to 1:70 000 live births.

Clinical features
Affected newborns are normal at birth and the first few days. Then, they present with rapidly evolving lethargy, poor feeding, hyperventilation or hypoventilation, hypothermia, abnormal posturing, encephalopathy, and coma due to cerebral oedema. The clinical presentation depends on the degree of enzymatic deficiency, with neonatal and late‐onset presentations and a high rate of mortality.

Seizures
Seizures are frequent during the early stages of hyperammonemia, especially in neonates, ranging from multifocal electrographic‐only seizures (see Figure 6) to focal seizures (see Video 9) to status epilepticus (see Figure 7) accompanied by severe encephalopathy.

EEG
EEG background activity is often discontinuous (see Figure 8). As seizure burden and interburst intervals have been reported to correlate with the severity of hyperammonemia, continuous EEG monitoring was suggested to be useful for prognosis and therapeutic management in infants with hyperammonemia. Interictal EEG (see Figures 9 and 10) may show variable aspects such as non‐specific multifocal spike–and sharp–wave discharges, unusually low‐voltage fast activity, continuous semi‐rhythmic activity with sharp, and burst suppression pattern in severe cases. EEG abnormalities seem to be correlated with UCD subtype, ammonia levels, duration of coma, and mortality during the neonatal period.

Treatment
In the acute stage, the main goal of treatment is to rapidly lower the blood ammonia levels by means of renal replacement therapy if needed and the use of ammonia scavengers such as sodium benzoate, phenylacetate, and phenylbutyrate. Long‐term management involves protein‐restricted diet and ammonia scavengers. Liver transplant may be required in severely affected patients.

Maple syrup urine disease
Maple syrup urine disease (MSUD) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by deficiency of branched‐chain ketoacid dehydrogenase complex, which is required to metabolize the three branched‐chain amino acids leucine, isoleucine, and valine. Classic MSUD is the most frequent form. The diagnosis is suggested by plasma amino acid analysis showing elevated concentrations of branched‐chain amino acids and alloisoleucine and is confirmed by genetics.

Incidence
The incidence of MSUD is 1 in 86 800 to 185 000 live births.

Clinical features
Affected newborns appear normal at birth, with symptoms most often developing between 2 and 7 days of age consisting of weight loss, progressive neurologic signs with lethargy, alternating hypotonia and hypertonia, severe encephalopathy, often non‐epileptic abnormal movements as boxing and pedaling (see Video 10), seizures, and coma.

Seizures
No specific seizure types have been reported in MSUD.

EEG
In the neonatal period, a distinctive electroencephalographic pattern, the comb‐like rhythm, has been described. This pattern consists of bursts of 5–7 Hz sharp mu‐like rhythms in the central and central‐parasagittal regions during wakefulness and sleep, with the most abundant bursts occurring during quiet (non‐REM) sleep (see Figures 11, 12, 13). These rhythms are described to disappear after the initiation of dietary therapy. The other EEG patterns described in MSUD include spikes, polyspikes, spike–wave complexes, triphasic waves, severe slowing, and bursts of periodic suppression.

Treatment
Treatment consists of dietary leucine restriction, branched‐chain amino acid‐free medical foods, judicious supplementation with isoleucine and valine, and frequent clinical and biochemical monitoring.

Phenylketonuria
Classical Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in both alleles of the gene for phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH). Milder forms result from mutations in PAH or from defects in the metabolism of tetrahydrobiopterin, the obligate cofactor of PAH.

Incidence
The global prevalence of PKU is 1:23 900 live births.

Clinical features
The clinical features of this disorder include growth failure, progressive developmental delay, and microcephaly in the absence of neonatal treatment started after detection on newborn screening.

Seizures
Though presentation in the neonatal period with seizures is uncommon, refractory epilepsy may occur in some untreated infants, with epileptic spasms or tonic seizures. PKU has also been seen to present between 3.5 months and 12 years of age with various degrees of mental‐motor retardation and focal or generalized seizures, semiology ranging from febrile seizure, myoclonia, to status epilepticus.

EEG
EEG findings in affected infants may range from mild abnormal background findings to focal or diffuse paroxysmal discharges, photoparoxysmal discharges, and hypsarrhythmia, and PKU has been reported in patients with West syndrome. However, no correlation has been observed between the EEG abnormalities and severity of clinical presentation. West syndrome with epileptic spasms and hypsarrhythmia has also been reported in children with dihydropteridine reductase deficiency, an enzyme involved in the metabolism of tetrahydrobiopterin. It has been observed that there is an independent, age‐related deterioration of the EEG in several patients with PKU, even though these patients are clinically well, presumably attributed to metabolic abnormalities associated with PKU. EEG shows slowing of background activity with generalized asymmetrical discharges in childhood presentations.

Menkes disease
Menkes disease is an X‐linked recessive multisystem disorder caused by a defect in copper transport due to pathogenic variants in the ATP7A, resulting in intracellular copper deficiency and the dysfunction of copper‐dependent enzymes. Biochemical markers are low serum levels of copper and ceruloplasmin.

Incidence
The incidence of Menke's disease ranges from 1:300 000 to 1:400 000 live births.

Clinical features
The clinical picture is characterized by early‐onset seizures accompanied by neurodevelopmental regression usually observed at around 3 months of age associated with features as “kinky hair,” cutis laxa, frontal bossing, muscular hypotonia, hypothermia.

Seizures and EEG
For the classic variant of Menkes disease, three electro‐clinical stages characterizing the evolution of epilepsy have been proposed: (a) early stage (median age 3 months), characterized by focal seizures (see Figure 14), usually at onset focal clonic possibly progressing to status epilepticus; (b) intermediate stage (median age 10 months) with drug‐resistant epileptic spasms (see Video 11 and Figure 15) associated with hypsarrhythmia or modified hypsarrhythmia; (c) late stage (median age 25 months), with multifocal seizures, tonic spasms, and myoclonus, with interictal EEG showing multifocal high‐amplitude activity, with intermixed slow waves. The long‐term prognosis of epilepsy in Menkes disease tends to be poor, with the majority of patients being resistant to all antiseizure medications.

Peroxisomal disorders
Peroxisomal disorders (PDs) include heterogeneous disease groups, with variable degrees of severity, classified according to their pathophysiology into peroxisomal biogenesis disorders (PBDs) and peroxisomal enzyme deficiencies (PEDs). Diagnosis is established by demonstration of elevated plasma very long‐chained fatty acid (VLCFA) levels and the relevant pathogenic mutation in one of the peroxisomal biogenetic factor (PEX) genes in PBDs. PEDs are genetically heterogeneous involving variants in ABCD1, ACOX1, and PAHX, among others. Clinical symptoms comprise typical craniofacial dysmorphism, severe neurological abnormalities at birth that include profound truncal and peripheral hypotonia, absence of visual and auditory responses, glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa, hepatomegaly, often cardiac defects, and early‐onset seizures usually beginning after the second week of life. Seizure semiology ranges from focal motor involving arms, legs, or facial muscles (see Video 12) and EPC in Zellweger syndrome to generalized tonic, clonic, and myoclonic seizures in neonatal adrenoleukodystrophy. Although EEG changes are often non‐specific with multifocal spikes and evolutionary diffuse slow background activity, lateralized periodic discharges (see Figure 16) with or without motor manifestation may be a clue to diagnosis. The occurrence of West syndrome has also been observed. Treatment remains supportive.

DISORDERS PRESENTING IN INFANCY AND CHILDHOOD

Glucose transporter 1 deficiency syndrome
Glucose transporter 1 deficiency syndrome (Glut1DS) is a brain energy failure disorder resulting from impaired glucose transport into the brain caused by pathogenic variants in the SLC2A1 gene. The diagnosis is confirmed on lumbar puncture by low cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) glucose levels with normal or low CSF lactate after a 4–6 h fasting period in the absence of hypoglycemia and other causes of hypoglycorrhachia. If pathogenic SLC2A1 variants are disclosed, a lumbar puncture may not be necessary. Inheritance is autosomal dominant.

Incidence
The estimated incidence of Glut1 DS is 1:83 000 live births.

Clinical features
Clinical features include infantile onset epilepsy, movement disorders, ataxia, microcephaly, developmental delay, learning difficulties, and dysarthria. Timely recognition is important, as seizures are generally resistant to anti‐seizure medications but may be completely controlled by the ketogenic diet. A characteristic and early feature during infancy, preceding the onset of seizures, is brief paroxysmal episodes of eye and head movements, referred to as aberrant gaze saccades. They consist of rapid, multidirectional eye movements accompanied by brief head movements in the same direction and separated by clear intervals of fixation. Paroxysmal complex movement disorders, including variable combinations of dystonia, choreoathetosis, myoclonus, tremor, tics, and stereotypies, have also been described, typically increased before meals.

Seizures
Epilepsy onset is reported between age 1.5 months and 11 years. Seizure types are multiple, however, generalized are more common than focal consisting in tonic–clonic, myoclonic, myoclonic–atonic, absence seizures, and atypical absences (see Videos 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18). It may be difficult to differentiate between typical and atypical absences as generalized 3 Hz spike‐and‐wave discharges can be preceded and/or followed by runs of frontal 2–2.5 Hz (see Figure 17). In rare cases, also tonic seizures and ES have been reported. Seizure semiology is described to change according to age, with focal seizures of earlier onset (median age 24 months) compared with atypical absences and myoclonia (median age 3.2 years). One particular phenotype, early‐onset absences associated with myoclonic or clonic manifestations involving limbs, neck, and/or upper face muscles (see Video 19) should raise the suspicion of Glut1DS. Trigger events as fasting or exercise have been reported. 12% of children with early‐onset absence epilepsy have been found to have Glut1DS.

Treatment
The ketogenic diet is an effective treatment for this condition as it provides alternative sources of energy substrate in the form of ketone bodies that the brain preferentially utilizes. In older children, adolescents, and adults, less restrictive variants such as the modified Atkins diet and the low glycemic index treatment may be used. Triheptanoin, an anaplerotic agent which replenishes the Krebs cycle metabolites, has been found useful in reducing the non‐epileptic paroxysmal manifestations and normalizing the brain bioenergetics profile in patients with Glut1DS. Drugs that impair GLUT1 function, for example, caffeine, phenobarbital, diazepam, chloral hydrate, and tricyclic antidepressants, are best avoided.

Mitochondrial disorders
Epilepsy is a frequent manifestation of mitochondrial diseases, with seizures reported to occur in 35%–60% of patients. Diagnosis requires testing of blood and CSF lactate (which may be variably elevated), MRI and MRS of the brain, molecular studies, and in some cases, muscle biopsy (ragged‐red fibers, cytochrome oxidase (COX) negative fibers) and analysis of the respiratory chain enzymes on the muscle biopsy. Associated clinical features include developmental delay, failure to thrive, ataxia, vision impairment, deafness, stroke‐like episodes, and evidence of multiorgan involvement. In children, six age‐related epilepsy phenotypes have been described associated with mitochondrial disorders: status epilepticus complicating multivisceral deficiency, early‐infantile DEE, infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS), and refractory status epilepticus with outcome of either death or major neurologic deterioration, EPC, and epilepsy with major seizure type of myoclonia, eventually reaching convulsive status epilepticus (Video 20). With disease progression and phenotype worsening, myoclonia may not be associated with a scalp EEG correlate and may require back‐averaging. EEG findings are non‐specific, though the presence of periodic discharges, bilateral (see Figure 19) or lateralized (see Figures 20 and 21), or of relative occipital predominance (see Figure 22) may provide a clue to diagnosis. The most common EEG abnormalities observed are generalized slowing and multifocal discharges.

Treatment is generally supportive and symptomatic. Sodium valproate must be avoided when a mitochondrial disorder is suspected, as it carries the risk of inducing fatal hepatotoxicity. Some patients may benefit from supplementation of thiamine, riboflavin, L‐carnitine, or coenzyme Q10. Arginine may be beneficial in reducing the stroke‐like episodes in patients with MELAS. Levetiracetam is the drug of choice for myoclonic seizures in MERRF syndrome, and lamotrigine may have a neuroprotective effect. Patients with refractory epilepsy associated with respiratory chain defects might benefit from a ketogenic diet. In mitochondrial disease patients, brutal and rapid occurrence of intractable seizures is a red flag usually pointing towards a poor short‐term outcome.

Cerebral creatine deficiency syndromes
Cerebral creatine deficiency syndromes (CCDS) comprise two autosomal recessive disorders (L‐arginine: glycine amidinotransferase (AGAT) deficiency and guanidinoacetate methyltransferase (GAMT) deficiency) and 1 X‐linked disorder (creatine transporter (CRTR) deficiency) that affect creatine biosynthesis or transport into the brain and muscle, leading to creatine brain depletion and hence epilepsy and various neurodevelopmental defects. Diagnosis is confirmed by molecular studies that include testing of three genes, namely GATM, GAMT, and SLC6A8. Brain MRI shows an abnormal hyperintense signal in the globi pallidi on T2‐weighted sequences. A marked reduction of the creatine signal peak on proton MRS is demonstrable in all three subtypes.

Incidence
The incidence of GAMT deficiency is 1:114 000 live births. The prevalence of CCDS is higher in selected populations such as children with autism spectrum disorder, ranging from .25% to 1%.

Clinical features
Clinical features include developmental delay, intellectual disability, behavior problems, autistic features, speech delay, movement disorders, hypotonia in early stages, dystonia in the later stage, and epilepsy in GAMT and CRTR. Age at onset is described between 8 months and 4 years.

Seizures
Multiple seizure types have been described, including myoclonic, generalized tonic–clonic, focal impaired awareness seizures, atypical absence seizures, possibly evolving to non‐convulsive status epilepticus (see Video 29), head nodding, and drop attacks.

EEG
EEG may show normal tracing, diffuse slowing, non‐specific sharp abnormalities, or high‐amplitude slow background with multifocal spikes.

Treatment
Early diagnosis is important as this disorder is treatable. Seizures and associated EEG abnormalities are more responsive to substitutive treatment with creatine monohydrate (400 mg/day) than to conventional anti‐epileptic drugs, as it replenishes deficient creatine monohydrate. Other therapeutic strategies to reduce guanidoacetoacetate (GAA) levels include high‐dose L‐ornithine supplementation, dietary arginine restriction, and sodium benzoate supplementation.

Congenital disorders of glycosylation
Congenital disorders of glycosylation (CDGs) are multisystem disorders caused by genetic defects in glycoprotein, glycolipid, and glycan synthesis. The diagnosis of many CDGs is suspected on isoelectric focusing of serum transferrin and confirmed by molecular genetic testing. The majority of congenital disorders of glycosylation are inherited in an autosomal recessive fashion; fewer are X‐linked. Neurological features include developmental delay, seizures, stroke‐like episodes, hypotonia, ataxia, and peripheral neuropathy. Semiology of seizures includes myoclonic, clonic, and focal seizures. Epileptic spasms have also been described (see Videos 30 and 31) in ALG1‐, ALG6‐, ALG11‐CDG, and CDG‐Ix, occurring along with myoclonic seizures and without associated hypsarrhythmia (see Figure 26). Interictal EEG findings include slowed background rhythm, focal and multifocal epileptic discharges, and generalized epileptic activity including burst suppression pattern. Treatment interventions are symptomatic as no specific treatment measures are currently available.

Lysosomal storage disorders
Lysosomal storage disorders (LSD) are a major category of diseases that involve defects in lysosomal enzyme function, lysosomal biogenesis, activation, trafficking, or membrane transporters. Over two thirds of LSDs are neurodegenerative, presenting as progressive myoclonic epilepsies (PME), some associated with epileptic encephalopathy.

Neuronal ceroid lipofuscinoses
Neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (NCL) is a group of genetically heterogeneous autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorders characterized by excessive accumulation of lysosomal lipofuscin. To date, 14 variants of NCL have been reported, neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (CLN) 1 to CLN14; for 13 of them, the affected genes have been identified (CLN1‐8 and CLN10‐14). Early diagnosis is crucial in CLN2, as a specific (enzyme replacement) treatment is now available, and damages that already occurred are irreversible.

Clinical features
Age at onset and clinical course vary according to the type of NCL. Common features include cognitive decline, progressive loss of vision, motor impairment, and seizures. Disease progression is characterized by the development of spasticity, truncal hypotonia, loss of head control, progressive loss of vision, near‐continuous myoclonus (see Video 32), frequent seizures, an extended vegetative state, and early death. Congenital NCL (CLN10) is a rare disease characterized by severe presentation with respiratory failure, hypotonia, and neonatal onset seizures.

Seizures
Early‐infantile NCL (CLN1) has symptom onset between 3 and 18 months and is characterized by acquired severe microcephaly and myoclonic seizures (see Video 33). Early hallmark symptoms of the late infantile form of NCL (CLN2) are language delay and epileptic seizures beginning between 2 and 4 years of age, with myoclonic, myoclonic–atonic (see Video 34), atonic (see Video 35), or absence seizures, over the course of the disease also tonic and tonic–clonic seizures. Drop attacks and progressive ataxia have also been reported.

EEG
Along with clinical presentation, EEG features can provide clues helping to early diagnosis and to differentiate between different types of NCL. EEG shows gradually progressive slowing, decrease of amplitude in background activity, and disappearance of sleep spindles (“vanishing EEG”) (see Figure 27 and Video 36).
10‐week‐old infant. CLN1. Vanishing EEG.
In CLN2, EEG plays an important role, as photosensitivity is considered to be an early marker in this condition, even before the onset of cognitive and motor regression and showing different features compared with photosensitivity in other epilepsies. A photoparoxysmal flash‐per‐flash response at low frequencies with occipital spikes is described as characteristic; however, it is a non‐specific electroencephalographic finding in CLN2 (see Figures 28, 29, 30, 31, 32) and may be associated with myoclonic jerks (see Video 37 and Figure 33). As stimulation of the central part of the retina is most effective in provoking a photoparoxysmal flash‐per‐flash response, it is important that the patient looks at the center of the lamp (see Figure 34). Early photosensitivity at higher frequencies (see Figures 35 and 36) has been reported in 27%–93% of CLN2 patients. In a recent study of 14 patients with CLN2, PPR was evident since the first EEG, which was performed at 3.6 (3.1–4.0) years, in 43% of patients, observed at low (1–3 Hz)‐frequency stimulation in 69% of patients and as a flash‐per‐flash response in 69% of patients. A photoparoxysmal pattern known as a “sentinel discharge” consisting of a high‐amplitude generalized polyspike–wave discharge seen at the onset of the photic train followed by posterior predominant 2–3 Hz spike–wave discharges or rhythmic slowing has also been noted. IPS may also trigger seizures in CLN2 (see Video 38), particularly in advanced stages of the disease. These phenomena reflect neuronal hyperexcitability during the degenerative process. PPR disappeared over time in about half (54%) of the patients. Besides, progressive slowing of the background activity and loss of physiological features, such as sleep spindles, have been described in CLN2. Though the utility of evaluating the photoparoxysmal response for early diagnosis of CLN2 is undoubtable, there is a lack of data if this response can be elicited in children below 1 year of age.

Susceptibility to PPR is different in the other variants of NCL, reported more frequently in CLN2, CLN5, and CLN6, and seems not to be a prominent feature in CLN1, CLN3, CLN8, and CLN11. It is important to underline that the absence of PPR does not exclude the diagnosis of NCL.

Treatment
Though various therapeutic strategies are currently being explored as treatment options for these fatal disorders, there is presently only one clinically approved drug that has been shown to effectively attenuate the progression of a specific form of neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, CLN2 disease (cerliponase alfa, a lysosomal enzyme infused into the brain ventricles of patients with CLN2 disease). Intravitreal enzyme replacement therapy has been recently shown to slow down retinal degeneration in CLN2.

Other lysosomal storage disorders
In the following section, seizure types and EEG findings in some of the common lysosomal storage disorders presenting with epilepsy are briefly described. All disorders described below are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner.

Gaucher disease
Seizures may be seen along with neuroregression in the neuronopathic forms of Gaucher disease, that is, Type 2 and Type 3. Type 2 Gaucher disease presents with rapidly progressive neurodegeneration with onset within 6 months of life and massive hepatosplenomegaly. Type 3 Gaucher disease presents in older children with chronic neurodegeneration and variable visceral involvement. Type 3 is further subclassified: Type 3a typically presents with progressive myoclonic epilepsy, and Type 3b presents with abnormal saccadic eye movements.
Seizures are common in Type 2 and Type 3a. The semiology includes focal seizures, tonic and myoclonic seizures (see Video 39), and epileptic spasms. The EEG may demonstrate several abnormalities including polyspikes with occipital predominance sensitive to photostimulation (see Video 40), diffuse slowing with high‐voltage sharp waves during sleep, and multifocal spike‐and‐wave paroxysms. Background slowing is the commonest finding seen (>90%) and may be helpful in identifying early neurological involvement. Epileptiform discharges present in half of the patients might not be associated with a clinical history of seizures; over one third of patients have clinical evidence of epilepsy. Enzyme replacement therapy may prolong life but does not improve the neurological manifestations.

GM2 gangliosidoses
In early (first 12 months) and advanced symptomatic (1–2 years) phases of Tay‐Sachs disease, an exaggerated startle response to sound (see Video 41) is the most prominent feature along with strabismus, opisthotonus, tonic seizures, and developmental delay. A macular cherry red spot is seen in 90% of affected children. Seizures typically begin within the first year of life and worsen in frequency and severity. They tend to be refractory, difficult to control with ASM, and can acutely and rapidly progress. The most common EEG finding is background slowing and slow spindles. Interictal epileptiform discharges are not prominent and consist of low‐amplitude spike– and sharp–wave discharges.

DISORDERS PRESENTING IN LATE CHILDHOOD AND ADOLESCENCE

Progressive myoclonic epilepsies (PME)
The syndrome of PME comprises more than 10 rare progressive disorders. It is recognized in the presence of myoclonus, progressive motor and cognitive impairment, sensory and cerebellar signs, and abnormal background slowing on EEG, appearing in individuals with prior normal development and cognition. The presentation is usually in late childhood or adolescence; however, it may affect all ages. Photosensitivity is a common feature in many etiologies of PME.
Lafora disease (also known as progressive myoclonic epilepsy 2A and 2B, or Lafora body disease) is a fatal form of PME caused by biallelic variants in essential glycogen metabolic enzymes Laforin (encoded by EPM2A) and Malin, also called EPM2B (encoded by NHLRC1). Symptoms start between 6 and 19 years of age, mainly at 14–15 years, often with generalized tonic–clonic seizures and focal visual seizures to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures, possibly misdiagnosed as juvenile myoclonic epilepsy or childhood occipital visual epilepsy (formerly called late‐onset benign occipital epilepsy Gastaut type). Further symptoms follow 3–24 months after the onset of epilepsy, with cognitive decline, vision loss, cerebellar dysfunction, and myoclonic seizures. After an evolution of 10 years, patients present with nearly continuous myoclonus, absence seizures, frequent generalized tonic–clonic seizures, and severe cognitive deficiency or with vegetative state. Erratic myoclonia without EEG correlate may be observed. EEG shows variable background slowing along with generalized epileptiform discharges (in 85%) (see Figure 37), focal discharges (in about a third), and photosensitivity to a fast‐frequency stimulus (see Figure 38). Some of the patients also show giant somatosensory‐evoked potentials.
Unverricht–Lundborg disease (also known as epilepsy with progressive myoclonus epilepsy 1 or Baltic myoclonic epilepsy) is caused by biallelic mutations of the cystatin B gene (CSTB). Cognitive and motor deterioration are slow, with stabilization in adulthood. Onset of symptoms is before the age of 18 years, generally between 7 and 13 years, with tonic–clonic, myoclonic, or absence seizures. Myoclonus may be triggered by tactile or photic stimulation (see Video 42), or induced by movement (see Video 43), and is usually more pronounced in the awake state. EEG shows interictal generalized spike‐and‐polyspike waves, possibly triggered by photic stimulation, and, after initially normal background at the onset of symptoms, progressive slowing.

SUMMARY
Here, we review the literature on seizures and EEG findings in IEM with emphasis on distinctive electro‐clinical features, which raise early suspicion of IEM. Numerous IEMs can present with seizures and epilepsy in infancy and early childhood, and early suspicion and prompt management can optimize neurodevelopmental outcomes in the treatable IEMs (see Table 1). Although EEG findings tend to be non‐specific, seizure semiology and certain distinctive EEG patterns might serve as useful clues to differential diagnosis. Also, video‐EEG allows differentiation between abnormal non‐epileptic movements (boxing and pedaling movements in MSUD) and seizures, and helps to determine seizure type and, if possible, epilepsy syndrome, for example, early‐infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy or infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, which are common in IEM. EEG is also essential for differentiation between non‐convulsive status epilepticus and metabolic encephalopathy (hyperammonemic encephalopathy in urea cycle defects). Moreover, EEG allows quantifying seizure burden, especially if electrographic‐only seizures are frequent as in urea cycle disorders, helps to plan treatment endpoints, and contributes to neurodevelopmental and epilepsy outcomes.
