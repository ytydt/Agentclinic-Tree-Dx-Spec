Breaking the shield of solid tumors: a combined approach for enhanced efficacy of CAR-T cells
The use of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells has enhanced the range of available therapeutic modalities in the context of cancer treatment. CAR-T cells have demonstrated considerable efficacy in the targeted eradication of blood cancer cells, thereby stimulating substantial interest in the advancement of such therapeutic approaches. However, the efficacy of CAR-T cells against solid tumor cells has been limited due to the presence of various obstacles. Solid tumors exhibit antigenic diversity and an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which presents a challenge for immune cells attempting to penetrate the tumor. CAR-T cells also demonstrate decreased proliferative activity and cytotoxicity. Furthermore, concerns exist regarding tumor antigen loss and therapy-associated toxicity. Currently, scientists are working to enhance the structure of the CAR and improve the survival and efficiency of CAR-T cells in recognizing tumor antigens in solid tumors. Chemotherapy drugs are frequently employed in the treatment of malignant neoplasms and can also be used prior to cell therapy to enhance CAR-T cell engraftment. Recent studies have demonstrated that chemotherapy drugs can mitigate the suppressive impact of TME, eliminate the physical barrier by destroying the tumor stroma, and facilitate greater penetration of immune cells and CAR-T cells into the tumor. This, in turn, increases their survival, persistence, and cytotoxicity, as well as affects the metabolism of immune cells inside the tumor. However, the effectiveness of the combined approach against solid tumors depends on several factors, including the type of tumor, dosage, population of CAR-T cells, and individual characteristics of the body. This review examines the principal obstacles to the utilization of CAR-T cells against solid tumors, proposes solutions to these issues, and assesses the potential advantages of a combined approach to radiation exposure, which has the potential to enhance the sensitivity of the tumor to other agents.

Introduction
The ongoing advancement of science and technology is giving rise to the emergence of novel cancer treatment modalities that move away from the harmful systemic exposure associated with traditional therapies, toward more targeted and effective approaches. Conventional cancer treatments, including chemotherapy and surgery, are not without shortcomings. They can prove ineffective in cases of recurrent or resistant cancer, as well as in instances of distant metastases.
Immunotherapy, particularly adoptive cell therapy, represents a promising avenue in the treatment of cancer. This approach entails the utilization of the patient’s own immune cells (autologous) or donor cells (allogeneic), which have been genetically modified to target tumor antigens, and are subsequently reintroduced into the body to destroy tumor cells.
The employment of adoptive cell therapy based on T cells with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) has resolved numerous challenges in the management of blood cancer. The use of anti-CD19 and anti-BCMA CAR-T cells for the treatment of B-cell leukemia and lymphoma has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The results of CAR-T cell therapy have been encouraging, leading to the registration of seven commercial CAR-T cell-based drugs worldwide. These include Kymriah, Yescarta, Tecartus, Breyanzi, Abecma, Carvykti, and Carteyva. Currently, numerous modifications of CAR-T cells with anti-CD19/CD20/CD22 and other antigens are being tested around the world.
The CAR-T therapy has transformed the approach to treating oncohematological diseases, primarily by offering significant opportunities to modify the CAR. The receptor can be incorporated not only into lymphocytes but also into macrophages (CAR-M), NK cells (CAR-NK), and other cell types. When located on a T cell, the CAR binds to a specific target antigen on the tumor cell surface, independent of the MHC (major histocompatibility complex). This binding event then triggers the activation of intracellular signaling pathways, which in turn initiate the amplification of CAR-T cells, their proliferation, and their mechanisms of cytotoxic action.
CAR receptors are composed of three principal components—extracellular, transmembrane, and intracellular domains. CAR-T cell specifically interacts with the tumor cells via recognition of the tumor antigen; this initiates the granzyme-perforin mechanism of T cell cytotoxicity and results in tumor cell death via apoptosis. Abbreviations: CAR—chimeric antigen receptor, CM1/CM2—co-stimulatory molecules, scFv—single-chain variable fragment, ITAM—immunoreceptor tyrosine-based motif, JAK—Janus kinase, STAT—signal transducer and activator of transcription, VL/VH—light/heavy chain variable fragment.
As the field of CAR technology has progressed, subsequent generations have incorporated additional co-stimulatory domains within the intracellular region. Modern CARs comprise of four primary domains:
Extracellular domain, comprising light and heavy chains, is represented by a single-chain variable fragment (scFv), and is responsible for binding to an antigen.
Hinge domain regulates the flexibility and stability of the receptor, facilitating binding of extra- and intracellular regions via the use of IgG or CD8 spacers.
Transmembrane domain serves to connect the hinge region to the endodomain of the CAR, thereby influencing the flexibility and efficiency of the receptor. This domain can be represented by CD3ζ, CD28, and CD8a.
The intracellular domain comprises a tyrosine-containing immunoreceptor activating motif (ITAM), which is constituted by a core signaling domain, CD3ζ or FcεRIγ, in conjunction with co-stimulatory domains, including CD28, 4-1BB (CD137), and OX40 (CD134). This domain serves to initiate a cascade of reactions that induce the granzyme/perforin mechanism of T cell cytotoxicity.
First-generation CARs consist of a single signaling domain that resembles the structure of the TCR. Due to the absence of co-stimulatory signals, these CARs exhibited poor proliferation, persistence, and antitumor cytotoxicity.
Second-generation CARs include co-stimulatory domains such as CD28, 4-1BB, ICOS, and OX40, among others. This results in the activation of CAR cells, an increase in cytokine production, the prevention of apoptosis, and an enhancement of proliferative and cytotoxic abilities.
Third-generation CARs contain costimulating molecules (CD28 and 4-1BB), which further enhance the cytotoxicity and stability of CAR-T cells.
Fourth-generation CARs, known as antigen-redirected unrestricted cytokine-triggered killing (TRUCK) cells or armored CARs, comprise components responsible for the secretion of interleukins IL-7, IL-12, IL-15, and IL-21. TRUCK CAR-T cells effectively support the immune response against tumor cells.
Fifth-generation CARs are based on the structural framework of the second generation. The intracellular domain of the cytokine receptor is similar in structure to a fragment of the IL-2Rβ chain. This results in the activation of JAK-STAT signaling pathways, which, in turn, enhances the antitumor immune response.
At present, there are five generations of CAR-T cells, defined by their endodomain structure.
In addition to enhancements to the CAR design and encouraging outcomes from its utilization in cell therapy for oncohematology, the treatment of solid tumors with this type of therapy remains ineffective. A number of clinical trials are currently underway with the objective of investigating the use of CAR-T cell therapy for the treatment of solid tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma, lung cancer, and gastric cancer.
These studies involve various modifications of the CAR, which targets antigens such as CEA, EGFR/EGFRvIII, GD2, HER2, MSLN, and MUC1. Nevertheless, research has demonstrated that CAR-T cell therapy based on the use of anti-GD2-CAR cells is ineffective for neuroblastoma, and anti-CAIX-CAR cells exhibit low efficacy for kidney carcinoma, with almost no clinical response. Similar results were observed in the treatment of retinoblastoma, pancreatic carcinomas, and colon cancer using CAR-T cells targeting specific antigens. These studies have revealed both the positive aspects and limitations of CAR-T therapy.
Crucially, the limited efficacy of CAR-T therapy for solid tumors gives the distinct characteristics between these tumors and hematological tumors. Solid tumors exhibit a distinctive structure, cellular composition, and defense mechanisms that diverge from those observed in hematological tumors. Such differences also impact the metabolism of CAR-T cells.

Solid tumors
The primary distinguishing characteristic of solid tumors is their development into a defined mass with specific boundaries, composed of diverse cell types. These include cancerous cells, healthy body cells (including immune cells), and extracellular matrix. In contrast, hematological tumors lack this defining feature. It is crucial to underscore that cancer cells display genetic polymorphism and atypia not only in comparison to healthy body cells but also within the tumor cells themselves. This diversity presents significant challenges for the development of targeted therapies.
Morphologically, tumor cells are composed of a core and a shell. As the tumor grows, changes occur within the lesion and the surrounding tissues. A fibrous capsule, composed of collagen fibers synthesized by fibroblasts, separates the primary tumor site from healthy cells. In the initial stages of tumor growth, dynamic interactions emerge between cancer cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME), facilitating the survival of cancer cells, local invasion, and the dissemination of metastases. As the tumor grows, disruptions in the flow of blood and oxygen result in hypoxia (low oxygen levels) and necrosis of tumor cells in its center. In response to hypoxia, cells within the TME begin secreting growth factors, which stimulate the growth of nearby blood vessels and increase nutrient supply. These vessels differ from normal vessels in that they lack a basement membrane, endothelium, and pericytes. The vascular wall of individual capillaries is represented by tumor cells themselves.
Tumor growth is influenced by the release of growth factors by tumor cells, which stimulate fibroblasts to produce collagen, fibronectin, and elastin. These substances create a fibrous capsule around the tumor, acting as a barrier for immune cells and impeding their infiltration. As the tumor expands, hypoxic and necrotic areas develop in the center, creating an unfavorable environment for immune cells, which are unable to function effectively in such an environment. Consequently, the efficacy of the treatment is diminished, and the patient dies prematurely.
Oncoproteins produced by tumor cells, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-beta), act as signaling molecules that promote tumor growth and vascularization. These oncoproteins also alter the behavior of surrounding cells in the TME. For example, TGF-β activates fibroblasts to produce collagen and other extracellular matrix components, forming a protective fibrous capsule around the tumor. VEGF stimulates endothelial cells to form abnormal blood vessels that provide nutrients and oxygen to the tumor. These vessels are often leaky and lack proper structure. In addition, oncoproteins such as IL-10 (interleukin-10) and TGF-β influence immune cells such as macrophages and Treg cells to assume a tumor-supportive role by suppressing immune responses and protecting the tumor from immune surveillance.

Tumor microenvironment
The tumor microenvironment (TME) exerts a significant influence on tumor development, affecting several key processes, including tumor growth, modulation of the immune response, invasiveness and metastasis, and tissue remodeling. The principal constituents of the TME are immune cells, stromal cells, and the extracellular matrix (ECM).
Different cells within the TME can either inhibit or promote tumor growth, depending on the tumor type and specific cell interactions. Abbreviations: NK—natural killer cell, Neu—neutrophils, B—B-lymphocytes, M1/M2—macrophages, DC—dendritic cell, Treg—regulatory T cell, Th1/Th2/Th17—T-helper cells, MSDC—myeloid-derived suppressor cell, CD4 + /CD8 + – T lymphocytes.
A substantial proportion of the TME is constituted by stromal cells, which include cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), pericytes, and endothelial cells. CAFs secrete a range of growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines that facilitate tumor growth, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling. They produce proteins that induce immunosuppression of tumor cells by attracting immunosuppressive cells and stimulate angiogenesis by producing FGF2 and VEGFA. Endothelial cells, which line the blood vessels, play a pivotal role in angiogenesis within the TME. They facilitate the formation of new blood vessels that provide the tumor with oxygen and nutrients, which are essential for its growth and survival. Endothelial cells are subject to a variety of signals from the TME, including those from CAFs and other stromal cells, which can enhance their proliferative and migratory capabilities, thereby further supporting tumor progression.
The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a complex network of proteins and glycoproteins that provides structural and biochemical support to tumor cells. The principal constituents of the extracellular matrix (ECM) include collagen, fibronectin, laminin, and proteoglycans. Collagen represents the primary structural protein, conferring mechanical strength to the tissue. Fibronectin and laminin are instrumental in cell adhesion and migration. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are enzymes that degrade components of the extracellular matrix, thereby facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis.
Furthermore, the TME is constituted by immune cells that perform specific functions. T-helper 1 (Th1) cells have been demonstrated to exhibit considerable antitumor efficacy through the production of a number of cytokines, including interferon gamma (IFNγ), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), interleukin-2 (IL-2), and interleukin-21 (IL-21). Moreover, Th1 cells facilitate the activation of the antitumor response by attracting cytotoxic T cells (CD8 +) and natural killer (NK) cells. Additionally, these cells secrete granzymes and perforin, which contribute to the destruction of target cells. T-helper 17 (Th17) cells, which synthesize the cytokine interleukin-17 (IL-17), have been demonstrated to promote the polarization of macrophages into an M1 phenotype and to attract antitumor immune cells, including natural killer (NK) cells and CD8 + T cells.
T-helper 2 (Th2) cells have been demonstrated to possess both antitumor and immunosuppressive properties. They secrete interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-13 (IL-13), which activate natural killer (NK) cells and promote the polarization of macrophages to the M2 phenotype, thereby supporting tissue repair and immune regulation. However, Th2 cells have also been demonstrated to impede B-cell activation and the generation of cytokines that suppress antitumor responses. Regulatory T cells (Tregs), which are present within tumors, exert an inhibitory effect on antitumor responses by suppressing the activity of T cells that produce cytokines such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and interleukin-10 (IL-10). Additionally, Treg cells consume IL-2 and inhibit the activity of antigen-presenting cells, thereby further suppressing antitumor activity. Conversely, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) produce the enzyme indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which has the potential to suppress antitumor responses by converting tryptophan to kynurenine. Kynurenine is an immunosuppressive molecule that has been demonstrated to inhibit the activity of various immune cells, including T cells.
The TME can be classified as either ‘hot’ or ‘cold’ depending on the composition of the immune cells and the level of immune activity observed in the tumor microenvironment. A «hot» TME is characterized by a high infiltration of immune cells, particularly cytotoxic T cells. Such tumors frequently exhibit indications of inflammation and are distinguished by a high mutational burden and immunogenicity. Tumors with a «hot» TME typically demonstrate enhanced responsiveness to immunotherapy, including checkpoint inhibitors, due to the preexisting activation of the immune system against the tumor. In contrast, «cold» TMEs exhibit low levels of infiltration by immune cells and a less pronounced inflammatory response. Tumors exhibiting low levels of immunogenicity and a reduced mutational burden are less responsive to immunotherapy. Therapies that enhance immune cell infiltration and activation represent a promising avenue for the treatment of cold tumors.
A comprehensive understanding of the composition, structure, and function of the tumor microenvironment (TME), as well as the interactions between its elements, is essential for the development of novel cancer treatment strategies and the enhancement of existing therapeutic approaches.

Mechanisms of development of tumor resistance
The development of resistance to tumor therapy is a complex and multifaceted process, the intricacies of which are not yet fully understood. One of the most prevalent causes of resistance to CAR-T cell therapy is the heterogeneity of antigens displayed on the surface of tumor cells. This can result in partial or complete evasion of the immune system, thereby impeding the ability of immune cells to target and destroy the tumor.
Additionally, the immunosuppressive TME, as well as the poor infiltration of CAR-T cells into the tumor stroma and the low levels of immune cell persistence within the tumor due to the aggressive local environment, such as hypoxia, nutritional deficiency, and acidosis, contribute to this resistance. Disrupted cellular interactions within the TME hinder CAR-T cell function. Loss of co-stimulatory signals critical for T cell activation, presence of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as CTLA-4 and PD-L1 collectively dampen CAR-T cell efficacy against solid tumors. This may also contribute to the development of resistance tumor cells. The TME can also form barriers to the infiltration of CAR-T cells, further limiting their efficacy.

The unfavorable environment within the tumor, including lactic acid accumulation and arginine and tryptophan deficiencies, can reduce T cell mobility, proliferation, and cytotoxicity, further contributing to therapeutic resistance. The efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy is limited by the inherent resistance of solid tumors to such treatment. These include the heterogeneity of tumor antigens, the capacity of the tumor to evade targeting by CAR-T cells, the presence of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and the existence of a fibrous tumor capsule. Furthermore, the depletion and anergy of CAR-T cells may also contribute to the resistance of solid tumors to this type of therapy.
The cellular composition of CAR-T cell therapies significantly influences their survival and efficacy in solid tumors. These drugs contain various T cell populations, including naive T cells (TN), central memory T cells (TSCM), central memory T cells (TCM), memory effector T cells (TIN), effector T cells (TEFF), and terminally differentiated T cells (TEMRA). Each of these populations has distinct metabolic profiles and cytotoxic capabilities that influence their ability to persist in the TME and exert antitumor effects.
Studies have shown that T cell subpopulations preferentially utilize different metabolic pathways. For example, T cells that rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), such as TN, TSCM, and TCM, exhibit self-renewal, stemness, and proliferative capabilities. Conversely, T cells powered by glycolysis, including TIN and TEFF, exhibit enhanced cytotoxic function but may undergo rapid aging, as seen in TEMRA cells.
The limitations of CAR-T therapy related to the tumor and its TME are not the only concerns. Cytokine storms, neurotoxicities, allergic reactions, “on-target/off-tumor” toxicity, tumor lysis syndromes, and graft-versus-host diseases are among the most common adverse reactions and complications that can occur during CAR-T therapy. To address these challenges, novel therapeutic strategies are being devised with the aim of enhancing the safety of CAR-T cells. To mitigate toxicity, enhance the efficacy of therapy, and circumvent the emergence of resistance, it has been postulated that a combination treatment approach, integrating radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and novel immunotherapy modalities (such as CAR-T), may prove advantageous. The combination of strategies may overcome limitations and improve the effectiveness of CAR-T therapy against solid tumors.

Chemotherapy for solid tumors
Chemotherapy represents the primary treatment for cancer, with its use being prevalent across the majority of cancer types. The pharmacological actions on tumor cells can be classified as cytostatic, which impedes and arrests tumor growth, and cytotoxic, which eradicates the tumor. A plethora of pharmaceuticals exist that target various aspects of cancer therapy. The most commonly used drugs include alkylating antineoplastic drugs (e.g., B-chlorethylamine derivatives, nitrosourea, and platinum drugs) and antimetabolites (e.g., purine and pyrimidine antagonists). Modern chemotherapeutic drugs include topoisomerase inhibitors, spindle formation inhibitors, intercalants, and targeted drugs based on gold and specific antibodies. The primary disadvantage of the majority of chemotherapy drugs is their systemic exposure and the adverse reactions that result. Moreover, it is important to note that each individual’s sensitivity to chemotherapy drugs is unique and requires careful analysis. To address these challenges, rigorous guidelines for selecting a chemotherapy regimen are employed, including individual dose calculation, course duration, administration method, and the utilization of contemporary targeted chemotherapy agents that exhibit reduced toxicity to the body.
In the treatment of solid tumors with chemotherapy, the combination of different chemotherapy drugs plays an important role in achieving the desired therapeutic effect and preventing resistance to chemotherapy. Examples of bioinformatics tools currently under development include those designed to assess the interaction of different chemotherapy agents when used in combination. Chemotherapy can overcome the limitations of CAR-T cells and enhance the tumor’s sensitivity to cell therapy. For example, cyclophosphamide has been demonstrated to reduce the number of suppressor cells within the TME, thereby promoting the growth of cytotoxic effector T cells (CD8 +) within the tumor. Carboplatin has been demonstrated to increase macrophage polarization and to reduce the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 checkpoint proteins on the surface of cancer cells. Furthermore, paclitaxel/doxetaxel facilitates the infiltration of CAR-T and immune cells into tumors, leading to the destruction of the tumor stroma and the creation of a more conducive TME for immune cell activity. This ultimately results in the cessation of tumor growth. Moreover, dacarbazine stimulates the secretion of cytokines by macrophages and tumor cells, thereby attracting a greater number of immune cells.
Nevertheless, it is important to note that while chemotherapy has positive treatment effects, it also carries significant side effects related to acute and chronic toxicity. Therefore, it is essential to implement careful monitoring and management strategies to mitigate these adverse effects.

Radiation therapy for solid tumors
Radiation therapy involves exposing tumor cells to high-energy radiation in order to eliminate them. This process causes ionization of particles within the cells, leading to local heating, changes in metabolism, and damage to the genetic material of tumor cells, ultimately resulting in their death. The efficacy of radiation therapy is contingent upon the differential radiosensitivity of healthy cells and tumor cells. Rapidly dividing cells, such as tumor cells and stem cells, exhibit higher sensitivity to radiation due to their elevated mitotic activity. Conversely, highly differentiated cells with low proliferative activity are more resistant to radiation, as they possess the capacity to repair damage.
Radiation therapy can be categorized as neoadjuvant (preoperative, aimed at reducing tumor size) or adjuvant (aimed at eliminating residual single tumor cells post-surgery). Neoadjuvant therapy can render inoperable tumors operable, while adjuvant therapy can help prevent the spread of metastasis. Radiation therapy is a standalone treatment that can be used for localized tumors, such as skin carcinomas and head and neck cancer, as well as in combination with other therapies for locally advanced cancer, including breast cancer and sarcoma.
The therapeutic effect of radiation therapy is based on two mechanisms of action: direct damage to the DNA of cancer cells and indirect DNA damage caused by free radicals. The latter is a consequence of the ionization or excitation of the water component of cells when they are exposed to radiation. Over time, cancer cell death occurs when cells are exposed to radiation, and this process is influenced by a number of factors. This can result in a number of different outcomes, including apoptosis or other forms of cell death, such as mitotic catastrophe, necrosis, autophagy, or aging. In essence, apoptosis represents the most prevalent form of cell death that arises as a consequence of radiation exposure. Furthermore, radiation exposure has been demonstrated to enhance the expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules, which are proteins that facilitate the recognition and destruction of cancer cells by immune cells.
Nevertheless, in certain instances, radiation therapy may also exert a beneficial influence on the tumor. The apoptotic cancer cells induced by radiation can activate M2 macrophages, which then release various anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β. Additionally, irradiated tumors have been observed to attract a considerable number of CD11b + Gr1 + neutrophils. TGFβ present in the TME has been demonstrated to promote the growth of pro-tumorous neutrophils (N2), activating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), SMAD, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-Akt, and other signaling pathways. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that irradiation may result in an elevated expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells. It can impede the capacity of activated T cells and natural killer cells to combat these types of cancer. Overall, radiotherapy is a crucial form of cancer treatment that, when used in conjunction with other methods, can address issues related to resistance and relapse.

Combined use of chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and CAR-T cells
The combined use of multiple therapies to treat solid tumors requires a strategic approach
The development of combined therapies for solid tumors has created promising prospects for more effective cancer treatment. Combined treatment strategies enhance the safety and efficacy of CAR-T cells by altering the TME, improving CAR-T cell tumor recognition, increasing immune and CAR-T cell proliferative activity, stimulating immune cells, inhibiting immunosuppressive molecules in the microenvironment, blocking tumor immune escape mechanisms, targeting multiple antigens, and reducing CAR-T cell therapy toxicity. Combination therapy is illustrated in Fig. 2.
Chemotherapy modulates the tumor microenvironment (TME) and affects the metabolism of tumor cells, making them more sensitive to chemotherapy and enhancing CAR-T cell infiltration and survival. In addition, radiation therapy reduces the size of the tumor. This makes the tumor more susceptible to chemotherapy and cell therapy.
The combination of chemotherapy and CAR-T cell therapy can reduce the number of chemotherapy cycles and minimize the negative impact on the human body. Before CAR-T cell therapy is initiated, a lymphodepleting chemotherapy regimen, typically consisting of cyclophosphamide and fludarabine, is administered 5–6 days prior. This regimen is designed to create an immunologically favorable environment for the proliferation and survival of CAR-T cells. Cyclophosphamide depletes Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). This reduces their immunosuppressive influence in the TME. Fludarabine helps reduce the total lymphocyte count, thereby reducing the patient’s immune response to the infused CAR-T cells. This allows for better expansion and persistence of the CAR-T cells in vivo.
Chemical compounds that enter tumor cells can increase the expression of specific antigens on their surface, making them more visible to immune cells. Key antigens that are upregulated include DAMPs (damage-associated molecular patterns), HMGB1 (high-mobility group box 1), MIC (MHC class I polypeptide-related sequence), and RAET1/LBP (retinoic acid early transcript 1/liver-specific protein). When tumor cells are damaged, they release DAMPs such as ATP, HMGB1, and heat shock proteins, which serve as “danger signals” that attract immune cells to the tumor site and activate the immune response. HMGB1 promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhances antigen presentation by binding to receptors on dendritic cells and macrophages such as TLR4 (Toll-like receptor 4) and RAGE (receptor for advanced glycation end products). Similarly, MIC and RAET1/LBP are ligands for the NKG2D receptor on natural killer (NK) cells and some T cells, facilitating the recognition and destruction of tumor cells. Several studies have demonstrated the synergistic effects of combining chemotherapy with CAR-T cell therapy. For example, a standard dose of carboplatin has been shown to increase the expression of DAMPs and other stress-induced antigens on tumor cells, which in turn enhances the efficacy of Erb-B-directed CAR-T cells. Carboplatin induces immunogenic cell death, resulting in the release of DAMPs such as calreticulin and ATP, which enhance dendritic cell recruitment and activation and subsequent T cell responses. In addition, the chemotherapeutic agent paclitaxel has been shown to upregulate MIC and RAET1/LBP. This increases the susceptibility of tumor cells to NK cell-mediated lysis.
In addition, chemotherapy can affect the TME by reducing its immunosuppressive properties. The primary mechanisms for reducing microenvironmental immunosuppression involve the effects of chemical compounds on tumor-associated Treg cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Chemotherapeutic agents can inhibit immunosuppressants such as IL-10, TGFβ, and immune checkpoint molecules, thereby improving the efficacy and persistence of CAR-T cells. At the same time, chemotherapy drugs have a greater effect on immunosuppressive cells than on normal regulatory T cells, thereby maintaining an adequate immune response and enhancing its strength. Studies suggest that initial chemotherapy with cyclophosphamide significantly increases the durability and efficacy of anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy. Additionally, chemotherapy-induced increased expression of mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) receptors on tumor cells leads to increased cytotoxicity of T cells.
The combination of cyclophosphamide (Cy) and fludarabine (Flu) is the most widely used lymphodepleting chemotherapy regimen in combination with CAR-T cells. This combination significantly enhances the efficacy of CAR-T cells because Flu decreases the anti-CAR immune response, increases homeostatic cytokine levels, and decreases the synthesis of the immunosuppressive IDO in TME. Another study used bendamustine to treat patients with Cy-resistant B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma. It was found to be less toxic and cause fewer side effects compared to Cy. In addition, another study showed that Bendamustine in combination with Flu was more effective than Bendamustine or Cy/Flu alone in the treatment of Hodgkin’s lymphoma with anti-CD30 CAR-T cells. At present, efforts are being made to optimize lymphodepletion chemotherapy protocols and the exploration of potential alternative compounds.
By inhibiting autoimmune and immunosuppressive cells, the combined use of chemotherapy and CAR-T cells increases the persistence of CAR-T cells in the body and the efficacy of the therapy. In addition, CAR-T cells can improve the efficacy of chemotherapy by reducing or eliminating the resistance of ovarian tumor cells through the release of IFNγ. Research suggests that chemoresistance may be caused by glutathione and cysteine produced by fibroblasts. IFNγ from CAR-T cells can alter the metabolism of fibroblasts, thereby restoring tumor sensitivity to chemotherapy through the JAK/STAT pathway.
Another way to improve the effectiveness of chemotherapy is to encourage macrophages to produce chemicals that help cancer cells bind to the T cells that are fighting the disease. After chemotherapy, some cancer cells also produce a chemical called CCL5 that attracts T cells to the area where the tumor is located. These T cells then produce IFNγ, which stimulates the growth of macrophages and other immune cells, such as dendritic cells, in the area. Macrophages and dendritic cells then produce another chemical called CXCL9. This chemical attracts even more T cells with a specific type of receptor (CXCR3) to the tumor.
Recent research has shown that certain chemotherapy drugs, such as taxanes (e.g., paclitaxel and docetaxel) and vinca alkaloids (e.g., vinblastine and vincristine), can increase the immunogenicity of tumor cells. These drugs can increase the exposure of calreticulin on the surface of tumor cells, a process known as immunogenic cell death (ICD). Calreticulin promotes phagocytosis of dying tumor cells and subsequent release of tumor antigens by acting as an “eat me” signal to dendritic cells and macrophages. Calreticulin exposure is typically associated with the endoplasmic reticulum, but during ICD it translocates to the cell surface. This exposure is often accompanied by the release of other danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as ATP and HMGB1 (high-mobility group box protein 1). ATP acts as a chemoattractant for dendritic cells, while HMGB1 binds to toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on dendritic cells, facilitating antigen presentation and T cell activation. Taxanes and vinca alkaloids induce these effects by disrupting microtubule dynamics, leading to mitotic arrest and apoptosis. This process increases the visibility of tumor cells to the immune system, facilitating their recognition and destruction by immune cells. The increased presence of tumor antigens in the microenvironment results in more robust activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), which are critical for targeting and eliminating residual tumor cells. Paclitaxel has been shown to induce the translocation of calreticulin to the cell surface while promoting the release of ATP and HMGB1. This creates a pro-inflammatory environment. This supports the recruitment and activation of dendritic cells and CTLs. Similarly, vincristine has been observed to enhance calreticulin exposure and increase the immunogenicity of tumor cells. This improves the overall efficacy of subsequent immunotherapies, such as CAR-T cell therapy.
In a clinical trial combining radiotherapy and CAR-T cell therapy, radiotherapy was used as a bridge treatment to reduce tumor burden and thereby enhance the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy. This approach not only improved tumor control and response rates, but also significantly reduced severe toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity. In addition, radiation therapy altered the TME to increase immune cell infiltration and tumor antigen expression, further enhancing the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy.

Conclusions
Over the past decade, there has been a profound transformation in the methodologies and strategies employed in the treatment of cancer. While traditional drugs, such as chemotherapy, target tumors in a general manner, new approaches to the treatment of solid tumors focus on targeted effects, thereby minimizing the negative impact of treatment. Although the issue of tumor resistance persists, there have been successful advancements in addressing it. The current evidence indicates that the combination of chemotherapy and CAR-T cells has the potential to enhance antitumor therapy for the treatment of solid tumors. This has paved the way for further research into their combined use, including in combination with radiation therapy.
