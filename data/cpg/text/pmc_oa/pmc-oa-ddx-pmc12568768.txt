Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced MRI in the Evaluation of Soft Tissue Tumors and Tumor-Like Lesions: Technical Principles and Clinical Applications
Dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI is an advanced imaging technique that involves intravenous administration of a contrast agent followed by serial imaging to characterize temporal enhancement patterns. This technique provides essential information on tissue vascularity, perfusion, and capillary permeability, which are essential for characterizing soft tissue lesions. DCE-MRI plays a valuable role in differentiating benign from malignant lesions, distinguishing neoplastic from non-neoplastic conditions, evaluating histological grades, and monitoring post-treatment changes by enabling both qualitative and quantitative assessments of tissue enhancement dynamics. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the technical principles of DCE-MRI, summarizes current analytical approaches, and discusses its clinical applications in the evaluation of soft tissue tumors and tumor-like lesions.

INTRODUCTION
Soft tissue lesions encompass a wide range of pathological conditions, including non-neoplastic, benign, and malignant tumors. In clinical practice, soft tissue masses are often assumed to be neoplastic. However, distinguishing actual tumors from tumor-like lesions—such as ganglia, hematomas, foreign body granulomas, and vascular malformations—presents a significant diagnostic challenge.
Accurate differentiation between benign and malignant soft tissue tumors is essential for appropriate clinical management and treatment planning. The 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of soft tissue tumors categorizes them as benign, intermediate (locally aggressive or rarely metastasizing), or malignant. However, many soft tissue lesions are not included in this classification system, and the imaging features of these unclassified lesions are often underrepresented in the literature.
Conventional MRI, particularly contrast enhancement, is pivotal for evaluating soft tissue lesions. However, conventional imaging techniques are often limited in their ability to distinguish malignant from benign entities or neoplastic from non-neoplastic lesions. Dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI is an advanced imaging technique that acquires sequential MR images over time following the administration of a contrast agent, thereby enabling the analysis of temporal enhancement patterns. This method provides not only high-resolution morphological information, but also functional insights into tissue perfusion, vascularity, capillary permeability, and interstitial space characteristics. These parameters may reflect underlying tumor biology and angiogenesis, offering potential utility in lesion characterization and treatment monitoring.
In this review, we examine the potential utility of DCE-MRI in the noninvasive characterization and differentiation of various soft tissue lesions, particularly in distinguishing benign from malignant or tumor-like entities.

TECHNICAL OVERVIEW OF DCE-MRI

Image Acquisition
DCE-MRI involves acquiring serial images before, during, and after intravenous administration of a contrast agent. Accurate assessment of perfusion-related parameters requires precise synchronization of contrast injection with image acquisition. To capture enhancement kinetics reliably, a high temporal resolution is needed, typically achieved with acquisition intervals of 5–10 seconds. This is most commonly performed using fat-suppressed three-dimensional (3D) T1-weighted gradient-echo volumetric sequences, which enable depiction of rapid enhancement dynamics.
Before dynamic scanning, the pre-contrast T1 of the tumor tissue should be determined using a T1 mapping sequence (e.g., the variable flip angle method). This pre-contrast T1 calibration step is essential for converting MRI signal intensities into absolute gadolinium concentrations. A standard dose of gadolinium-based contrast (usually 0.1 mmoL/kg) is administered via a rapid, consistent injection, commonly at 2–3 mL/s using a power injector, followed by a saline flush. Rapid injection ensures a well-defined bolus arrival and a sharp contrast uptake curve, which are critical for accurately capturing the peak enhancement and early wash-in slope. Maintaining a consistent injection rate and timing across patients helps minimize variability in DCE data. The DCE scan should continue long enough to capture the complete wash-in phase and at least part of the washout phase. For soft tissue lesions, a scan duration of approximately 5–6 minutes is commonly used after contrast injection. Shorter acquisitions (e.g., 2–3 minutes) risk missing the washout phase, which can compromise parameter accuracy and repeatability.
Recent advancements in DCE-MRI, such as ultrafast acquisition enabled by acceleration techniques (e.g., compressed sensing or parallel imaging), have significantly improved temporal resolution without sacrificing spatial detail. Ultrafast DCE-MRI enables the detection of rapid contrast uptake in the early arterial phase, providing additional diagnostic information compared with conventional protocols. These developments have enhanced the ability of DCE-MRI to assess tumor vascularity, characterize soft tissue lesions more precisely, and predict histological grade and tumor aggressiveness. By integrating advanced pulse sequences and accelerated imaging techniques, DCE-MRI continues to evolve as a powerful tool for functional assessment of soft tissue tumors and tumor-like lesions.
In practice, the DCE-MRI protocol for soft tissue tumors should be standardized and optimized to reliably capture contrast dynamics. A typical protocol would include pre-contrast T1 mapping, followed by a fast 3D T1-weighted dynamic sequence with a temporal resolution of approximately 5–10 seconds, acquired for about 5 minutes during and after a bolus injection to ensure full lesion coverage.

Pharmacokinetic Models for DCE-MRI Analysis
Quantitative analysis of DCE-MRI requires pharmacokinetic modeling to accurately characterize the distribution of contrast agents within tissues and derive physiologically relevant parameters. Several tracer kinetic models have been developed for this purpose, each providing distinct insights into tumor microcirculation and vascular properties.
The most widely adopted pharmacokinetic model in DCE-MRI is the Tofts model, a standard one-compartment Kety model that assumes negligible intravascular contribution to tissue contrast concentration. This model yields two key parameters: the volume transfer constant (Ktrans) and the volume fraction of the extravascular extracellular space (Ve). The extended or modified Tofts model (ETM, also referred to as EXTOFTS) further incorporates the vascular plasma volume fraction (Vp) to account for the intravascular signal component, making it particularly suitable for soft tissue tumors with substantial vascular compartments. Recent studies have demonstrated that the modified Tofts model is the most frequently applied in clinical and research settings. Model selection depends largely on tumor vascularity: for weakly vascularized tumors, the simpler Tofts model is generally sufficient, whereas highly vascular tumors require the extended model to avoid biased parameter estimates.
Beyond these classical models, several advanced approaches have been developed to enhance accuracy and physiological relevance. The approximated tissue homogeneity, conventional compartmental, and distributed parameter (DP) models incorporate both spatial and temporal dynamics of contrast agent distribution. These models enable a more refined assessment of tissue perfusion and permeability, thereby potentially improving diagnostic performance compared with conventional models. Among them, the DP model is particularly noteworthy for its ability to separately quantify blood flow and vascular permeability by introducing additional parameters, including Vp and mean transit time (MTT). This capability reduces the confounding effects of altered tumor microenvironments inherent in conventional models and provides a more comprehensive characterization of tumor vascularity.
Ultimately, the selection of an appropriate pharmacokinetic model should be guided by the distribution pattern of the contrast agent within the lesion, as appropriate model selection is essential for reliable extraction of perfusion parameters and optimization of the clinical utility of DCE-MRI.

Arterial Input Function
In quantitative DCE-MRI, accurate modeling of tissue perfusion requires the definition of the arterial input function (AIF), which represents the time-dependent concentration of the contrast agent within a feeding artery. The AIF serves as a critical reference in pharmacokinetic modeling, enabling the estimation of tissue-specific perfusion and permeability parameters.
The AIF is typically determined by directly measuring signal intensity changes in a major artery located within the imaging field of view (FOV), preferably near the lesion of interest. In clinical practice, AIFs can be derived using several approaches, including individually measured, population-averaged, reference tissue-based, and jointly estimated AIF methods. Each approach has distinct advantages and limitations in terms of accuracy, reproducibility, and applicability across different anatomical regions.
Unlike applications in relatively anatomically consistent sites such as the breast or prostate, soft tissue lesions can arise in diverse anatomical locations. This anatomical heterogeneity introduces variability in arterial size and position within the FOV, complicating the acquisition of an individual AIF. Therefore, when performing individual AIF measurements in soft tissue imaging, it is crucial to select an artery that exhibits homogeneous, robust enhancement with minimal partial volume effects. In cases where a suitable artery is not present within the FOV, a population-averaged AIF is commonly employed. This method relies on previously characterized enhancement profiles, which are often categorized into low, intermediate, or high types based on the characteristics of the vascular input curve.
Importantly, the choice of AIF type and the extraction technique can significantly influence the resulting quantitative perfusion parameters. Variability in AIF selection, along with differences in region-of-interest (ROI) measurement strategies, can affects the reliability and comparability of DCE-MRI–derived kinetic parameters. Therefore, standardization of AIF acquisition—ideally with automated AIF extraction to reduce operator bias—and analysis methods is critical for ensuring consistent and accurate interpretation of DCE-MRI data in the evaluation of soft tissue lesions.

ANALYTICAL APPROACHES
DCE-MRI analysis is generally categorized into three methodological approaches: qualitative, semiquantitative, and quantitative.
Accurate ROI placement is essential for reliable assessment. ROIs should be positioned within the most solid and avidly enhancing regions of the lesion while avoiding areas of hemorrhage, necrosis, or perilesional edema. Narrow volumes of interest or focal ROIs are preferred, as they provide more precise sampling and reduce contamination from non-representative tissue components.

Qualitative Analysis
Qualitative analysis represents the most fundamental approach to interpreting DCE-MRI data, relying on subjective visual assessment of time–intensity curves (TICs), which depict signal intensity changes over time following contrast administration. These curves reflect lesion enhancement kinetics and provide indirect information regarding tissue perfusion, vascular permeability, and the volume of extracellular extravascular space (EES).
TIC morphology is influenced by various physiological and pathological factors, including vascular density, vessel permeability, and interstitial space characteristics. Several classification systems have been proposed in the literature, with many authors categorizing TICs into three to five types based on enhancement patterns. Typically, TICs are classified according to curve morphology, which includes an initial rapid or gradual enhancement phase followed by either continuous increase, plateau, or slow-to-rapid washout (Fig. 1). Malignant lesions generally exhibit rapid arterial enhancement due to high vascularity and reduced interstitial volume, followed by either a plateau or slow-to-rapid washout phase. In contrast, benign lesions tend to demonstrate delayed and gradual enhancement, reflecting slower perfusion and a more abundant interstitial matrix. However, substantial overlap in TIC patterns has been reported, and certain lesions may not conform to classical enhancement profiles. Despite these limitations, qualitative TIC evaluation remains a valuable initial step in the characterization of soft tissue lesions and can assist in differential diagnosis, particularly when interpreted alongside conventional MRI findings and semiquantitative or quantitative DCE-MRI parameters.

Semiquantitative Analysis
Semiquantitative analysis involves the extraction of numerical parameters from the shape of the TIC without pharmacokinetic modeling, serving as a useful tool for the initial assessment of lesion vascularity and complementing qualitative and quantitative approaches in comprehensive DCE-MRI interpretation. This method is less complex and can be performed with shorter acquisition times, as it does not require AIF measurements.
Novel kinetic parameters derived from ultrafast DCE-MRI have expanded the clinical utility of this technique. For example, the maximum slope (MS, %/s) quantifies the steepness of the upslope of the TIC, while time to enhancement (TTE, s) represents the delay between contrast arrival in the aorta and the onset of lesion enhancement (Fig. 2).

Commonly Used Parameters Include
• Time to peak (TTP, s): Interval between contrast injection and the point of maximum signal enhancement.
• Maximum enhancement (ME): The highest signal intensity reached during the enhancement process.
• MS (%/s): The steepest rate of signal increase, reflecting the wash-in phase.
• Initial area under the curve (iAUC): The integrated signal intensity or concentration over a specified time period, typically within the first 60–90 seconds post-injection.
• Area under the TIC (AUTIC or AUC-TC): The total area under the enhancement curve, representing cumulative contrast uptake.
• Washout slope: The rate of signal decrease following peak enhancement.
These parameters reflect various physiological characteristics, including perfusion rate, vascular permeability, and interstitial volume, and may assist in differentiating benign from malignant lesions. For example, Zhang et al. reported that malignant soft tissue tumors demonstrated significantly shorter TTP and higher ME, AUC-TC, and MS values than benign lesions, suggesting the potential utility of these parameters for tumor differentiation. Since different studies have proposed varying cutoff values for each parameter, no absolute threshold has been defined for differentiating benign from malignant tumors. However, malignant tumors generally demonstrate higher MS or ME values and shorter TTE or TTP values than benign tumors. Furthermore, Li et al. found that iAUC and TTP were significant predictors of tumor grade: high-grade sarcomas exhibited higher iAUC values, indicating greater early enhancement volume, and shorter TTP values, indicating faster TTP enhancement, than low-grade tumors.
Another valuable semiquantitative parameter is the ME ratio, defined as the peak signal intensity relative to baseline, and serves as an indicator of contrast delivery efficiency and capillary density.

Quantitative Analysis
Quantitative analysis applies pharmacokinetic modeling to evaluate the exchange of contrast agents between the vascular compartment and EES, enabling objective assessment of tissue perfusion and vascular permeability.
When using the Tofts model, three key parameters can be derived: Ktrans, the rate constant (Kep), and Ve. These parameters provide insights into tumor microvascular characteristics and are less susceptible to interobserver variability than qualitative assessments, making them reliable for tumor grading and characterization.
• Ktrans (min-1): Measures the rate at which contrast agent moves from the intravascular space into the EES, influenced by both blood flow and vascular permeability. High values indicate highly angiogenic or well-perfused tumor tissue and are associated with aggressive malignancies with leaky neovasculature.
• Kep (min-1): Represents the reflux rate of contrast from the EES back into the plasma and is calculated as Ktrans divided by Ve. Elevated Kep values suggest rapid exchange kinetics, which correlate with increased tumor cellularity and vascularity. High Kep indicates rapid contrast washout, which can occur in tumors with high perfusion and relatively small EES volume.
• Ve (%): Denotes the proportion of EES relative to total tissue volume. This parameter is influenced by tissue architecture, necrosis, and extracellular matrix composition. High Ve values indicate large interstitial spaces and may reflect necrotic or poorly differentiated tumor regions; however, their reliability can be limited by confounding factors such as peritumoral edema.
Additionally, Vp is a parameter introduced in extended pharmacokinetic models and reflects microvascular volume. Although often negligible in most tissues, Vp can be significant in highly vascular tumors. Malignant lesions have been shown to exhibit significantly higher Vp values than benign lesions.
Significantly, the diagnostic performance of DCE-MRI may be further enhanced by combining quantitative parameters with morphological MRI features and TIC-based kinetic analysis, particularly in solitary soft tissue lesions that exhibit non-specific findings on conventional MRI or when clinical information or other imaging modalities are unavailable (Fig. 3).

CLINICAL APPLICATIONS OF DCE-MRI

Soft Tissue Tumors

General Interpretation
The angiogenic and perfusion characteristics of soft tissue tumors are key determinants of their imaging behavior. Malignant tumors, including sarcomas, metastases, lymphomas, and superficial carcinomas, are typically characterized by rich vascularity and reduced interstitial space. These tumors commonly demonstrate early enhancement followed by a plateau or washout phase, along with elevated quantitative parameters such as Ktrans, Kep, and iAUC. These findings are consistent with high angiogenic activity and increased vascular density and permeability. Conversely, Ve values are often lower in malignant lesions, likely due to higher cellular density and reduced interstitial volume compared with benign tumors, which tend to have a more expanded extracellular matrix.
In addition to quantitative parameters, semiquantitative metrics, such as iAUC, TTP, and TTE, have demonstrated diagnostic value. These parameters are associated with tumor size, vascularity, necrosis, and invasive potential. Several studies have shown that iAUC and TTP can effectively differentiate between low- and high-grade soft tissue sarcomas. Furthermore, Li et al. reported that DCE-MRI parameters—including Ktrans, Kep, Ve, TTP, and iAUC—varied significantly according to the Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) histological grades of sarcomas (Fig. 4). Although numerous studies have proposed a wide range of semiquantitative and quantitative values, making it difficult to establish a single universally meaningful threshold, many studies have suggested that these parameters, in conjunction with TIC morphology, are valuable for differentiating benign from malignant tumors and low- from high-grade soft tissue lesions (Table 1).

Myxoid Tumors
Myxoid tumors represent a distinct subset of mesenchymal neoplasms characterized by an abundant extracellular mucinous stroma. Differentiating benign from malignant myxoid tumors can be particularly challenging due to overlapping imaging features on conventional MRI and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). Their high water content and loose myxoid matrices often produce similar signal intensities on T2-weighted images and comparable apparent diffusion coefficient values across tumor grades, thereby limiting the discriminative performance of DWI.
A recent study demonstrated that the quantitative DCE-MRI parameters—Ktrans and iAUC—were significantly higher in malignant myxoid tumors (Fig. 5) than in their benign counterparts (Fig. 6). These findings reflect increased vascular permeability and perfusion, which are more prominent in malignant lesions due to neovascularization. Incorporating DCE-MRI into the routine evaluation of myxoid soft tissue tumors may improve diagnostic accuracy and support clinical decision-making.

Lipomatous Tumors
Lipomatous tumors represent a heterogeneous group of mesenchymal neoplasms ranging from benign lipomas to malignant liposarcomas, with intermediate entities such as atypical lipomatous tumors (ALTs). Although they originate from adipose tissue, these tumors exhibit varying degrees of cellular atypia, proliferative activity, and vascularity.
Conventional imaging often reveals overlapping features among these entities. For example, although thick or irregular internal septa may suggest ALTs or well-differentiated liposarcomas, they are not definitive markers. Therefore, accurate differentiation based solely on MRI morphology remains challenging. Definitive diagnosis requires immunohistochemical staining for markers such as murine double minute 2 (MDM2) and cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4). Additionally, myxoid liposarcomas may mimic benign lipomatous tumors on imaging, further complicating radiologic interpretation (Fig. 5).
DCE-MRI has emerged as a valuable noninvasive imaging modality for assessing vascularity and internal architecture in lipomatous lesions. Although qualitative DCE-MRI parameters alone have not consistently improved diagnostic accuracy in differentiating benign from malignant lipomatous tumors, quantitative analysis offers important insights into tissue perfusion and capillary permeability (Fig. 7).
In non-myxoid lipomatous tumors, multiple parameters, including Ktrans, Kep, and iAUC, showed significant differences between benign and malignant groups (Fig. 4). Furthermore, a recent study employing an ultrafast DCE-MRI technique reported that TTE demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance, while additional parameters such as Ve and iAUC also significantly differentiated benign from malignant lipomatous tumors.

Benign Tumors Mimicking Malignancy on DCE-MRI
Although DCE-MRI provides valuable functional information for differentiating malignant from benign soft tissue lesions, certain benign entities may exhibit imaging features that mimic malignancy. This overlap is particularly observed in benign lesions with dense vascularity, such as hemangiomas, neurofibromas, and nodular fasciitis (NF).
Hemangiomas are vascular tumors that may demonstrate early, rapid enhancement and washout kinetics on DCE-MRI, a pattern often associated with malignant tumors. Their high vascular component affects perfusion and permeability parameters, such as Ktrans and Kep, potentially elevating these values and complicating differentiation from soft tissue sarcomas.
Nevertheless, DCE-MRI has proven effective in characterizing vascular anomalies based on flow dynamics. In evaluating peripheral vascular malformations, DCE-MRI can reliably differentiate high-flow lesions (e.g., arteriovenous malformations) from low-flow lesions (e.g., venous malformations). Studies have demonstrated that high-flow vascular malformations exhibit significantly shorter arterial lesion enhancement and contrast rise times than low-flow malformations. This temporal analysis of enhancement provides valuable clues for differentiating hemangiomas from other hypervascular lesions, such as metastases (Fig. 8).
NF is another benign lesion that frequently mimics malignancy due to its rapid growth, cellular histology, and dynamic enhancement behavior. Post-contrast MRI often reveals either homogeneous or peripheral enhancement with variable signal intensity, depending on the histological subtype. Cellular variants may demonstrate intermediate signal intensity on T2-weighted images and strong contrast enhancement. On DCE-MRI, NF can exhibit diverse enhancement kinetics, including rapid initial enhancement followed by washout or persistent enhancement, overlapping with typical TICs of malignant tumors (Fig. 9). Accordingly, histopathological confirmation remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, particularly in lesions with ambiguous imaging features.
Tenosynovial giant cell tumors (TGCTs) can be classified into localized and diffuse types. On DCE-MRI, diffuse TGCT may demonstrate TIC patterns characterized by rapid initial enhancement followed by a plateau phase, consistent with a hypervascular lesion (Fig. 10). Additionally, localized TGCT may exhibit homogeneous enhancement and TIC patterns with rapid enhancement and slow washout, reflecting increased capillary permeability due to synovial inflammation.
Myositis ossificans (MO) is a self-limiting benign neoplasm composed of spindle cells and osteoblasts, characterized by heterotopic ossification within soft tissues, typically affecting large muscles such as the quadriceps or brachialis. MO can radiologically mimic soft tissue sarcomas, particularly in its early stages before calcification becomes apparent. The imaging characteristics of MO evolve over time. In the early proliferative phase, lesions may appear hypervascular with peripheral rim enhancement and central hypovascularity on contrast-enhanced imaging. During this stage, DCE-MRI findings may overlap with those of malignant soft tissue tumors. Some early-phase MO cases have demonstrated TIC patterns characterized by rapid enhancement followed by washout, potentially mimicking malignancy and resulting in diagnostic uncertainty (Fig. 11). However, MO lesions typically exhibit TIC with gradual and persistent enhancement, a pattern consistent with benign behavior. As the lesion matures and ossification progresses, vascularity decreases, and imaging characteristics become more specific. Awareness of the dynamic enhancement patterns of MO and their temporal evolution is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary biopsy or intervention.

Intermediate-Grade Tumors
Intermediate-grade tumors constitute a biologically heterogeneous group that displays locally aggressive behavior with low or rare metastatic potential. These lesions often demonstrate imaging features that overlap with those of malignant tumors, particularly on DCE-MRI.
Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a prototypical intermediate-grade neoplasm with aggressive local invasion. On DCE-MRI, DFSPs typically demonstrate a TIC pattern characterized by rapid initial enhancement followed by a plateau phase, indicating high vascularity and permeability. Quantitative perfusion parameters, including Ktrans, Kep, Ve, and iAUC, were significantly elevated in DFSP compared with benign fibrous lesions such as dermatofibromas and keloids, reflecting their rich cellularity, high mitotic activity, and neovascular proliferation (Fig. 12).
Desmoid-type fibromatosis (DF) is another intermediate-grade lesion that, although histologically benign, demonstrates local invasiveness with a high recurrence rate. On DCE-MRI, DF often shows strong, homogeneous contrast enhancement involving over 80% of the tumor volume. TICs frequently exhibit rapid early enhancement followed by a plateau phase, corresponding to increased vascularity and collagen content (Fig. 13). Variable enhancement patterns, such as inhomogeneous or peripheral enhancement, have also been reported, likely reflecting differences in cellularity and stromal composition.
Solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare fibroblastic mesenchymal tumors characterized by a classic hemangiopericytoma-like vascular pattern and are classified as intermediate (rarely metastasizing) and malignant according to the recent 2020 WHO classification. On imaging, the presence of a large, solid, and highly vascularized mass with a prominent vascular pedicle that displaces adjacent structures may suggest SFT. SFTs may exhibit variable TIC patterns, including a rapid initial rise in signal intensity followed by a slower increase or plateau phase (suggestive of benign but hypervascular or intermediate-grade lesions), persistent enhancement (benign lesions), and rapid enhancement with subsequent slow or rapid washout (hypervascular tumors). These TIC patterns likely reflect the vascular nature of SFTs and may help in identifying tumors with worse clinical outcomes or higher malignant potential (Fig. 14).
Overall, intermediate-grade tumors frequently exhibit DCE-MRI enhancement patterns characterized by a plateau or washout phase and elevated perfusion metrics that overlap with those of malignant lesions. Therefore, although DCE-MRI provides valuable diagnostic insights, its interpretation should be integrated with morphological MRI findings and histopathological correlation to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

Tumor-Like Lesions

Chronic Granulomatous Inflammation or Abscess
Infectious conditions such as abscesses may be difficult to distinguish from malignant lesions, such as primary cancer or metastases, on conventional MRI, particularly depending on disease stage. Several studies involving the lungs, brain, and spine have employed DCE-MRI for quantitative analysis to differentiate lesions such as tuberculomas or pyogenic abscesses from malignant lesions. These studies indicate that DCE-MRI can assist in distinguishing an abscess from a neoplasm: an abscess typically exhibits a leaky but poorly perfused rim (high Ktrans, low Vp) and a prolonged enhancement curve, consistent with chronic inflammatory vascularity, in contrast to the rich neovascular networks characteristic of malignant lesions.

Hematoma
A chronic organizing hematoma is a benign, non-neoplastic lesion that may clinically and radiologically mimic soft tissue neoplasms due to its progressive enlargement and heterogeneous internal composition. Differentiating chronic hematomas from malignant tumors is often challenging, particularly in postoperative settings or in patients with a history of trauma or surgery.
In a study by Del Grande et al., arterial enhancement was observed in only two of 12 postoperative hematomas, while the remaining 10 showed non-arterial, delayed enhancement, supporting their benign nature. In clinical settings, chronic hematomas have demonstrated slow, progressive enhancement on DCE-MRI, producing a TIC pattern consistent with that of benign lesions (Fig. 15). Importantly, the absence of early arterial enhancement and the presence of a gradual enhancement pattern strongly suggest a non-neoplastic pathology, such as chronic hematoma.

Postoperative Surveillance: Evaluation of Residual or Recurrent Sarcoma
Accurate assessment of residual disease and early detection of local recurrence are critical components of postoperative surveillance in patients who have undergone sarcoma excision. Conventional anatomical MRI often lacks specificity to distinguish recurrent tumors from postoperative changes, such as fibrosis, edema, or hematoma. DCE-MRI is a valuable technique for identifying viable malignant tissues over time.
Quantitative DCE-MRI parameters, including Ktrans, Kep, and iAUC, as well as TIC patterns, are significantly correlated with the presence of residual tumors (Fig. 16). A recent study further demonstrated that relative plasma flow (rPF) and mean transit time differ significantly between recurrent and non-recurrent lesions, with rPF showing the highest diagnostic performance. Recurrent lesions consistently exhibit elevated rPF, likely reflecting neovascularization and active tumor perfusion.
Incorporating DCE-MRI into conventional MR protocols is therefore recommended to enhance the accuracy of detecting recurrent tumors and improve the specificity of distinguishing recurrent sarcoma from postoperative scarring.

LIMITATIONS OF DCE-MRI
1) Lack of standardization: Protocols, temporal resolution, analysis models (qualitative, semiquantitative, and quantitative), and ROI placement vary considerably among institutions and studies, limiting reproducibility and comparability.
2) Technical challenges in quantitative analysis: Accurate measurement of absolute perfusion and permeability requires AIF determination, rapid 3D imaging, and appropriate correction methods, all of which can be complex to implement.
3) Limited lesion specificity: Enhancement patterns may be non-specific, as inflammatory or granulomatous lesions can mimic malignant tumors.
4) Patient cooperation requirements: Image acquisition spans several minutes and often necessitates breath-holding or motion control, which may be challenging for some patients.
5) High cost and time demands: Compared with conventional MRI, DCE-MRI involves greater expense and longer acquisition and analysis times, which may limit its routine clinical use.

CONCLUSION
While conventional MRI remains the cornerstone for evaluating soft tissue lesions, particularly those with characteristic imaging features such as typical schwannomas or lipomas, the additional use of DCE-MRI may not be necessary in all cases. However, when soft tissue lesions present with non-specific or indeterminate conventional MRI findings, especially in the absence of definitive clinical or ancillary imaging data, DCE-MRI offers valuable functional insights. By assessing tissue perfusion and microvascular characteristics, DCE-MRI provides indirect information on tumor cellularity, vascularity, and angiogenic activity, thereby supporting lesion characterization and differential diagnosis.
DCE-MRI is highly valuable for differentiating benign from malignant soft tissue tumors, distinguishing between low- and high-grade soft tissue malignancies, and evaluating residual or recurrent sarcomas versus postoperative scars. However, its widespread application is constrained by the lack of standardized protocols, the requirement for contrast agents, and the complexity of quantitative analysis. Furthermore, the clinical utility of semiquantitative and quantitative parameters remains incompletely defined, although quantitative parameters such as Ktrans and Kep generally demonstrate higher values in malignant or high-grade sarcomas.
Overall, DCE-MRI serves as an important adjunct to conventional MRI in selected cases of indeterminate soft tissue lesions or during postoperative monitoring. Its greatest utility lies in the functional characterization of microcirculation and vascular permeability, although its integration into routine clinical workflows requires further standardization and validation.

Variable time–intensity curves for DCE-MRI. SI–time curves demonstrate variable enhancement patterns depending on the hemodynamic characteristics of the lesion. The SI of the soft tissue lesion may increase gradually or rapidly after contrast administration, followed by one of three characteristic enhancement patterns: persistent, plateau, or washout (slow or rapid). These curve morphologies reflect the underlying vascularity and permeability of the lesion. DCE = dynamic contrast-enhanced, SI = signal intensity

A single soft tissue metastasis from a gastrointestinal stromal tumor in a 67-year-old woman. A: Axial T2-weighted MR image of the thigh demonstrates a well-defined nodular mass lesion (arrow) with intermediate-to-high signal intensity and an incomplete dark signal rim in the subcutaneous fat layer. B: The mass (arrow) shows strong enhancement on a fat-suppressed, contrast-enhanced axial T1-weighted image. Mild edema is present in the adjacent superficial fascia. The imaging characteristics of the mass lesion are non-specific. C: DWI and apparent diffusion coefficient maps demonstrate diffusion restriction (arrow) within the lesion. D: DCE-MRI parameters obtained by using the modified Tofts model show elevated Ktrans, Kep, Ve, and iAUC values. The time–intensity curve reveals a malignant pattern characterized by a rapid initial increase in signal intensity followed by a plateau phase. This case showed non-specific findings on conventional MRI; however, DWI and DCE-MRI revealed malignant features. Although a specific diagnosis could not be made, the lesion could be regarded as malignant. A high-grade (FNCLCC grade 3) dedifferentiated liposarcoma of the upper arm in a 53-year-old man. A: Fat-suppressed, contrast-enhanced axial T1-weighted MR image of the right upper arm shows an ill-defined, heterogeneously and strongly enhancing mass with a fascial tail sign (arrows) and internal necrosis. B: DCE-MRI parameters obtained using the modified Tofts model demonstrate elevated Ktrans and Kep values.
