A rapid evaluation of the reporting and publishing practices of patient and public involvement and engagement in health research within a UK university institute
Involving patients and the public in health research helps ensure that studies are of high quality and focus on the issues that matter most to the people affected. However, researchers do not always explain clearly how they have done this, making it hard to understand how these voices have shaped the research. This study looked at over 500 research papers published by authors from a UK Russell Group university to see how well the involvement of patients and the public was reported. Only one in five papers mentioned how patients and the public were involved in the main text, and fewer still acknowledged contributions from patients or public partners. Very few papers included patient authors or collaborators from charities and community organisations. Although most papers were freely available online, only 10% included a plain English summary to help non-specialists understand the research. To better understand these findings, the researchers worked closely with a group of patient co-authors who had experience collaborating with university researchers. Together, they developed seven recommendations to help improve how the involvement of patients and the public is reported in future studies. This work highlights the need for universities and research institutes to reflect on their practices and provide better support for researchers to work meaningfully and transparently with patients and the public. Clear and consistent reporting of the involvement of patients and the public is essential for building trust, improving research quality, and ensuring that health research truly serves the communities it aims to help.

Background
Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement is important to ensure research addresses the issues that matter most to patients and the public. However, reporting and publishing of these activities is variable and inconsistent. This rapid review aimed to assess how effectively, consistently, and transparently Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement is reported on in papers published by authors affiliated with a heath research institute at a UK Russell Group university.
Methods
A rapid review of all papers published by institute-affiliated authors (1st August 2021 to 31st December 2024) identified using PubMed. We also consulted with a group of patient co-authors to understand their experiences of being a Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement co-author in collaboration with researchers from the same institute.
Results
We retained and reviewed 523 papers for inclusion of Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement. There was significant variation in the reporting across the papers included. Overall, 21% of papers had reference to Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement activity in the main body of the paper, and 17% of papers included Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement in the acknowledgements. In terms of co-authorship, 5% of papers included an author with an affiliation to a charity/non-governmental organisation that represented Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement -related interested, e.g. Alzheimer’s Society, Caribbean and African Health Network and 1% of papers included a patient/lived-experience author. A total of 97% of papers were published open access but only 10% of articles included in this review had a plain English or lay summary. Only one paper included a published GRIPP2 form. The Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement group reviewed and contextualised our findings, supporting analysis and development of seven key recommendations to improve comprehensive, consistent, and transparent reporting of Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement in health research at an institute level.
Conclusions
This work demonstrates the challenges that Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement faces and the importance of institutions reflecting on the practices of their academics, and the structures they put in place to encourage good and equitable practices of working with patents and the public.

Background
Public involvement in research is the practice of involving members of the public, patients/services users, and family members/carers in research projects in advisory capacities. There are many different terms used to describe this and, they are often used interchangeably. However one of the more common terms is Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement (PPIE). In PPIE, research is done ‘with’ or ‘by’ patient and/or public members as active partners in different aspects of the research across the research cycle including generating research questions, grant writing, study design, data collection, analysis, evaluation, and dissemination.
In healthcare research, PPIE is considered best practice as it improves the relevance and quality of the research and ensures the findings reach the public. There has been much work done to understand and improve PPIE in health research. This has included requirements from funders to include PPIE in grant proposals and to publish research findings open access. Additionally, some journals now include the option or requirement to submit a ‘Plain English’ or lay summary with manuscript submissions, to aid understating for non-specialist audiences. Furthermore, in the UK specifically, UK Standards for Public Involvement in Research were published in 2019 to further support researchers, PPIE contributors and funders to reflect on and improve PPIE in research. Inclusion and reporting of PPIE has improved somewhat with the implementation of these policies and of the development of the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public 2 (GRIPP2) reporting guidelines, although it is important to acknowledge the critiques that have been made of the GRIPP2 in relation to risk of tokenism and reinforcing power imbalance. However, transparent, accessible, and consistent reporting is still lacking. This may be attributed to several factors including, complexities in measuring impact and engagement, funding availability to support PPIE activity, publishing systems and journal requirements, research time scales, differences across study designs and patient group preferences for inclusion.
In the UK, the Research Excellence Framework (REF) is the system for assessing the excellence of research in UK higher education institutions, providing accountability for public investment in research and informing future allocation of funding to institutions for research. Institutions are assessed through the evidencing of impact through the submission of high-quality publications articulated within impact case studies which demonstrate how the research has made an impact beyond academia. The next REF will complete in 2029 and a key change from REF2021 is an increased focus on public engagement. In the context of REF, ‘public engagement’ describes an approach to involving the public in meaningful roles in the development, uptake and/or application of research. Thus, it is important to consider how higher education institutions are involving and engaging patients and the public in the work they do and (given the focus on publications for impact case studies) how they are documenting and reporting these activities in publications.
The Wolfson Institute of Population Health (WIPH) at Queen Mary, University of London (QMUL) was formed in August 2021 with a focus on research that explores the causes of poor health and health inequalities, how to detect disease earlier, and improving the health and quality of life of individuals and populations. The institute has six centres for research: Centre for Public Health and Policy, Centre for Primary Care, Centre for Cancer Screening, Prevention and Early Diagnosis, Centre for Psychiatry and Mental Health, Centre for Evaluation and Methods, and Centre for Preventive Neurology. The institute also has a cross-cutting PPIE theme with a commitment to support infrastructure, activities and research-led activities to improve the equitable inclusion of the voices of patients and the public; striving to deliver research that is fair, useful and informed by what matters most to people from all backgrounds. This paper forms part of the activities developed within this theme.

Methods

Aim
This review aimed to assess how effectively, consistently, and transparently PPIE is reported on, in papers published by authors affiliated with the WIPH and to develop recommendations to improve reporting and publishing practices.

Review design
A rapid review designed to capture evidence related the reporting and publishing of PPIE in health research within the WIPH was selected given both time and resource limitations. Evidence-based guidance for conducting rapid reviews was followed to ensure transparency and rigor on all aspects of the research process (e.g., search strategy, study selection, data extraction, synthesis). The review is reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 (PRISMA 2020) and interim guidance for the reporting of rapid reviews.

Search strategy
PubMed was searched from 31/12/2020 (date of the last REF) and later amended to 01/08/2021 (formation of the WIPH) to present (31/12/2024). The search strategy was developed in consultation with the institute communications manager. The advanced search function was used to search for publications where authors were affiliated to the WIPH at QMUL. Articles identified were then manually screened by LP (supervised by AT and HS) to identify only articles where first and/or last author were affiliated to the institute. First and last author was chosen as the first listed author is typically responsible for the majority of the writing for publications, while the last listed author is usually the senior individual holding the responsibility for the development of the research. All publication types were included to ensure we captured the full spectrum of how PPIE is represented, conceptualised, and communicated across the whole scholarly landscape as many types of publication explicitly discuss or are relevant to PPIE practices, challenges, or development, even if not reporting empirical findings.

Screening process and data extraction
References for articles identified in the search were imported to Excel. Full text articles were initially keyword searched for data relating to PPIE and data was extracted by LP. Keywords included: Patient and Public Involvement (PPI), Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement (PPIE), patient involvement, patient engagement, patient participation, patient contribution, public involvement, public engagement, public participation, public contribution, lived experience. Data extracted included: whether the paper was published open access, whether any credited authors were either a patient, charity or associated organisation, inclusion of a GRIPP2 checklist, inclusion of a plain English/lay summary, and any other description, mention, or acknowledgement of PPIE within the manuscript. Data were extracted from all sections of the article, including but not limited to author affiliations, methods, results, discussion, and acknowledgements sections to capture any and all reporting of PPIE. Where data were extracted from was also noted. Extraction was reviewed with AT and HS.

Patient and public involvement and engagement
PPIE co-authors were consulted during the review process. Corresponding authors of papers where PPIE co-authors were identified were contacted and asked to contact their PPIE co-authors and invite them to contribute to our review. Findings were shared with PPIE contributors who responded and they shared their interpretations and their experiences of co-authoring publications alongside WIPH academics as part of a workshop or in 1-2-1 discussions. Recommendations for best practices for including PPIE member as co-authors were followed and PPIE co-authors were offered compensation for their time as per NIHR guidelines and recommendations. From the review findings and in collaboration with the PPIE co-authors, recommendations for reporting of PPIE in research and co-authorship were developed and refined. PPIE is reported in line with the GRIPP2-SF.

Results

Review statistics

Search yield
The search yielded n = 527 articles which were then screened for eligibility based on first and last author affiliation with WIPH, and duplicates were removed. From this n = 4 articles were excluded and N = 523 articles were retained. Figure 1 shows a modified PRIMSA Flow Diagram showing the review process.

Area of study
All study designs and publication types were included in the review. Where review articles were included, they were treated as the unit of analysis themselves, rather than extracting from the studies within them, and so duplication of primary study inclusion did not affect the synthesis. The three most frequent study designs were qualitative studies (n = 69), cross-sectional studies (n = 50), and cohort studies (n = 45). There were n = 82 reviews included with systematic reviews (n = 45) being the majority. Additionally, n = 16 study protocols were included. Reporting of patient and public involvement and engagement
There was significant variation in the reporting of PPIE (or lack thereof) across the papers included. Various terms were used to refer to PPIE activity and the level of detail provided also varied. Overall, n = 109 (21%) of papers had reference to PPIE activity in the main body of the paper, and n = 91 (17%) of papers included PPIE in the acknowledgements. Interestingly only one paper also included a published GRIPP2 form: it was a mixed-method study on factors influencing health status or contact with health services. However, only n = 34 (7%) of papers included an author with an affiliation as a patient author/to a PPIE group and/or to a charity/non-governmental organisation that represented PPIE-related interests, e.g. Alzheimer’s Society, Caribbean and African Health Network. Of these, n = 26 (5%) were charity/non-governmental organisation affiliated authors, n = 7 (1%) were patient/lived-experience co-authors, and n = 2 papers included both.

Accessibility to patients and the public
An important aspect of research being accessible to the public is that articles need to be open access as broadly the general public are unable to access subscription journal content behind paywalls. Of the n = 523 papers, 97% were published open access. However, another important aspect of making research accessible to patients and the public is inclusion of a plain English summary or lay summary, yet only 10% of articles included in this review had a plain English or lay summary.

Patient and public involvement and engagement
Six PPIE contributors (with a range of experience) representing four out of six of the research centres within the WIPH took part in workshops or 1-2-1 discussions with HS to review the findings and support interpretation of the results.
PPIE contributors discussed a range of positive and negative experiences. Generally, they discussed feeling meaningfully involved across the different stages of the research cycle within projects, including publication and dissemination activities, but noted some instances of involvement feeling tokenistic or lacking the support to enable meaningful involvement. PPIE contributors recognised that researchers did not always have the resources (e.g. funding) to properly involve and support PPIE and emphasised the importance of PPIE not being tokenistic or being done as a ‘box-ticking’ exercise. In discussing how papers reported PPIE, PPIE members agreed that transparency is important. For research where PPIE was conducted, it is important that researchers include this, not just in their acknowledgements, but in the methods and/or results of their papers. In research where PPIE was not conducted, it is important to also report that it did not happen and provide justification for why.
Training and support were discussed as particularly important in two ways to enabling meaningful PPIE co-authorship. Firstly, specialist training for writing for publication was considered important but can be more challenging for PPIE contributors to access (as it is often aimed at/offered for researchers and academics) and there can sometimes be significant costs attached, creating barriers to access. Additionally ongoing support was often needed for administrative tasks related to co-authorship, this included, knowing what to put in terms of affiliation, or support to set-up an Open Researcher and Contributor ID (ORCiD).
Secondly, training for researchers and academics to understand and support PPIE was also discussed as important. While the PPIE group did not give examples, the authors imagine this would go beyond the one-off training already available for academics delivering PPIE, and instead take the form of ongoing support to develop the practice in reality, helping contributors to address and work through the challenges as they arise. PPIE contributors reported sometimes having better experiences as patient advisors working with industry partners (such as pharmaceutical companies) as the companies often have specially trained people and resources to support and enable PPIE more generally. In comparison, not every research project or university department has a PPIE coordinator (a role which provides support for PPIE activities including coordinating meetings, arranging contracts/agreements and payment/expenses; typically, the first point of contact for PPIE contributors) or the funding/resources to support such a role. Further consideration is needed around the skills, resources and processes required to effectively support PPIE contributors, particularly in relation to their involvement in the preparation and authorship of manuscripts. PPIE contributors emphasised the importance of researchers taking the time to set out expectations and explaining requirements in advance of becoming a co-author. In terms of understanding the time commitment, they felt that in their experience, being asked to be an author can feel like a big task but often does not involve much writing, mainly only reviewing and or commenting on a manuscript written by the researchers. Some PPIE contributors did also express appreciation of being provided greater opportunity to be involved in more of the writing. However, PPIE contributors also highlighted that support may initially be needed to explain functions like tracked changes within Word documents if they were unfamiliar. The benefits and challenges related to technology were also discussed more broadly: online meetings often made it easier to take on PPIE roles removing the need to travel (especially for those with high symptoms burdens), however when technological difficulties were experienced, PPIE contributors lacked the IT and technology support that researchers had access to through their institutions.
Some PPIE contributors noted that their first experiences of being asked to comment on papers were challenging due to not knowing the conventions and expectations of academic writing and the complexity of the science being written about. PPIE contributors also discussed the complexity and difficulty of managing the need to be expert enough to understand the research and be able to contribute but also not so expert that they lost the lay perspective.

Discussion
To our knowledge, this is the first review exploring the reporting and publishing practices of a single academic institute. Whilst a limitation of this work is that is focuses only on the reporting and publishing practices within a single institute at a single UK institution, previous work exploring this at an individual journal levels has found similar proportions of papers reporting PPIE. For example, of 3,000 papers published in BMJ Open in 2020, 21% of the papers included PPIE, which is similar to our findings of 23% of the 523 papers published by department-affiliated authors since 2021 reporting PPIE activity.
In terms of PPIE inclusion relating to co-authorship, an analysis of 341 papers published in BMJ Open (between 2013 and 2024 and 2015–2016) found only two papers had patients listed as co-authors (0.6%). In contrast our review found that n = 34 (7%) papers included an author with either an affiliation as a patient author/to a PPIE group or to a charity/non-governmental organisation that represented PPIE-related interests, of which n = 9 (2%) did include a patient/lived-experience co-author. Whilst this overall greater rate of PPIE co-authorship may be attributed to our inclusion of charity/non-governmental organisation that represent PPIE-related interests, it also suggests an increase in the numbers of patient-authored publications and patient authors, especially since 2020. Additionally it is important to note that these figures may actually be higher than we have identified due to challenges relating to identifying patient-authored publications.
Great variation has been identified in the reporting, or lack thereof, of PPIE. With the introduction of a PPIE reporting policy at the BMJ, there was a 10-fold increase in the proportion of papers reporting PPIE compared with pre-implementation. Additionally, there have been comprehensive recommendations and guidance published for the inclusion of patient co-authors and similarly checklists like the GRIPP2 exist to improve reporting of PPIE. Drawing these recommendations together with the findings of our review and the discussions with PPIE contributors, we aim to further this through making the following recommendations in relation to involving PPIE in the dissemination of research and the reporting of PPIE in publications:
Ensure how PPIE contributors were involved is detailed in the methods and where appropriate, to detail outcome of PPIE in the results. If appropriate include this under a PPIE sub-heading.
Where PPIE has not been possible, it should be clearly documented and justified in the methods or under a PPIE sub-heading.
PPIE contributors should be offered the opportunity to support dissemination as co-authors, not just through academic outputs but in ways that are meaningful to them.
Expectations for what is involved, and the time commitment should be clearly set out.
Training, support, and financial reimbursement provided to support people to become co-authors (including administrative support e.g., set-up of ORCiDs).
Standardised affiliations used (e.g. PPIE group member/contributor or patient author) but also make clear that this may identify them as having a specific condition.
Use reporting guidelines for PPIE such as GRIPP2 mindfully and where appropriate.
Ensure that PPIE contributors are thanked in acknowledgements.
Use consistent terminology to describe the type of involvement or engagement and define less used terms.
Publish open access and include a plain English summary where possible.

However, it is important to recognise and acknowledge that whilst we make these recommendations with the aim of ensuring that inclusion and reporting of PPIE is comprehensive, consistent, and transparent, we do not believe that researchers should be ‘doing’ PPIE just to tick a box. It is of the utmost importance that through the provision and use of these recommendations that PPIE activity and the involvement of patients and the public in research does not become a tokenistic exercise. Nor should they be included and involved without appropriate institution or research funding and support to reduce risk of harm to patient and public co-contributors. It may also be pertinent for funding bodies to also consider that while they make PPIE a requirement in funding applications, it is not as common for it to be a requirement for researchers to report and/or publish on their PPIE activities, especially beyond producing yearly funder reports, i.e. in peer-reviewed publications in journals. It is important that we acknowledge and recognise this impact that bringing in lived experience has to research in the most appropriate ways and that power is distributed throughout the research process to include those with lived experience through the accreditation as equal authors of the research outputs. While this study was small in scale, the need for ongoing support with tasks such as setting up an ORCiD draws attention to the wider infrastructures of academic publishing. These systems are often designed with researchers in mind, and can inadvertently exclude patients and members of the public from full participation in knowledge generation. This raises important questions about whether mechanisms like ORCiD could be re-imagined to foster more inclusive recognition, or whether they risk reinforcing existing hierarchies of credit and authorship. Future research should also look at the experiences and recognition of the role of PPIE coordinator in research publications and their role in supporting PPIE contributors as co-authors.

Conclusions
This literature review provides an overview of reporting of PPIE in academic publications with authors affiliated to the WIPH at QMUL. This activity has provided an opportunity for reflection on WIPH structures and processes in relation to the work undertaken as part of a central theme across the Institute. Despite variability, there are examples of good and best practices emerging, ensuring that PPIE activity is undertaken appropriately, and is transparently reported. Our results have been presented to the wider institute, and our recommendations are being taken forward by the Institute leads for PPIE and in collaboration with the newly set-up PPIE Staff Forum to better support researchers with the challenges faced and to support best practice. This including better recognition of and signposting to the UK Standards for Public Involvement in Research. We recommend other institutions consider reviewing and reflecting on their own practices. Future research should aim to use the recommendations made from this work to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of PPIE in health research.
