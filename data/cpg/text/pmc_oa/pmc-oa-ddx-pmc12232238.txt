Management, Safety, and Efficacy Evaluation of Nutraceutical and Functional Food: A Global Perspective
ABSTRACT
Growing health consciousness and aging populations have driven surging demand for traditional and complementary medicines (T&CM), including nutraceuticals, functional foods, and food and medicinal substances (FAMS). Chronic disease burdens and preventive healthcare trends further amplify this growth. However, fragmented regulatory frameworks, inconsistent definitions, and divergent safety evaluation systems across regions hinder global trade and consumer trust. This review compares T&CM governance in China, the United States, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Australia, India, Russia, African nations, and faith‐based Kosher/Halal certification systems, analyzing legislative milestones and risk management models. Key disparities exist in standardization, ingredient approvals, and evidence requirements. While Asia and the West maintain robust regulations, African countries struggle to align traditional practices with modern safety standards. Emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI)‐powered quality control, multi‐omics‐based validation, and blockchain traceability are critical for ensuring the safety and efficacy of T&CM. The analysis underscores the urgent need for an internationally harmonized regulatory framework, supported by strategic positive/negative ingredient lists and cross‐border cooperation. Policy recommendations emphasize integrating traditional knowledge with scientific validation to optimize market accessibility while ensuring consumer safety. This study's novel contribution lies in its systematic evaluation of T&CM's role in bridging cultural heritage with global public health, offering actionable insights for policymakers to foster equitable trade and innovation in the wellness industry.

Introduction
With the rapid development of social economies and technology, the prevalence of suboptimal health conditions is increasing, prompting a heightened focus on public health across nations. In this context, substances possessing both medicinal and dietary properties have become a hot topic of global attention and research (Q. Ye, Zhou, et al.). In 2024, the Chinese Institute of Food Science and Technology, in collaboration with the International Federation of Food Science and Technology, jointly released the “Top Ten Research Hotspots in Global Food Safety and Health for 2023–2024.” Among them, “Food as Medicine”—which leverages food resources to help meet personalized health needs—was identified as one of the key areas of focus (Sun).
“Food as medicine” refers to the concept that many foods also function as medicines, with no clear boundary between the two. Understanding the role of food in maintaining health and treating diseases is deeply rooted in ancient cultures. For example, ancient texts from Indian Ayurveda, Chinese medical classics, the Old Testament of Judaism and Christianity (Books of Moses and Daniel), ancient Greece (Hippocrates), Islam (Quran), and Finland (Kalevala) provide insights into the concept of food and medicine being the same (Niu et al.; Grant et al.). In 2024, Academician Bin Cong of the Chinese Academy of Engineering defined food and medicine homologous substances as natural substances that possess both medicinal and nutritional functions coexisting within a single entity. Cong also emphasized that medicinal substances should not be toxic to the body and must be safe for long‐term consumption (Cong).
Adopting a healthy dietary pattern can mitigate the risk of developing chronic metabolic disorders, including Type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases, and hypertension. The Mediterranean diet, recognized as a gold standard nutritional model, emphasizes the abundant consumption of whole grains, leafy vegetables, and seasonal fruits—all excellent sources of dietary fiber, essential vitamins, and bioactive phytochemicals such as polyphenols with antioxidant properties. Furthermore, this dietary regimen advocates moderate intake of marine‐derived proteins, particularly fatty fish and shellfish, which provide cardioprotective omega‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) including eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). Long‐term adherence to the Mediterranean diet has been shown to prevent approximately 30% of cases of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (Damigou et al.; Esmaeilinezhad et al.).
The global understanding and regulatory classification of products positioned between conventional foods and medicines—variably termed nutraceuticals, functional foods, dietary supplements, food and medicinal substances (FAMS), complementary medicines, or food supplements—remain inconsistent across international jurisdictions. This definitional ambiguity primarily stems from divergent national perspectives shaped by ethnic characteristics, cultural traditions, geographical environments, and regional customary practices. Currently, no universally harmonized framework exists to establish standardized nomenclature or categorization criteria for these hybrid products within the global regulatory landscape.

Conceptual Framework of Traditional and Complementary Medicine
In formulating its operational framework for the Traditional Medicine Strategy 2014–2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) adopted the consolidated terminology traditional and complementary medicines (T&CM) (WHO). The conceptual delineation comprises two distinct components:
Traditional medicine (TM): Traditional medicine has a long history. It is the sum total of the knowledge, skill, and practices based on the theories, beliefs, and experiences indigenous to different cultures, whether explicable or not, used in the maintenance of health as well as in the prevention, diagnosis, improvement or treatment of physical and mental illness.
Complementary medicine (CM): The term “complementary medicine” or “alternative medicine” refers to a broad set of healthcare practices that are not part of that country's own tradition or conventional medicine and are not fully integrated into the dominant healthcare system. They are used interchangeably with traditional medicine in some countries.
While this taxonomic framework carries inherent conceptual constraints, it acknowledges substantial international variations in categorical definitions. This study examines products categorized within the T&CM framework, with country‐specific definitions of nutraceutical and functional food systematically compared in Table 1. To ensure conceptual coherence and minimize terminological ambiguity, we strategically employ T&CM as the operational designation for nutraceutical and functional food throughout this analysis, provided such usage does not compromise semantic precision or cross‐cultural comprehension.

In the present study, we conducted a comparative analysis of the management, safety, and efficacy of T&CM across regions including China, Japan, South Korea, and India in Asia; the United States (US) in the Americas; the European Union (EU) and Russia in Europe; and Australia in Oceania. Our study also provides an overview of T&CM in Africa, as well as Jewish and Halal T&CM. A global analysis of T&CM management, safety, and efficacy can provide actionable guidance for streamlining international trade, with findings indicating that establishing a standardized international regulatory framework could significantly enhance the global safety and effectiveness of T&CM.

In China, T&CM mainly includes health foods, nutritional supplements, and FAMS (Table 1). In the US and the EU, the T&CM are relatively more specific, with the US focusing on dietary supplements and the EU on food supplements (Table 1). In Japan, the focus is primarily on health foods and foods with health claims (FHC), such as foods for specific health uses (FOSHU) and foods with nutritional function claims (FNFC) (Table 1). Development History of T&CM Laws and Regulations in China
The development history of T&CM in China is extensive; however, few relevant laws or regulations exist. The regulatory system for T&CM has been continuously developed and improved since 1982. The “Food Hygiene Law of the People's Republic of China (Trial),” promulgated in 1982, was the first policy and regulation to mention the management of T&CM, stipulating that no drugs should be added to food. The “Food Hygiene Law of the People's Republic of China,” enacted in 1995, was the first to grant T&CM legal status. In 2009, the “Food Safety Law of the People's Republic of China” imposed strict supervision and management of T&CM. The “Health Food Registration and Filing Measures,” introduced in 2016, marked the beginning of the dual‐track system of registration and filing (China Food and Drug Administration). In 2021, the National Health Commission issued the “Regulations on the Management of the Catalogue of Substances That Are Both Food and Chinese Medicinal Materials,” which, for the first time, established management standards for FAMS. The regulations outlined the required safety evaluation procedures and materials for FAMS. They also emphasized that provincial health administrative departments should propose modifications or additions to the FAMS catalog, implement dynamic management and food safety risk monitoring of the catalog, and update it as necessary to ensure food safety and protect public health (National Health Commission of the People's Republic of China). The “Implementation Details for the Technical Evaluation of New Functions and New Product Technology of Health Foods (Trial),” released in 2023, marks the beginning of a new era of development for the health food industry (X. D. Chen et al.; State Administration for Market Regulation).
As mentioned previously, in China, according to relevant laws and regulations, T&CM are mainly divided into three categories: FAMS (National Health Commission of the People's Republic of China), health foods (Health Food Management Regulations), and nutritional supplements (Notice on the Issuance). Before being made publicly available, it is necessary to prepare the requisite application materials according to the declared category, ensuring compliance with regulations before commercialization through the regulatory system.

Development History of Dietary Supplement Regulations in the US
The development of dietary supplement regulations in the US includes the passage of the Food and Drugs Act in 1906; the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act in 1938; the Food Additives Amendment in 1958; and the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) in 1994. This marked the establishment of core regulatory laws for dietary supplements in the US, along with a regulatory framework governed by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (Bailey; G. Li).
The DSHEA legally defined “dietary supplements” in 1994 (FDA). As the regulatory authority for dietary supplements, the FDA has published many dietary supplement ingredients on its official website, which currently includes 81 ingredients; this directory is regularly updated (FDA). In the US, the application for dietary supplements does not require registration or filing, and manufacturers must assume full responsibility for product safety. However, when planning to market a dietary supplement containing a new dietary ingredient (NDI), it is necessary to submit the relevant materials to the FDA at least 75 days before the dietary ingredient is introduced or delivered for interstate commerce (FDA).

Development History of Food Supplement Regulations in the EU
In 2002, the EU established the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and passed the European Parliament Directive 2002/46/EC, which partially harmonizes the rules for marketing food supplements, including labeling information. This directive applies to all food supplements sold and provided as food, including their ingredients. In 2006, in accordance with the European Commission Directive 2006/37/EC, the EFSA Management Board adopted a cooperative strategy to establish a network of coordination centers for the exchange of scientific information on health risk assessments among EU Member States. European Parliament Directives 2002/46/EC and 1999/21/EC define food supplements and food for medical use, respectively (Coppens et al.; Santini et al.; Petkova‐Gueorguieva et al.; G. Li).
Most countries in the EU regulate the marketing of food supplements similarly to food products, without submitting documents for application before marketing. Registration with the Health Administration Department is typically sufficient. However, if substances not included in the inventory list are used to prepare food supplements, production companies must submit the relevant documents for assessment by the EFSA.

Development History of Health Foods and FHC Regulations in Japan
In Japan, the term “functional food” first appeared in a project of the Ministry of Education in 1984. In 1991, the Ministry of Health and Welfare (now called the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare) (MHLW) introduced the FOSHU system. In 1996, the American Chamber of Commerce in Japan (ACCJ) recommended that Japan relax its regulations on dietary supplements to eliminate trade barriers between the US and Japan in the dietary supplement sector. In 2001, a new regulatory system was introduced, comprising the FHC, FNFC, and the newly established FOSHU. In 2005, the MHLW revised the existing FOSHU, FNFC, and other systems. These changes introduced new subsystems of FOSHU, including (1) standardized FOSHU, (2) qualified FOSHU, and (3) FOSHU disease risk reduction claims (Ohama et al.; Nagata and Yamada; G. Li and Wang). According to Japan's Pharmaceutical Affairs Law, consumables are divided into drugs and non‐drugs (foods), with foods being further subdivided into health foods, FHC, FNFC, and FOSHU.
Japan has implemented different regulatory systems for health foods, namely FOSHU and FNFC, and a licensing system for FOSHU. For FNFC, a self‐certification system that does not require registration or filing was applied. As long as the relevant specification standards are met, function claims can be made as stipulated in the regulations. The relevant materials must be provided for new ingredients (raw materials) (Ohama et al.; Nagata and Yamada; G. Li and Wang).

Development History of HFFs Regulations in South Korea
In South Korea, the National Health and Welfare Commission introduced the “Health/Functional Food Act” in 2002, which officially came into effect in 2004. This act established a framework for managing the safety, efficacy, and labeling of HFFs, making them a distinct category independent of traditional foods. The term HFFs refers to food supplements containing nutrients or other substances (in concentrated forms) that have nutritional or physiological effects to supplement a normal diet. HFFs are divided into two categories: generic and product‐specific. Generic HFFs include nutrient supplements, ginseng, and its derivatives, and other health supplements. They are classified into three categories according to their health claims: nutrient function claims, reduction‐of‐disease risk claims, and other‐function claims. Products not listed in the generic HFFs catalog are considered product‐specific HFFs. HFFs are primarily regulated by the Korean Food and Drug Administration (KFDA) (Kim et al.).

Development History of Complementary Medicines Regulations in Australia
In Australia, complementary medicines, including vitamins, herbal remedies, and mineral supplements, are regulated as complementary medicines. The main legislative instruments for the regulation of complementary medicines include the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989, the Therapeutic Goods Regulations 1990 (amended in 2024), and the Australian Regulatory Guidelines for Complementary Medicines issued by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) in 2004. Complementary medicines are therapeutic goods that are wholly or principally composed of one or more specified active ingredients, each of which has distinct characteristics and traditional uses [Australia Therapeutic Goods Regulations 1990; G. Li ].
The TGA classifies complementary medicines into three categories based on their risk level: listed medicines (AUST L), assessed listed medicines [AUST L(A)], and registered complementary medicines (AUST R). AUST L products are considered low‐risk and can only make low‐level therapeutic claims selected from the Permissible Indication List. They do not require a TGA market assessment for listing. AUST L(A), introduced by the TGA in 2018 as a subcategory of AUST L, must use ingredients from the Permissible Indication List but are permitted to make at least one medium‐risk claim, which may include low‐risk claims. All claims must provide evidence of efficacy for the TGA to conduct a pre‐market assessment of the product. For AUST R, there are no restrictions on the Permissible Indication List; sponsors can apply to use a new ingredient and can make high‐, medium‐, and low‐risk claims, with the TGA conducting a pre‐market assessment of the product [Australia Therapeutic Goods Regulations 1990; G. Li ].

Development History of T&CM Regulations in India
In India, at the beginning of the 1990s, laws and regulations related to food processing, such as the Insecticides Act of 1968, Food Products Order of 1995, Agricultural Produce Act, and Essential Commodities Act of 1995, were established for the first time to provide a regulatory framework for the Indian food and nutrition sector. The Food Safety and Standards Act (FSSA) was enacted in 2006, and the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) was established to regulate food industry management. The FSSA was officially implemented in 2011, marking a new period of development for the Indian functional food and dietary supplement industry. In 2016, the FSSAI promulgated health supplements, nutraceuticals, food for special dietary use, food for special medical purposes, functional food, and novel food regulations, categorizing nutraceutical products into eight classes (Chakraborty). Notably, in India, “food for special medical purpose” specifically refers to weight loss products, which are foods designed to replace a normal diet. As the term “nutraceuticals” is relatively new, it is highlighted here for introductory purposes.
The term “nutraceutical” is a portmanteau of the words “nutrient” and “pharmaceutical.” The term was originally coined by Stephen DeFelice in 1989, who defined nutraceuticals as “food or parts of food that provide medical or health benefits, including the prevention and/or treatment of disease” (Petrini). According to the FSSA, a nutraceutical is a naturally occurring chemical compound that confers physiological benefits and helps maintain good health; it can be purified from food or nonfood sources (Chakraborty). The definition provided by FSSA differs from that by Stephen DeFelice; the FSSA definition does not emphasize disease prevention and treatment.
In India, applying for different T&CM requires the preparation of different application materials according to the declaration category, with an emphasis on pre‐market regulation. This process primarily involves a registration and approval system that is considerably similar to that in China. Except for novel foods, other categories of T&CM can only be applied to products within an officially published catalog.

Development History of T&CM Regulations in Russia
Russia places great emphasis on food safety issues and has introduced a series of policies and regulations to ensure food safety. In 2010, the President of the Russian Federation signed Decree No. 120, approving the “Russian Federation Food Security Doctrine.” In 2016, the government of the Russian Federation approved the “Strategy for Improving the Quality of Food Products in the Russian Federation until 2030.” The core regulations related to T&CM mainly include the Technical Regulations of the Customs Union “On food safety” (TP CU 021/2011), the Technical Regulations of the Customs Union “Food products in terms of their labeling” (TP CU 022/2011), and the Technical Regulations of the Customs Union “on the safety of certain types of specialized food products, including dietary therapeutic and dietary preventive nutrition” (TP TS 027/2012). To further standardize T&CM, the Technical Standardization Committee No. 036 “Functional Foods” was established in 2008 and was reorganized into the Technical Committee for Standardization 36, “Foods for Special Dietary Uses,” in 2014 (Tutelyan et al.).
In Russia, T&CM primarily includes nutraceuticals, functional foods, and foods for special dietary use. Foods for special dietary use are divided into five categories: sports foods, dietary foods for preventive purposes, foods for medical use, foods for pregnant and breastfeeding women, and baby foods (Tutelyan et al.). The national registration system for Biologically Active Food Supplements currently implemented in the Russian Federation aligns with the guidelines of the Codex Alimentarius Commission and is similar to the relevant legislation in Canada, the United Kingdom, and the US. However, the Russian registration system is stricter than those in other countries (Tutelyan et al.).

Overview of the Basic Status of T&CM in Africa
In Africa, traditional medicinal products, nutraceuticals, and functional foods are gradually developing and improving with advancements in science and technology. The legal status of functional foods, nutraceuticals, and herbal medicines varies across countries. In many African countries, herbal medicines lack official recognition, regulatory frameworks, and legal support. Only a few countries have established national policies or legal and regulatory systems to regulate traditional medicine/complementary and alternative medicine. For example, South Africa formulated its policy as part of the national drug policy in 1996. In Ethiopia, the relevant policies were established under the 1999 Health, Drug, Science, and Technology Policy. Although some countries have enacted relevant laws and regulations, budgets are insufficient to support the development of traditional medicine (WHO; Bahorun et al.). Therefore, this section provides a brief overview of T&CM in Africa without further discussing the management and safety evaluation of T&CM.
The continent of Africa boasts an abundance of plant resources, with over 68,000 species, including 35,000 endemic species. Among them, 2,500–3,000 species are medicinal plants, with notable examples including cloves, kola nuts, myrrh, acacia gum, African plum, African nutmeg, moringa, and fenugreek (Yuan et al.). As the side effects of natural medicines in Africa are minimal, their demand is increasing annually. Although it is widely acknowledged that Africa currently relies mainly on herbal medicines, there is growing evidence of strong interest in nutraceutical and functional food. One example is the oil extracted from the argan nut from Morocco, which is used in both the beauty and food industries owing to its antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties (He). Moreover, African potatoes are gaining popularity worldwide as a healthy food (Liao).

Overview of Kosher and Halal T&CM
The importance of the Kosher and Halal food markets is global, with significant demand across various regions. As of 2013, there were 1.8 billion Muslims worldwide, accounting for approximately 25% of the global population. The largest Muslim populations in Asia are found in Indonesia, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Iran, and Turkey (Ren and Zhang). In some cases, certifying food as either Kosher or Halal can be challenging, as Jewish or Islamic dietary laws govern which foods are Kosher or permissible for Jewish or Muslim consumers to eat. In Judaism, food is divided into two major categories Kosher and non‐Kosher; only Kosher food can be consumed. Grains, vegetables, and fruits are considered Kosher. Permitted meats include ruminant animals with split hooves (e.g., beef, lamb, venison). Conversely, the meats of pigs, horses, rabbits, camels, and other non‐ruminant animals are non‐Kosher and forbidden. In the seafood category, aquatic animals without fins, scales, bones, and shellfish, such as eels and catfish are not permissible. Moreover, most insects are not considered edible, and it is prohibited to mix milk and meat. However, honey from bees and shellac, a secretion from insects, are permitted by most Jewish and Halal authorities. For Muslims, pork is also forbidden; however, camels and rabbits are permissible, and milk and meat can be mixed. The consumption of alcohol and other intoxicants is prohibited (Z. Li and Zhou; Jiang). Based on the above regulations of Judaism and Islam regarding food, the following sections will introduce T&CM developed with plants and animals as the main raw materials to better understand Kosher and Halal T&CM.
Organic kale, which is typically rich in vitamins A, C, and K and minerals such as calcium, iron, and potassium, is highly nutritious (Ding et al.). Fresh organic kale is considered harmless and compliant with Kosher dietary laws. However, repeated inspections of fresh organic kale often revealed the presence of aphids. According to Kosher dietary law, certain insects are considered unclean and inedible. As aphids fall into this category, special attention must be paid to inspecting and removing these insects before consumption, in accordance with Kosher dietary laws. Although organic agricultural products generally do not use chemical pesticides, which may lead to an increased presence of insects, they typically contain fewer chemical residues, increasing their health benefits. In the US, a high‐end snack company has a line of Kosher‐certified chip products, including beets, carrots, and sweet potato chips. The company applied for and obtained certification for the use of organic kale chips. During the prolonged drying process, green leafy vegetables are transformed into crispy chips, and aphids are essentially decomposed. As these insects have dried to the point of decomposition, they are no longer considered present according to Kosher dietary laws (Price; Regenstein).
Gelatin is the primary ingredient used in the manufacture of hard and soft capsules for encapsulating medications, dietary supplements, and other health products. It is produced through the hydrolysis of collagen and is mainly found in the skin, bones, tendons, ligaments, and other connective tissues of animals. Fish gelatin, which is certified as Kosher, typically comes from fish species that comply with Kosher dietary laws, such as salmon, trout, and cod. Fish gelatin derived from these fish can be used to produce capsules, food, and other products that meet Kosher standards, whereas gelatin extracted from shellfish or catfish does not meet the requirements of Kosher dietary laws (Price; Regenstein).
In summary, regarding the formulation of laws and regulations for T&CM, China, the US, Japan, and Australia established core legal frameworks for T&CM in the 1990s, whereas the EU, South Korea, India, and Russia established their core legal frameworks for managing T&CM at the beginning of the 21st century. The legal system for T&CM in the African region is gradually being established, and Kosher and Halal T&CM must meet Kosher requirements.
T&CM is legally recognized in various regions. ‌China‌ has established a food safety management system centered on the Food Safety Law of the People's Republic of China, supplemented by departmental regulations to govern FAMS, health foods, and nutritional supplements. ‌The US‌, ‌South Korea‌, ‌Australia‌, and ‌India‌ have enacted specialized legislation to regulate T&CM. ‌The EU‌ implements the European Parliament Directive 2002/46/EC, facilitating cross‐border evaluation of food supplements through data‐sharing mechanisms among member states. ‌Japan‌ manages health foods and FHC through its FOSHU system. ‌Russia‌ oversees T&CM via a legislative framework coordinated by legal and food committees. In ‌Africa‌, only a few countries, such as South Africa and Ethiopia, have established legal frameworks for T&CM regulation (Figures 1 and 2).

Specialized institutions in different countries or regions manage T&CM to ensure its safety and effectiveness. Globally, T&CM management varies, with three main systems: the registration and approval system, the record‐filing system, and a combination of the two. In ‌China‌, T&CM is regulated by the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) and the National Health Commission (NHC) under a registration and approval system. In the US, dietary supplements are mainly managed by the FDA, while in the EU, food supplements are mainly managed by EFSA, implementing a record‐filing system with an emphasis on post‐market supervision. ‌Japan‌ employs multi‐agency oversight (e.g., MHLW), regulating health foods and FHC, while in South Korea, KFDA manages HFFs through a record filing system and approval system. Australia‌’s TGA emphasizes ‌pre‐ and post‐market management‌ for complementary medicines. In India, FSSAI manages T&CM, while in Russia, Rospotrebnadzor manages T&CM, implementing a registration and approval system. (Figures 1 and 2).
Globally, herbal supplements face rigorous safety oversight through country‐specific regulatory frameworks. China implements a positive list approach for FAMS, restricting ingredients to those with over 30 years of documented consumption history that are cataloged in the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China. Meanwhile, the US and the EU emphasize Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP) to standardize medicinal plant cultivation and harvesting processes (M. Zhang et al.). In contrast, India requires herbal substances to demonstrate either 15 years of domestic use or 30 years of documented application in their origin countries before inclusion in its T&CM compendium (Bhupathiraju et al.).

Results and Discussion
An increasing number of consumers worldwide are using T&CM to boost their immune systems and help fight viruses and diseases. The US is the largest dietary supplement market globally, with the highest penetration rate of dietary supplements. The market is mature, and sales of dietary supplements reached approximately $55.7 billion in 2020 (G. Li). Currently, there are approximately 85,000 dietary supplement products in the US alone. Dietary supplements are widely used, with reports indicating that approximately half of American adults and one‐third of children use dietary supplements. Multivitamin/multimineral supplements (MVM) are the most commonly used category, with approximately one‐third of adults using them (Bailey). Europe is one of the major consumers of food supplements worldwide, with Germany, Italy, the UK, and France accounting for two‐thirds of the total European consumption. The EU food supplement retail market was valued at $22.69 billion in 2020 (G. Li). According to data from the “China Consumer Goods Industry Health Action Report,” the scale of the Chinese health and functional food market increased rapidly from 222.7 billion Yuan in 2019 to 298.9 billion Yuan in 2022, reflecting a compound annual growth rate of over 13% over four years. The market is expected to continue to grow in the coming years (Santini et al.; H. Huang et al.). Australia, with its vast territory and abundant resources, has implemented strict government regulations, leading to a positive consumer perception of its products, with over half of the population using complementary medicines (Bhuyan et al.). In India, a survey indicated that 23% of the population is either overweight or obese, which in turn can contribute to the development of diabetes and heart disease (Bhupathiraju et al.). These factors have led to enhanced recognition and increased use of T&CM.
To ensure consumer health and product safety, various countries have introduced policies and regulations to supervise and manage T&CM. Based on the different understandings and perceptions of T&CM and consumption habits, each country or region has adopted different supervision, management methods, and assessment approaches.

Different Regulatory and Management Methods for T&CM Globally
First, in terms of the level of regulatory system standardization, Asian countries (such as China, Japan, South Korea, and India), the EU, Russia, the US, and Australia have established relatively comprehensive T&CM management systems. While countries in the African region are gradually focusing on T&CM, the establishment of a robust management system still requires significant effort. Owing to the special dietary requirements of Muslim and Jewish communities, the establishment of the T&CM system must comply with the Jewish or Islamic requirements for Kosher and Halal food, respectively.
Second, the regulatory approaches are categorized into pre‐ and post‐market regulations. In countries such as the US and the EU, where most dietary supplements are produced under Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) conditions and are generally considered low‐risk, regulatory frameworks are increasingly focusing on post‐market reviews rather than pre‐market approval. In Australia, India, and Russia, the primary system retains pre‐market approval with post‐market oversight. In addition, the regulatory approach in China is gradually shifting to post‐market regulations. Specifically, in 2023, China issued the “Interim Rules for the Technical Evaluation of New Functions and Products of Health Foods,” which clearly positioned new functions of health foods into three categories: supplementing dietary nutrients, maintaining or improving the health status of the body, and reducing risk factors for disease occurrence. This is similar to the Japanese new FOSHU system in terms of disease risk reduction claims. Moreover, China is increasingly focusing on post‐market regulation, as reflected in the “Interim Rules for the Technical Evaluation of New Functions and Products of Health Foods.” This includes a dedicated chapter on post‐marketing evaluation (State Administration for Market Regulation), which requires manufacturers to develop a post‐marketing evaluation plan for new functional health foods. Manufacturers must collect data on product sales, consumer demographics, health assessments, and feedback from complaints. They also must conduct a comprehensive analysis of consumer groups and health effects and submit an annual self‐inspection report for post‐marketing evaluation. This approach is similar to post‐market oversight, which is primarily employed by the US and the EU.
Third, a significant disparity exists in the application and management methods of T&CM, hindering their global circulation. As mentioned previously, the application and management of T&CM considerably differ owing to differences in geography, cultural background, and dietary habits among countries. For example, ginseng is managed in China as a health food, FAMS, and traditional Chinese medicinal material, whereas in the EU, the US, and other countries and regions, it is regulated as a dietary supplement. Moreover, Hypericum perforatum is considered a dietary supplement or registered herbal medicine in Europe, the US, and other countries and regions, whereas in China, it can only be used as a medicinal material (Oketch‐Rabah et al.). Even among English‐speaking countries, such as the US, Australia, and Canada, which share similar cultures and legal systems, there are significant differences in the regulation and application of T&CM. For example, melatonin is regulated as a dietary supplement in the US, as a natural health product (NHP) in Canada, and as a prescription medication in Australia (L. Huang et al.).
There is also a significant difference in the management and application of herbal medicines. Depending on the regulations applicable to food and drugs in each country, medicinal plants may be defined as foods, functional foods, dietary supplements, or herbal medicines in different countries. This complicates the regulation of herbal medicines for countries, leading to confusion among patients and consumers. In China, herbal medicines are managed as prescriptions, over‐the‐counter drugs, self‐medication, dietary supplements, health foods, and functional foods. In Japan, herbal medicines are classified as prescription and over‐the‐counter drugs, dietary supplements (referred to as health foods), and functional foods for regulation. In India, Russia, and Iran, herbal medicines are categorized as prescription and over‐the‐counter drugs, as well as dietary supplements for regulation. In Brazil, herbal medicines are classified as herbal drugs (including prescription and over‐the‐counter drugs), functional food, probiotics, bioactive substances, and cosmetics for regulation (WHO). Even within the same plant medicines, there are significant differences in their application across various countries. For example, Aloe vera and fenugreek are used as medicines in China and Africa. In China, A. vera is primarily used to treat constipation, convulsions, and fungal skin infections, while in Africa, it is used primarily to treat asthma and bronchitis. Fenugreek is used in China to treat kidney yang deficiency, cold lower abdominal pain, and cold hernia abdominal pain, whereas, in Africa, it is mainly used for its anti‐diarrheal, antispasmodic, anti‐amoebic, anti‐dysmenorrheal, and anti‐diabetic effects. Fenugreek is also used to promote milk secretion in nursing mothers and placental expulsion in Africa (Bahorun et al.; Chinese Pharmacopoeia Commission).
In summary, different countries adopt varied management systems to ensure the safe implementation of T&CM. In China, for instance, a strict approval system is primarily implemented for T&CM. For health foods with novel functionalities, China mandates post‐market supervision. Enterprises must collect sales data, and consumer feedback, and submit annual reports to ensure traceability. In the US, while the FDA implements a post‐market surveillance approach for dietary supplements, it mandates compliance with GMP and enhances lifecycle monitoring through AI technologies, such as real‐time tracking of post‐market adverse reactions (Warraich et al.). The EU has undertaken legislative initiatives in food production and control. For example, “Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915 of April 25 2023 on maximum levels for certain contaminants in food” sets maximum limits for specific contaminants in food products (European Union). Countries such as Japan, South Korea, Australia, Russia, and India have also established comprehensive T&CM management systems to ensure safety, including evaluations of nutritional value and toxic substances in T&CM products. Regulatory disparities between T&CM and medicinal materials across global jurisdictions have impeded their international circulation and sustainable development. Nevertheless, these divergences create opportunities for cross‐national policy learning and systemic improvements in T&CM governance, thereby establishing a foundation to broaden T&CM's scope of applications (Figure 3).

Challenges in the Safety and Efficacy Assessment of T&CM

Categories and Forms of T&CM in Various Countries
In China, FAMS, health foods, and nutritional supplements mainly include medicinal materials, fungi, probiotics, nucleic acids, vitamins, minerals, and components isolated from animals, plants, and microorganisms (Tian et al.; Cheng et al.). In the US, dietary supplements primarily include vitamins, minerals, herbs, amino acids, whey proteins, and creatine. In the EU, food supplements mainly include vitamins, minerals, amino acids, essential fatty acids, fibers, and various plant and herbal extracts. In Japan, health foods and FHC mainly include vitamins, minerals, mushrooms, probiotics, herbal components, and other plant components. In South Korea, HFFs mainly comprise nutrient supplements, ginseng and its products, and health supplements, including vitamins, minerals, essential amino acids, proteins, dietary fiber, and essential fatty acids. In Australia, complementary medicines primarily include 14 categories of products, such as vitamins, minerals, amino acids, herbs, and homeopathic preparations. In India, T&CM mainly includes vitamins and minerals, amino acids, trace elements, plant or plant components, ingredients for nutritional health products, strains used as probiotics (live microorganisms), and prebiotics. In Russia, a positive list (permitted ingredients) and a negative list (prohibited or restricted ingredients) are implemented for nutraceutical product management. The positive list mainly includes 160 ingredients, such as amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals, bioactive substances, enzymes, and probiotics. The negative list includes 190 medicinal plants without a history of human consumption. The main components of functional foods are living microorganisms and substance complexes derived from animal, plant, microbial, and mineral sources. In terms of foods with special dietary uses, there are specialized functional foods for pregnant women, nursing women, and children. Functional foods for preterm and low birth weight infants include milk powder. Notably, these functional foods have undergone more detailed market segmentation in Russia, with specialized products developed for various population groups, including pregnant women, children, athletes, and patients with specific diseases. Detailed information on the T&CM in various countries is provided in Table S1.
In summary, T&CM from various countries contain vitamins and minerals. China, the US, Australia, and India have a broad range of T&CM, including herbs. A unique category of 106 FAMS exists in China, consisting of medicinal materials listed in the Chinese pharmacopeia that are traditionally consumed as food, with a proven history of over 30 years of consumption; examples include American ginseng, honey, and licorice (Table S2). American dietary supplements also include herbs such as American ginseng; however, there is no emphasis on these being medicinal materials included in the American pharmacopeia. Although South Korea does not include herbs in its HFFs, it does include ginseng and its derivatives, indicating that ginseng plays an important role as an HFF in the country. Australia also lists homeopathic preparations in a separate category. The popularity of Ayurveda, an ancient Indian medical system, has also contributed to the acceptance of T&CM. Four hundred plants, including turmeric, chili peppers, and garlic—provided they have a proven history of use for at least 15 years in India or at least 30 years in their country of origin—are used as T&CM owing to their nutritional and therapeutic effects (Bhupathiraju et al.).

Safety and Efficacy Assessment
In most countries in Asia, Europe, the Americas, and Oceania, relatively comprehensive safety and efficacy assessment mechanisms for T&CM have been established. In China, different types of T&CM products have been evaluated for safety and efficacy. For instance, FAMS is primarily assessed for their composition and toxicity, with sanitary inspections conducted, including microbial indicators, chemical contaminants, and toxins. For nutritional supplements, products on the positive list generally do not require a toxicological assessment, whereas other products are mainly evaluated for their nutritional roles, metabolic processes in the human body, and toxicology. Health food safety is assessed based on categories such as fungal health foods, probiotic health foods, nucleic acid health foods, wild animal and plant health foods, amino acid chelates, and health foods produced using macroporous adsorption resin separation and purification processes. For example, probiotic and fungal health foods require a safety evaluation of the strains used.
In the US, the FDA does not approve the safety and efficacy of dietary supplements. The Botanical Safety Consortium (BSC) is a collaboration among the FDA, the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, and the Health and Environmental Sciences Institute (HESI). The mission of this consortium is to provide a platform for scientists from the government, academia, consumer health groups, industry, and nonprofit organizations to integrate existing safety data and the latest toxicological data to assess the safety of botanicals in dietary supplements. Dietary supplement manufacturers and distributors are responsible for ensuring the safety of their products and providing clinical trial results or other research evidence to support the rationality of their efficacy and use as stated on the label. The FDA regulates dietary supplements on the market through inspections of production facilities, product sampling inspections, and investigations of adverse events (Oketch‐Rabah et al.; FDA).
In the EU, The EFSA also plays a key role in providing scientific opinions and assessing the safety of dietary supplements. The safety assessment of dietary supplements in the EU follows the European Directive on Food Supplements (Directive 2002/46/EC) and related implementation rules. Food supplement safety control includes basic analysis, such as microbiological analysis, heavy metals, and sensory analysis; specific analysis, including pesticides, genetically modified organisms, mycotoxins, colors, preservatives, and allergens; basic nutritional value analysis such as for carbohydrates, fats, proteins, and energy value; and specific nutritional value, including vitamins, minerals, and other bioactive substances (Vujić and Pollak). Maximum levels for certain contaminants in food are listed in Table S3.
In Japan, raw materials used for the manufacture of “health foods” need to be self‐assessed according to safety guidelines established by the MHLW. This primarily involves conducting component analysis, identifying harmful substances, and performing toxicological research (Ohama et al.; Nagata and Yamada).
In South Korea, the KFDA conducts quality and safety assessments. For HFFs, safety assessments mainly evaluate the history of use, production processes, nutritional value, and toxicity. For new substances that are not generic HFFs, relevant materials must be provided to the KFDA, including information on the source and properties of the new ingredient, the content of functional components (or indicator components), processing methods, and scientific evidence of the safety and efficacy of HFFs (Kim et al.).
In Australia, the safety and efficacy of complementary medicines are assessed by the TGA. Data for assessing drug safety mainly include application history, bioactivity, toxicology, clinical research, and adverse reactions (Australia Therapeutic Goods Regulations 1990).
In India, The FSSAI aims to ensure the safety, efficacy, and quality of T&CM and protect consumer health. The T&CM regulations follow the FSSA, which stipulates requirements for the registration, production, sale, and advertising of T&CM. For non‐specific foods and food ingredients, the primary evidence required is safety or risk assessment, demonstrating that they will not have adverse effects on specific groups of people, such as pregnant women, children, or older adults (Chakraborty).
In Russia, the Ministry of Health and Federal Service for the Supervision of Consumer Rights Protection and Human Welfare (Rospotrebnadzor) conducts safety and efficacy assessments of nutraceutical products. There are specific regulations—the national sanitary legislation SanPiN 2.3.2.1078‐01 Hygienic Safety and Nutritional Value Requirements for Foods—for assessing nutraceuticals (dietary supplements), including their sensory, physical, and chemical properties, functionality, and role in modern diets. For functional foods, which are beneficial for enhancing physical endurance, immunity, and digestion, over 10 indicators are assessed, including body mass index, gut microbiota, and calcium excretion dynamics. Special dietary foods, foods containing genetically modified organisms (GMOs), and GMO‐derived raw materials should not be used in the manufacturing of infant food, medical and disease prevention food, or food for pregnant and nursing women (Tutelyan et al.). Details on safety assessments in various countries are provided in Table S4.
Safety assessments of T&CM exhibit both similarities and differences across various countries. Notably, countries remain cautious about products containing genetically modified ingredients, as exemplified by the EU assessment of GMOs (when applicable). In Russia, GMOs and GMO‐derived raw materials are not allowed in products intended for pregnant women and children. Most African countries prohibit the inclusion of GMOs in T&CM. For Jewish and Muslim communities, there is no consensus regarding the acceptability of GMOs.
However, the research and assessment requirements and methods for the safety and efficacy of botanical T&CM are more complex than those for other T&CM. A single plant may contain hundreds of natural components, and a product containing a mixture of plants may include even more. Isolating each active component from each plant is time‐consuming and resource‐intensive. In practice, such an analysis is not feasible, particularly in the case of mixed plants. Overall, botanical components, which are important products of T&CM, are a mixture of known and unknown chemical substances that are difficult to fully characterize. The combination of traditional experiences and the latest research findings may promote the development of T&CM and the advancement of new treatment methods. Furthermore, T&CM composed of a combination of multiple plants and/or non‐botanical components can vary significantly in their proportions. Typically, these combinations have not undergone antagonism testing, nor have the potential side effects resulting from their concurrent use with other drugs or T&CM been determined either. Consequently, predicting the safety of such products is highly challenging, and establishing a causal relationship between the product components and side effects is not feasible (T. Ye, Xu, et al.).

T&CM Promote Global Cultural Exchange and Trade Cooperation
T&CM, derived from food and medicines, represents the crystallization of global human culture and the long‐term synergistic development of dietary and medical cultures. According to incomplete statistics, more than 3000 dual‐use food and medicinal plants exist worldwide. In China, more than 1000 food and medicine dual‐use plants are used in pharmaceuticals (prescription and over‐the‐counter drugs), health products, special medical foods, medicinal cuisine, and cosmetic products (L. Huang et al.). T&CM are rich in chemical components such as polyphenols (e.g., anthocyanins and flavonoids), plant sterols, saponins, curcumin, and allicin. They exert various effects such as enhancing immunity; preventing cardiovascular diseases, certain cancers, and neurodegenerative diseases; and reducing blood pressure and cholesterol levels (T. Ye, Xu, et al.; XiLin et al.).
Given the substantial potential of T&CM in preventing and treating diseases, global public health could be significantly improved through significant exchange, cooperation, and commercial trade. For example, China promotes traditional Chinese medicine, including Chinese medicinal materials and FAMS, such as goji berries and ginseng. These products are not only widely used in traditional Chinese medicine but are also popular worldwide. Indian berry spices are renowned globally, and through trade and cultural exchange, they have gained prominence in regions such as Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Europe. High‐quality honey and bee products produced by countries such as Turkey and Iran are entering international markets through trade cooperation. Moreover, Russian birch sap and other locally functional foods have been recognized and accepted by many countries. Koumiss is rich in nutrients, containing various unsaturated fatty acids, proteins, and vitamins, and has a broad consumer market in China, Russia, Central Asia, and Eastern Europe (XiLin et al.). The fruit of the African baobab tree is rich in vitamin C and other nutrients and is processed into powder and beverage forms, which are promoted in the global health food market. Through these means, T&CM not only fosters global economic cooperation but also enhances cultural exchange and scientific and technological communication, thereby promoting global connectivity.
In addition, of the 106 medicinal materials on the Chinese “list of substances that are both food and traditional Chinese medicines,” only 53 are used as health foods, spices, or herbal medicines in Europe. Among the top 40 best‐selling herbal supplements in “2020 mainstream multi‐channel sales in the United States,” only 14 varieties (or products containing purified extracts) are used as spices or Chinese herbal medicines in China. These similarities and differences in the application of T&CM provide a broad space for cooperation and connectivity among countries (L. Huang et al.; State Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine).

Future Outlook

Driving Quality and Diversity in T&CM Through Technological Advancements
Currently, nutraceuticals and functional foods are mainly land‐based, with few marine T&CM. The ocean is rich in biological resources and serves as a vital source for the research and development of T&CM. In recent years, marine active components such as astaxanthin, seaweed polysaccharides, and marine collagen, have gained significant attention in the health sector, highlighting the unique application potential of marine‐derived T&CM (Gao et al.).
In addition, the variety of T&CM is expected to expand, with an increasing emphasis on quality. Advancements in technologies such as genetic modification, biosynthesis, big data analytics, AI, functional genomics, and metabolomics, will enable the achievement of precise nutrition and personalized recommendations. For example, genetic modification technology can be used to precisely improve crops, enabling the cultivation of varieties with enhanced resistance to diseases and adverse environments. Crops that are less prone to diseases and pests not only offer enhanced nutritional or medicinal values but also hold the potential for producing T&CM that meet Kosher standards. Moreover, biosynthesis through microorganisms or cell factories can be employed to produce standardized plant extracts, enhancing the quality and consistency of T&CM. AI can further enable personalized nutrition by recommending suitable T&CM based on individual genetic information and lifestyle. New technologies such as functional genomics and metabolomics provide novel research avenues for exploring changes in human nutritional needs and biological responses to food. For instance, in‐depth research in the field of functional foods has revealed the anti‐inflammatory and anticancer activities of anthocyanins as well as their roles in preventing cardiovascular diseases, controlling obesity, and alleviating diabetes (Zhu et al.; S. Li et al.).
Although advanced technologies provide new tools and methods for the development, production, and regulation of T&CM, it is necessary to establish a balance among innovation, safety, ethics, and acceptability. For example, while the development and use of genetically modified foods are limited to the African continent, many African countries still consider genetically modified foods unsafe, and some government agencies continue to refuse to incorporate these foods into their food systems (Bahorun et al.).

Enhancing the Safety and Efficacy of T&CM Through New Technologies and Big Data
First, the safety assessment of T&CM can be strengthened by improving the quality of data, such as by adhering to Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) and Current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMPs) and ensuring that test materials are adequately identified and consistently produced. These measures enhance the accuracy of data (M. Zhang et al.). For botanical dietary components included in the pharmacopeia, the safety and efficacy of T&CM are more likely to be secure, as these components must meet strict regulations. These regulations typically include requirements for identification, purity, composition, and limits of contaminants.
Second, new technologies can ensure the safety and efficacy of T&CM, as they can improve the accuracy, efficiency, and reliability of the assessment. For example, high‐throughput screening technology can efficiently test the effects of numerous compounds on specific biological targets, enabling the identification of natural products within T&CM that may have potential anticancer activity (S. Li et al.). Genomics can be used to analyze all genes in the genome and their effects on organismal functions (Magnitov et al.). Moreover, metabolomics can be used to study the metabolic changes in the body following T&CM intake to assess their direct impact on health (J. Zhang et al.). Proteomics can be used to assess the impact of specific T&CM on protein expression to understand their potential health benefits (Pirola et al. 2025). Bioinformatics can be used to predict the interactions between compounds in T&CM and drug targets (X. Chen et al.). Furthermore, nanotechnology can be used to develop nanoparticles to improve the bioavailability and targeting of the active ingredients in T&CM (Liu et al.). Cell culture and tissue engineering can be used to assess the toxicity of T&CM in specific cell types (e.g., hepatocytes) using 3D cell culture models (Yee et al.). Moreover, remote monitoring technologies employed in clinical trials can be used to monitor the impact of T&CM on cardiovascular health using wearable devices (Kyriazakos et al.).
Finally, digital methods can improve the efficiency of the safety assessments of T&CM. (1) Establishing and integrating professional databases for T&CM to collect and integrate basic information, traditional uses, safety evaluation materials, and other relevant data can provide comprehensive support for safety assessments. For example, the Evaluation and Utilization Professional Committee of Food and Medicine Homologous Materials of the China Association of Chinese Medicine initiated the construction of a food and medicine homologous database, aiming to serve government supervision and management, public inquiries, professional research and development, and international exchange (China Association of Traditional Chinese Medicine). (2) Using big data analysis and AI algorithms to comprehensively analyze the history of use, chemical components, and toxicological data of T&CM, and building predictive models can help assess potential safety issues. For instance, the US Dietary Supplement Ingredient Database (DSID) can perform detailed chemical analyses and accurately estimate intake levels of dietary supplements (Bailey). (3) Using digital platforms for food safety risk monitoring to collect and analyze the market performance and consumer feedback of T&CM promptly allows for the rapid identification of emerging safety concerns. This, in turn, enables rapid responses and adjustments to relevant catalogs or product standards, ensuring the continuous protection of public health. (4) Establishing a quality traceability system for T&CM enables the monitoring of the entire process—production, processing, storage, and sales—of T&CM. This system enables the timely detection and control of safety risks associated with T&CM (Lin et al.).

Enhancing Global Trade of T&CM Through Strategic List Management
Against the backdrop of ongoing improvements in global T&CM laws and regulations, expanding the positive list (permitted ingredients) and implementing negative list (prohibited or restricted ingredients) management have emerged as key trends in the T&CM industry development (Wang and Deng). For example, China first published the “List of Foods that are also Medicinal Materials” in the annex of the “Sanitation Management Methods for Prohibiting the Addition of Drugs to Food” in 1987, which included 33 varieties. After nearly 40 years of development, it expanded to 102 varieties in 2023, and in August 2024, Rehmanniae Radix, Ophiopogonis Radix, Asparagi Radix, and Citri grandis Exocarpium were added to the list of FAMS (People's Daily Online), reflecting a gradual increase over time. In addition, the US, EU, and other countries have published most dietary supplement ingredient directories, regularly updating them on the related websites. India has published a list of plants that can be used as T&CM, and Russia has formulated a positive and negative list directory for T&CM. The implementation of a positive list may encourage enterprises to conduct more scientific research and innovation to identify new ingredients and applications. The implementation of a negative list can minimize the risk of unsafe products entering the market, which may lead to the withdrawal of products containing restricted ingredients, fostering the availability of safer products. Therefore, establishing internationally standardized positive and negative lists can further promote international trade in T&CM. Implementing such lists is an important means to ensure the safety and efficacy of T&CM; however, it also requires a balance between the rigor of regulations and the need for market flexibility.

Promoting the Global T&CM Market Through International Regulations and Cooperation
In terms of food safety, four international organizations are recognized as sources of internationally accepted standards: the Codex Alimentarius Commission, the International Office of Epizootics, the International Plant Protection Convention, and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). The main task of the Codex Alimentarius Commission, a joint body of the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and WHO, is to protect consumer health and ensure fairness and justice in the international food trade. The food safety standards set by this organization, which stipulate the maximum allowable limits for additives, contaminants, pesticide residues, and veterinary drugs, can serve as reference standards for food in international trade. In terms of food safety management, regulations on GMPs, good hygiene practices (GHPs), and hazard analysis and critical control points (HACCP) are specifically designed for individual facilities, production lines, and product compositions, as well as their corresponding processing procedures. These regulations form the backbone of the safety management system within the food supply chain (Aruoma). The primary objective of ISO 22000 is to harmonize global standards for food safety management. This comprehensive standard ensures the safety of food products throughout the entire supply chain, from the farm to the consumer's table (H. Chen et al.).
Based on these international regulations, countries should strengthen cooperation and exchange in the field of T&CM, jointly formulate and improve international standards and specifications, and promote the healthy development of the global T&CM market. Even if full regulatory harmonization is not achievable, regulatory or agreement cooperation remains a practical and effective option for ensuring consistency and safety across borders. For example, to support cooperation between regulatory agencies, the International Cooperation on Herbal Medicine (IRCH) was established in Ottawa in 2005 with the support of the WHO, which now has more than 20 member countries (Dwyer et al.; Oketch‐Rabah et al.). Since its establishment in 1998, the International Alliance of Dietary/Food Supplement Associations (IADSA) has evolved into an alliance of more than 50 dietary supplement associations across 6 continents, with more than 9500 companies worldwide as members (Aruoma; Ghosh et al.). With the improvement of laws and regulations and in‐depth cooperation, the safety and efficacy of T&CM will be more strongly guaranteed, thereby enhancing consumer trust and promoting continued market growth.
In summary, this study uses China, the US, the EU, Japan, South Korea, Russia, India, and Australia as examples of eight countries (international organizations) to compare and analyze the management, safety, and efficacy of T&CM in each country. It also provides an overview of African, Jewish, and Halal T&CM. Further research, including in‐depth discussions and analyses of the management, safety, and efficacy of T&CM in South America, Africa, and Central Asia, is required or gain a more comprehensive understanding of the global development of T&CM.

Conclusion
This review offers a global perspective on the management status, safety, and efficacy evaluation of T&CM. A relatively complete management system for such products has been established in regions such as Eurasia, North America, and Oceania. In Africa, efforts are underway to improve the management system for T&CM, with particular attention given to meeting the specific dietary requirements of Jewish and Muslim communities. The regulatory approaches are categorized into pre‐market and post‐market regulations. Moreover, there are notable variations in the application and management methods for T&CM worldwide. Although evaluating the safety and effectiveness of T&CM, particularly medicinal herbs, presents significant challenges, establishing a standardized international framework could potentially enhance the global safety and effectiveness of T&CM.
