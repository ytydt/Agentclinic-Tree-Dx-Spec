Highlights
Generative AI use in cancer patient education surged in 2024 across global studies.
ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 dominate applications for patient information research.

Background
Generative AI (GenAI) tools, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), are increasingly used across clinical contexts; including to support patient information needs. As these technologies become more prevalent, understanding their utilisation and evaluation in practice is critical. This scoping review aimed to map existing literature on GenAI applications in education for patients with cancer and identify trends in evaluation practices.
Methods
A scoping review was conducted following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. PubMed and Medline databases were searched for studies published between January 2019 and November 2024. Fifty-four eligible articles were analysed for GenAI models used, treatment modalities, education contexts, prompt sources, and evaluation domains and metrics.
Results
Most studies (81.5 %) were published in 2024, with over half (55.6 %) originating from the USA. ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 were the most frequently used models. Decision-making and general disease information were the predominant education contexts. Evaluation of GenAI outputs was reported in 96 % of studies, with accuracy (61.1%), readability (42.6 %), and quality (29.6 %) as the most common domains. More than half (50.8 %) of evaluation metrics were custom scales, indicating limited use of standardised tools. Patient-centred frameworks were rarely applied.
Conclusion
GenAI shows promise in enhancing patient education for cancer care, but evaluation practices lack standardisation and cultural responsiveness. Future research should prioritise validated frameworks, patient-centred metrics, and prompt engineering strategies to ensure safe, equitable and effective integration of GenAI in clinical care.

Introduction
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) refers to artificial intelligence systems capable of producing novel content such as text, images, or audio. Large Language Models (LLMs) are a subset of GenAI trained on vast datasets to generate human-like responses to submitted queries or ‘prompts’. Public awareness of, and engagement with, GenAI platforms has increased rapidly over recent years. As of July 2025, ChatGPT reportedly had over 700 million active users and processed approximately 18 billion queries per week.
Alongside the increased utilisation of GenAI across professional and personal contexts, limitations with platform performance have also been highlighted. In a variety of circumstances, GenAI models have been shown to provide factually incorrect information (often referred to as AI ‘hallucination’), offer responses with low comprehensibility, and exhibit limited reliability when citing source references. These challenges highlight the importance of user awareness in the safe and effective application of GenAI. Given these limitations, there is an emerging field of ‘prompt engineering’ centred around the intentional design and structure of input prompts to guide GenAI towards producing more accurate and contextually relevant outputs.
Within the context of healthcare, GenAI applications are forecast to grow at the highest rate of any industry. While AI can be deployed across a range of health-related scenarios, patient education has emerged as an area of focus. A recent scoping review identified 201 studies relating to the use of LLMs in patient education, with the most common theme relating to answering patient questions, alongside a wide range of other indications. More specifically, a blinded study found that patients preferred responses generated by ChatGPT over those provided by physicians in domains of both empathy and quality.
In the field of Radiation Therapy, patients with cancer often face many questions and uncertainties regarding their illness and its management over the course of their treatment journey. Radiation Therapy involves complex procedures and terminology, often requiring patients to navigate unfamiliar concepts. GenAI may serve as a resource to improve understanding and promote informed discussions with healthcare providers.
This scoping review aims to map the current literature on GenAI use in education for patients with cancer and examine trends in evaluation practices. By synthesising available evidence, we seek to inform future research and guide safe, equitable integration of GenAI into clinical care.

Methods

Study design
The scoping review was conducted as per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework. The search strategy was informed by a generalised GenAI scoping review (9), with further refinements to align with the focus of this study which exclusively targeted information needs of patients with cancer. A study protocol was not registered, nor were protocol databases utilised to inform study design.

Search strategy
A reference librarian was consulted to support a comprehensive approach to selection of terms. PubMed and Medline (Ovid) databases were utilised to identify articles associated with three main MeSH terms and associated keywords: “Artificial Intelligence”; “Patient Education”; and “Neoplasm”. Results were limited to those published between January 1, 2019, and November 28, 2024 to ensure results were reflective of contemporary GenAI platforms. Detailed information of the search strategy and restrictions is outlined in Supplementary Table 1. Citation management and screening was performed using Rayyan software.

The selection process involved two stages. Initially, two reviewers (KO and AL) independently screened titles and abstracts. This was to confirm retrieved results as relevant to the terms of the search strategy. Full-text articles were then sought, with those available undergoing independent assessment by the same two reviewers as per eligibility criteria outlined in Table 1. In cases where there were discrepancies between reviewers regarding eligibility, a collaborative discussion was conducted until a consensus was reached. Only studies deemed eligible by both reviewers were included in the final analysis.

Data extraction and analysis
Data extraction from the included articles was performed by one reviewer (KO) using a structured analysis table consisting of the following fields: 1) year; 2) country; 3) cancer type; 4) education context; 5) treatment modality; 6) GenAI model; 7) prompt source; 8) inclusion of GenAI output evaluation; 9) GenAI output evaluation domain(s); and 10) GenAI output evaluation measure(s). Study design and patient demographics were not systematically extracted because most reports evaluated GenAI outputs rather than enrolled patient samples. Where data were ambiguous or not clearly defined, categorisation was based on consensus between reviewers. Multiple responses per field for an individual article were recorded and coded accordingly.
Data were analysed using descriptive statistics with Microsoft Excel. Figures were created using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism (v10.5.0). As this review analysed published literature, ethics approval was not required.

GenAI models
Frequency of GenAI models utilised across studies.
ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 were shown to be the most frequently used GenAI models, featuring in 38.4 % and 29.1 % of the 54 studies, respectively (Fig. 2). Bard was reported in 10.5 % of studies, with 12 additional models identified but reported less frequently. Notably, 17 studies (31.5 %) utilised more than one model for comparative evaluation of GenAI platforms.

Distribution of treatment modalities and associated education contexts across studies.
Studies were categorised based on associated treatment modality and education context (Table 4). Surgery was most commonly identified modality (70.4 %), followed by radiation oncology (53.7 %), then medical oncology (50.0 %). More than half (53.7 %) of studies related to more than one treatment modality, whilst 10 studies (18.5 %) did not relate to a specific modality. Treatment decision-making and general disease information were the most frequently explored education contexts. Fig. 3 shows the distribution of papers across education contexts by modality. Decision making remained the most common domain for each individual modality, with broadly similar trends in distribution observed across education contexts between modalities.
Prompt sources were categorised into five groups: clinician-led (crafted by health professionals), patient-led (crafted directly by patients), educational resource-derived (sourced from patient information resources), online search trends (common public search queries), and multiple sources. The most common were clinician-led prompts (31.5 %), followed by prompts derived from educational resources (24.1 %) or online search trends (24.1 %). Patient-led prompts featured in 9.3 % of studies, while 11.1 % used multiple sources.

Evaluation practices
Frequency of GenAI output evaluation domains across studies.
Evaluation of GenAI outputs in response to input prompts was conducted in 52 of 54 studies (96 %). The two exceptions explored patient experience interacting with a GenAI platform, rather than content generated. Across all studies review, 143 individual evaluation domains were identified (median 2 domains per study; range 0–9). Individual domains were subsequently grouped into 30 distinct categories (Fig. 4). The most common domains were accuracy (61.1 %), readability (42.6 %), quality (29.6 %), and comprehensiveness (24.1 %).
Data were also extracted regarding the specific evaluation metrics used to measure associated domains. In total, 179 individual metrics were identified, subsequently grouped into 18 common items. Seventeen items represented distinct, standardised metrics, while one overarching ‘custom scale’ item was used to categorise bespoke tools developed by study authors. Of relevance, custom scales represented more than half (50.8 %) of all metrics identified across studies.

Fig. 5 illustrates connections between GenAI output evaluation domains and associated metrics. Readability was frequently measured through standardised metrics including the Flesch Kincaid Grade Level and Reading Ease Score. Conversely, accuracy, the most common evaluation domain, was predominately assessed using custom scales with only limited use of standardised tools such as the F1 scale. Several standardised metrics encompassed multiple evaluation domains. For example, PEMAT assessed actionability, understandability, reliability, comprehensiveness and quality; DISCERN evaluated quality and reliability; and NLAT-AI included effectiveness, accuracy, safety, actionability and appropriateness.

Discussion
To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first comprehensive scoping review focused on the use and evaluation of GenAI outputs for patient education in cancer care. This study provides a foundational understanding of current research and highlights key opportunities for future exploration.
Most included studies were published in 2024, reflecting the emerging nature of GenAI in healthcare. While over half originated from the USA, contributions from 15 other countries indicate global interest. Among the diverse AI models that were employed, ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 represented a clear majority, establishing OpenAI’s platforms as popular tools for GenAI applications in this field. Of note, most GenAI models identified in this review originate from North American companies which may in part explain the prevalence of studies from the same region.
Surgery was the most frequently studied treatment modality, with decision-making and general disease education the most common education contexts. A scoping review by Aydin et al explored GenAI for patient education across medicine more broadly and identified similar themes: ‘answering patient questions’, ‘interpreting medical information from a patient perspective’, and ‘providing lifestyle recommendations and improving health literacy’. Importantly, the distinction between high-risk decision-making questions and lower-risk general disease inquiries warrants attention. Decision-making is a critical and vulnerable point in the patient journey, where the implications of treatment choices are substantial. This is particularly relevant in the context of cancer care, given the serious nature of disease outcomes and the complexity of contemporary therapeutic approaches, including pharmaceutical interventions and radiation therapy. These decisions require clinical accuracy and emotional sensitivity, and potential errors in GenAI responses are particularly consequential. In contrast, inaccuracies in general disease education may pose a lower immediate risk but remain an important consideration. Misinformation may adversely affect patient engagement with healthcare services; therefore, it is essential to ensure that GenAI responses do not inadvertently include unreliable information derived from online training data.
Clinician-led prompts were the most common input source, followed by educational resources and online search trends. Notably, patient-led prompts were rarely used. Future research should ensure that GenAI outputs align with the tone and complexity of language used by diverse patient populations during interactions with these platforms. Only a small number of studies investigated prompt variation strategies. Variations included running identical prompts multiple times to assess consistency, tailoring prompts to specific demographics, and directly specifying readability levels. This highlights an important gap regarding the role of prompt engineering in improving the quality of GenAI outputs. AI competency more generally is an area of emerging focus in clinical education, and the design of GenAI prompts for both healthcare practitioners and their patients represents one of many considerations for future practice.
Accuracy, readability, and quality were the most frequently investigated GenAI response evaluation domains. However, the wide variety of evaluation domains suggests inconsistency in how GenAI output quality is being defined. Notably, patient-centred care domains such as empathy and emotional support were significantly underrepresented, while cultural responsiveness did not feature at all. Over half of the reviewed studies used custom scales to evaluate GenAI outputs. This reliance on non-standardised, subjective tools introduces variability in how quality is interpreted, often influenced by the researcher’s professional and cultural context. This limits comparability across studies and underscores the need for standardised GenAI evaluation frameworks.
It was beyond the scope of this review to systematically extract study findings and analyse trends in GenAI performance where consistent measures were utilised. However, it was noted anecdotally during the review process that conclusions varied considerably between studies using the same evaluation measures, GenAI models and clinical contexts, further highlighting the importance of standardised evaluation frameworks and influence of prompt design on response quality.
Though these findings suggest that GenAI can respond to a wide range of health-related queries in the context of cancer care, there are still valid concerns regarding the influence of GenAI on treatment decision making. It has been shown that GenAI can exhibit algorithmic bias, due to training data which lacks sufficient representation of diverse and minority populations. Indigenous and minority groups are frequently underrepresented in health data systems, limiting the extent to which GenAI can accurately capture and reflect their health needs. Without intentional design and application, GenAI tools run the risk of perpetuating or exacerbating current inequities in care.
Additionally, digital literacy is becoming recognised as a key determinant of health with direct implications for equity in the application of AI technologies. This relates not only to an individual’s familiarity with technology, but also structural barriers such as internet access and financial constraints.
This review was limited to English-language studies and relied on published literature available within Medline and PubMed databases. These databases were chosen following a similar scoping review and guidance from the reference library to fit the project's capacity. Some relevant studies outside this scope may therefore have been omitted. As a rapidly evolving field, it is also acknowledged that studies published more recently will not have been included in this review, potentially omitting important advances in GenAI model performance and shifts in their application within clinical practice. This limitation reflects the dynamic nature of GenAI research and underscores the need for ongoing review to capture emerging evidence and trends.
Future research should prioritise validated evaluation frameworks, patient-centred and culturally responsive metrics, as well as prompt engineering strategies. Comparative studies across GenAI platforms and exploration of cultural adaptation are essential to ensure safe, equitable integration of these tools into cancer care.

Conclusion
GenAI demonstrates significant promise in supporting education for patients with cancer, particularly in areas such as treatment decision-making and general disease understanding. Across the reviewed literature, accuracy, readability, and quality emerged as the most commonly evaluated domains. However, evaluation practices remain inconsistent, with widespread use of custom metrics and limited application of patient-centred or culturally responsive frameworks.
To realise the potential of GenAI in patient education for cancer care, future research should prioritise the development of standardised evaluation frameworks, explore prompt engineering strategies, and embed equity-focused approaches that reflect diverse patient needs. These efforts are essential to ensure GenAI is integrated into cancer care in ways that are safe, equitable, and clinically meaningful.
