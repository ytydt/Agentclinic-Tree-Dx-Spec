Optimization of the Nonclinical Biological Evaluation of Medical Devices Using Toxicologic Pathology Best Practices
Abstract
Innovation in the medical device industry is challenging due to the increasing physicochemical, structural, and engineering complexity of devices; proliferation of combination products; advent of long‐term implants; and variable approaches to assessing tissue reactions. Nonclinical evaluation of biological responses to implanted devices requires careful study design and implementation of pathology practices to obtain the best data for translating device‐related tissue reactions (“toxicity”) and associated procedure‐induced effects to predict possible outcomes in human patients. This paper outlines optimal pathology practices and related regulatory considerations that frame the nonclinical interpretation of biological responses induced by medical devices and combination products. These pathology practices have been developed by expert pathologists with extensive experience in medical device evaluation to address current guidance found in existing ISO Series standards, regulatory documents, and globally accepted “best practice” recommendations for toxicologic pathology assessment of therapeutic entities. Accordingly, the practices in this paper provide an optimized framework for biological evaluation of tissue responses to medical devices and combination products (both degradable and permanent devices) that should improve the relevance and utility of animal safety data provided to health authorities for human risk assessment.
Nonclinical evaluation of biological responses to implanted medical devices and combination products in animals is required to predict possible outcomes and risks in patients. This paper describes globally accepted pathology “best practices” that 1) effectively address ISO standards and current regulatory guidance for medical device development and 2) provide a physiologically relevant approach to rationally interpreting the biological significance of device‐related tissue responses.

Introduction
The discovery and development of medical devices to address “unmet medical needs” —— defined as the gap between necessary healthcare services and those actually available to patients —— is inherently complex. Advances in materials and structural designs for novel medical devices, the growing use of combination products blending devices with bioactive (“drug”) products, and the rising numbers of long‐term implants (degradable and permanent) all contribute to the lengthy and multidisciplinary nonclinical[  ] development process. This process involves disparate scientific disciplines spanning basic biological, physical and engineering sciences as well as data translation from animal studies to predict potential biological responses that might occur in human patients. Scientists in these divergent communities have differing areas of expertise, including biology, chemistry, engineering, materials science, and medicine (medical or veterinary medical). Fundamental medical specialties necessary for device development include laboratory animal medicine, pathology, surgery, and toxicology. In such a communal endeavor, scientists with divergent or partially overlapping areas of expertise bear joint responsibility for the development of medical devices, but the disparate backgrounds and development‐related activities mean that key individuals often do not speak the same scientific language and may approach the design and interpretation of animal studies from different frames of reference. This situation does not mean that the engineering and biology communities never collaborate, but rather that such interactions are the exception, occurring most frequently in established medical device companies and not with start‐up firms or academic institutions. The paucity of communication across scientific disciplines hinders the effective design and implementation of efficacy and safety testing for devices and combination products. In particular, the divergence in analytical objectives and approaches between materials scientists and safety biologists (pathologists and toxicologists) frequently compromises the quality of biological evaluations of tissue responses.
Such fundamental divergence in scientific objectives and approaches is clearly exemplified by comparing the methods for biological evaluation of tissue responses guided by International Organization for Standardization (ISO) (Series 10993 on “biological evaluation of medical devices”) with globally accepted pathology practices used by toxicologic pathologists. At present, the nonclinical biological evaluation of medical devices and device‐led combination products (where “led” refers to the portion of the combination product responsible for its primary mode of action) is primarily guided by ISO 10993 standards, sometimes with adjustments made by a regional health authority (e.g., the U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA] guidelines). However, a more physiologically relevant approach to biological evaluation of device‐related tissue responses is established in the principles and time‐tried practices of the toxicologic pathology profession that are routinely employed in nonclinical safety testing for most classes of novel therapeutic entities, but which are not being applied consistently, let alone optimally, by medical device innovators and regulatory bodies. In particular, we note substantial discrepancies in how the existing ISO 10993 Series standards, FDA guidelines, and Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)‐compliant recommendations for pathology evaluation from the FDA and Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) are being followed by sponsors, regulatory agencies, and even toxicologic pathologists in their application to the biological evaluation of medical devices and combination products. These discrepancies include differences in study design, designated methods for biological response evaluation, diagnostic terminology, choice of analytical approach and scoring schemes for data generation, interpretation, and reporting for medical device studies.[  ] These differing perspectives complicate the evaluation of device‐associated biological responses by toxicologic pathologists, which increases the variability in biological response data and confounds decision‐making regarding risk assessment by medical device innovators and health authorities.
As a discipline, toxicologic pathology in nonclinical development continues to focus mainly on evaluating chemical safety and characterizing biological reactions to new therapeutic candidates, including traditional drugs (i.e., small molecules) and newer precision medicine products (biologics, cell and gene therapies, etc.).[  ] Toxicologic pathology practices are more established and harmonized for the biological evaluation of conventional therapeutic products. The increasing complexity of medical devices[  ] including the growing availability of combination products (which integrate two or more therapeutic modalities, often matching a bioactive cargo or coating with a device) highlights the need to devise more effective approaches for evaluating biological reactions and their implications (as adverse [“harmful”] or non‐adverse [“safe” or “well‐tolerated”]).[  ] Fortunately, measurement of tissue responses to divergent therapeutic classes can be readily adapted to medical device evaluation in animals (which is especially needed for Class II and Class III devices).[  ] Toxicologic pathologists have been working diligently for decades to devise optimal practices (including suitable harmonized terminology) and expand the number of reference publications that inform biological evaluation of medical devices. However, these methodological improvements have yet to be incorporated into engineering‐centric development standards, regulatory documents, and journal publications that are currently accepted by medical device innovators and materials engineers as preferred resources for designing biological evaluation of medical devices and combination products. Importantly, established toxicologic pathology practices for non‐device biomedical products maintain good scientific principles for rigorous analysis while generally being more flexible than the criteria in existing standards for biological evaluation of medical devices. Rather than continuing to follow the historical trend of segregating biological evaluation of medical devices from other therapeutic modalities, toxicologic pathologists who have broad experience with medical devices as well as many other biomedical product classes encourage adaptation of the existing, well‐established, and flexible pathology‐based practices for non‐device products to support development of implants. This shift would include adapting the globally harmonized terminology, semi‐quantitative scoring systems and interpretive narratives, and other pathology practices for evaluation of medical devices. Aligning pathology evaluations of medical devices with long‐accepted standards for other biomedical product classes will improve the consistency of data generation and reporting for therapeutic products generally and thus enhance the scientific quality (as measured by data accuracy, consistency, and ease of interpretation and reporting) of pathology assessments for medical devices. Accordingly, this shift will substantially improve risk assessment for informing clinical biocompatibility of medical devices.
This review paper offers an overview of currently accepted biological evaluation practices used by toxicologic pathologists for study design and the generation and interpretation of high‐quality pathology data for medical devices. Adoption of these practices will result in more rigorous, predictable, efficient and consistent outcomes that are acquired more economically and using fewer animals. The intended audience for this paper includes academic and industry‐based developers of medical devices/combination products, regulatory reviewers, and other scientists with no or limited experience with the toxicologic pathology field and pathology “best practices” for evaluating tissue responses. This well‐informed perspective on efficient and effective biological evaluation has been crafted by toxicologic pathologists with considerable experience in evaluating medical devices and combination products. Content includes an overview of discordant views among various stakeholders regarding approaches to biological evaluation of tissue responses in nonclinical animal testing of medical devices (concentrating on existing ISO guidelines) and also offers expert guidance for understanding and navigating regulatory document components that affect pathology (“biological”) evaluations of medical devices. A glossary of core pathology concepts and terms relevant for nonclinical safety studies is included as Table  1 . The pathology‐based “points to consider” in this paper are intended as expert recommendations to promote communication among medical device innovators, researchers, development scientists (including pathologists), and regulatory reviewers to ensure that well‐conceived and rigorous but nonetheless flexible evaluations of implant‐related biological responses will yield readily interpretable and physiologically relevant data sets from animal studies that support nonclinical risk assessments and regulatory decision‐making. Notably, a case study comparing how an existing ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E rubric and a tissue and device‐specific pathology grading scheme will yield divergent interpretations regarding the nature and severity of a device‐related tissue response is offered to show how a rigidly structured analytical standard as currently expected and implemented by many device developers may not provide an accurate and biologically relevant assessment of the tissue changes.

Differing Perspectives Among Sponsors, Regulatory Agencies, and Toxicologic Pathologists
Historically, the development of medical devices has used a separate nonclinical pathway from that of various drug products. Guidance for safety testing of medical devices under ISO standards has not kept pace with the rapid evolution of device technologies and remains less flexible than the biological evaluation practices established in regulatory guidance for drugs.[  ] This rigidity often renders biological response data obtained strictly according to ISO standards partly or completely irrelevant for risk assessment, which in large part reflects the absence or limited incorporation of toxicologic pathology expertise into generating the ISO approaches for addressing biological evaluation for medical devices.[  ] For example, ISO standards emphasize assessing “biocompatibility,” defined as “the ability of a device material to perform with an appropriate host response in a specific situation”.[  ] This term assumes that the goal of nonclinical safety testing is to identify compatibility (a positive attribute) with tissues and/or the body (tissue reactivity). In contrast, with nonclinical testing of drugs the primary safety attribute is to determine the relationship of a test article with tissues changes interpreted as adverse (a negative attribute), where “adversity” is typically defined as something that causes “harm to the test animal”[  ] or that induces a “test item‐related change in the morphology, physiology, growth, development, reproduction or life span of the animal model that likely results in an impairment of functional capacity to maintain homeostasis and/or an impairment of the capacity to respond to an additional challenge”.[  ]
A safety assessment focused on adversity can also be applied to medical devices and is arguably more relevant for risk assessment compared to biocompatibility.[  ] Importantly, determination of adversity with respect to the tissue response induced by a medical device cannot be based on a simple mathematical value calculated using differences in group mean scores between a test material and a control material[  ] since findings may change over time (e.g., necrosis early in the study may fully resolve, or implant degradation products may migrate to regional lymph nodes, which is noted only in subchronic or chronic studies). Although the FDA has begun to use the term “adversity” when discussing the nonclinical safety of medical devices,[  ] the majority of medical device evaluations and the literature continue to discuss “biocompatibility” as defined in the current ISO 10993 standards on “biological evaluation of medical devices” as the sole or main measure of nonclinical safety for medical devices. This remains the most common approach to the biological evaluation of medical devices in tissue, although much of the ISO 10993 series more accurately applies to ensuring manufacturing consistency and not to analyzing tissue reactions to medical devices. Granted, the ISO “biocompatibility” assessment may be suitable for evaluating some simple tissue implants (e.g., any material component introduced to assess short‐term muscle “irritation” as originally outlined in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E). That said, a rigid application of the “biocompatibility” approach using pre‐set scoring rubrics as defined in ISO 10993–6 typically yields inadequate or erroneous analytical breadth and unclear interpretation of biological responses for certain organs and complex devices (see Section 4 and the case study below). Therefore, we recommend against using biocompatibility as the sole measure of safety for medical devices and especially device‐led combination products.
Risk assessment is also plagued by differing perspectives regarding the relevant outcome for defining safety. Current medical device standards, as influenced heavily by chemical and engineering fields, often utilize “risk assessment” as terminology for any potential hazard (both theoretical and likely) with an attempt to model and/or minimize all risks, including physical failure (fragmentation and spills). In contrast, guidance for drug development as devised by expert biologists (including wide‐ranging input from toxicologic pathologists and toxicologists) interprets “risk assessment” as indicating whether or not biological responses in animals can identify and predict tissue reactions, and especially adverse (harmful) ones, that might occur in human patients. These differing perspectives have become magnified by the rigid constraints frequently imposed by existing standards on the designs and biological evaluation methods allowed to developers of medical devices and combination products. For example, ISO 10993 contains recommendations for analyzing both mechanical/engineering risk and biological risk. Sections of ISO (including the entire ISO 10993‐18:2020/Amd1:2022 standard[  ] and ISO 10993‐1:2018[  ]) are devoted to chemical/leachable risk management and regulatory requirements for medical device development while also including steps to identify and rank potential risks of a device including improper use or mechanical failure, risk management of in vivo devices over time, and exposure to broken devices and components. These engineering factors are amenable to quantitative analysis and decision‐making using specific pre‐set algorithms. However, the in vivo biological assessment and estimation of possible clinical impact(s) rely instead on biological evaluations to detect local (ISO 10993–6:2016[  ]) and systemic (ISO 10993‐11:2017[  ]) tissue responses (“toxicity”) of implanted biomaterials, which by their qualitative or semi‐quantitative nature require that the analysis be performed by a pathologist who can devise relevant lesion grading criteria that account for many variables in data generation and interpretation; such data are generally unsuitable for statistical analysis given their partly subjective nature[  ] and the inability to predefine universal rubrics for grading tissue reactions. Such factors make it critical that biological risk assessment be performed by experienced toxicologic pathologists with a full understanding of the “test system” (i.e., animal species) and the entire study design. For instance, species or strain variations or an age‐related tissue change may be completely irrelevant when seen in an animal study, but its classification as a clinical risk by inexperienced microscopists can misinform adversity determinations and subsequent risk assessment decisions. Inappropriately interpreting in vivo biological responses as indicating risk in medical device development can result in failed studies and unnecessary repetition of animal experiments, which is both time‐ and resource‐consuming. Specific examples of discrepancies in biological response evaluations and recommendations for interpreting regulatory documents guiding such evaluations from the perspective of experienced toxicologic pathologists are presented in Section 4 below.

Recommendations for Appropriate Pathology Practices in Medical Device Testing
To enhance the relevance of nonclinical device testing for human risk assessment, pathologists should be consulted in designing relevant biological response endpoints in study protocols for medical devices and combination products and outlining these procedures in detail within the study protocol and/or pathology report. Moreover, the broad knowledge of experienced medical device pathologists with respect to relevant, physiologically based study procedures (design, conduct, sample collection, and tissue evaluation for data generation, interpretation, and reporting) should be incorporated widely into future iterations of ISO standards and regulatory agency guidance documents for biological evaluation of tissue responses in developing medical devices. This pathology‐focused approach will ensure that analytical methods for each medical device study will provide suitable data to answer study‐specific safety questions while still addressing the general regulatory guidelines and ISO standards. An unsuitable study design or inappropriate data generation can easily result in a need to repeat studies, which is consistent with neither good scientific or animal use principles nor with GLP requirements. Many medical device studies require a tailored approach due to inherent nuances associated with the implant (chemical and/or physical attributes), the implantation site, or even the limitations of the available animal model. Therefore, it is recommended that each study's parameters be developed on a case‐by‐case basis and timepoints chosen to reflect the degradable or non‐degradable characteristics of the device to be tested. The choice of parameters may require extensive literature searches, comparisons to predicate device studies (if available), and/or pilot studies to determine optimal procedures. Furthermore, we emphasize that meeting with regulatory agencies to discuss nuances of regional or national requirements for nonclinical safety testing prior to performing pivotal safety studies may save time, cost and effort.
The following recommendations for optimal biological evaluation (pathology “best practices”) of medical devices and combination products are adapted from globally accepted pathology practices for developing drugs. In particular, the inclusion of appropriate control groups (see Section 5.1 below) and regular use of the pathology peer review process (see Section 6.2.2 below) should be considered as indispensable in generating accurate data regarding product‐related biological responses for medical devices studies as they are for drug development.[  ] These optimized pathology practices should be used to analyze biological responses for medical device studies instead of (or at least in addition to) the current limited menu of methods typically applied by industrial sponsors based on rigid interpretation of guidance in existing regulatory documents.

Toxicologic Pathology Perspective on Biological Evaluation as Expressed in Critical Regulatory Documents
Existing guidance particularly relevant for in vivo animal study design to assess implanted biomaterials is found in ISO 10993–6:2016[  ] for local effects and ISO 10993‐11:2017[  ] for potential systemic toxicity. These two primary standards inform biological evaluation in assessing tissue reactions to novel medical devices and combination products.
These two ISO 10993 series standards were written from the perspective of assessing in vivo tissue responses to simple material implants placed into bone, muscle, and subcutaneous tissue, with later additions of other tissues (notably brain) in the 2016 version. These two standards attempt to cover the subject of device development and testing for a diverse group of products and are inherently broad; therefore, not all sections are applicable to all types of devices. Instead, these two standards are meant to provide general examples, so they should not be considered as inflexible study directives (“mandates”) appropriate for addressing all study‐specific questions that might arise in medical device testing. Importantly, these ISO standards were written in isolation from both current industrial recommended (“best”) practices for evaluating biological responses[  ] as well as FDA and ICH (International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use) guidance documents for toxicological/pathological review of in vivo studies for other biomedical products.[  ] Substantial differences may be found in the guidance for performing typical analyses between medical device testing and drug testing, including expected best practices for nonclinical studies, the accepted harmonized diagnostic nomenclature for product‐related tissue changes, the types of accepted tests, differences in testing flowcharts, recommendations on the use of control groups and data peer review, and variations in facility and personnel standards. The complexity of device and combination product development adds to the burden of providing specific standards or guidance documents that cover every scenario, or even all common scenarios.[  ] Although standards and guidance documents clearly include intrinsic statements that the stated design and analysis practices should be interpreted as “informative” examples and not inflexible mandates (e.g., ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E), sponsors and contract research organizations (CROs) may lack familiarity with available regulatory documents, lack experience in regulatory interactions, and may be faced with inexperienced regulatory reviewers who interpret examples from standards and guidance documents as rigid requirements to be followed prescriptively regardless of their relevance or scientific validity for a particular study (with its discrete set of objectives) or product type. Additionally, though utilized globally as the standards (Table  2 ) for medical device materials and testing, critical variations exist in some countries regarding how ISO 10993 series and related standards are applied in the development of device and combination products.[  ]

For many reasons, including the increasing complexity of medical devices and combination products, the ISO series standards with their pre‐set rubrics generally do not reflect current best practices for pathology evaluation (clinical pathology, macroscopic [gross] pathology, and histopathology [microscopic pathology]), especially with respect to development of biologically relevant grading systems and diagnostic terminology (e.g., as noted by outdated rubric examples in Annex E of ISO 10993–6:2016). The flexible descriptive language, as well as the harmonized procedures and diagnostic terminology developed by toxicologic pathologists for safety testing of drugs, are better suited to characterizing subtle nuances and providing detailed interpretations in the narrative summary suited for assessing the biological implications of tissue response and can be readily adapted and applied to safety testing of medical devices and combination products. In ISO 10993‐11:2017,[  ] the potential tiered approach to systemic histopathology for medical devices using “sentinel organs” does not follow the generally accepted organ system terminology or tissue organization used in nonclinical testing for other biomedical products. The most scientifically justifiable practice for tissue sampling in device studies would be to collect the full set of tissues that are comparable to those for all nonclinical toxicology and carcinogenicity studies.[  ] For medical devices, the frequent choice to use a tiered approach that only screens a limited number of core tissues (Tier I), while triggering the expanded Tier II histopathology of all tissues in Annex F of ISO 10993‐11:2017 only if abnormalities are found in the core tissue screening[  ] runs the risk of missing systemic effects induced by the implants. In addition, the expanded tissue list in ISO 10993‐11:2017 is less extensive than the comprehensive approach for histopathologic examination generally considered necessary for a thorough biological evaluation of systemic toxicity for other classes of biomedical products.[  ] Typically for drugs, the full list of major organs and tissues is examined microscopically in the initial GLP‐compliant studies of 2 weeks or more in duration, generally only proceeding to a reduced tissue list, if warranted, once a demonstrated lack of effect allows the pathologist to exclude certain tissues from further microscopic examination in later studies. Accordingly, any tissue sampling for systemic toxicity of medical devices should be designed based on the type of material or device, its manufacturing and processing conditions, known class effects or failure modes for the intended clinical use, implant locations, duration of contact and degradation potential, and identified in‐life effects rather than assuming that the limited Tier I tissue list will reliably identify systemic toxicity. Comprehensive screening of tissues for systemic effects is particularly warranted for complex/invasive medical devices and chronically implanted devices. For such products, this extensive tissue survey should include, but not be restricted to, known systemic filtering organs (e.g., kidney, liver, lymph nodes [regional and distant], and spleen) and highly vascular organs representing potential sites of embolization (e.g., brain, heart, lung). Sampling to evaluate potential transfer to regional draining lymphoid tissues[  ] or migration to adjacent non‐target tissues can also be important depending on the biomaterial, device, and implantation site (e.g., gastrointestinal tract sampling is not normally required for Tier 1 evaluation of medical devices but should be included for implants placed in the abdominal cavity or in abdominal organs).
In ISO 10993–6:2016[  ] and ISO 10993‐11:2017[  ] as well as the recent FDA guideline on considerations for animal studies,[  ] citations within the bibliography are often outdated or irrelevant (e.g., the citation applies to simple or seldom‐used materials or uses an inadequate or archaic scoring system). Such discrepancies cause confusion in selecting and applying biological response endpoints in guidance documents among pathologists, sponsors and, sometimes, regulators. Notably, the ISO 10993 series standards and the lateral standards developed for medical device evaluation[  ] do not exemplify, use, or cite references to current globally accepted practices for generating and interpreting pathology raw data (with respect to observed tissue reactions) and preparing detailed pathology descriptions (a cornerstone of toxicologic pathology reports for all other kinds of biomedical products). These discrepancies in recommended pathology content for medical device and combination product studies are occurring despite a concerted effort by toxicologic pathologists to publish general as well as organ‐ and device‐specific recommendations and substantive “points to consider” white papers regarding relevant biological evaluation for medical devices and combination products.[  ]
Additionally, a recent FDA guidance document for medical devices[  ] has indicated that compliance with GLP scientific principles should be included for nonclinical studies of medical devices. The Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) is the mechanism to codify laws, rules, and regulations in the United States. The CFR Title 21 Chapter I is reserved for the FDA Department of Health and Human Services, with subchapters for general considerations (subchapter A), specific drug products (subchapters C─F), and medical devices (subchapter H). The GLP guidance for FDA is codified in CFR 21 Subchapter A Part 58[  ] to identify the general provisions for organizations and personnel, testing facilities and their operation, equipment, test and control articles, protocol preparation and conduct of nonclinical laboratory studies, requirements for records and reporting, and disqualification of testing facilities. In part, the confusion over GLP applicability to medical device studies arises from portions of the GLP regulations (21 CFR 58) that exclude proof‐of‐concept studies (such as engineering functionality studies). Instead, GLP regulations are applied to nonclinical safety (i.e., “toxicity” or “toxicology”) studies that seek to identify and characterize tissue reactions in vivo and define whether or not they are adverse.[ 5,29] Importantly, the consideration for indirect contact with medical devices (i.e., a device or device component through which a fluid or gas passes to gain physical contact with body tissue, while the device itself does not physically contact body tissue[  ]), has significantly expanded since the development of this guidance. However, the language used to provide direction for this purpose has not been updated. Given the expansion of testing strategies for medical devices and combination products, the somewhat vague references in current FDA guidance that studies “should be conducted in accordance with recognized Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) regulations”[  ] can cause significant confusion for many sponsors regarding what device studies fall under the GLP umbrella. Often, device “functionality” may be an integral factor in study design, and since many pivotal medical device studies are also utilized to assess potential adverse events (including systemic toxicity), compliance with GLP is understood as warranted, whenever possible, in medical device testing. The FDA does recognize that it may not be possible to conduct all study components or even complete studies (e.g., efficacy studies) in full compliance with GLP, and as with drug testing these GLP exceptions are often permissible for medical devices if accurately reported and rationally justified.[  ] GLP‐compliant study designs are intended to ensure that study components and procedures, as well as sample collection, tissue analysis, data interpretation and documentation, and archiving of data and biological samples are reliable, reproducible, traceable, and transparent. For GLP compliance, animal studies are conducted in accordance with a written and signed study protocol (including any subsequent signed protocol amendments) in conjunction with established facility standard operating procedures (SOPs) and sometimes additional written procedures for study‐specific tasks. A system of signatures, tracking changes, and auditing of procedures and records provides periodic checks during the course of the study to ensure data completeness and quality.[  ] Thus, GLP compliance reinforces regulatory confidence in testing facilities and personnel, the study design and conduct, the consistency of experimental procedures, the quality of generated data and reports describing them, and data retention. While typically found in industrial settings, academic institutions should consider implementing a GLP program if they routinely perform studies to support clinical testing of novel products.[  ]
The study pathologist is solely responsible for the content of the pathology report[  ] as stated in GLP guidance, which notes that the signature of the pathologist on the final pathology report is the means by which pathology raw data are established for the study.[  ] Descriptive terminology for nonclinical studies is harmonized for toxicologic pathology of drugs,[  ] and in a well‐administered and controlled nonclinical study, the identification and severity of treatment‐related tissue findings is typically consistent among trained pathologists.[  ] Assignment of a single properly trained and experienced pathologist to assess each study ensures that the tissue analysis is stable, while the use of an experienced peer review pathologist increases the accuracy of the final pathology data and their interpretation, thereby reinforcing the credibility and validity of the data.[  ] Current globally accepted best practices for toxicologic pathology allow pathologists to use their training and experience to establish and apply study‐specific criteria for severity grades and terminology for nonclinical studies evaluating drugs;[  ] the same flexibility could be, and indeed should be, used routinely for medical device evaluations. Industry sponsors should remember that FDA guidance documents are prominently headed with “Contains Nonbinding Recommendations”.[  ] Similarly, the ISO 10993 series standards are intended to provide general guidance rather than act as rigid benchmarks; indeed, Annex E of ISO 10993–6:2016 is labeled as “informative” and provides “examples” of potential rubrics for semi‐quantitative scoring.[  ] Notably, the provided ISO examples for cell and tissue responses (as well as all device materials) are not appropriate or accurate for all species, tissues, clinical indications, or devices, so use of the example rubrics as an inflexible menu of lesion scoring criteria[  ] for every device and situation with no study‐specific adjustments is scientifically indefensible and should be avoided. Instead, the study pathologist should devise[  ] a tailored semi‐quantitative rubric for particular tissue responses for each scenario that considers the animal species, tissue, indication, device, and time point (Figure  1 ).

For example, medical device pathologists distinguish between “infiltrates” and “inflammation,” with infiltrates representing quiescent accumulations of leukocytes (so‐called “inflammatory cells”) that form in tissue without causing damage to the parenchyma while inflammation connotes that the accumulating leukocytes are associated with evidence of tissue injury (e.g., disruption, edema, hemorrhage, or other parenchymal alterations) that is being driven partly or completely by the presence of the leukocytes (Figure  2 ).

Ideally, the fundamental deficiencies encoded in existing regulatory guidelines for medical devices should be subject to correction by expert medical device pathologists to improve tissue evaluation and data interpretation, thereby ensuring the relevance of biological response data. As examples of such codified deficiencies, Annex E of ISO 10993–6:2016 does not list apoptosis (self‐programmed death of individual cells); edema; hemorrhage; the presence of foreign material (e.g., device particles, bone or hair shaft fragments); and the progress of wound healing along the device insertion route as factors to capture in grading tissue responses while Table E.3 inappropriately conflates various leukocyte (“inflammatory cell”) types with necrosis (i.e., a generic term for cell death) in producing an “inflammation” score even when the necrosis involves the organ parenchyma and not the accumulating leukocytes (Table  4 ). Importantly, pathologists comprehend the nuances implied by a comprehensive menu of additional diagnostic options for additional or unusual tissue changes—none of which are listed in existing ISO standards—that often provide further clarity regarding the implications of biological responses associated with implanted devices (Table  5 ).

Some authors further make a distinction in the type of macrophage response seen in tissue near implanted devices by distinguishing between pro‐inflammatory (M1) and anti‐inflammatory (M2) macrophage phenotypes.[  ] However, the practicality of attempting phenotypic identification of macrophage states with cytologically overlapping features but divergent cell functions in tissue sections without application of immunophenotypic markers continues to be debated.[  ] Recent thought suggests that the highly variable microenvironment and signaling in response to different stimuli (e.g., different test materials) result in overlapping macrophage functions and immunophenotypes.[  ] Additionally, a consistent and irrevocable time‐dependent progression of M1 to M2 macrophages throughout the injury and repair processes is likely an over‐simplistic concept of biological responses to implanted materials.[  ]

Pathology Evaluations Should Be Flexible and Tailored to the Specific Needs of the Study
For pathologists, or any other morphologist, evaluating tissue responses induced by medical devices, it is increasingly difficult and sometimes impossible to apply currently accepted best scientific practices for pathology evaluation of product‐related tissue responses while attempting to base the lesion scoring criteria on the examples and language provided for biological analysis commonly cited in ISO 10993 series standards[  ] and the FDA guidance document for animal studies.[  ] Industry sponsors and regulatory agencies frequently expect pathologists to strictly adhere to the scoring schemes stated in the example rubrics in FDA guidelines and ISO standards for descriptors, scoring criteria, and data interpretation. However, this perceived requirement that biological evaluations of medical devices should rigidly adhere to general examples in ISO standards and FDA guidance documents without considering whether these examples are scientifically rational endpoints applicable to the study objective(s) often results in pathology data sets for medical devices that lack biological relevance and cannot adequately address questions related to the risk posed by specific patterns of tissue responses. Ultimately, such major discrepancies in the design and conduct of the pathology evaluation hinder both nonclinical data interpretation and nonclinical data translation for human risk assessment (as discussed in Section 4 above).
The nature of regulatory documents guiding biological evaluation of tissue responses needs to be properly understood. Such documents provide a starting point for study design, but by intent they are generic and not intended to be all‐encompassing. Early involvement of a pathologist with relevant medical device experience and substantial familiarity with current guidelines as well as pathology “best practices” for study design, diagnostic nomenclature, and reporting standards and their application to biological evaluation (macroscopic and microscopic) of medical devices is essential when planning pivotal in vivo studies to ensure that the study design is time‐ and cost‐effective while also tailored to efficiently meet specific study objectives.[  ] Incorporating multiple critical pathology endpoints into one study is possible with careful planning and a well‐crafted protocol, and this approach can successfully provide necessary biological response data while satisfying animal welfare requirements and the 3Rs of animal research (i.e., replacement, reduction, and refinement).
From a pathologist's perspective, inappropriate scoring for the tissue and implant types and forced use of inflexible and simplistic subtractive scores (measured as the variation between tissue reactions between dissimilar control and test articles in animals) is the most egregious error that may regularly result in uninterpretable or misleading study conclusions. In considering the implications of various device‐associated tissues responses, reliance on numerical values produced by inappropriate semi‐quantitative scoring schemes often leads to a meaningless interpretation. Many tissue‐ and organ‐specific biological responses to implants (e.g., eye) do not fit neatly into the ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E scoring examples in Tables E.3 and E.4, which were originally designed for intramuscular and subcutaneous implants (in Table E.3) and neural implants (Table E.4). As is, parameters such as “fatty infiltration” are not biologically relevant for assessing tissue responses in either subcutaneous or neural implantation sites (Table 4), so scoring rubrics for these tissues should never include fatty infiltration. Inclusion of irrelevant parameters suggests to inexperienced stakeholders that this finding is a universally expected tissue change that requires a score, and that such parameters represent appropriate replacements for a more appropriate morphological diagnosis (e.g., increased numbers of adipocytes in response to a subcutaneous implant should be recorded as “increased cellularity, adipocytes”, as indicated in Table 5). Moreover, including irrelevant parameters with zero scores will potentially dilute the total and group averages. Thus, a “forced fit” approach where the analysis rigidly adheres to the example scoring rubrics in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E can easily result in erroneous conclusions about relative reactivity differences between test and control implants. A real‐world example of this point will make this difficulty glaringly obvious.
The following example illustrates the practical consequences of the ISO 10993 limitations discussed in Section 4, where divergent conclusions regarding the biological response to an ocular implant result from using the ISO standard scoring rubric (Table  6 ) versus implementing a purpose‐built scoring scheme devised by a toxicologic pathologist (Table  7 ). In a nonclinical rabbit study, a single injection of a polymer into the vitreal cavity of one eye was followed by a 4‐week observation period. Application of the standard scoring rubric in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E (Table 6) suggested that the intravitreal polymer, when compared to a saline injection (negative control) in the contralateral eye, was well‐tolerated based on the “group total” and “average” values for the response specifically at the intravitreal injection site. However, after a pathology peer review, the final pathology data were revised to score all features in the eye (vitreal cavity but also all surrounding affected ocular compartments, Table 7), which clearly indicated that this polymer was not tolerated well in the context of the entire organ. Additionally, the injury to compartments adjacent to the vitreal cavity, including the lens (fiber degeneration indicative of cataracts) and retina (necrosis, atrophy and detachment) are irreversible and collectively would have resulted in permanent vision loss. Based on this revised biological evaluation together with in vivo clinical observations and a parallel safety study in another nonclinical species, the risk of functional damage was deemed to be too great, and this polymer formulation was considered unsuitable for further nonclinical or clinical development.

Biological evaluations of many other tissues cannot be forced to fit into the example scoring rubrics found in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E. Accordingly, the various cell layers and types, tissue micro‐compartment designations, expected cell and tissue response parameters, and severity scoring options offered in Annex E should not be used for all implants or tissues, and should not be implemented formulaically even for those tissues mentioned in Annex E. Unique devices and unusual implant sites need to be approached on a case‐by‐case basis, and the correct scoring rubric should be crafted by the pathologist based on the actual appearance of tissue responses observed during the study, tempered as needed by their prior experience and supplemented when warranted by consultation with other team members or perusal of available published scoring schemes (if any). Rigid application of the wrong scoring rubric just because it exists in a published guidance document, while ignoring the genuine value provided by de novo, purpose‐made scoring schemes for pathology evaluation devised to fit the particular study objectives, medical device attributes, and implantation site features, will generally yield incomplete or inaccurate data and erroneous conclusions on tissue responses and their adversity. Such flawed results can needlessly waste animals and personnel resources, increase the time and costs of medical device development, and potentially imperil patients.

Use of Control Groups and/or Control Materials
The purpose of including control groups in the study design is to recognize and minimize the impact of animal variability (e.g., genetic, environmental, inter‐animal, test article versus “treatment” [procedure]‐related, experimenter, nuisance factors and concurrent diseases, and any other confounding variables) on the analysis of tissue reactions associated with medical devices.[  ] Randomization of animals, group sizes, power calculations,[  ] and investigator financial interests related to various approaches to study design and bias control will not be considered further in this communication.
For medical devices and associated implantation procedures, types of controls vary in their applicability and availability. Possible controls for animal studies include positive, negative, vehicle, sham, and untreated groups.[  ] The usefulness of such controls will be evident to everyone involved in study planning, but the study pathologist should be consulted to identify which, and whether, any additional control groups might be needed to ensure the quality of the pathology data and their interpretation. The types of experimental controls most applicable to medical devices are positive (e.g., a United States Pharmacopeia [USP] reference polymer); positive reference control (e.g., predicate medical device); and sham (e.g., surgical approach without device implantation). ISO 10993–6:2016 mentions the use of sham surgical controls, but it favors comparisons of the test device to positive or “comparative” controls. ISO 10993‐11:2017 also supports the use of sham controls. Where used, sham controls are helpful in clarifying data interpretation, and particularly in modifying (usually downgrading) findings attributed to the test device based on the extent of tissue reactions related to the procedure (as defined by contemporaneous evaluation of the test, reference, and sham groups). Sham controls can also be critical in adjusting for confounding tissue responses elicited by introduced location marker materials for bioabsorbed devices and secondary medical devices (surgical closures and drains) used in the surgery. While negative controls are seldom effective as the only control group in a medical device study, they can be valuable (or even necessary) in combination product studies.
Unfortunately, regulatory documents for medical device testing often employ the wrong terminology for control types used for medical device studies. For instance, in ISO 10993–6:2016, the Annex E conclusion states that tissue response differences should be compared between the negative control (classically defined as a saline or vehicle control with no tissue reaction) and the test material. In actuality, true negative controls seldom exist for medical device testing, and studies generally compare one or more positive controls, such as reference material (e.g., USP polymer) or a predicate (similar marketed device material) to the test material. In the same way, guidelines may not specify a control, may suggest irrelevant controls, or may provide confusing recommendations regarding controls, such as the use of sham or surgical controls. For example, ISO 10993‐11:2017 includes conflicting advice regarding the use of negative, vehicle, or sham controls. In some cases, guidance is just not attainable. In ISO 10933–6:2016, expected controls are defined as materials with clinical acceptability and characterized biocompatibility, and control or reference articles are expected to match the physical attributes such as shape, size, and especially surface condition of the test device. Although this goal is desirable, these equivalent characteristics, which substantially define the relevance of positive (reference) controls, are seldom achievable with modern materials. For instance, USP reference standard polymers (e.g., polyethylene or polypropylene) do not share the physicochemical characteristics of many novel test materials (e.g., thermo‐responsive polymers). As expected by local testing under ISO 10993–6:2016, a simple quantitative divergence in the tissue reaction between disparate test and control materials would represent an inconclusive difference at best. Similarly, if the test material is similar or identical to a reference material, this control would not provide sufficient comparison against the test materials in the study.
Compared to drugs, controls for medical device studies are often more complex but also need to be designed and interpreted in a more nuanced manner. From a pathologist's perspective (example in Table 7), appropriate interpretation would preclude the use of a simple tissue reactivity score as found in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E (e.g., the average score for a test device minus the average score for a presumably relevant control device [example in Table 6]). In particular, pathologists concur that rigid application of simple scoring schemes is contraindicated for any medical device study with numerous permutations (e.g., simultaneously testing inflammatory and other tissue reactions to biomaterials at many time points, using multiple types of controls, and incorporating both males and females). Whether or not the study design would be most appropriate with positive or negative controls (or both), with or without inclusion of sham and/or vehicle controls, should be judiciously considered for each test device and the specific objectives of the study. Additionally, careful attention must be given to the suitability of placing control and test materials in the same animal (at one or more sites distant from each other) or scattered among different animals. The decision regarding this design parameter is often device‐dependent. For example, ISO 10993–6:2016 specifications for intramuscular implantation studies recommend placing the control material on one side and the test material on the contralateral side of the same animal (with both materials located in a paravertebral location) but specifically excludes simultaneous intra‐animal control and test materials for neural tissue implants. Other types of implants, sensitive tissues, and combination products with an active pharmaceutical ingredient may also be incompatible with simultaneous insertion of test and control articles in a single animal. Studies to assess systemic toxicity done under ISO 10993‐11:2017 should always consider the use of control and test groups in separate animals.
A current trend being considered in nonclinical toxicity testing is the use of virtual control groups (VCG) to reduce animal usage. This laudable goal is currently undergoing proof‐of‐concept studies for selecting, qualifying, validating, and using subject‐matched control datasets for drug development.[  ] Indeed, the majority of current feasibility studies in this new field of VCG inquiry have concentrated on standardized nonclinical safety testing for drug evaluation and not the often specially designed studies used for medical device evaluation. Accordingly, VCG usage is not yet scientifically feasible for nonclinical studies of implanted devices. The reasons for this disqualification are the frequent lack of true negative control groups; poorly defined and highly variable positive control materials; wide variation in test materials (and manufacturing processes); concurrent use of both test and positive control materials in the same test animal; and the lack of large historical control databases to serve as the basis for assembling VCG data relevant to a particular nonclinical species and implantation site. Moreover, VCG are unlikely to afford sufficient discrimination among subtle nuances in tissue responses for morphologically complex reactions like inflammation, which is the hallmark pattern for typical safety studies in medical devices.[  ] These design parameters indicate that the incorporation of VCG will not be scientifically justifiable in the foreseeable future for assessing tissue responses during safety testing of medical devices.

Tissue Collection
Ideally, a pathologist with medical device experience should be consulted early in the study design phase to determine how the implant site, associated tissue reactions, and other organs should be collected, handled (including any measurements and photographic documentation), and preserved at necropsy (i.e., animal autopsy or pstmortem examination), with a focus on maintaining the integrity of the tissue:implant interface. On a study‐by‐study basis, the pathologist may decide that they need to be present in the necropsy suite to oversee dissection of the implant site or other tissues; “on call” at the test facility so that they can enter the lab to participate in the necropsy, if necessary; available by remote communication to answer questions during the necropsy; or not be directly involved in the dissection or necropsy data reporting. This decision will depend on various factors, including the complexity of organ and tissue structures at the implant site, the expertise of the technicians performing the necropsy, and whether the implant site will be removed and preserved intact for later processing or actually removed and dissected at necropsy. With many medical devices, pathologists affirm that the implant and surrounding tissues should be removed and fixed en bloc (i.e., intact, without dissection to separate the implant from the tissue), using intravascular perfusion with fixative solution if warranted, prior to macroscopic pathology examination. En bloc preservation best maintains the integrity of the tissue:implant interface (especially for soft, easily traumatized tissues like brain) and minimizes dissection‐related tissue damage and histology artifacts that can complicate or preclude microscopic examination.[  ] In these cases, the detailed macroscopic examination is delayed and conducted simultaneously with tissue trimming to prepare fixed specimens for histological processing.
To screen for systemic changes (under ISO 10993‐11:2017 and globally accepted pathology “best practices”), a broad set of tissues representative of major organs and systems is evaluated macroscopically and collected at necropsy, while histopathologic evaluation is performed on a subset of these tissues using a tiered approach.[  ] For studies conducted under GLP, the CRO will generally collect the complete list of tissues that are harvested for drug safety studies,[  ] with selective microscopic examination of the subset of tissues relevant to the specific medical device. Care must be taken with GLP‐compliant studies for medical devices that the most appropriate lymphoid tissues are collected (usually the closest regional lymph nodes or mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue [MALT] draining the actual implantation site) rather than only sampling the standard visceral lymph nodes that are assessed for drug studies.[  ] When examining local tissue responses to medical devices, regional draining lymph nodes should always receive a macroscopic and microscopic evaluation. In assessing the systemic impact of medical devices, tissue selection should include all tissues contacting or located near the device (to explore local tissue responses) and also organs that filter materials transported away from the implant site (including the regional draining lymphoid tissues, any relevant MALT and serosa‐associated lymphoid structures, and spleen).[  ] If “wear” debris (small particles produced by physical interaction of moving surfaces), fragments (larger particles breaking off of failed devices), or micro‐ or nano‐particles extruded (often from enzymatic activity at the tissue:implant interface) from the device are of potential concern, then systemic evaluation needs to be extended to include multiple major organs, particularly those sensitive to embolic sequela (e.g., brain, heart, kidney, liver, and lung), and additional lymphoid tissues.[  ]

Documentation of Tissue Reactions to Devices
All pathology procedures should be fully documented for medical device studies, adhering to GLP principles as much as possible. These GLP principles are designed to guarantee the quality of data generated during nonclinical safety studies. The comprehensive documentation used for GLP studies provides accurate, reliable, reproducible, traceable, and transparent macroscopic and microscopic datasets. Methods to align data collection with GLP principles are well‐described in the literature,[  ] are familiar to pathologists generally and have been time‐tested during successful nonclinical development of numerous non‐device biomedical products.[  ] Importantly, these GLP principles and methods are also generally adaptable and applicable to medical device studies.[  ]

Appropriate Photographic Representation of Biological Responses
When documenting macroscopic (gross) findings, verbal descriptions and especially photographs of the device in situ are extremely valuable for the histologist (to ensure that appropriate tissues are sampled for microscopic evaluation) and pathologist (to ensure that tissue reactions are examined thoroughly) (Figure  3 ). Nomenclature conventions for macroscopic findings are not available in ISO 10993 series standards. However, toxicologic pathologists are experienced in providing suitable descriptions for macrosopic findings based on their diagnostic pathology training and expertise using INHAND glossaries for harmonized microscopic nomenclature.

When used in GLP studies for data generation relevant to device‐related pathologic findings, and thus for data interpretation, gross photographs are considered to be pathology raw data and need to be appropriately annotated (labeled), described (using detailed figure legends to explain key features of the tissue response), and archived.[  ] In many cases, gross examination of the implant site and associated tissue responses needs to be delayed until tissue trimming to ensure that preparation of specimens for microscopic evaluation does not produce potentially confounding artifacts (see Section 5.2 above); in such instances, additional photographic documentation at tissue trimming can aid the pathologist in defining potential regions of interest for microscopic evaluation, defining features to examine and possible scoring criteria to use in the microscopic evaluation, and to identify handling artifacts that should not be attributed to the implanted device. In general, the need for multiple stages of photographic documentation should be identified well in advance of necropsy, preferably during protocol development. For the macroscopic pathology evaluation (whether at necropsy on unfixed tissues or at tissue trimming for fixed tissues, or both), comments that provide context for specific diagnoses in various photographs and, in many cases, the photographs themselves (usually as well‐annotated “representative” depictions of key findings) should be incorporated as part of the pathology report. These macroscopic observations may need to be correlated with microscopic findings, particularly for studies conducted under GLP.
For microscopic evaluation, the collection, annotation, and reporting of photographic data from all tissues, even normal tissues, for some or all animals in a medical device study (i.e., “exhaustive photographic documentation”) is an expensive and time‐consuming process for sponsors and pathologists. Comprehensive photographic documentation typically adds dozens or hundreds of images that should be well‐annotated and must be archived as pathology raw data. This type of exhaustive documentation for a nonclinical study seldom, if ever, adds value (especially for minimally annotated images) since correct interpretation of biological responses in response to implanted devices requires a substantial degree of tissue‐specific anatomy and cytology knowledge that is rarely attainable without dedicated advanced study of pathology. Moreover, exhaustive documentation is not consistent with the spirit of the “least burdensome” provisions (defined as “the minimum amount of information necessary to adequately address a relevant regulatory question or issue through the most efficient manner at the right time”) as promulgated by the FDA.[  ] Instead, for GLP‐compliant studies, the “representative illustration” approach for depicting tissue responses where the most common findings are photographed and characterized by detailed annotations and descriptions is the currently accepted pathology best practice for documenting microscopic findings for other biomedical product classes (Figure  4 ).[  ] In this approach, appropriate descriptions of key findings are included in the report narrative and/or figure legends, and by design such explanatory text better ensures the accuracy and interpretability of pathology raw data compared to exhaustive photographic documentation with little or no labeling and limited interpretative discussion. Representative photographs prepared and annotated by the study pathologist are generally used for informational and communication purposes rather than as a substitute for microscopic evaluation when making primary morphological diagnoses of tissue sections. Importantly, unusual “non‐representative” findings that inform the interpretation may also be photographed and annotated at the pathologist's discretion to provide a more nuanced assessment of the tissue response. In general, this “representative illustration” approach should be adopted for medical device studies as it is designed to provide a data set of the highest value at the least cost in terms of labor and time. In contrast, exhaustive photographic documentation is a futile endeavor that should be avoided in data generation for nonclinical studies of medical devices and combination products.

Despite recent integration of digital whole slide imaging (WSI) into the GLP pathology workflow, digital images are not typically used (yet) for primary diagnoses of biological responses,[  ] although some companies are investigating their ability to replace evaluation of glass slides with WSI. A potential advantage of such digitized images is that they are amenable to automated or semi‐automated quantitative analyses such as cell counts, morphometry, or stereology (as discussed under Section 6.2 below). When incorporated into pathology reports, portions of WSI should be annotated appropriately in the same fashion as conventional photomicrographs of tissue sections.
Pathology data capture systems that are validated in compliance with CFR Part 11 (which covers appropriate use of electronic records[  ]) or non‐validated but well‐organized tabulation systems (e.g., Microsoft® Excel spreadsheets with pivot tables, Word tables) are generally used for pathology data capture during medical device studies. Non‐validated data recording does not adhere to GLP (and thus results in a GLP exception), but such data tables are audited for accuracy and thus are still suitable for interpretation. Similarly, other sources of imaging, such as standard radiography, computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), may not be compliant with CFR Part 11. Thus, imaging technology that cannot be fully validated and is necessary for data generation should be audited and described as an exception in the GLP Compliance Statement for the study.
In summary, toxicologic pathologists know by long experience in testing biomedical products that selective photographic documentation of representative tissue findings (with appropriate annotation) to supplement rather than supplant appropriately detailed pathology descriptions is both easier and more informative than the box‐checking enterprise of exhaustive photographic documentation with limited or no annotation that is the typical expectation for conventional medical device studies. Unfortunately, until this evidence‐based perspective used in drug testing is also adopted for evaluating medical devices, sponsors and regulatory agencies may continue to request a more burdensome approach of supplying exhaustive photographs as appendices to detailed medical device reports that already incorporate well‐chosen and effectively annotated representative images. Nonetheless, a detailed narrative description characterizing key tissue responses supplemented by a limited number of well‐annotated representative images of key tissue responses for a carefully selected subset of samples to depict those tissue responses is superior in terms of both value and resource utilization (compared to exhaustive photographic documentation) in understanding the principal features of the tissue response to a given medical device. The use of representative photographic documentation (gross and/or microscopic), undertaken on a case‐by‐case basis at the discretion of the study pathologist, is generally sufficient to demonstrate relevant pathology findings, provide macroscopic spatial context for the device and anatomic site, and/or document relative biological response scores for principal microscopic findings within a study.

Tissue Evaluations and Scoring a Biological Response
No one‐size‐fits‐all study design is available for microscopic evaluation of implanted medical devices and combination products. Instead, pathology analysis is based on detailed knowledge of local, regional, and systemic anatomy relevant to the implant location; species variations; and consideration of the biomaterial type, its intended activity, and the clinical indication for the device. Implant study design with respect to the pathology evaluation must be tailored to answer specific questions regarding safety (including biocompatibility) and sometimes functional performance.[  ]
Semi‐quantitative scoring to achieve the intended objectives as stated in ISO 10993–6:2016 (local) and ISO 10993‐11:2017 (systemic) evaluation needs to be flexible so that pathology endpoints may be targeted to the device and specific anatomic and physiological characteristics of the implant site(s). The biological evaluation and scoring recommendations provided in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E are general (inherently broad), so these example scoring systems are neither optimized nor validated for most tissues. Moreover, these general points are intended to be informative and not prescriptive. Therefore, strict implementation of such example scoring rubrics and calculations may result in inaccurate and/or uninterpretable pathology data leading to an inability to identify the existence and extent of biologically consequential tissue changes. In general, a conclusive determination of relative local tissue reactivity (test device compared to relevant control[s]) using only the simple mathematical calculations presented in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E does not work for some local implants (e.g., see intraocular implant example in Section 5 and Table 7), and indeed cannot effectively assess the complicated biological responses associated with many complex medical devices, combination products, or devices with surface or immunomodulator modifications. Quantitative evaluation of tissue responses may be helpful in some circumstances. In our experience, quantitative analysis for medical devices involves digital analysis of object numbers (e.g., cell counts, stereology) or dimensions (morphometry), but such methods can be applied reliably only with prior planning with respect to how to adequately and uniformly sample the specimen and mitigate for potential tissue collection and processing artifacts.[  ] While artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) applied to digital image analysis may eventually streamline the planning needed to ensure appropriate tissue sampling and quantitative tissue analysis, validation and infrastructure issues with respect to maintaining vast quantities of electronic data will need to be addressed prior to widespread implementation of AI‐based methods for routine analysis of device‐related tissue responses.
With respect to the emerging field of combination products where all elements of the regulated therapeutic candidate (drug/device, biologic/device, or drug/device/biologic) must be assessed for potential toxicity or other risks,[  ] we advise that the same rigorous terminology, biological criteria for diagnosing and interpreting tissue responses, and principles for assigning adversity and performing risk assessment should be used to evaluate device‐led, drug‐led, and biologic‐led combination products. In practice, current ISO 10993 standards for biological evaluation of medical devices are not consistent with expectations for drug‐based determinations of toxicity, let alone capable of efficiently and effectively addressing such expectations for combination products. Fortunately, the established guidelines and toxicologic pathology best practices for evaluating drugs are also readily adaptable to evaluating the medical device components of combination products, so defaulting to or adapting the drug‐based approach to any combination product is generally favored by toxicologic pathologists tasked with assessing tissue responses to implanted devices and combination products.
Furthermore, the scoring system examples in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E seldom, if ever, are appropriate templates for use in assessing systemic responses to implants under ISO 10993‐11:2017, especially in terms of evaluating combination products. Development of a relevant scoring rubric with inclusion of a full interpretive narrative is necessary to effectively communicate the toxicological (“biological”) impact of therapeutics (drugs or medical devices) on whole body systems. In the authors’ experience, scoring systems frequently need to be created de novo and refined to some degree by the pathologist over the entire development lifespan of a medical device. Numerous recent publications provide recommendations for tissue‐ or device‐specific evaluation approaches to devising such scoring schemes.[  ]

Avoid Routine Use of Masked (“Blinded”) Histopathological Evaluation
The benefits and pitfalls of masked (“blinded”) pathology evaluation have been discussed in several publications related to the practice of toxicologic pathology as applied to nonclinical studies. Although an initial masked analysis is sometimes useful to control observer bias in a research setting, selection of masked microscopic evaluation as a routine approach to assessing tissue responses is not aligned with current best practices for data generation in toxicologic pathology, and in fact can render a pathology data set uninterpretable.[  ] Therefore, the initial pathology evaluation generally should be an informed (“non‐blinded”) analysis rather than a masked assessment.[  ]
Masked pathology evaluation is warranted in some situations. For example, masked evaluation is suitable for testing specific hypotheses and discriminating whether potential pathology findings identified during an initial informed assessment are genuine. However, routine masking of the pathologist has limited benefit and, in fact is often detrimental when seeking to understand the true nature of a tissue response when assessing safety and potential human relevance. Masked evaluation adds time and cost to the biological response evaluation, for several reasons. First, discriminating the range of background/spontaneous changes from treatment‐related findings is time‐consuming at best and may be impossible. Second, masked evaluation will substantially delay the study timeline since data analysis and interpretation must wait until the data are unmasked. Finally, masked microscopic evaluation typically results in an abundance of findings, many of which are not related to the test device, as the pathologist tends to over‐diagnose in order to “not miss anything”;* excessive diagnoses increase the “noise” (incidental findings and artifacts) and obscure the “signal” of interest (genuine test device‐related tissue responses). This issue is especially common for early studies when the pathologist is first starting to work with a unique device, and may be problematic since it is not uncommon to have only one small pilot study prior to launching a GLP study to enable a regulatory submission to support clinical testing.
The optimal analytical approach for pathology analysis is an informed evaluation in which the toxicologic pathologist has awareness of all information, including the device identity, when performing their examination. Providing the pathologist with information regarding the study materials typically expedites tissue evaluation and report preparation without introducing unacceptable bias or compromising data generation and interpretation.[  ] An informed evaluation generally provides a more accurate and credible pathology data set as the improved signal‐to‐noise ratio better supports identification of potential treatment‐related changes. Importantly, many devices can only be evaluated in an “informed” fashion since masking is impossible (even with time‐intensive efforts) due to their unique identifying characteristics, which can be seen readily in tissue sections. The frequent use of a single test group in medical device studies obviates the need for masking. When a masked evaluation is performed, current best practice states that the only information to be withheld from the pathologist is information regarding the treatment (e.g., test device vs. positive control device vs. other control).[  ]

Pathology Peer Review is a Cost‐ and Time‐Effective Replacement for Exhaustive Photographic Documentation of Macroscopic and/or Microscopic Findings
As is the current practice for drugs, a superior and substantially less burdensome approach for documenting tissue responses in medical device studies would be to incorporate proper study design, adhere to GLP principles (where possible), select an experienced and qualified pathologist for the evaluation,[  ] and undertake a peer review of the pathology data.[  ] Pathology peer review procedures are an established feature of nonclinical drug development to ensure the quality of pathology data,[  ] which are widely recognized as adding substantial value to nonclinical studies by the FDA[  ] and other global regulatory authorities.[  ] Existing peer review procedures are already being adapted for and incorporated into medical device studies by toxicologic pathologists.
Pathology peer review of the preliminary diagnoses and interpretations of one pathologist (the “study pathologist”) by a second individual (the “peer review pathologist”) is designed to ensure the accuracy and consistency (credibility) of research data that are, by their essential nature, subject to some degree of subjectivity in their acquisition. Pathology peer reviews by independent third parties, rather than “in house” peer reviews, are preferred for GLP studies. At present, peer reviews need not be performed at GLP‐compliant test sites since their function is a quality check rather than the generation of pathology raw data.[  ] The GLP character of the data is maintained by ensuring that the original evaluation by the study pathologist is performed in a GLP‐compliant setting subject to a suitable quality assurance (QA) audit.
Pathology peer reviews should always be undertaken as an informed evaluation since their purpose is to check the quality of the initial assessment.[  ] If necessary, a pathology working group (PWG) composed of multiple experienced pathologists may be undertaken to adjudicate differences between the study pathologist and peer review pathologist and to reach a diagnostic consensus for any contentious findings.[  ] Such PWGs are employed most frequently for unusual tumors in carcinogenicity bioassays but may be assembled if warranted for other unusual tissue responses and thus could conceivably be used when warranted for medical device studies.
Pathology peer review typically requires examination of a selected subset of prepared tissue sections by the peer review pathologist, including all tissues from at least 10% of randomly selected test and control animals and all target organs or lesions from all test and control animals.[  ] White papers highlighting key “Points to Consider” concepts are also available for digital pathology peer review.[  ] Along with examination of the tissue sections and opportunity for the peer review pathologist to request all supporting data and documentation (for in vivo clinical observations, clinical pathology, necropsy, histology and histopathology phases of the study), peer review by an independent pathologist situated outside of a primary testing CRO ensures the quality of the pathology (gross and microscopic) data with respect to the terminology, morphological diagnoses, severity scoring, and biological response interpretation. Importantly, peer review under GLP provides confirmation of adequate protocol tissue availability for histopathology evaluation and interpretation[  ] and that tissues presented for peer review were actually collected from appropriate test animals.
Oversight of the pathology components of the medical device testing program by a GLP‐trained and experienced toxicologic pathologist, and peer review of the preliminary pathology diagnoses by a similarly qualified and independent toxicologic pathologist, is a far superior method to exhaustive photographic documentation to ensure the credibility of pathology data and that scientifically accurate interpretation has occurred. This concept is particularly important in light of the 20 February 2024 FDA Letter to Industry warning about fraudulent third‐party testing on medical devices.[  ]

Considerations for Determination of Complete Bioabsorption
Identification of medical device degradation or bioabsorption and its impact (or lack thereof) on tissues is not well‐described in standards and guidance documents. Nonetheless, staging the extent of degradation and determining the degree of bioabsorption is an oft‐requested endpoint by regulatory reviewers.[  ] A state‐of‐the‐art description of the immunological and tissue responses to different medical devices and the complete early bioabsorption through reparative processes is beyond the scope of this publication. We could not identify an extant comprehensive review that properly reflects the current understanding and variation in biological responses to different biomaterials that would help to define bioabsorption, which generally needs to be determined on a case‐by‐case basis. Therefore, inclusion of a preformulated scoring rubric to estimate bioabsorption and residual biomaterials in tissues would suffer from the same scientific fallacies and prospects for misapplication that occur with the suggested scoring rubrics in Annex E of ISO 10993–6:2016.
Complete bioabsorption may or may not occur depending on such factors as the implant material, type and activity of the device, and implant location. Trace amounts of sequestered (residual) biomaterial and immune cells in the tissue near implantation sites may occur in situations featuring physiologically complete bioabsorption and restoration of tissue integrity (Figure  5 ). Certain implants can leave end‐of‐process biomaterial trace deposits such as fragmented or mineralized debris sequestered within macrophages or multinucleated giant cells, encompassed by a fibrous capsule or matrix, or trapped within bone trabeculae; such residues may be accompanied by persistent accumulation of long‐lived immune sentinel cells, including lymphocytes, macrophages, and/or multinucleated giant cells. The pathologist may judge these trace deposits and cell aggregates as innocuous and consistent with completion of the bioabsorptive process at the implantation site. Residuals present in trace amounts (i.e., rare or barely perceptible) observed within a tissue that has reached a “steady state” of repair and regeneration (where “steady state” as applied to the tissue response indicates either restoration or at least no further progression) as well as residuals that are passive bystanders that do not interfere with tissue healing or restoration of structural integrity can be interpreted to be within the physiologically expected anatomic limits (“normal”) expected of a dynamic tissue and serve as evidence that the bioabsorptive process has been completed.

Limitations of Toxicologic Pathology in Medical Device Evaluation
Macroscopic and microscopic evaluation of tissue changes and differentiation between normal and abnormal tissue organization are qualitative or semi‐quantitative assessments where the resulting data rely heavily on the experience and informed professional judgment of the observer.[  ] A well‐designed study with clear objectives and well‐planned controls, appropriate collection and processing of tissues, selective photographic documentation, and where warranted pathology peer review provides the appropriate path to maximizing the scientific veracity of the biological response evaluation.
Macroscopic pathology evaluations cannot identify all changes associated with a medical device, particularly if the device is large or has been bioabsorbed. Therefore, closer macroscopic observation is often best performed not at necropsy but when fine trimming and additional photographic documentation are undertaken during histology specimen preparation. Biotribology forces (i.e., interactions influenced by friction and fluctuating lubrication) can lead to fracture, wear, loosening, and/or failure of medical devices that may be difficult to confirm by macroscopic or microscopic examination in animal studies. Explantation of the device to test for post‐implantation function or failure may disrupt surrounding tissue and destroy some, if not all, of the tissue:implant interface, which may complicate interpretation. Regardless, postmortem determination of device failure is an important evaluation that will be best served by extensive planning and collaboration between the development team and the study pathologist.[  ] Failure testing includes a review of all engineering, in vitro, and in vivo efficacy and safety data as well as imaging and pathology data to make the final determination regarding whether device function has been altered and/or partial or complete device failure has occurred. Despite this need for data integration across scientific disciplines, we are not aware of Design Failure Mode and Effect Analysis approaches that incorporate pathology analysis endpoints.
Similarly, the 2D anatomic representation of tissues available in flat tissue sections mounted on glass slides or digital images of such sections cannot identify functional changes in cells or organs. However, histopathologic (microscopic) evaluation usually allows inferences to be made on potential functional changes and their degree of adversity (harm[  ] caused to the individual bearing the implant). In‐life observations and clinical pathology findings may also provide evidence of functional changes that can sometimes be correlated with tissue changes. For certain implantation sites, periodic in vivo imaging and/or postmortem micro‐computed tomography (µ‐CT) prior to tissue trimming can also help correlate tissue responses to functional changes or device failure. These correlations and their interpretations should be described in the pathology report narrative and (where warranted) may be included in the pathology data tables. Correlations (or lack thereof) and identification of potential adversity based on in vivo and postmortem examinations are important components of the nonclinical risk assessment.

Why Biological Evaluation of Tissue Responses in Animal Studies Can Fail
Poor communication among study participants may lead to poor study designs, resulting in insufficient or inappropriate sampling for analytical analysis and histological evaluation, many times as a result of competing endpoints between bioengineering and biological evaluation objectives. The study protocol should be assessed by all participants, including the pathologist responsible for evaluating the biological responses, before finalization to confirm that the correct or important questions are being asked, that the entire plan is feasible, and to assess whether multiple studies will be needed to meet all program objectives. Any major variance from the regulatory guidance with respect to GLP compliance, study design features, and analytical approach should be discussed in advance with the regulatory agencies, if possible, and documented (along with the scientific justification for the variance) in protocol amendments and the final reports.

Concluding Perspectives
Pathology evaluations are a substantive, and perhaps the most critical component in biological assessments regarding the safety, efficacy, and risk of many Class II and Class III medical devices and combination products prior to clinical use. The advent of increasingly complex medical devices, longer contact time in patients, recognition of potential systemic impacts of medical devices, and increasing use of combination products incorporating a device component with a bioactive substance have increased the importance of clearly identifying the range of tissue reactions, and especially adverse findings, that may be incited by implanted medical devices.
Toxicologic pathologists who specialize in nonclinical evaluation of implanted medical devices play a critical role in supporting medical device development, especially for devices that require a nonclinical study to investigate implant biocompatibility and safety. Historical differences in the development of medical devices versus drugs as well as disparate training backgrounds among engineers, materials scientists, pathologists, toxicologists, and regulatory scientists have resulted in different perspectives regarding appropriate and necessary elements of nonclinical study designs for medical devices. Divergent perspectives regarding the desired study objectives, feasible and reliable endpoints for evaluating biological responses, acceptable diagnostic nomenclature, and data interpretation and reporting conventions for pathology endpoints may substantially complicate translation of animal data to assess potential patient risk. In this paper, we have emphasized that the toxicologic pathology literature increasingly provides resources that are important not only to medical device pathologists but also to other stakeholders (e.g., bioengineers, materials scientists, toxicologists, and regulatory reviewers) seeking experienced counsel regarding suitable approaches to evaluating biological responses to medical devices and combination products.
Engagement of a knowledgeable and experienced toxicologic pathologist should be a routine practice to align the tissue analysis in a study protocol with general principles found in guidance documents (e.g., by FDA and ISO) and published expert recommendations on biological response evaluation (mainly white papers prepared by working groups of the Society of Toxicologic Pathology [STP]) and European Society of Toxicologic Pathology [ESTP]). Indeed, such engagement is often a critical first step to determine optimal approaches to tissue collection, data generation and documentation (descriptive text and photographic depictions), and scoring systems relevant to the species and medical device (with respect to type, composition, and implantation site), thereby ensuring that data are accurate and interpretability is clear for a medical device study. In addition, the development of combination products requires understanding and implementation of many additional guidance documents and standards from multiple agencies relevant to drug development. Again, toxicologic pathologists are intimately acquainted with the contents of such drug‐related commentaries, and in particular the optimal means of integrating their precepts with those needed for biological evaluation of medical devices.
In conclusion, expert perspectives of experienced toxicologic pathologists should be incorporated automatically in each nonclinical study protocol that assesses a medical device or combination product. A protocol allowing appropriate flexibility to the biological response evaluation (in establishing features to be scored as well as relevant scoring criteria) will yield satisfactory results more consistently than will strict adherence only to the inflexible and generic example scoring rubrics enshrined in current guidance documents and ISO standards. Such flexibility will also offer additional benefits, including lower cost and more rapid evaluation because common pathology practices can be applied more quickly and reproducibly with substantially less labor compared to the multiple parameters in the ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E scoring examples.[  ] Moreover, our collective experience in assessing medical device safety has shown that utilizing simplistic example rubrics often significantly handicaps pathology data generation and interpretation, resulting in inappropriate adversity determinations and skewed or useless risk assessments. In principle, following well‐established industrial practices for effective pathology evaluation[  ] to capture and communicate scientifically accurate findings during nonclinical studies is as applicable to medical devices and combination products as it is to other biomedical products. Furthermore, extension of these established pathology principles to medical device studies would only require modest modifications to account for the variations in medical devices and differences in study objectives, application of data collection procedures specific to medical device categories and implantation sites, and awareness of potential unique terminology requirements. Such modest modifications would yield out‐sized benefits in terms of data reliability and reproducibility while also making interpretation, and thus regulatory decision‐making, easier. Importantly, toxicologic pathologists also understand the limitations of the conventional pathology evaluation process, including when and where scoring rubrics similar to the examples in ISO 10993–6:2016, Annex E may be used and when they should be spurned. Going forward, the authors strongly recommend that medical device studies increase the extent to which toxicologic pathology expertise is incorporated into study design and execution. Routine implementation of pathology “best practice” principles will provide a more rational decision‐making process for innovators and regulatory agencies developing medical materials and protecting human health, respectively.
