Investigation into the determinants of occupational safety for achieving zero accidents in multi-storey building construction projects: A Bayesian belief network approach☆
Using Bayesian Belief Network to analyse work accidents at high-rise projects.
Examined the human, organisational, work environment, and payment system aspects.
Found that simplifying safety design and improving the training are important for achieving zero accidents.
The Indonesian construction sector is vital for economic development but faces high accident rates because of inadequate occupational health and safety (OHS) measures. This study examines causal factors in high-rise construction accidents, focusing on human factors, organization, work environment, and payment systems. A Bayesian Belief Network (BBN) was used to evaluate OHS implementation and identify key elements for achieving zero accidents. Data were collected through a structured questionnaire completed by 28 experts assessing 277 workers across 33 critical safety variable relationships. Analysis using RStudio’s bnlearn revealed that regulatory requirements strongly influence safety design, corporate culture, and OHS management, while overly complex safety designs hinder supervision and training. Findings show that workers’ physical and mental conditions are vital for zero accidents, whereas safety awareness alone is insufficient. Improved training enhances payment systems, boosting motivation and productivity. The study recommends simplifying safety design and refining OHS procedures to reduce accident risks. Background
Indonesia is a developing country with a construction sector that plays an important role in development, but it has a high risk of occupational accidents because of a lack of attention to occupational health and safety (OHS). Occupational accidents can be caused by human, environmental, organisational and equipment factors, all of which affect worker productivity. Therefore, the analysis and implementation of OHS are essential to prevent accidents and achieve the vision of zero accidents. Construction companies must implement OHS in accordance with Law Nos. 1/1970 and Law No 13/2003 to protect workers from potential hazards. Human factors, site conditions, and the work environment are key to project productivity and success, with research pointing to human factors as the main cause of workplace accidents. Construction projects play an important role in development, contributing around 10 % of global GDP, and Indonesia’s construction sector GDP reached IDR 32,000 billion in 2023. However, high project realisation is also accompanied by a significant number of work accidents, of which 80–85 % are caused by human factors such as negligence, unsafe behaviour, stress and fatigue. BPJS Ketenagakerjaan recorded 860,709 work accident cases, with an increase in cases from 234,740 in 2022 (27.27 %) to 360,635 in 2024 (41.90 %). These data, together with the growth of the construction sector since 2022, indicate the need for further evaluation to reduce occupational accidents. Inadequate payment systems can demotivate work and increase the risk of accidents, suggesting that dissatisfaction with pay can reduce compliance with safety procedures. Other studies have demonstrated that low pay encourages excessive overtime, fatigue, decreased concentration, as well as high labour turnover due to poor pay systems. In addition, inappropriate safety incentives can encourage risky behaviour and disregard for safety. Effective OHS implementation not only creates a sense of security for workers but also reduces work accidents and improves project productivity. OHS research focuses on identifying the causes of accidents, especially in the context of project complexity and multiparty involvement, and on evaluating the effectiveness of control measures. In addition to human factors, unsafe working environments, non-ergonomic tool design, and lack of risk management contribute to work accidents in the construction sector. In this context, this research highlights human factors as the main cause of accidents to support more comprehensive risk management and improve safety and productivity in the construction sector.

Previous research on safety in building construction in Thailand using the Bayesian belief network (BBN) has focused only on organisational aspects. The findings showed that accident factors could be reduced by 73.3 % by improving safety behaviours influenced by management commitment, participation, learning and leadership. These behaviours increase to 74.6 % when there is assignment from leaders, management commitment, and active participation. Worker control by leaders, such as attendance monitoring, can increase safe behaviours by 77.7 %. Previous research in the UK using secondary data from hospitals analysed with BBN, found the main causes of construction accidents were workers' negligence of regulations, failure to comply with work procedures, operation of equipment without safety devices, poor project management, and low knowledge and skills of workers. In Indonesia, accidents in high-rise building construction projects were found that the main causes of accidents in the UINSA Surabaya Building were falling on heavy equipment and falling from a height, caused by lack of concentration and rushing. Another previous study, using interviews and focus group discussions, found that out of 100 accidents in construction projects, the causes involved workers or work teams (70 %), workplace problems (49 %), lack of equipment including PPE (56 %), material mismatches, and unergonomic equipment design, and non-ergonomic equipment design (27 %), human factors causing workplace accidents, including age (34 %), ability and experience (21 %), drug/alcohol use (68 %), gender (23 %), stress (82 %), fatigue (63 %), and work motivation (54 %). From this description, this study seeks to uncover the possibility of workplace accidents by considering organisational factors, human factors, work environment and payment system as new variables that become novelty in the research. This research uses the BBN to investigate a construction project. This research position is unique because there is still a lack of research that integrates organisational factors, human factors, work environment and payment systems acyclically using the Bayesian belief network. Research methodology
This study aims to identify the potential causes of workplace accidents involving workers in high-rise building construction projects. It addresses two key research questions: (RQ1) what are the causal relationships between the factors that contribute to achieving zero accidents in high-rise construction projects; and (RQ2) which of these factors have the most significant influence on the occurrence of work-related accidents. This research enhances the understanding of occupational safety by applying a BBN to model probabilistic relationships and address uncertainty across influencing factors. The BBN methodology employed in this study involves probabilistic inference, parameter learning (conditional probability), and network structuring.
The BBN structure was developed using a hybrid approach that combined expert knowledge with empirical evidence from previous studies in the field of occupational safety. This knowledge-informed foundation guided the identification of causal relationships, which were systematically arranged into a directed acyclic graph consisting of 33 causal relationships. The structure was then processed using the bnlearn package in RStudio, which facilitates parameter learning and probabilistic inference based on expert-assigned probability values. This hybrid modelling approach ensured that the resulting network was both theoretically sound and analytically reliable.

Data collection and analysis
This study investigates the factors contributing to workplace accidents in a 14-story building project at the Institut Teknologi Sepuluh Nopember (ITS), Surabaya. The project, carried out by PT Adhi Karya, a state-owned construction company, involved a total of 277 workers (151 non-subcontractors and 126 subcontractors). To evaluate potential accident causes, a structured questionnaire was developed based on validated frameworks from previous occupational safety research, consisting of 33 indicators representing cause-and-effect relationships in an acyclic structure. Each indicator was rated on a 0–100 scale to reflect the likelihood of contributing to an accident. A complete list of the 33 indicators and their descriptions is presented in Table A1 in Appendix A.
A total of 28 construction experts participated in the assessment. Their selection was based on specific criteria to ensure credibility and relevance, including: (1) a minimum of five years of professional experience in the construction sector; (2) direct involvement in high-rise building projects; (3) active roles in safety-related positions such as HSE staff, project managers, site supervisors, or safety consultants; (4) possession of nationally recognised HSE certifications; and (5) willingness to participate in the structured evaluation process. While formal statistical reliability measures such as Cronbach’s alpha were not the primary focus of this study, consistency across expert assessments was ensured through a well-defined evaluation framework, the use of standardised definitions for all variables, and alignment with findings from prior research. These combined measures helped to minimise subjectivity and reinforce the reliability of the expert-driven evaluations used in the BBN model.

The causal relationships in the BBN were validated through a triangulated approach involving expert judgement, relevant literature, and a structured evaluation using a predefined questionnaire comprising 33 safety-related variables with clear definitions. This process ensured consistency and reduced subjective bias in expert assessments. Conditional probabilities were derived from the same experts based on their professional experience and then processed using the bnlearn package in RStudio for parameter learning and internal consistency. While the model was developed from a specific high-rise construction case study, its structure reflects general principles of occupational safety, making it robust and adaptable to other construction contexts with appropriate data inputs.

Discussion
Bayesian Belief Network (BBN) analysis showed that Regulatory Requirements had a significant positive effect on Design for Safety, with a coefficient of 0.865. Safety regulations, including safety standards, evacuation access, and fire protection equipment, are important in project planning. The study of the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS supports this finding, although other factors, such as site conditions and project specifications, still play a significant role.
Regulatory Requirements had a significant positive effect on Corporate Culture with a coefficient of 0.813, demonstrating the importance of regulations in shaping safety culture. Regulations should encourage safety policies and practices that strengthen management and worker commitment. The study at the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS supports this finding, although other factors, such as management commitment and worker involvement, also play an important role.
Regulatory Requirements have a significant positive effect on Safety and Health Management, with a coefficient of 0.896, demonstrating the important role that regulations play in improving the effectiveness of safety policies, procedures, and training. The study at PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in ITS supports this finding, although management commitment and thorough evaluation also influence the effectiveness of Safety and Health Management.
Design for safety has a significant positive effect on Preplanning with a coefficient of 1.339. Good design ensures that Preplanning considers safety, thereby reducing the operational risk of the project. The study at the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS supports this finding, showing that a mature safety design supports more structured and safe planning.
Corporate Culture has a weak positive influence on Preplanning, with a coefficient of 0.163, indicating an insignificant influence. Corporate culture plays a more abstract role in shaping long-term vision and strategy, whereas Preplanning is more influenced by project technical and operational factors. The study at the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS supports this finding, where corporate culture focuses on strategic vision, while Preplanning is mainly determined by the technical and operational aspects of construction.
Safety and Health Management has a weak positive influence on Preplanning (coefficient 0.132), suggesting that its contribution is not dominant. Safety and Health Management is more relevant at the implementation and supervision stages of a project, whereas preplanning is influenced by technical and operational design. The study at the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS confirmed that while the OHS helps identify risks and provides safety guidance, its main role remains as a supporting factor in the initial planning.
Design for safety has a significant negative effect on Management and supervision (coefficient −1.205), where complex designs shift the focus of supervision from safety oversight to technical issues, reducing the effectiveness of supervision. This finding is relevant to the practice at the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS, where complex design for safety increases the supervision burden. Efficient and practical designs are important for optimal safety supervision and implementation.
Corporate Culture has a significant positive effect on Management and supervision (coefficient 1.268), suggesting that a safety-focused culture improves supervision effectiveness. The study at PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in ITS supports this finding, where Corporate Culture strengthens supervision and creates a collaborative work environment, contributing to successful supervision management and improved safety.
Safety and Health Management has a weak positive influence on Management and supervision (coefficient 0.112), suggesting that its contribution is not dominant. Supervision is more influenced by skills, experience, and communication than safety policies. The study at the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS confirmed that while OHS guidelines are important, supervision depends more on practical skills and communication.

In a study at the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS, strong Safety and Health Management implementation significantly improved the quality of training, ensuring the relevance of training to the safety and operational needs of the project.

In the study of the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS, thorough preplanning significantly contributed to the provision of equipment that met safety standards, enhancing project success.

In the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS, training is tailored to the needs of the project, improving workers’ competence, productivity, and safety in the workplace.

As a novelty finding, the study of the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in ITS, supervision focuses on monitoring workers’ performance and coordination with project administration, but its influence on the payment system remains limited.

The results of the BBN analysis show that the physical and mental condition of workers at the PT Adhi Karya high-rise building in the ITS has a weak positive influence on the achievement of zero accident, with a coefficient of 0.256. This finding suggests that good physical and mental conditions can help reduce the risk of accidents, although the effect is not strong due to mental factors such as fatigue and task pressure. Therefore, in addition to maintaining the physical condition of workers, PT Adhi Karya needs to pay attention to workload management and psychological support to support the achievement of zero accidents.
The results of the BBN analysis show that safety awareness has a weak negative influence on the achievement of zero accidents, with a coefficient of −0.101. This indicates that awareness without real action only contributes to accident risk reduction. The challenges faced at PT Adhi Karya’s high-rise buildings in ITS include awareness that has not been followed by consistent implementation, especially in involving third parties. Therefore, the company must strengthen its supervision and ensure that all workers, including subcontractors, apply appropriate safety practices.
The results of the BBN analysis show that environmental conditions have a weak positive influence on achieving zero accidents, with a coefficient of 0.312, which means that a good working environment can reduce the risk of accidents, although the impact is not significant. At PT Adhi Karya’s high-rise building in the ITS, the company has complied with regulations related to working environment conditions, but challenges such as inadequate lighting during night work and noise can still affect the effectiveness of these efforts. Therefore, improved lighting and noise mitigation must be considered to achieve zero accidents.
The BBN analysis results show that worker skills have a weak positive influence on achieving zero accident, with a coefficient of 0.031, indicating that while worker skills can help prevent accidents, they are not the main factor. In PT Adhi Karya’s high-rise building at the ITS, the company has focused on improving skills through training, but external factors, such as suboptimal working environment conditions, also affect work safety. Therefore, in addition to improving workers' skills, attention should be paid to other factors, such as work environment conditions and the fulfilment of safety standards, to achieve zero accidents.
The BBN analysis results show that the payment system has a weak positive influence on achieving zero accident with a coefficient of 0.22, indicating that although the effect is small, a good payment system can support work safety. As a novelty finding, in PT Adhi Karya’s high-rise building at the ITS, a fair and transparent payment system increases workers’ motivation and compliance with safety procedures, which reduces the risk of accidents. However, an inadequate payment system can demoralise workers and affect work safety; therefore, companies must maintain fair and transparent payment systems.
The BBN analysis results show that Operational equipment has a weak positive influence on achieving zero accident, with a coefficient of 0.181. Although the contribution is small, safe and standardised Operational equipment is still important. In PT Adhi Karya’s high-rise building at the ITS, the implementation of a good OHS system ensures that equipment meets safety standards, which can reduce the risk of accidents and improve work efficiency. However, it is important to continuously monitor the condition of equipment and provide adequate training for its safe use to support the achievement of zero accidents.
The BBN analysis results show that safety equipment and personal protective equipment have a weak positive influence on achieving zero accidents, with a coefficient of 0.266, although the contribution is small. The effectiveness of PPE is highly dependent on worker compliance, suitability to the type of hazard, and support from training and supervision. At PT Adhi Karya’s high-rise building in the ITS, although PPE is adequate, challenges remain regarding worker compliance, so the company needs to increase supervision and awareness to ensure consistent use of PPE.
These findings demonstrate the complexity of interactions among organisational, technical and human factors in achieving zero accidents in high-rise construction projects. The BBN analysis revealed that “design for safety” has a significant negative influence on both “management and supervision” (coefficient = −1.205) and “training” (coefficient = −2.662). This indicates that certain safety designs, despite being well-intentioned, may introduce complexity that burdens implementation efforts. In this context, the term “overly complex safety design” refers to technical design elements that demand increased supervisory attention or require intensive training to be understood and applied correctly on site. This interpretation was based on expert judgement collected through a structured evaluation and contextualised by professional experience in high-rise construction projects. Although the study did not apply a formal complexity index, the significant negative coefficients in the BBN model serve as empirical indicators that such complexity can reduce the effectiveness of both supervision and training. These findings highlight the importance of aligning safety design with field-level operability, ensuring that design decisions support rather than hinder practical safety enforcement.
In response to these findings, several practical strategies are suggested to support the adoption of simplified and more field-adapted safety designs in construction projects. These strategies focus on improving usability, reducing procedural complexity, and enhancing implementation at the site level. One approach involves using visual-based safety instructions and standardised signage in place of complex, text-heavy manuals to facilitate faster comprehension. Another strategy is to embed safety mechanisms directly into tools, equipment, or workflows to reduce reliance on additional procedural steps. In addition, involving frontline workers in feedback loops during the design phase helps to identify operational inefficiencies and technical burdens that may hinder real-world application. These approaches are feasible even for firms with limited resources or inconsistent regulatory compliance. Rather than relying on expensive technologies or complete system redesigns, they emphasise behavioural integration and process alignment. Practical steps such as implementing lean documentation, conducting focused low-cost training sessions, and aligning safety procedures with existing national standards can promote compliance while maintaining operational simplicity. Overall, these strategies support the broader objective of embedding safety in everyday construction practices in ways that are intuitive, accessible, and sustainable across diverse project types.

In addition to its direct effects on safety awareness, worker skills, and environmental conditions, training also acts as a key intermediary in the BBN model, linking other critical factors that influence safety outcomes. The variables “worker’s mental and physical condition” and “payment system” are not directly connected through a causal arc. However, both are indirectly influenced by the “training” node. As shown in Table 4, training has a significant positive influence on “worker’s mental and physical condition” (coefficient = 0.868, No 17) and on “payment system” (coefficient = 0.835, No 26). This indicates that effective training interventions serve as mediating factors that simultaneously improve worker well-being and supports compliance with performance-based incentive systems. Based on expert evaluation, training methods such as practical scenario-based instruction, site-specific refresher sessions, and interactive demonstrations were identified as most effective. These approaches help reduce psychological fatigue, enhance physical readiness and align worker behaviour with safety performance expectations and reward mechanisms. The central role of training, as supported by the model results, reinforces its importance as a strategic entry point for improving multiple aspects of safety performance. These findings reaffirm the role of training as a strategic leverage point in promoting a safer, more productive, and reward-aligned construction environment.

Conclusions
This study addressed RQ1 by identifying 33 causal relationships among safety-related variables that influence the achievement of zero accident outcomes in high-rise building construction. Among these, 23 were positive relationships (13 significant and 10 weak), indicating that PT Adhi Karya has implemented effective occupational safety measures in several areas. The remaining 10 negative relationships (8 weak and 2 significant) reveal aspects that require further improvement to optimise safety performance. Overall, these findings reflect the company's strong commitment to implementing procedures that support the realisation of zero accidents on site.
As a response to RQ2, the study found two significant negative relationships, specifically between safety design and both supervision and training. This implies that overly complex safety designs may hinder the achievement of zero accidents by increasing supervision burdens and complicating safety training. The results underscore the need for simpler and more practical safety designs that facilitate effective implementation and enhance worker understanding in the field.
The study used a BBN to model these relationships, combining expert knowledge with probabilistic inference. The findings highlight the importance of regulatory compliance, supervision, training, worker conditions, and design effectiveness in creating a proactive safety system that supports zero accident goals. These insights provide a strong foundation for safety planning and accident prevention in similar construction contexts.
Although the findings were derived from a specific case study of a high-rise construction project in Surabaya, the model’s causal structure is based on widely recognised safety principles. The BBN framework can be adapted to other construction types, such as infrastructure and residential projects, and to different geographic regions in Indonesia. Nevertheless, recalibration of conditional probabilities is recommended using local expert input and project-specific data to ensure contextual accuracy. Future studies may expand this model across multiple project types to enhance its generalisability and practical applicability in promoting zero accident outcomes. (5)
