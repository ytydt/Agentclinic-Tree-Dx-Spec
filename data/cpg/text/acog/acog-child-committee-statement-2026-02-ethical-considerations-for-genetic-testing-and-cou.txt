Number 24 (Replaces Committee Opinion No. 410, June 2008; Committee Opinion No 693, April 2017; and Committee Opinion 805, April 2020) 
This Committee Statement was developed by the American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists' Committee on Ethics in collaboration with committee member Mara Black, MD. 
ABSTRACT: Given the increasing availability and complexity of genetic testing, it is imperative that practicing obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals maintain a firm comprehension of the benefits, limitations, and risks of genetic testing offered in their practices. The use of genetic testing has the potential to improve the care of patients and their families; however, the nuances and possible implications of test results can be challenging to interpret and effectively communicate, highlighting the importance of appropriate pretest and posttest counseling as well as expert consultation, when applicable. The challenges for practicing obstetrician–gynecologists often are compounded by severe limitations in time, limited expertise with new testing or rare results, and potentially limited scientific literacy among patients. This document seeks to explore the ethical considerations obstetricians and gynecologists should consider when offering genetic testing in their practices. 
Summary of Recommendations and Conclusions 
Obstetrician–gynecologists have a responsibility to establish and regularly update clinical practice standards regarding genetic screening, diagnostics, and referral to ensure current and evidence-based practice. 
When subspecialty knowledge or expertise is required to optimize counseling or interpret and manage results, referral to or collaboration with a professional with expertise in medical genetics or other appropriate specialties should be considered. 
Counseling and the consent process should use clear, accessible language and a shared decision-making approach centering the patient's perspective, values, and goals. 
The results of genetic testing should always be made available to a patient unless the patient explicitly opted out of receiving certain findings before testing. Referral must be offered if a physician is unwilling or unable to provide appropriate counseling on the interpretation of results and all management options. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists should understand the tests their practices offer patients and the options for additional testing and follow up and should be able to explain the major findings of the tests. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists should be aware of the potential effect of genetic information on personal privacy, familial relations, insurance coverage, and employment discrimination. 
Access to clinically indicated genetic testing, as well as access to appropriate care to act on the results of genetic testing, should be offered as an essential part of exercising reproductive autonomy. 
Efforts to resolve bias and unequal representation within reference databases are of the utmost importance to ensure equity in genetic testing for diverse populations. 
Background 
It has become standard of care to offer genetic testing in several areas of obstetric and gynecologic practice, most notably in reproductive and oncology care. The scientific basis, clinical indications, and utility of genetic testing in obstetrics and gynecology are explored in more detail in other American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists (ACOG) guidance, listed at the end of this document under Additional Resources. Readers are encouraged to refer to these documents for more information. 
For the purposes of this document, genetic testing refers to any testing used to diagnose or screen for a genetic condition. This document primarily focuses on the ethical implications of testing used to obtain molecular or cytogenetic data. However, it should be noted that there are similar ethical considerations in other methods used to screen, diagnose, and assess risk and prognosis for genetic conditions. These other methods may include imaging, physical examination, family history, and collection of biological specimens. 
Although the use of genetic testing has the potential to improve the care and health of patients and their families, genetics is a complex and rapidly evolving field. The nuances and possible implications of test results can be challenging, even to experts in the field. The challenges for practicing obstetrician–gynecologists often are compounded by severe limitations in time, limited expertise with new testing or rare results, and potentially limited scientific literacy among patients. This document seeks to explore the ethical considerations obstetricians and gynecologists should consider when offering genetic testing in their practices. 
Clinical Utility in Obstetrics and Gynecology 
Recent decades have been marked by scientific and technologic advancements that have rapidly expanded access to genetic testing in obstetrics and gynecology. Genetic tests are now widely used to screen for, diagnose, prevent, and treat conditions with a genetic etiology to the benefit of individuals and families. Genetic testing can improve targeted screening, prevention, and treatment of certain cancers 1 . Testing in the reproductive space can help patients make decisions in line with their family-building goals, provide answers in the setting of pregnancy loss or fetal anomaly, and prepare for the needs of offspring with genetic conditions 2 . The specific tests recommended for practice are covered in more detail in the ACOG documents listed in Additional Resources. Obstetrician–gynecologists should be aware that genetics within obstetrics and gynecology, and within medicine, is a rapidly evolving field and that recommendations likewise are expected to evolve in the coming years. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists have a responsibility to establish and regularly update clinical practice standards regarding genetic screening, diagnostics, and referral to ensure current and evidence-based practice . 
Patient decisions regarding genetic testing are complex and are best facilitated by clinicians with applicable expertise, time, and resources for counseling. Although health care professionals ordering testing should be familiar with initial management of primary findings, greater subspecialty knowledge may be required to fully manage all results as the complexity and scope of genetic testing available to patients increases. For example, an obstetrician who orders expanded carrier screening should be able to counsel a patient regarding the inheritance of autosomal recessive conditions and be able to facilitate appropriate additional testing such as carrier screening for the reproductive partner or diagnostic testing in a pregnancy. However, such testing also may lead to results with implications for maternal health outside of pregnancy, the management of which may be beyond the obstetrician's scope of practice 3 4 . In these cases, referring to another health care professional may allow the patient to receive the optimal care for their condition. 
When subspecialty knowledge or expertise is required to optimize counseling or interpret and manage results, referral to or collaboration with a professional with expertise in medical genetics or other appropriate specialties should be considered. When patients are referred for counseling, continued engagement of the primary health care professional may facilitate holistic shared decision making. 
Secondary and Incidental Findings 
Many commonly used genetic tests have the potential to reveal information that is beyond the primary intended findings of the test. In some cases, these are intentionally collected secondary findings. For example, in whole exome and whole genome sequencing, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics releases a list of genes, updated regularly, that are recommended for opportunistic screening, because identification and management of these conditions could reduce morbidity or mortality associated with the condition 5 . 
In reproductive care, genetic testing may be recommended to assess risk for a genetic condition in an expected or current pregnancy. However, results may have long-term implications for the health of parents and other family members that may require additional evaluation and management 3 4 6 . In rare cases, testing intended to assess for fetal aneuploidy can even detect maternal cancers 7 . 
Some genetic testing modalities have the potential to reveal an unexpected presence or absence of biological relatedness. Findings can include nonpaternity, nonmaternity, or consanguinity between reproductive partners. When applicable to the testing being pursued, the patient should have the opportunity to discuss these risks and potential implications in their case without a partner present. These findings can have broader implications for the patient and family beyond the medical implications for diagnosis and risk assessment. Personal, familial, and cultural attitudes toward biological relatedness may affect how these results are interpreted by the patient and their family. Biological relatedness to others may be foundational to how a patient understands themselves and their relationships and, in some cases, may be tied to physical safety and financial stability. Unexpected findings regarding biological relatedness can lead to worsening of familial relationships and adverse mental health outcomes 8 . Although there is currently heterogeneity in reporting practices for these findings by clinical laboratories, it is important to disclose the possibility of these results during pretest counseling 9 . 
The potential for secondary or incidental findings and the nature of these findings (eg, disease-causing variants, fetal sex, suspicion for cancer, or unexpected familial relationships) should be explicitly disclosed to the patient, who should have the option to opt in or opt out of these findings where possible. Patients also should be supported in opting for different or no testing if the possibility of secondary or incidental findings is unacceptable to them. 
Uncertainty in Genetic Testing 
Although the field of genetics has made extraordinary gains in recent decades, there remains much that is unknown. Anticipatory guidance is essential to contextualize results. Pretest counseling should include the possibility of finding a pathogenic variant (“positive” result), finding no pathogenic variant (“negative” result), or finding a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). However, a negative result never fully excludes the possibility of a genetic condition, and a positive result may not lead to clinical certainty. Even when a specific genotype is known, clinical prognostication may be difficult due to variable expressivity, incomplete penetrance, or mosaicism. In some cases, the clinical spectrum of a genotype can be vast. Additionally, rather than diagnosing a specific condition, genetic testing may reveal a predisposition to developing a condition that also may be affected by a variety of other patient and environmental factors. A patient's course is not determined by genotype alone. The context of a test is vital to its interpretation 10 . 
Although not all genetic testing used in obstetrics and gynecology reports VUS, there are many tests that do, and this possibility, when applicable, should be shared with patients. Variants of uncertain significance are variants for which current evidence is insufficient to classify the variant as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, likely benign, or benign. A VUS can make answering specific clinical questions difficult and may leave obstetrician–gynecologists, patients, and families unsure about how to proceed with health care screenings, management, or reproductive decision making. Patients and their obstetrician–gynecologists should also be aware that, as more information becomes available, the results of their genetic testing may be classified and interpreted differently in the future. In some cases, testing can yield additional diagnoses, risk considerations, and reassurances years after testing is completed. When applicable, the process for additional testing and reanalysis should be made available to the patient 11 . 
Uncertain results can make clinical decision making difficult, cause significant anxiety, and, in some cases, affect relationships, the parenting experience, and the use of health care resources 12 . For some patients, the risk of an uncertain result may be a reason to decline testing or to choose more limited testing. 
Counseling and Informed Consent 
Comprehensive counseling is key to informed consent for genetic testing. Informed consent for genetic testing requires educating patients on the purpose of the proposed test, including the condition or conditions being tested for and whether the test is a screening or diagnostic test. Simple language is important for setting pretest expectations regarding the nature and timing of results 13 . 
Counseling and the consent process should use clear, accessible language and a shared decision-making approach centering the patient's perspective, values, and goals . To avoid creating undue pressures in favor of testing, genetic testing should be presented within the context of potential findings, further work-up, and relevant interventions 14 . The potential for genetic tests to present greater burdens than benefits for some individuals should be acknowledged. Teach-back methods are recommended to ensure comprehension and support shared decision making 15 . 
Pretest counseling should include the expected timing of results and how these results will be communicated to the patient. These practical considerations may meaningfully affect the clinical utility and actionability of results (for example, in the prenatal setting, where gestational age at diagnosis may affect potential options) and should be included in discussions regarding the risks and benefits of testing. Although the cost of genetic testing has declined significantly in the past decade and is covered by some insurance providers for specific indications, cost varies by test type and laboratory. It remains a reality that cost may be a meaningful consideration for a patient deciding on pursuing testing. It is important for the patient to have the opportunity to discuss expected costs and potential pathways for coverage or reduced payment with the medical institution, laboratory, or insurance company, as appropriate. 
The results of genetic testing should always be made available to a patient unless the patient explicitly opted out of receiving certain findings before testing. Referral must be offered if a physician is unwilling or unable to provide appropriate counseling on the interpretation of results and all management options. Concerns about the potential for results to be misunderstood or cause distress do not justify denying patients access to their own clinical information. 
The Role of Third Parties and Direct-To-Consumer Testing 
Third parties, such as commercial laboratories or other laboratories not associated with the institution or practice at which a patient is receiving care, are widely used for genetic testing. Some genetic tests used routinely by obstetrician–gynecologists, such as expanded carrier screening panels, are developed by commercial laboratories 16 . For some types of genetic testing, third parties allow patients to access tests that otherwise would not be available at their local institution or would be available only at a cost or speed that would be prohibitive. In many practices limited in health care professionals with the expertise or time to provide appropriate counseling, third parties often provide educational materials for patients and health care professionals, genetic counselors who can discuss results with patients, and genetic professionals who can discuss rare or complex results with the ordering clinician. Although third parties have the potential to expand access to genetic testing and the necessary associated supports for patients and health care professionals, it is important to recognize the ethical concerns that can arise through their use and the obligations of the health care professional ordering tests through them. 
To fulfill their obligations to a patient, an obstetrician–gynecologist should understand the test they are ordering, including its potential results and the limitations of the test. Commercially available panels may include genes beyond those recommended by professional societies; in choosing and counseling about a test, obstetrician–gynecologists should be aware of these potential discrepancies and ramifications 17 . Obstetrician–gynecologists should be aware of educational resources available to their patients through third parties and be able to answer questions regarding key points relevant to a test being ordered. It is important to recognize that the incentives for third parties may be incongruent with the best interest of the patient, because third parties stand to benefit financially from the ordered test as well as from the data collected through testing. It is important to recognize that these incentives may be present in interactions with third parties. Obstetrician–gynecologists should strive to engage in shared decision making with the patient, accounting for the medical indications and patient preferences without undue pressure toward testing. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists should understand the tests their practices offer patients and the options for additional testing and follow up and should be able to explain the major findings of the tests. 
Additionally, conflicts of interest may occur when obstetrician–gynecologists have a research interest or financial stake in an entity involved in the testing, screening, or interpretation of genetic material. Relationships with industry that may pose conflicts of interest regarding recommendations for genetic products and services should be disclosed 18 . For more on interactions with industry, see ACOG Committee Statement No. 2, 
Professional Relationships with Industry (Obstet Gynecol 2022;140:904–11). 
Patients may increasingly seek genetic testing that is outside of current professional society recommendations. For example, some patients may be frustrated if their family history is significant for breast cancer but does not meet the current clinical thresholds for recommending genetic testing. In such instances, counseling should address whether testing is not clinically indicated because potential risks outweigh potential benefits as opposed to not clinically recommended due to insufficient evidence or lack of insurance coverage within current practice standards. In these circumstances, patients may consider obtaining direct-to-consumer (DTC) testing with appropriate counseling 19 . Physicians should be familiar with practice recommendations regarding popular DTC testing relevant to gynecologic or obstetric care (eg, 
BRCA , 
MTFHR ). Clinicians should discuss the importance of obtaining confirmatory testing from a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–approved laboratory before proceeding with major interventions (eg, risk-reducing surgery for 
BRCA ), because many DTC testing services are not clinically validated 20 . For more information on DTC testing, see Committee Opinion No. 816, 
Consumer Testing for Disease Risk (Obstet Gynecol 2021;137:e1–6). 
Privacy and Legal Considerations 
There have been concerns that genetic information obtained from clinically indicated tests could be used to discriminate against patients. To address this potential barrier to patients pursuing beneficial genetic testing, federal and state legislatures have enacted laws, such as the 2008 Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act 21 , prohibiting discrimination based on genetic information for health insurance (ie, eligibility, benefits, or premiums) and for employment purposes 21 22 . Notably, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act does not apply to small employers with fewer than 15 employees or to the provision of life, disability, or long-term care insurance. In addition, the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA) provides protection for patients with pre-existing conditions, including genetic conditions. Under the ACA, insurance companies cannot use pre-existing conditions to establish eligibility and premiums for health insurance. Additionally, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) includes genetic information in the privacy protections given to health information. 
The possibility of personal or financial risks related to genetic testing will vary considerably among specific genetic tests and clinical contexts. When appropriate, clinicians may discuss delaying nonurgent genetic testing to minimize risks related to patients’ current insurance and employment status. For more complex insurance, employment, or legal concerns, formal genetics or legal consultations may be indicated. 
Patients should be informed about the potential for secondary research using their genetic data, whether testing is obtained in academic research settings or through commercial providers. Broad consent terms may allow genetic data to be used for research or commercial development without the obligation to disclose specifics of such activities or return individual results. Although protecting the privacy of research participants is a cornerstone of modern research ethics, de-identification or anonymization of genetic data is inherently limited. The ability to identify some individuals within a genomic dataset already has been demonstrated and highlights the unique vulnerabilities of genetic and genomic data 23 . 
Obstetrician–gynecologists should be aware of the potential effect of genetic information on personal privacy, familial relations, insurance coverage, and employment discrimination . It is important to recognize that the legal and personal implications of genetic test results may change over time, because neither science nor culture nor the law are static entities. Health care professionals counseling patients regarding genetic testing today cannot predict how future legislation, or interpretation of current law, may affect how a patient's genetic information could be used. These uncertainties also apply to offspring and other relatives of the patient who could be affected by shared diagnoses or risk factors. Despite these uncertainties, it is important to be familiar with the legal protections currently in place for genetic information, as well as their limitations. 
Effect on Nonconsenting Related Individuals 
Identification of disease or disease predisposition in an individual may expose the existence of disease or disease predisposition among their relatives 24 25 . In addition to counseling each patient about their own personal risk, obstetrician–gynecologists should counsel patients regarding the implications of genetic testing for family members, including potential future offspring. If genetic testing reveals clinically significant mutations with heritable potential, patients should be strongly encouraged to share the results with affected or at-risk family members. Some patients may indicate that they do not plan to disclose the fact that they underwent testing, nor the results of such testing, with relevant family members. This may result in ethical tension between the patient's well-established right to privacy and the physician's duty to inform parties. Nevertheless, it is not appropriate for physicians to notify at-risk family members without their patient's consent. The patient should decide when to consider disclosure of test results to family members. If there is any uncertainty regarding applicable legal disclosure parameters, physicians should consult with a qualified legal professional. Specific legal parameters governing disclosure of private health information are set out in the Privacy Rule implemented after passage of HIPAA, which has been interpreted to offer that physicians have no broad legal duty to warn patients' relatives of potential genetic risk. However, physicians in this circumstance should explore their patient's concerns regarding the relevant disclosure and offer to support them in discussing the results of genetic testing with family members 26 . 
Reproductive Autonomy 
At a practice level, there remains significant debate regarding what genetic information should be available to patients and their families in the prenatal and preconception period. This debate must be placed in the larger context of the significant legal barriers that have been put in place in many states to restrict the reproductive care available to patients. 
It is possible to obtain genetic information about potential offspring in the preconception or prenatal period that directly affects the care a patient might receive while pregnant or the care of a resulting child in the newborn or pediatric period. Management plans guided by results of genetic testing may include selection of embryos, abortion, adoption planning, preparation for the care of a child with a medical condition, and even in utero therapy. The information that would be considered useful in the preconception or prenatal period is not the same for every family and may depend on their values, risk tolerance, life circumstances, and willingness or ability to access possible interventions. In exercising reproductive autonomy, a person may make decisions in line with their values and life circumstances that include obtaining extensive, minimal, or no genetic information about potential offspring. What variants might be considered actionable or useful varies over time as our understanding of the human genome expands and surveillance or treatments for some conditions become available. There is broader interest in obtaining information regarding the risk of conditions that are life-limiting, that significantly affect quality of life, or for which outcomes can be altered by early intervention or preparation. Lesser support has been noted for obtaining genetic information regarding conditions with minimal effect on quality of life, late-onset disease, or no medical implications. For patients deciding on expanded genetic testing, especially those pursuing testing beyond the current standard of care, it is reasonable to discuss the potential utility to the patient of the test or tests in question, as well as the value of maintaining privacy, autonomy, and an open future for potential offspring if the information that would be obtained would not be imminently useful in the preconception, prenatal, or pediatric period 27 . 
Access to clinically indicated genetic testing, as well as access to appropriate care to act on the results of genetic testing, should be offered as an essential part of exercising reproductive autonomy . The ethical imperative to provide accurate and comprehensive information to patients regarding their genetic test results and their options for management is additionally recognized by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics 28 . It remains a reality, however, that the execution of ethical and professional obligations may be limited by the laws, institutional policies, or resources of a place of practice. 
Equity in Access to Genetic Testing 
Although individual obstetrician–gynecologists may be limited in resolving disparities imposed by insurance structures, regional resources, and local politics, using standard protocols for presenting genetic test offerings is important in alleviating disparities in access at the practice level 29 30 . Physician or staff biases regarding a certain demographic group, either toward or against intervention, may inadvertently influence patients. Therefore, it is important to recognize these biases and work to counsel patients in a neutral and nonjudgmental manner. Practices should develop protocols for offering genetic testing, and testing should be offered based on clinical indication, not limited by assumptions regarding patient interest in testing, results of testing, or patients’ intended utilization of test results. Obstetrician–gynecologists should be aware of the ways in which protocols for genetic testing may disproportionately lay the benefits and burdens of genetic testing with certain demographic groups and should strive to implement protocols that provide the greatest clinical utility without unduly disenfranchising a specific group. Within established protocols, risk and preference assessments should inform individualized shared decision making. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists should be aware of the potential for differential access to genetic testing, counseling, and follow-up care to exacerbate health disparities. Access to the benefits of the most up-to-date genetic testing has been afforded primarily to those who have insurance coverage or can afford to pay out of pocket. Disparities in access to genetic testing and information are particularly acute in the setting of in vitro fertilization, in which the availability of assisted reproductive technologies, including preimplantation genetic testing, is largely stratified across socioeconomic lines 31 . 
Considering the potential harm of differential access to genetic testing, ACOG has issued a position statement challenging insurance policies and preauthorization requirements that may restrict obstetrician–gynecologists with relevant expertise from providing genetic counseling or ordering genetic testing. Such policies may inappropriately impede access to necessary, and increasingly routine, health care 32 . Providing and supporting access to genetic counselors, clinical geneticists, and obstetricians and gynecologists with specialized additional training in genetics benefits many patients, especially those with more complex genetic concerns. However, mandatory referral for care that is otherwise within the scope of the training and qualifications of the primary health care professional may disadvantage patients who could benefit from counseling and care related to genetic testing in the setting of an established, trusting patient–physician relationship. To avoid barriers to care, referring physicians are encouraged to use remote consultation when appropriate and to develop sustainable collaboration with subspecialists to improve access to expanding offerings in an evolving discipline. 
Disparities in Interpretation and Utilization of Genetic Testing 
Access to genetic testing is an important issue; so too is the translation of genetic information into meaningful clinical information. Long-standing disparities in who has access to clinical genetic testing and who is included in research have led to reference databases that disproportionately use genomic data from people of European decent. This disparity can lead to estimated variant allele frequencies that are incongruent with a patient population undergoing testing and higher rates of VUS in patients who are not of European decent. As discussed above, VUS can be a source of uncertainty and anxiety for patients and families and limit the clinical utility of a test 33 . 
Efforts to resolve bias and unequal representation within reference databases are of the utmost importance to ensure equity in genetic testing for diverse populations. 
Conclusion 
Genetic medicine and its role within obstetrics and gynecology is a complex and rapidly evolving field. For practicing obstetrician–gynecologists, it is important to be informed regarding not only medical advancements but also the ethical, legal, and psychosocial considerations in genetic testing.
