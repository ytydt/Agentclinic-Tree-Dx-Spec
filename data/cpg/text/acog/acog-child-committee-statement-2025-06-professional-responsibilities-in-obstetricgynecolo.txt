Number 19 (Replaces Committee Opinion No. 500, August 2011) 
This Committee Statement was developed by the American College of Obstetrician and Gynecologists' Committee on Ethics in collaboration with committee member Julie Chor, MD, MPH. 
ABSTRACT: The education of health care professionals is essential to maintaining standards of medical competence and access to care by patients. Inherent in the education of health care professionals is the problem of disparity in power and authority, including the power of teachers over learners and the power of practitioners over patients. Although there is a continuum of supervision levels and independence from student to resident to fellow, the ethical issues that arise during interactions among all teachers, learners, and their patients are similar. In this Committee Statement, the Committee on Ethics of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists discusses and offers recommendations regarding the professional conduct and ethical responsibilities of practitioners toward patients and participants in research in educational settings; of learners and teachers toward one another; and of institutions toward patients, learners, and teachers. 
Summary of Recommendations and Conclusions 
On the basis of the principles outlined in this Committee Statement, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists makes the following recommendations and conclusions: 
The supervisory role of the teaching physician is paramount to ensuring safe, effective, and equitable patient care and to supporting trainees’ progression toward competent independence in providing such care. 
In addition to focusing on educating trainees on medical knowledge and skill acquisition, teaching physicians and teaching hospitals must foster professionalism among trainees. 
Professional obligations to patients in teaching hospitals include the disclosure of known potential benefits and risks inherent in receiving care in the teaching setting and the provision of adequate supervision at all levels of training. 
Respect for patient autonomy requires that patients be allowed to choose not to be cared for or treated by trainees when it does not compromise patient safety. 
Teachers should ensure that trainees participate in pelvic examinations under anesthesia only when patients have provided explicit consent for their participation, the examination is relevant to the surgical procedure, the patient has recognized the trainee as a member of their care team, and the examination is performed under direct supervision. 
There is a fundamental ethical responsibility for the teacher at all levels to impart wisdom, experience, and skill for the benefit of the trainee, without expectation of direct benefit to the teacher. 
It is essential that teachers recognize their own biases and ensure that trainees are exposed to the full spectrum of services provided in our field, including abortion, assisted reproductive technology, and end-of-life care. 
Teachers should not place trainees in situations in which trainees are expected to provide care or perform procedures for which they are not qualified, prepared, or adequately supervised. 
In rare circumstances, and when this does not compromise patient safety and well-being, trainees should have the right to decline participation in a patient's care when participation in this care creates a clear conflict of conscience for the trainee. 
Ensuring a safe learning environment and supporting trainee well-being is essential to educating and supervising trainees at all levels. 
Trainees have an obligation to be honest, conscientious, and respectful in their relationships with their patients and their teachers, while also advocating for professional standards when teachers do not uphold such standards. 
Institutions should maintain a well-established reporting and review process for investigating allegations of unethical behavior or incompetent conduct by their teachers and trainees, including sexual misconduct or suspected misconduct, without risk of retaliation. 
Background 
Education in obstetrics and gynecology, as in other fields of medicine, carries professional obligations to patients, trainees, and teachers. The education of health care professionals is essential to maintaining standards of medical competence, professionalism, and access to care by patients. One in five hospitals in the United States is a teaching hospital, accounting for more than half of all U.S. hospital admissions, surgeries, and births 1 . In teaching hospitals, health care is provided within the context of a therapeutic triad consisting of the patient, the trainee, and the teacher. Each member of this triad has ethical and moral responsibilities to each other, and the teaching hospital has ethical and moral responsibilities to all three of these parties. Inherent in the education of health care professionals are disparities of power and authority that exist in the relationships between teachers and trainees and between practitioners and patients that have an effect on the educational process 2 . 
In the context of this Committee Statement, medical students, residents, and fellows are referred to as “trainees” throughout the document. Residents and fellows have a dual responsibility as teacher and trainee and must understand their ethical responsibilities to both the trainees they teach and the patients for whom they provide care. Although there is a continuum of levels of supervision and independence from student to resident to fellow, overarching ethical considerations may arise during interactions among all levels of trainees, teachers, and their patients. Further, the line between trainees and teachers in medicine is fluid and nonlinear—all clinicians learn from and teach each other throughout professional development. In this Committee Statement, the ethical obligations of teachers apply to all those in the teaching role, wherever they may be in the educational continuum, and the obligations of trainees apply to all those in the learning role. 
If health care professionals are to benefit society, they must be well-educated and experienced. Much of the education and experience obtained in medical training is through direct patient care, resulting in patients receiving care from individuals with varying degrees of expertise. Physicians must learn new skills and techniques in a manner consistent with the ethical obligations to benefit the patient ( 
beneficence ), to do no harm ( 
nonmaleficence ), to respect a patient's right to make informed decisions about health matters ( 
autonomy ), and to attempt to balance benefits and burdens to patients in a fair and equitable manner ( 
justice ) 3 4 . These obligations must not be subordinated to the need and desire to learn or teach new skills. Although the benefits of medical education generally accrue to society at large, potential burdens fall primarily on individual patients, especially Black, Indigenous, and People of Color; individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds; and individuals with public insurance or no insurance, who are more likely to receive their care at teaching hospitals 5 6 7 . Therefore, inherent in educating physicians-in-training is a tension between a commitment to helping trainees progressively gain the skills, knowledge, and professionalism to become autonomous, unsupervised physicians and a commitment to providing safe, patient-centered clinical care 5 . 
The supervisory role of the teaching physician is paramount to ensuring safe, effective, and equitable patient care and to supporting trainees’ progression toward competent independence in providing such care 5 8 . Over the past several decades, medical education increasingly has formalized guidance and processes for providing trainees with graduated responsibility and autonomy. This evolution in medical education is best reflected in the ACGME’s (Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education) Milestone Project, which has moved medical education into competency-based education and assessment 9 . To support trainees' progress, teaching physicians must provide trainees with constructive feedback on their successes and areas for improvement in achieving graduated competency. The increased focus on appropriate supervision, feedback, and graduated independence based on competency acquisition is essential to counter potential risks to patients associated with obtaining care from trainees with less clinical experience, skills, or knowledge. This approach is especially important in light of disparities with regard to populations disproportionately cared for in teaching hospitals and the fact that many patients may not be aware that they are seeking care at a teaching hospital or may not have alternative hospitals in which to obtain care. Fortunately, there is some evidence that obtaining care in teaching hospitals actually may be associated with improved outcomes compared with nonteaching hospitals 6 . 
In addition to focusing on educating trainees on medical knowledge and skill acquisition, teaching physicians and teaching hospitals must foster professionalism among trainees . Professionalism is one of the six core general clinical competencies included in the ACGME’s educational milestones 9 and has been defined succinctly as, “dedication to the priority of the patient's interests” 10 . The imperative to educate trainees about professionalism early in undergraduate medical education and to continue through residency training and beyond is underscored by research demonstrating that individuals who are disciplined for unprofessional behavior in medical school and residency are at greater risk of facing future state board disciplinary action as attending physicians 11 12 13 . Additionally, a national survey of obstetrics and gynecology residency program directors found that 30% of respondents had dismissed or not renewed a resident due to unprofessional behavior 14 . Although professionalism can be and is taught through formal curricular approaches such as lectures and role-playing, professionalism is largely learned through the 
informal curriculum , in clinical encounters and experiences, and through the 
hidden curriculum , in observing teachers' behaviors, beliefs, and attitudes 15 16 17 18 . The role of behavioral modeling, medical culture, and socialization in helping individual trainees form a medical professional identity cannot be overstated 15 16 17 18 . Therefore, as potential role models and mentors, teaching physicians must strive to consistently demonstrate professionalism in clinical care. Furthermore, teaching hospitals and training programs must create environments to support professional identity formation and behavior 19 . 
The purpose of this Committee Statement is to help clarify the professional responsibilities of teachers and trainees in obstetrics and gynecology toward their patients; the responsibilities of teachers and trainees toward one another; and the responsibilities of academic institutions to patients, trainees, and teachers. This Committee Statement has been revised to incorporate expanded guidance regarding patient refusal of care by trainees, pelvic examinations under anesthesia with trainees, sexual misconduct in academic medicine, and recognizing and addressing burnout among and bias against trainees. 
Ethical Issues and Considerations 
Responsibilities of Teachers Toward Patients 
Although patients are given the opportunity to consent to or refuse treatment by trainees, the obligations of the profession, the institution, teachers, and patients in academic hospitals should be uniform and explicit. 
Professional obligations to patients in teaching hospitals include the disclosure of known potential benefits and risks inherent in receiving care in the teaching setting and the provision of adequate supervision at all levels of training 20 . The teaching physician should encourage patients to participate in the teaching process and engage them in contributing to the development of a new generation of health care professionals. A situation may arise in which a patient refuses, for a number of reasons, to have a trainee involved in their health care. For example, a patient may express concerns about receiving care from an inexperienced trainee or a trainee of a particular gender. Furthermore, particularly when addressing sensitive topics or considering performing invasive examinations in obstetrics and gynecology, patients may request limiting the number of health care professionals involved or those of a certain gender due to a previous history of trauma 21 . Such refusals should initiate discussion and counseling and should be handled with compassion and respect; trauma-informed approaches should be applied universally 21 . 
Respect for patient autonomy requires that patients be allowed to choose not to be cared for or treated by trainees when it does not compromise patient safety 22 23 24 . However, there may be emergency clinical situations in which a patient’s request to not have trainees involved in their care cannot be fulfilled due to medical acuity in cases of unstable patients 24 . Additionally, some patient refusals are rooted in bias and discrimination that should not be tolerated and are not appropriate reasons to exclude trainees from patient care experiences. Although disentangling motivations for patient refusals can be challenging, protecting trainees, colleagues, and other health care professionals from bias and microaggressions is imperative 
25 26 27 . Guidance for both individual teachers and institutions on how to address such situations is addressed in the Ethical Responsibilities of Teachers to Trainees section. 
One procedure that requires particular attention when considering the involvement of trainees is the pelvic examination under anesthesia. Today, many medical schools employ health educators to teach trainees how to perform pelvic examinations. Nonetheless, the pelvic examination under anesthesia can be of benefit to trainees, presenting an opportunity to learn about anatomy and pathology under the guidance of teaching physicians and without concerns about causing patients physical discomfort. However, research has documented, and public outcry has made clear, that patients expect to be asked for consent before having trainees perform pelvic examinations under anesthesia 28 29 30 31 32 . Additionally, medical students have expressed moral distress in being told to perform pelvic examinations under anesthesia without clear patient consent for their participation 33 34 35 . Furthermore, federal rules as well as several states require by law that consent be obtained specifically for pelvic examinations under anesthesia, under penalty of state licensing board disciplinary action or being charged with a misdemeanor 36 37 38 39 . 
Obtaining explicit informed consent from patients about whether they would accept or decline to have a trainee perform a pelvic examination under anesthesia is essential to protect patient autonomy, ensure full transparency, and foster an ethically sound learning environment 40 41 . Leading professional organizations in obstetrics and gynecology (including the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists) have recommended that 
teachers should ensure that trainees participate in pelvic examinations under anesthesia only when patients have provided explicit consent for their participation, the examination is relevant to the surgical procedure, the patient has recognized the trainee as a member of their care team, and the examination is performed under direct supervision 37 40 42 . Excellent guidance is available for how to approach these discussions with patients 36 . When patients are not making decisions for themselves, as may be the case with minors or those with brain injury or intellectual disability, consent for pelvic examinations under anesthesia must be obtained from the patient's surrogate decision maker (eg, a parent, spouse, designated health care proxy, or guardian); however, when possible and clinically appropriate, the health care professional also should obtain the assent of the patient for such examinations 43 . 
Ethical Responsibilities of Teachers to Trainees 
The teacher–trainee relationship exists at multiple levels among faculty members, medical students, residents, and fellows. 
There is a fundamental ethical responsibility for the teacher at all levels to impart wisdom, experience, and skill for the benefit of the trainee, without expectation of direct benefit to the teacher . Furthermore, although the specialty of obstetrics and gynecology includes providing care that may be in conflict with the personal beliefs of some individuals, 
it is essential that teachers recognize their own biases and ensure that trainees are exposed to the full spectrum of services provided in our field, including abortion, assisted reproductive technology, and end-of-life care. The relationship between teacher and trainee in medical education inevitably involves the imbalance of power and the risk of exploitation of a trainee for the benefit of the teacher 2 . Because much of medicine is learned in a preceptor–trainee relationship, great care must be taken that the teacher does not exploit the trainee. An example is the teacher who expects a trainee to spend time that is out of proportion to the educational value involved on a research project but gives little or no credit for such a contribution. Trainees are likely to model their behavior on that of their teachers 44 . 
Adequate and appropriate supervision of trainees is of utmost importance. 
Teachers should not place trainees in situations in which trainees are expected to provide care or perform procedures for which they are not qualified, prepared, or adequately supervised 5 8 . To do otherwise violates an ethical responsibility to the trainee as well as to the patient. A healthy relationship between teachers and trainees allows trainees to request assistance or supervision without fear of humiliation or retribution. Teaching should take place in an atmosphere that fosters mutual respect. Furthermore, trainees should never attempt procedures independently or even counsel patients if they lack the experience, knowledge, or skills to do so without supervision. 
In rare circumstances, and when this does not compromise patient safety and well-being, trainees should have the right to decline participation in a patient's care when participation in this care creates a clear conflict of conscience for the trainee 45 . 
Ensuring a safe learning environment and supporting trainee well-being is essential to educating and supervising trainees at all levels . Although these priorities must be addressed at the institutional level (the role of institutions in addressing these issues is discussed later in this document), individual teachers also are responsible for identifying and addressing circumstances that compromise trainee safety and well-being. A greater focus on wellness is imperative, because the prevalence of burnout, a response to work-related stress, is high among medical students and residents 46 47 48 49 . A national survey of obstetric and gynecology residents administered at the time of the CREOG (Council on Resident Education in Obstetrics and Gynecology) in-training examination with a response rate of 93% found that 51% of respondents experienced burnout, 32% experienced depression, and 0.4% reported suicide attempt during residency 48 . Reports of burnout and mental health problems increased with year of training 48 . Additionally, given the high prevalence of trauma among the general population and the frequency with which trainees address sequelae of trauma in patients seeking obstetric and gynecologic care, a high percentage of trainees undoubtedly are processing their own personal traumas as they care for patients 21 . Although residency programs and institutions play an essential role in addressing wellness, these high levels of burnout and distress among obstetrician–gynecologist trainees call on teachers to look for evidence or signs of distress, especially because trainees do not always recognize these symptoms or concerns themselves 48 49 . At the individual level, teachers can help to identify trainees in need of assistance through mentoring relationships. One model for engaging trainees is through the “cup of coffee intervention,” whereby the teacher signals to the trainee that they appear stressed and offer to have a cup of coffee to discuss their situation 50 . Ultimately, teachers also have a responsibility to inform training program leadership of concerns regarding trainee well-being. 
Given the high prevalence of experiencing abusive remarks from patients, teachers have an important role to play in protecting trainees from incidents of bias and abuse 51 . Specifically, one survey of more than 800 U.S. physicians found that 59% had heard an offensive remark about a personal characteristic 51 . Addressing racism, discrimination, and bias directed at trainees is of particular importance to the field of obstetrics and gynecology. Compared with other medical specialties, obstetrics and gynecology has a relatively high proportion of Black, Hispanic, and female-identifying physicians, who face disproportionate disrespect, discrimination, or abuse from patients 26 52 . Teachers have a responsibility to address discriminatory incidents toward trainees, because inaction in such situations further victimizes and contributes to the emotional toll on trainees 25 26 52 53 . Several frameworks have been proposed to help individual teachers in addressing discriminatory behavior directed at trainees 26 27 53 . Common to these frameworks is the importance of 1) addressing and acknowledging the event, 2) supporting and valuing the victim, and 3) debriefing with the team 26 27 53 . 
The relationship of a teacher to a trainee involves not only trust and confidence but also power and dependency. It is the role of the teacher to foster independence in the trainee while nurturing the trainee in the learning process. This is a complex relationship, the boundaries of which can become obscured in the intense setting of a clinical preceptorship 54 . For example, the long hours spent by teachers and trainees in relatively arduous and isolated circumstances may foster amorous (romantic or sexual) relationships. Regardless of the situation, the power imbalance makes an amorous relationship between a teacher and trainee ethically suspect. Accordingly, romantic relationships between medical supervisors and trainees are not acceptable, even if consensual. Given that these relationships are not acceptable, if the parties wish to pursue their relationship, the supervisory role should be eliminated 54 . Occasionally, situations may arise that challenge these proscriptions; for example, the spouse of a professor of obstetrics and gynecology might matriculate at the professor's medical school. These are rare circumstances, however, and should not contravene the general rule of avoiding these relationships. 
Academic medicine and academic medical centers hold dual roles as places of learning and of employment. Accordingly, teachers are subject to and trainees are protected by laws and professional guidelines that prohibit sexual harassment, sexual assault, and sexual misconduct between persons of authority (teachers) and their subordinates (trainees) 
40 55 56 57 . Institutions are required to educate trainees and teachers about unprofessional behavior, including sexual harassment, mistreatment, abuse, and coercion 55 57 . Despite such educational efforts, trainees who receive sexual advances or verbal, nonverbal, or physical sexual conduct may not readily recognize such actions or behaviors as sexual misconduct. Alternatively, they may avoid reporting such actions due to fear of retaliation. Therefore, it is incumbent on those who witness or hear of such occurrences to support the trainee and help them report these events. Institutions are required to have confidential mechanisms in place to ensure safe reporting regarding such behavior 55 . The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists also has a grievance procedure for members to report sexual harassment, assault, or misconduct 55 . Additionally, in the educational environment, witnesses of sexual misconduct against trainees may be legally required to report such events and should contact their institution's Title IX office with any such concerns 57 58 . 
Ethical Responsibilities of Trainees Toward Patients and Their Teachers 
Trainees have an obligation to be honest, conscientious, and respectful in their relationships with their patients and their teachers, while also advocating for professional standards when teachers do not uphold such standards . They should act in ways that preserve the dignity of patients and do not undermine relationships between patients and their physicians. It is critical for trainees to be self-reflective regarding their limitations and to recognize their responsibility to ask for assistance and supervision when needed. Unfettered communication between trainee and teacher is essential to foster an atmosphere that allows, and even encourages, trainees to request help and constructive feedback. When such communication does not occur, both education and patient care are impaired. 
When possible, trainees should seek out and engage in opportunities to develop and improve procedural skills before and in conjunction with performing procedures or surgeries on patients. Improvements in technology continue to allow for increased training in the virtual setting for trainees. Specifically, technology has allowed surgical training using laparoscopic, robotic, and hysteroscopic surgery simulation and has improved resident education in these areas. Obstetric simulators also have allowed for teaching emergency techniques, maneuvers, and management strategies without putting patient safety at risk. Although simulation often improves clinical education, simulation cannot completely substitute for educational experiences with real patients. 
Inherent in the teacher–trainee relationship is the vulnerability of the trainee in dealing with perceived unethical behavior or incompetent conduct of a teacher. Forms of inappropriate behavior can include discrimination based on race, ethnicity, gender identity, or other factors. In addition to these examples, trainees in the field of obstetrics and gynecology also may observe the use of sexually offensive language or inappropriate conduct between teacher and patient 40 . Chaperones are recommended during sensitive examinations, including breast, genital, and rectal examinations, to protect both patient and health care professional. This recommendation also protects the trainee from being put in a position where they may be witness to a confusing or sexually inappropriate scenario between patient and health care professional. Therefore, to protect trainees and understanding the power imbalance between trainee and teacher, trainees should not be used as chaperones during sensitive clinical examinations 40 . If a trainee does observe inappropriate behavior or conduct, the matter should be brought to the attention of the appropriate institutional authority. Mechanisms that are nonjudgmental and without penalty should be defined clearly to encourage an open dialogue about these observed behaviors, and institutions should inform trainees regularly of mechanisms for reporting improper conduct. 
Trainees must hold in confidence any information about patients acquired in the context of a professional relationship, upholding patient privacy in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) 43 59 60 . Social media presents many positive opportunities for social networking, learning about the cultures of prospective teaching hospitals, and discovering new avenues for learning. However, social media also can present opportunities for lapses in professionalism that trainees must be educated about and take caution to avoid, including posting clinical information about patients and online contact between patients and health care professionals that can obscure the boundaries of the patient–professional relationship 59 . Additionally, trainees must be educated early in their undergraduate medical training and be cognizant throughout their careers of the potential effect that their personal social media presence may have on their professional identity, because patients, colleagues, and employers may view even what are thought to be private or personal accounts 59 . 
Ethical Responsibilities of Institutions to Patients, Trainees, and Teachers 
Academic medicine and academic medical centers provide society with a public good through the training of generations of health care professionals. At the same time, academic medical centers rely heavily on trainees to provide care and augment workforce capacity. For example, the care provided after hours, on weekends, and on holidays is largely the burden of trainees. Academic medicine’s mission to train future health care professionals and academic medical centers' reliance on trainees to provide care exists in tension with individual patients' autonomy around how and from whom they receive their care. Accordingly, it is imperative for academic medical centers and professional organizations to educate the public about the value of medical training and the important role that trainees play in many aspects of their care and to allow patients to opt out of care by trainees when it does not compromise patient safety (please refer to the Ethical Responsibilities of Teachers to Trainees section). Furthermore, academic medical centers also have a responsibility to build systems and infrastructure to relieve some of the burdens experienced by trainees and to decrease the reliance on trainees for care. One such example is to incorporate advanced-practice clinicians to augment clinical capacity and more optimally incorporate trainees in hospital workflow. 
Institutions have ethical obligations to trainees, patients, and teachers, including an obligation to provide a work environment that enhances professional competence. However, the goals of providing a broad clinical experience and maintaining continuity of care for patients must be balanced against the neglect of trainees' physical health and mental health, as well as patient safety 46 61 . Lack of sleep, heavy workloads, and increasing amounts of responsibility without commensurate levels of authority are sources of great stress and burnout in medical education, especially during residency 46 47 48 62 63 . The potentially negative effect of such an educational experience on the trainee's developing attitude toward patients and the profession must be considered 46 47 . Addressing trainee wellness, burnout, and mental health at the programmatic and institutional level requires a commitment to providing a supportive work environment that includes ensuring that trainees work reasonable hours, establishing a balance between medical education and responsibility for patient care, providing adequate ancillary and administrative support services, and, in the case of residents and fellows, providing reasonable salaries and benefits 49 50 64 65 . Further, teachers also may experience burnout and trauma in the workplace. Therefore, institutions also have an obligation to identify and implement structural changes to maximize physician well-being, educate teachers on how they may be affected by burnout and trauma, and provide confidential and robust mental health resources for those teachers who experience burnout and trauma. 
A source of substantial stress for some trainees is the conflict between family responsibilities and the demands of medical education 64 66 . For many trainees, sleep deprivation caused by long work hours results in fatigue, irritability, and anxiety. The inability to relate with consideration or affection to a partner or spouse or to participate in any effective way with childcare or other domestic responsibilities may seriously impair family relationships. Also, because residents and fellows are often at an age when they begin families, the demands of family building, pregnancy, the postpartum period, and child rearing often parallel their ongoing career goals, which can lead to both personal and institutional conflicts requiring special attention. Surveys of physicians, and specifically female obstetrics and gynecology residents, document high rates of delaying family building due to training, heavy workloads, and lack of childcare and that many are concerned about or have experienced infertility 67 68 69 . Further, some trainees express interest in fertility preservation because of these fertility concerns 67 68 69 . 
Providing supportive family-building policies, including ample time for residents and fellows of all genders to sustain family relationships without adversely affecting the educational experience or imposing excessive burdens on colleagues, is a challenging task but one that must be confronted. Shared positions and more flexible timelines for completing educational requirements can be helpful in solving such conflicts. In addition to implementing supportive family-building policies, programs and institutions should consider supporting their trainees (medical students, residents, and fellows) in accessing fertility services available to them. 
Just as with the populations for whom they care, trainees at all levels may have medical conditions for which they will need urgent, emergent, or episodic or chronic care. As such, institutions have the responsibility to ensure that trainees are afforded the opportunity to access needed care, even during work hours 55 . Training in a surgical specialty such as obstetrics and gynecology poses particular health risks to trainees who become pregnant. Studies demonstrate that trainees in surgical specialties experience higher rates of pregnancy complications such as preterm prelabor rupture of membranes and a correlation between shift work and preterm birth 70 71 72 73 . To fully support the health needs of their trainees, therefore, institutions must implement policies that support those who pursue pregnancy during training. Such policies can include offering flexible rotations, schedule modifications, and shorter shifts during pregnancy 74 . For those trainees who experience or whose partners experience pregnancy complications or unintended pregnancy 75 , institutions should help to provide time and resources to accommodate facing these circumstances. Additionally, ensuring access to abortion care is essential to fully supporting trainees' reproductive health needs. In the wake of the Supreme Court's decision overturning 
Roe v. Wade , access to abortion care is heterogeneous and state-dependent. Accordingly, ensuring access to this care may require programs and institutions to assist trainees with travel and medical leave 72 . 
Institutions have an obligation to protect patients and trainees from unprofessional health care clinicians. 
Institutions should maintain a well-established reporting and review process for investigating allegations of unethical behavior or incompetent conduct by their teachers and trainees, including sexual misconduct or suspected misconduct, without risk of retaliation 40 . Access to such a process can protect patients and facilitate fair and just relationships between trainees and teachers in these scenarios. Relatedly, clear protocols and institutional policies are needed to address discrimination and harassment aimed at patients, trainees, and teachers. Institutions must consider federal laws that mandate safe workplaces that are free from discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, and national origin 24 . Discrimination and bias against trainees are of particular concern, given trainees' increased exposure to such incidents and relative lack of authority and decision-making autonomy compared with attending physicians 76 . Several models and frameworks have been proposed and implemented to guide institutional approaches to discrimination, bias, and harassment aimed at hospital employees, including institutional strategies to educate teachers and trainees about bias 24 26 53 76 77 78 . Institutional protocols and policies addressing patient refusals of care from specific health care professionals based on race, ethnicity, or gender must consider employees' rights to a safe work environment, patients' rights to refuse medical care and to the informed consent process, and the responsibility of institutions to stabilize patients and provide emergency care under EMTALA (the Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act) 24 76 79 . 
Finally, as concerns about cost containment increase, education could become a low priority. It is the responsibility of all physicians and institutions involved in the education of health care professionals to ensure that cost-reduction efforts do not diminish the opportunities for education in clinical medicine. Institutions have an ethical responsibility to develop policy statements and guidelines for the inclusion of trainees in patient care in ways that ensure sound medical education and high-quality medical care. 
Conclusion 
Much of medical education takes place within the context of direct patient care. Inherent in this model of education are potential ethical challenges arising from conflicting responsibilities and priorities. Furthermore, both patients and trainees may be vulnerable to power differentials, biases, and discrimination within medical education. Therefore, teachers, trainees, and institutions have an obligation to develop approaches and policies that foster trainees' progression toward competent independent practice while ensuring the provision of safe, effective, and equitable patient care.
