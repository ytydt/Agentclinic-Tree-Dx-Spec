This Committee Statement was developed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' Clinical Consensus Committee—Obstetrics in collaboration with Mark Turrentine, MD, and Romeo Galang, MD, MPH. 
ABSTRACT: Large-scale catastrophic events, either natural or human-influenced, highlight the requisite for emergency plans that specifically address the needs of obstetric and gynecologic patients. Pregnant, postpartum, and lactating individuals and their newborns and infants can be adversely affected by disasters and disaster-related environmental conditions. Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals have a unique role in developing and carrying out an emergency preparedness plan that addresses safety and medical needs in the event of a disaster. 
Summary of Recommendations and Conclusions 
Based on the principles outlined in this Committee Statement, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) makes the following recommendations and conclusions for obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals: 
Inform patients of local risk and seasonality for natural disasters and severe weather emergencies and encourage them to develop emergency preparedness plans, including plans for evacuation, supplies, and access to important documents. 
Advise patients to follow public safety advisories during emergencies, including advisories about air and water safety, sheltering, and evacuation. 
Encourage patients to develop a plan for obstetric care in the event of a disaster, including where to seek prenatal and obstetric services in the event of local health care facility closures, how to access their prenatal care records, and what to do in the event of an unintended out-of-facility birth. 
Advise patients to seek medical care for urgent maternal warning signs and to inform health care professionals, including emergency responders and shelter staff, if they are pregnant, recently pregnant, or breastfeeding or chestfeeding. 
Promote lactation as a strategy to continue infant feeding when disaster-related conditions cause interruptions in either formula or safe water supply and promote relactation as a strategy for patients unable to establish breastfeeding or chestfeeding or who experienced an interruption in breastfeeding or chestfeeding. 
For individuals using reversible contraception, facilitate method continuation, such as providing prescriptions to enable sufficient supply. 
Review signs of emotional distress and trauma and advise patients to seek care if needed. 
Background 
Disasters are events, including natural disasters, infectious disease outbreaks, and human-caused events, that result in widespread injury, death, or severe property damage. Natural disasters include severe weather or geological events that can occur seasonally or without warning, such as temperature extremes, floods, hurricanes, wildfires, earthquakes, landslides, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions. Infectious disease outbreaks constituting public health emergencies include significant outbreaks of emerging or re-emerging infectious diseases, such as the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic, the 2014–2016 Ebola outbreak, and the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Human-caused disasters can be intentional or accidental in nature, and they can be associated with the release of explosive, chemical, biological, nuclear, or radiologic agents. Human-caused disasters also include human-based technological incidents, such as a building or bridge collapse, or events related to manufacturing, transporting, storage, and use of hazardous materials. 
Large-scale catastrophic events, either natural or human-influenced, highlight the requisite for emergency plans that specifically address the needs of obstetric and gynecologic patients. Catastrophic disasters can significantly affect the health care system and disrupt the health care needs of pregnant individuals and gynecologic patients, particularly during the acute or recovery phase of the disaster 
1 . Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals have a unique role in developing and carrying out an emergency preparedness plan that addresses safety and medical needs in the event of a disaster. 
Recommendations and Conclusions 
Anticipating Disaster-Related Effects on Pregnant and Postpartum Individuals 
Inform patients of local risk and seasonality for natural disasters and severe weather emergencies and encourage them to develop emergency preparedness plans, including plans for evacuation, supplies, and access to important documents. 
Pregnant, postpartum, and lactating individuals and their newborns and infants can be adversely affected by disasters and disaster-related environmental conditions. Pregnancy-related morbidities may arise in the setting of or be exacerbated by disaster-related environmental exposures; disruption in availability or safety of food and utilities; loss of social and financial support systems; new or increased stressors and mental health needs; and interruption in the delivery of human services, including access to medication and health care. These disaster-related conditions often can be anticipated, and emergency planning can mitigate negative effects. The involvement of obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals in emergency preparedness and response is essential. 
Although each disaster has unique hazards and scale of effect, there are common challenges that can be experienced by individuals regardless of the nature of the disaster. Patients should be informed of local risk and seasonality for natural disasters and severe weather emergencies and be encouraged to develop emergency preparedness plans, including plans for evacuation, supplies, and access to important documents. This is especially important in areas where natural disasters are seasonal and may be likely to occur. Information about emergency preparedness and hazard-specific safety messages, including for pregnant, postpartum, and breastfeeding or chestfeeding individuals, are available through the American Red Cross and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)

Emergency Preparedness Plan Considerations for Individuals Who Are Pregnant or Postpartum 
Encourage patients to develop a plan for obstetric care in the event of a disaster, including where to seek prenatal and obstetric services in the event of local health care facility closures, how to access their prenatal care records, and what to do in the event of an unintended out-of-facility birth. 
Maintaining health care for individuals in the antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum periods is of the utmost importance in disaster settings to prevent excess morbidity and mortality. Health care professionals outside the perimeter of the disaster should expect and be willing to accept displaced individuals to ensure the continuation of care. Accessing medical records is vital in maintaining obstetric care. Interruptions in electricity and internet services may prohibit accessing electronic records. In preparation, health care professionals should ensure that patients are well informed of their health histories, review how to access their medical records, and provide copies of key portions of their medical records, as needed. Also, health care professionals caring for obstetric patients should increase patients' awareness of the signs of preterm labor and other obstetric emergencies and should advise patients to seek care for urgent maternal warning signs 14 . 
Advise patients to seek medical care for urgent maternal warning signs and to inform health care professionals, including emergency responders and shelter staff, if they are pregnant, recently pregnant, or breastfeeding or chestfeeding. 
Obstetric care at a designated facility is ideal; however, several factors may contribute to difficulty accessing obstetric health care facilities during a disaster. 
Entire health care facilities may be rendered unavailable due to infrastructure damage, interruptions in power and water, or shortages in staffing and medical equipment. 
Health care facilities also may be operating under emergency conditions and increased patient volume, which could alter the services available to pregnant individuals. 
Also, physical barriers, such as impassible roads, demolished bridges, and fire lines, may serve as obstacles to accessing obstetric care facilities. 
These hindrances may result in unintended out-of-facility births. 
As part of emergency preparedness planning, patients should be made aware of these possibilities and of what to do in case of an unintended out-of-facility birth, including how to contact emergency medical services and the necessary components of an emergency birth kit 15 . 
These kits have the necessary equipment should a birth occur outside a birthing facility and should include 15 : 
Ten blue pads 
One plastic peri-bottle 
One newborn hat 
Six packs of lubricant jelly 
Four gloves, sterile surgical pairs 
Two plastic newborn cord clamps or cord ties 
Scissors 
Twelve alcohol prep pads, individually wrapped 
One dozen sanitary pads 
One dozen sterile gauze pads 
One bulb syringe 
One bottle of sterile water 
Neonatal thermometer 
Ten newborn diapers 
One–two newborn blankets 
Battery-powered radio with extra batteries 
Promote lactation as a strategy to continue infant feeding when disaster-related conditions cause interruptions in either formula or safe water supply and promote relactation as a strategy for patients unable to establish breastfeeding or chestfeeding or who experienced an interruption in breastfeeding or chestfeeding. 
During a disaster, postpartum individuals who are not breastfeeding or chestfeeding may have difficulty providing food for their newborns or infants. Some may plan to feed their newborns or infants with formula; however, during a disaster, there may not be access to clean water for sterilization of equipment or access to powdered or concentrated formula. 
Encouraging and establishing breastfeeding or chestfeeding as a part of routine care ensures that parents can feed their newborns and infants 16 . 
Additionally, health care professionals should be educated in lactation to assist new parents in initiating breastfeeding or chestfeeding in the immediate phase of a disaster. For postpartum parents who have experienced an interruption in breastfeeding or chestfeeding or those who have not previously established lactation, relactation can be established and should be encouraged

When a parent is unable to meet the nutritional needs of their newborn or infant through their own breastfeeding or chestfeeding, donor human milk may be an option; however, access to this resource during a disaster event may be challenging 19 . For those parents who choose not to begin relactation or are beyond several months from delivery, ready-to-feed infant formula in a single-serving bottle could be advised to be used. Information on infant and young child feeding in emergencies, including materials for patients, is available through the CDC 20 and the Breastfeeding Handbook for Physicians, 3 
rd edition 19 . 
Considerations for Contraception in Emergency Preparedness Plans 
For individuals using reversible contraception, facilitate method continuation, such as providing prescriptions to enable sufficient supply. 
Individuals should be made aware that disaster-related conditions may affect access to contraception. Postdisaster conditions may affect the availability of pharmacies, supply chains for certain products, and accessibility to health care professionals for contraception counseling and prescribing. Interruptions in electricity may affect home storage of items requiring refrigeration, and extreme temperatures may affect items requiring storage at room temperature. When possible, emergency health care facilities should stock and dispense various contraceptive products. 
Individuals may desire to initiate, continue, or switch methods of contraception—including switching to a long-acting reversible contraception method—to avoid pregnancy under postdisaster conditions 21 . Patients should be provided their preferred contraception for a period of 3 months to avoid lapses in coverage. Emergency contraception also should be provided to patients experiencing disaster conditions. Latex condoms should be made available and directly promoted to both men and women, because they provide protection against both sexually transmitted infections and unintended pregnancy when used consistently and correctly. 
Mental Health and Emotional Well-Being in Emergency Preparedness Plans 
Review signs of emotional distress and trauma and advise patients to seek care if needed. 
Disasters can cause disruptions of whole communities, families, and individual lives. Loss of usual sources of social support, safety, and stability can precipitate and exacerbate grief, anxiety, and depression. Additionally, social isolation and loneliness are associated with heart disease and stroke, suicide, and premature death 22 . 
Health care professionals should review the risks of poor mental health and threats to emotional well-being and advise patients to seek care for signs of emotional distress requiring medical attention. Patients should be encouraged to take care of their emotional health and practice healthy stress management, including attention to adequate nutrition, hydration, sleep, and exercise. The CDC offers information and resources for mental health care during and after disasters 
23 24 . 
Conclusion 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals play a critical role in providing anticipatory guidance and safety messages for addressing obstetric and gynecologic needs during disasters and other public health emergencies. Potential effects of disasters frequently can be anticipated, and emergency preparedness planning can mitigate effects and promote the maintenance of good health for pregnant patients, newborns, and infants in disasters. Plans for continuity of obstetric care should consider identification of appropriate health care facilities for the patient's health care needs, access to medical records, and guidance in case of an unintended out-of-facility birth. Lactation and relactation are strategies to continue breastfeeding or chestfeeding when disaster-related conditions are unsafe for formula feeding or cause interruptions in breastfeeding or chestfeeding. Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should consider issues of access and coverage in counseling about and prescribing contraception. Patients should be made aware of emotional signs of distress and seek care if needed.
