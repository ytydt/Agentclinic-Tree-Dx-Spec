Number 23 (Replaces Committee Opinion No. 587, February 2014) 
This Committee Statement was developed by the American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists' Committee on Advancing Equity in Obstetric and Gynecologic Health Care in collaboration with Rose L. Molina, MD, MPH, and Brownsyne M. Tucker Edmonds, MD, MPH, MS. 
ABSTRACT: Effective communication between physicians and patients is the cornerstone of the patient -physician relationship. Effective communication practices can build positive relationships, enable sharing power, and lead to mutual trust. Moreover, effective patient -physician communication improves patient experiences and health outcomes. The building blocks of relationship-centered communication include acknowledgment of a patient's identity and experiences, clarity of information, patient activation and participation, knowledge-related power and authority, emotional proximity and shared experiences, and managing health care and relational goals. In practical terms, this translates to communication behaviors that demonstrate humility to close the patient -physician relationship distance and achieve effective communication, particularly for marginalized patient populations. 
Summary of Recommendations and Conclusions 
Based on the principles outlined in this Committee Statement, the American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists (ACOG) makes the following recommendations for how obstetrician–gynecologists can improve communication with patients: 
Utilize relationship-centered communication skills with every patient to help mitigate power differentials within the patient–physician relationship, especially with patients from marginalized communities. 
Pause and reflect to interrupt biases and apply empathy and cultural humility to improve relationship-centered care, especially when there is racial, ethnic, or linguistic discordance between patients and physicians. 
Apply best practices when working with interpreters and patients who speak languages other than English. 
Background 
Effective communication between patients and physicians is the cornerstone of the patient–physician relationship. 
Relationship-centered care Box 1 is a framework for understanding the dyadic relationship between physicians and patients, as well as other important relationships relevant to health care delivery, such as those with partners, family members, and other clinicians 1 . In the patient–physician relationship, communication practices should build positive relationships, enable sharing power, and lead to mutual trust. Successful communication is paramount to good clinical care, and some professional organizations, such as the American Society of Clinical Oncology, have issued guidelines on specific communication skills to use in clinical practice 2 . Effective patient–physician communication has been associated with lower rates of malpractice claims 3 4 and better patient experiences and health outcomes 5 , such as receipt of treatment for depression 6 and emotional well-being 7 . 
Box 1. 
Glossary 
Affective communication: A style of communication aimed at putting a patient at ease and releasing their emotions; it consists of several components, including empathy, reassurance, and support.* 
† 
Cultural competence: This framework seeks to promote “culturally sensitive” practice and describes the trained ability of a clinician to identify cross-cultural expressions of illness and health. It has been criticized as promoting overly reductionist, simplistic, and static depictions of culture, often reduced to race and ethnicity, and frames culture and race and ethnicity as residing only in the “Other,” normalizing dominant White culture. 
‡ 
Cultural humility: Originally described as a tool to educate physicians to work with the increasing cultural, racial, and ethnic diversity in the United States, it is a lifelong process of self-reflection and self-critique whereby the individual not only learns about another's culture, but starts with an examination of their own beliefs and cultural identities. 
§ 
Cognitive heuristics: “Shortcuts” in clinical decision making influenced by one's own cognitive biases used by practitioners to ensure efficient practice. Although beneficial for efficiency in patient management, heuristics do increase the likelihood of misjudgment. 
‖ 
Humility: Self-awareness, empathic openness toward others, and profound appreciation for the privilege of patient care. 
¶ 
Relationship-centered care: A conceptual framework of care in which all participants appreciate the importance of their relationships with one another; it is founded on the following principles: 1) that relationships in health care ought to include the personhood of the participants, 2) that affect and emotion are important components of these relationships, 3) that all health care relationships occur in the context of reciprocal influence, and 4) that the formation and maintenance of genuine relationships in health care is morally valuable. 
# 
Shared decision making: A communication approach characterized by a bidirectional flow of information between patients and physicians, resulting in deliberation and negotiation between these parties, which is followed by the physician and patient jointly deciding on a treatment strategy.**

The building blocks of relationship-centered communication include acknowledgment of a patient's identity and experiences, clarity of information, patient activation and participation, knowledge-related power and authority, emotional proximity and shared experiences, and managing health care and relational goals 8 . In practical terms, this translates to communication behaviors that demonstrate 
humility to close the patient–physician relationship distance and achieve effective communication, particularly for patients from marginalized populations 9 . 
Empathic communication is especially important when caring for marginalized patient populations, whose individual and collective health care experiences frequently are marked by dismissal, dehumanization, or outright discrimination that results in distrust of health care systems or clinicians or both. Empathic and 
affective communication , both demonstrated by attending to emotions, values, and beliefs, may convey an acknowledgment of the full humanity of the patient, who may not expect such behavior based on prior lived experience 10 . Greater affective communication behaviors, particularly related to emotion-handling and rapport-building, has been associated with improved trust in clinicians among patients from minoritized groups 11 12 . Such communication skills may be particularly important in racially or ethnically discordant patient–physician dyads. 
Though the evidence is somewhat mixed, there are compelling data to suggest that racial and ethnic concordance can improve health experiences and outcomes for Black, Indigenous, and other people of color. Furthermore, a diverse workforce in any setting is likely to improve patient–physician relationships, even when patients and physicians do not have racial concordance 13 . Although the importance of building a diverse physician workforce representative of the nation's myriad communities cannot be overstated, workforce diversification is only one of many important strategies needed to improve care for patients from minoritized groups. The growth in patient diversity far outpaces health care workforce diversity. In 2012, the United States Census Bureau projected that the U.S. population will be “majority-minority” by the year 2043 14 , whereas the physician workforce remains majority White (56.2%) and male (64.1%) 15 . Recent studies estimate that it would take 66–92 years of sustained doubling of Black and Hispanic medical student matriculants to address the deficit in the physician workforce to reach parity with the U.S. population 16 . Since the 2024 U.S. Supreme Court decisions striking down the limited consideration of an applicant's race with the goal of creating a diverse student body, the prospect of racial parity is moving even further out of reach 17 18 . According to the AAMC, Black and Hispanic medical school admissions declined by roughly 11% for both groups in the first admission cycles after the ruling 19 . Therefore, it is critically important that all physicians are responsible for improving the experience and quality of care for patients of color. Developing the communication skills that can build rapport, enhance trust, and improve patient experiences must become an essential element of medical education competencies. 
Beyond considerations of racial and ethnic concordance, the literature also emphasizes that physicians should strive to understand and respect patients' lived experiences and needs, which may shed light on how they might interact with the health care system. Physicians should also intentionally incorporate patient goals and values into their care, especially for LGBTQ+ (lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer+) patients, patients with different cultural backgrounds, and patients with alternative views on medicine. These populations are more likely to feel respected and follow treatment recommendations when they feel that their preferences are explored and included in their care decision making. 
Cultural humility promotes, “…an orientation to care that is based on self-reflexivity, appreciation of patients' lay expertise, openness to sharing power with patients, and to continue learning from one's patients” 20 . In contrast, 
cultural competence conveys a sense of knowing or mastery that feels finite and may lend itself to narrow, one-size-fits-all notions or assumptions about patients' preferences or values. Cultural humility instead assumes a posture of “not-knowing” and requires constant recognition of one's inability to even begin to understand any given patient's cultural or lived experiences without engaging and exploring that person's history with openness and humbleness. Approaching patients with genuine interest and humility may mitigate some of the judgmental and paternalistic behaviors that have contributed to the growing mistrust of physicians and science. However, patients from minoritized communities should not be put in the position of educating their clinicians about their lived experiences that are not relevant to their medical concerns. 
The past decade has seen a rise in disinformation and widespread loss of trust in science and medicine. The most acute manifestation has been seen in the nation's response to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and the rise in vaccine hesitancy in many communities, spanning socioeconomic class, geography, racial and ethnic groups, and religious backgrounds 21 . The need for skills and tools to foster trust and confidence in physicians has never been more important. The dynamics that drive mistrust among individuals from marginalized and minoritized communities are largely set against a backdrop of historical and present-day mistreatment and bias that they experience in clinical care 22 . Therefore, strategies to mitigate implicit bias and foster cultural humility are particularly critical to improving the care of patients from marginalized populations. Through its “Combating Misinformation” resource 23 , ACOG is working to combat misinformation related to obstetric and gynecologic health care. For more details on cultural humility, implicit bias, and approaches to care that build understanding, trust, and respect for patients, see ACOG Committee Statement No. 10, 
Racial and Ethnic Inequities in Obstetrics and Gynecology , and ACOG Committee Statement No. 11, 
Addressing Social and Structural Determinants of Health in the Delivery of Reproductive Health Care 13 24 . 
Although communication tools and approaches to help clinicians communicate effectively are essential, they do not address all barriers patients face in effective communication and mutual understanding in their health care. In addition to bias and stigma, patients from marginalized communities also face challenges accessing and participating equally in care decisions when clinicians and health systems fail to provide resources and strategies to facilitate their engagement. 
The growing linguistic diversity of the U.S. population poses important challenges for equitable health care delivery due to persistent language-access barriers. Despite federal regulations, such as section 1557 of the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act 25 , the National Standards for Culturally and Linguistically Appropriate Services 26 , and policy imperatives for health care systems to provide meaningful language access, patients who speak languages other than English and those with limited English proficiency experience inequities in care experiences and outcomes 27 . Studies have shown clinician bias related to patient language preference, ethnicity, and educational background 28 29 30 31 . Language discordance between physicians and patients threatens effective, relationship-centered communication, even when qualified interpreters are present 32 . Conversely, language concordance is associated with improved experiences 33 and outcomes 34 . In a study of Spanish-speaking pregnant and postpartum patients, language concordance and warm interpersonal dynamics were the most important factors in choosing a prenatal care clinician 35 . Expanding a multilingual clinician workforce and verifying language proficiency among bilingual and multilingual clinicians are important health-equity initiatives for enhancing language access for patients who communicate in languages other than English. 
Patients who have specific educational or developmental challenges also may have limited health literacy. The responsibility for recognizing and addressing the problem of limited health literacy lies with all entities in the health care system. Universal precautions, such as using plain language and providing written information at a 6 
th -grade reading level, help ensure that patients with limited health literacy can engage with their medical information and participate in decision making 36 . 
It is important to consider differently abled populations, including the Deaf and Hard of Hearing community, and ensure that all information is fully accessible and understood. Recommendations for improved patient–physician communication include the following: incorporating communication with neurodiverse individuals and patients with disabilities into undergraduate medical education and specialty-specific training, providing appropriate accommodations to eliminate barriers in communication, and using a dual person-centered and culturally humble approach 37 38 . Health care professionals should center the patient in all conversations, including Deaf individuals, and should learn how to appropriately collaborate with sign language interpreters and augmentative and alternative communication tools 39 . Equitable care requires that communication strategies be tailored to the unique needs of patients and designed to mitigate the barriers for patients to fully participate in care.

Advancing digital technology poses additional distractions and challenges in practicing relationship-centered communication skills. Administrative pressures to document clinical notes in a timely manner obligate physicians to simultaneously use a computer while engaging with patients, which may compromise rapport building 45 . Thoughtful integration of technology, such as artificial intelligence transcription programs, could support or enhance rapport and be a useful tool to facilitate conversations. Additionally, translation programs and the use of personal devices should be vetted for concerns regarding privacy and data sharing. Although advances in artificial intelligence will enhance some aspects of clinical diagnosis and treatment, these advances should be met with caution when technology threatens the centrality of the patient–physician relationship 46 . 
Being mindful of computer distractions allows for deliberate use of supportive nonverbal cues to communicate effectively and build positive relationships. Patient–physician communication in virtual platforms also is important given the growth of telehealth since the COVID-19 pandemic. Some suggestions for telehealth communication include waiting a few seconds for responses due to lag time with broadband connections, continuing small talk to build rapport, using a professional background, and looking into the camera 47 . For additional information, see ACOG Committee Opinion No. 798, 
Implementing Telehealth in Practice 48 . 
Documentation in the electronic medical record system for encounters and patient messages is also an important mode of communication that requires attention for mitigating potential bias and applying plain language. Clinicians should aim to write about patients using respectful language that provides accurate information without stigmatization or bias Table 2 . The CREATE TRUST Communication Framework for Patient Messaging Services includes quality standards for communication: Correct, Referenced, Empathic, Authentic, Thorough, Engaging, Tailored, Respectful, Understandable, Safe, and Timely 49 . 
Obstetrician–gynecologists should use relationship-centered communication skills with all members of the care team in addition to the patient, such as doulas, family members or support people, and other health care professionals. To maintain and build communication skills, obstetrician–gynecologists should engage in skills training, such as simulation, role play, and observed coaching, to receive constructive feedback about opportunities for improvement 50 51 . 
Pause and reflect to interrupt biases and apply empathy and cultural humility to improve relationship-centered care, especially when there is racial, ethnic, or linguistic discordance between patients and physicians. 
Physician's daily practice and medical decision making are shaped by 
cognitive heuristics (or shortcuts) that are adaptive, protective, and efficient in many circumstances. These heuristics, however, can pose the risk of creating cognitive errors that may bias medical decision making. For example, physicians most readily rely on decisional shortcuts, such as Availability, Anchoring, Representativeness, and Confirmatory heuristics 52 . In keeping with these widely deployed heuristics, implicit or unconscious biases operate in all individuals, resulting from our natural tendencies to rely on pattern recognition as a mental shortcut to assess for safety and threat. These shortcuts become detrimental to clinical reasoning and medical decision making, because the patterns that are recognized and used to make judgments often are informed by stereotypes and “controlling images” that are “uniformly negative” and designed to dehumanize and discount marginalized populations 53 . Therefore, strategies and technologies that support and encourage reflective practice are critical to mitigating the effect of unconscious bias and protecting against medical errors in the care of patients from marginalized populations. 
Shared decision making , one model of relationship-centered care, is another skill that can facilitate more equitable care and care outcomes. Although shared decision making has been endorsed by many professional societies as an optimal strategy for navigating clinical decision making in the face of equipoise, uncertainty, or both 54 , there remains substantial variation in understanding how to successfully deploy these skills in the traditional, time-limited clinical encounter. 
Efforts to shift the power dynamics and center the whole person are further bolstered by communication strategies that attend to emotion and build rapport. Empathic communication is a core competency in communication skills training programs such as Vital Talk, which trains clinicians on NURSE and other tools Box 2 . Effective trainings share a common focus on emotional handling, active listening, and showing support and respect for patients. Additionally, the trainings explore patient perspectives with a posture of curiosity and humility. In the broadest sense, humility is needed to recognize that the clinician is not the only expert in the encounter; the patient's expertise and lived experience also should be acknowledged and respected. More specifically, cultural humility is needed to mitigate a health care professional's tendency to make assumptions and prejudgments about patient values and preferences without exploring them with the patient. 
Although empathic communication and cultural humility are strategies to engender trust and connection, there are still times when, despite best efforts, biases operate or discrimination occurs. It is important that harms are recognized and that an attempt is made toward repair. In psychology, such breaches in the therapeutic alliance are referred to as repairing “cultural rupture” 55 . Communication-training programs, such as Vital Talk's Building Connection, address this directly, providing physicians with skills to do the following: 1) explore the patient's experiences of bias or discrimination, 2) acknowledge the validity of the patient's lived experience and concerns, and 3) seek to repair the breach with the patient in an attempt to restore a relationship with the patient or family or both 56 . 
A related but separate set of principles that can aid in addressing a patient's experiences of marginalization or oppression is trauma-informed care. Applying a trauma-informed approach can aid in the recognition of the ways that historical and collective trauma may show up in the clinical encounter and avoid activating or escalating conflict with patients. As detailed in ACOG's Committee Opinion No. 825, 
Caring for Patients Who Have Experienced Trauma , engaging in patient-centered communication and care can be accomplished by seeking patient input on how best to make them comfortable and can be particularly valuable for establishing trust and rapport. Individuals with a history of trauma often have adopted coping strategies to survive past and present adversities and overwhelming circumstances; those strategies may involve adopting unhealthy behaviors. It is important to understand that trauma is experienced uniquely by each individual; therefore, the ways in which individuals react to and recover from trauma also will be unique 57 . Asking about individual experiences with trauma or exploring whether patients have personal or family experiences of being treated unfairly in previous health care experiences can open the door to valuable dialogue. 
Notably, although some patients may be open to sharing their experiences, others may find such disclosures activating or retraumatizing. Indeed, there is no “one-size-fits-all” approach to communicating effectively with patients. Communication needs may be different for patients presenting with different communication and learning styles. For example, although strong eye contact and handshakes typically may be encouraged as a communication best practice, this may be uncomfortable or undesirable for a neurodivergent patient 58 . Supporting patient understanding and adherence to treatment recommendations includes knowing a person's learning preference (eg, visual, auditory, tactile) to facilitate more effective patient engagement and improve outcomes 58 . It is best to ask, rather than assume, a patient's preferred communication and learning styles. 
Apply best practices when working with interpreters and patients who speak languages other than English. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists who do not have adequate proficiency in languages other than English should always work with certified medical interpreters when communicating with patients with limited English proficiency. Ad hoc interpreters, such as family members, friends, and unqualified bilingual staff, should not be relied on for interpretation except for rare emergencies or in situations where no medical interpreters are available (eg, for languages of lesser diffusion). Reasons for not using ad hoc interpreters include uncertain fluency in both languages, variable skills in interpreting distinct from fluency level, and challenges with maintaining objectivity and impartiality. Table 3 . Demonstrating empathic communication skills is particularly important to strengthen the patient–physician relationship when an interpreter is present. 
Ensuring effective patient–physician communication with patients with disabilities also is critical for advancing equitable care 59 60 . Considerations for patients who are Deaf or Hard of Hearing include asking patients about preferences regarding communication modalities, such as American Sign Language, Certified Deaf Interpreter, and Communication Access Realtime Translation. See ACOG Committee Statement No. 18, 
Access to Obstetric and Gynecologic Care for Patients With Disabilities , for additional guidance on sign language interpreters and augmentative and alternative approaches to communication 39 . 
Obstetrician–gynecologists should continue to advocate for sustainable practice models that increase the duration of visits, especially when interpreters are needed, to provide the same quality care as in English. Increased time for visits that require an interpreter is crucial to improve relationship-centered care. 
Conclusion 
Effective communication practices can build positive relationships, enable sharing power, and lead to mutual trust. Moreover, effective patient–physician communication improves patient experiences and health outcomes. Effective communication training should be a core competency in undergraduate medical education and should be revisited in continuing medical education as care dynamics become more complex and real-world constraints threaten the patient–physician relationship. Relationship-centered communication is particularly important in caring for patients from marginalized populations to mitigate power differentials within the patient–physician relationship. Reflective practices can aid in disrupting implicit biases and cognitive shortcuts relied on in clinical decision making, which can lead to diagnostic errors and relational ruptures that result in harm to patients. Developing skills in empathic communication and cultural humility can help clinicians build rapport and earn trust, particularly in racially, ethnically, or linguistically discordant encounters. Lastly, learners and clinicians need training and support to ensure that they use best practices when working with interpreters and patients who speak languages other than English.
