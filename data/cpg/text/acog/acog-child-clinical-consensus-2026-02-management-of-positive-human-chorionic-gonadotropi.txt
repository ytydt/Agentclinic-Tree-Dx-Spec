Number 11 (Replaces Committee Opinion No. 278, November 2002) 
Committee on Clinical Consensus—Gynecology. This Clinical Consensus was developed by the American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists' Committee on Clinical Consensus—Gynecology in collaboration with committee members Lee-may Chen, MD, Anne E. Porter, MD, and Akiva P. Novetsky, MD, MS 
Summary: Positive human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) test results have been associated with unnecessary workup and treatment, including invasive procedures and chemotherapy. It is important for health care professionals to consider alternative explanations for positive hCG results when pregnancy and malignancy have been excluded, particularly before proceeding with more invasive interventions. Due to the multiple potential etiologies of persistently elevated hCG, health care professionals should evaluate test results according to a systematic framework. When serum hCG test results are elevated, the first steps are to evaluate for pregnancy (both intrauterine and ectopic). Appropriate retesting to rule out various etiologies and to identify the main cause of persistently elevated hCG is necessary to avoid misdiagnosis or mismanagement of elevated hCG levels. 
Background 
The human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) test is an assay using serum or urine that is commonly used in the management and detection of early pregnancy and gynecologic conditions, including gestational trophoblastic disease and gonadal germ cell malignancies. In these conditions, positive test results may lead to further testing or interventions. In certain clinical scenarios, a positive hCG test result may represent a false-positive (eg, testing failure) or may be due to other diagnoses unrelated to pregnancy or gynecologic neoplasms. 
Positive hCG test results have been associated with unnecessary workup and treatment, including invasive procedures and chemotherapy. It is important for health care professionals to consider alternative explanations for positive hCG results when pregnancy and malignancy have been excluded, particularly before proceeding with more invasive interventions. This Clinical Consensus statement summarizes the various causes of elevated hCG levels at the point where pregnancy and gynecologic malignancy have been excluded for the patient. This Clinical Consensus also provides health care professionals with an algorithm to guide the management of persistently elevated hCG levels. Diagnosis and management of gestational trophoblastic disease and pregnancy were deemed out of scope for this document; however, health care professionals may consult the Society of Gynecologic Oncology's recommendation on the Epidemiology, Diagnosis, and Treatment of Gestational Trophoblastic Disease and ACOG Practice Bulletin 193, Tubal Ectopic Pregnancy , respectively, for additional guidance. Although false-positive and elevated hCG levels can occur in male populations, this document focuses primarily on individuals assigned female at birth. 
Definition 
Positive hCG 
Human chorionic gonadotropin is a heterodimeric glycoprotein produced by trophoblastic tissue and is comprised of two subunits: alpha and beta. The alpha unit is nearly identical to the alpha subunits of pituitary luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and thyroid-stimulating hormone. The beta subunit of hCG is highly homologous with the beta subunit of LH, but it is distinguished by a unique C-terminal peptide. 
Although often referred to generically, multiple hCG forms exist, with similar but not identical structures. Two forms (hCG and hyperglycosylated hCG) play a critical role in pregnancy, and another form (sulfated hCG) is produced by the pituitary gland 1 . Hyperglycosylated hCG is produced in pregnancy by placental trophoblasts and is associated with implantation. 
Most commercial serum and urine hCG assays are enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, or “sandwich assays.” Two antibodies, a monoclonal antibody targeted to a specific region of the beta unit of hCG and an antibody that is labeled with a tracer, bind and trap the hCG molecule. These assays can provide a qualitative (positive or negative) or quantitative result Figure 1 .

Both urine and serum hCG tests can be qualitative; however, serum hCG tests can more specifically quantitate hCG levels. Qualitative tests are used clinically to determine the presence or absence of pregnancy, often with a rapid diagnosis; quantitative tests are used in evaluation and management of early pregnancy as well as in determining the need and the success of treatment for malignancies. 
Generally, the reference range cutoff for serum hCG is less than 5 international units/L in premenopausal nonpregnant individuals. There may be variation in laboratory reporting, with some facilities including chronologic age reference cutoffs 2 . The prevalence of positive hCG results using a cutoff of more than 5 international units/L in postmenopausal individuals aged 55 years or older is as high as 8% 3 , because pituitary hCG production increases with low levels of estradiol. As a result, the hCG cutoff for that age group has been recommended to be 14 international units/L. Therefore, hCG levels should be interpreted with FSH levels in perimenopausal and menopausal individuals 4 . 
Urine pregnancy tests may detect a serum hCG level greater than 25 international units/L, although some variability may exist depending on urine concentration and specific gravity. Clinicians should check the specific requirements of the laboratory test ordered. 
False-Positive hCG 
The term “false-positive hCG” is sometimes used to describe an hCG level that is detectable outside of pregnancy, gestational trophoblastic disease, or malignant germ cell tumor. In fact, the detection of hCG is often not false but rather is explained by an alternative diagnosis. An hCG result that is positive due to assay interference, such as heterophilic antibodies, in the absence of circulating hCG is estimated to occur in 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000 serum tests 5 . Interference due to cross-reactive antibodies also has been referred to as “phantom hCG” 6 . For the purposes of this document, “false-positive” hCG will refer only to results caused by assay interference. 
True-Positive hCG 
In this document, true-positive hCG will refer to other causes of elevated hCG that do not occur as a result of assay interference. The presence of hCG as a tumor marker in gestational trophoblastic disease and malignant germ cell tumors is well recognized. Outside these gynecologic settings, an elevated hCG level has been described in other clinical conditions, including nongynecologic malignancies, pituitary source, and familial hCG syndrome. 
Methods 
This Clinical Consensus document was developed using an a priori protocol in conjunction with the authors listed above. The a priori protocol was modeled after the Clinical Consensus methodology; a full description of the Clinical Consensus methodology has been published separately (https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-consensus/articles/2021/09/clinical-consensus-methodology) . The description below is specific to this Clinical Consensus document. 
Literature Search 
A literature search was performed from 2000 to February 2024 for clinical questions as noted in the outline. American College of Obstetricians & Gynecologists’ medical librarians searched Medline through Ovid and PubMed for human-only studies written in English. MeSH terms and keywords can be found in Appendix 1 (available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E455 ). Search terms for racial and ethnic disparities in management of positive hCG test results in nonpregnant patients were incorporated into the literature review, and recommendations were drafted with the intent to promote health equity and reduce these disparities. A bridge literature search was completed in March 2025. Any updated literature was incorporated into the text and recommendations, as appropriate. 
Study Selection 
Qualifying studies passed both title and abstract screen and full-text screen and met the following inclusion criteria: conducted in countries ranked very high on the United Nations Human Development Index 7 , included female participants, and included all study designs. Studies that passed full-text screen by the authors were included in a summary evidence map (Appendix 2, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E456 ). 
Consensus Voting and Recommendation Development 
At a meeting of the Committee on Clinical Consensus—Gynecology, a quorum of two-thirds of eligible voting members was met, and the committee held a formal vote for each proposed recommendation. All recommendation statements met or exceeded the 75% approval threshold required for consensus. 
Summary of Consensus Recommendations 
Health care professionals should have an understanding of the various etiologies for elevated hCG levels in nonpregnant individuals without gynecologic malignancy. 
Health care professionals should follow a systematic approach to retesting and evaluating test results that considers patient history, outcomes from previous tests and assays, and patient risk for other underlying causes of a positive hCG test result. 
Health care professionals should be aware of alternative retesting methods to identify heterophilic antibodies that would result in false-positive hCG test results. 
hCG, human chorionic gonadotropin. 
Causes of Persistently Elevated hCG 
Health care professionals should have an understanding of the various etiologies for elevated hCG levels in nonpregnant individuals without gynecologic malignancy . 
Human chorionic gonadotropin testing is well known for its use as a marker for gynecologic malignancy, including gestational trophoblastic neoplasia. Persistent low levels of serum hCG also have been reported in patients with quiescent gestational trophoblastic disease 8 . The use of hCG in this setting is outside the scope of this document. 
Persistently elevated hCG levels in nonpregnant individuals without gynecologic malignancy can result from a variety of etiologies. Several of these causes are listed in Box 1 .

Heterophilic Antibodies 
As described previously, the majority of commercial assays used to detect hCG use monoclonal antibodies in a sandwich assay. Some individuals have circulating factors in their serum that interact with the hCG antibody. The most common are heterophilic antibodies 5 . These are human antibodies directed against animal-derived antigens used in immunoassays. Multiple studies have reported the presence of serum antibodies that can produce a positive serum assay 9 10 . These antibodies often bind animal proteins and may occur more often in patients who have frequent contact with goats, rabbits, and mice. The antigens used in commercial assays are often animal-generated, and interference from the anti-animal antibodies can cause false-positive test results. 
Pituitary hCG 
Another potential cause of elevated hCG levels, particularly in postmenopausal and perimenopausal patients, is hCG produced by the pituitary 1 11 . Decreased production of ovarian estrogen in perimenopausal and menopausal patients results in decreased negative feedback on gonadotropin-releasing hormone. Elevated levels of hCG produced by the pituitary are seen in conjunction with elevated FSH and LH levels. These increased levels may occur due to the proximity of the beta subunit of hCG to the beta subunit of LH of chromosome 19 and increased gonadotropin-releasing hormone stimulation 1 6 . 
Genitourinary Disease 
Patients with renal failure have been reported to have elevated levels of serum hCG due to failure of the kidneys to filter the protein into urine 12 13 . The levels seen are typically less than 100 international units/L 1 and often less than 25 international units/L 12 . In perimenopausal and postmenopausal patients with renal failure, pituitary hCG and renal failure together may produce a greater likelihood of persistent low levels of hCG on serum assays. 
Patients with prior bladder reconstruction using bowel, or enterocystoplasty, also may have elevated urine hCG levels, although the mechanism is not well understood 14 . 
Familial hCG Syndrome 
Familial hCG syndrome is a rare genetic defect that affects both women and men 15 at an estimated incidence of 1 in 60,000 families 6 . In this syndrome, affected patients produce hCG with absent or modified C-terminal peptide, leading to elevated hCG levels in some assay results 6 . 
It is important that health care professionals consider the different causes of elevated hCG outside of pregnancy and gynecologic malignancy when evaluating patients with persistently elevated hCG test results. Appropriate retesting to rule out various etiologies and to identify the main cause of persistently elevated hCG is necessary to avoid misdiagnosis or mismanagement of elevated hCG levels. 
Management of Persistently Elevated hCG Levels 
Health care professionals should follow a systematic approach to retesting and evaluating test results that considers patient history, outcomes from previous tests and assays, and patient risk for other underlying causes of a positive hCG test result . 
Due to the multiple potential etiologies of persistently elevated hCG levels, health care professionals should evaluate test results according to a systematic framework. When serum hCG test results are elevated, the first step is to evaluate for pregnancy (both intrauterine and ectopic). Depending on the hCG level as well as the individual’s obstetric and gynecologic history, gestational trophoblastic disease would be next in the differential diagnosis. Molar pregnancy and trophoblastic malignancy may be associated with hCG levels greater than 100,000 milli-international units/mL. For lower-level hCG elevations, pelvic imaging and serial hCG testing are appropriate steps to assess for retained products of conception, ectopic pregnancy, persistent gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, and malignancy. Heterophilic Antibodies 
Health care professionals should be aware of alternative retesting methods to identify heterophilic antibodies that would result in false-positive hCG test results . 
Because different immunoassays use different antibodies, a different hCG assay may not yield a false-positive test result and therefore may provide a more accurate assessment. Direct communication and consultation with the clinical laboratory and laboratory medicine specialists are important to discuss alternative assays and options for complex situations. Because the interfering antibodies are too large to be filtered into the urine, paired testing with urine hCG should be performed for results with a serum hCG higher than 50 milli-international units/mL. A negative urine hCG in the setting of a serum hCG higher than 50 milli-international units/mL is diagnostic of heterophilic antibodies 16 . 
Serial dilutions of the serum also can be performed to rule out heterophilic antibody interference. In the setting of a true-positive hCG test result, the hCG concentration should fall approximately linearly based on the dilution. Lack of linearity confirms assay interference. Additionally, some laboratories can preabsorb serum to remove heterophilic antibodies before performing the assay. If the result becomes negative after removal of the heterophilic antibody, interference can be confirmed. 
It is important to note that not all laboratories can perform the above-mentioned ancillary testing. Discussions with laboratory directors or academic centers may be helpful in obtaining necessary testing or determining whether referral to a center with more detailed testing capability is warranted. 
Pituitary hCG 
In postmenopausal patients with elevated FSH levels and stable low levels of hCG (less than 14 international units/L), it is most likely that the hCG detected is produced by the pituitary 17 . If FSH is in a menopausal range (40 international units/L or higher), no further testing is required 17 . However, if there is concern about the clinical picture, a trial of an estrogen-containing hormone pill may confirm the diagnosis by restoring the negative feedback on the hypothalamus, with resultant suppression of pituitary hormone production. Caution should be exercised in patients with contraindications to oral contraceptive pills. Dosing and duration of this method have not been well studied; however, repeat hCG testing after 1 month can be considered. Protection of the endometrium with progesterone is recommended for patients with a uterus on prolonged systemic estrogen therapy. 
Familial hCG Syndrome 
A thorough family history may help elicit this diagnosis. Most hCG immunoassays are unable to detect hCG without the normal C-terminal peptide region; as such, repeating the hCG test using a different assay may help identify this cause for an elevated hCG level. Despite being a rare condition, health care professionals still should consider the possibility of familial hCG syndrome in the absence of intrauterine and ectopic pregnancy and gestational trophoblastic disease. 
Exogenous hCG 
In clinical settings, exogenous hCG has been administered to facilitate ovulation during fertility treatment. However, exogenous hCG also has been used in nonclinical settings to induce weight loss and to stimulate endogenous androgen production in athletes 6 . Regardless of how the exogenous hCG is administered, levels should decrease within 24–48 hours after administration, provided no subsequent doses have been taken 6 . 
Nongynecologic Malignancy 
Human chorionic gonadotropin levels can be elevated in multiple tumor types as a paraneoplastic syndrome. Elevated hCG has been described in many tumor types, including but not limited to breast, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, lung, squamous cell cancers, and renal tumors 18 . In situations in which hCG levels continue to rise and all other differentials have been ruled out, clinicians should consider appropriate imaging or consult with their oncology teams to evaluate for nontrophoblastic causes of elevated serum hCG levels. 
Patient Notification and Follow-Up 
Patients with evidence of hCG assay interference should be notified that they are at risk for recurrent false-positive hCG assay results. These patients should be instructed to inform all future health care practitioners of this issue, and the information should be documented in the patient's medical record.
