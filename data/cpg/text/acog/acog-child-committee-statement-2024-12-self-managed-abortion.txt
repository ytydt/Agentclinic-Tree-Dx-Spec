This Committee Statement was developed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' Committee on Advancing Equity in Obstetric and Gynecologic Health Care in collaboration with Nisha Verma, MD, MPH, Diane Horvath, MD, MPH, and Keith Reisinger-Kindle, DO, MPH, MS; with consultation from Ghazaleh Moayedi, DO, MPH, and Kristyn Brandi, MD, MPH. 
ABSTRACT: Self-managed abortion (SMA) refers to actions people take to end a pregnancy outside the formal health care system. There are a variety of reasons people choose to self-manage their abortions, and these reasons may vary based on regional contexts. For some people, medically delivered abortion care is no longer, or has never been, available in their community. Available options might be inaccessible or unacceptable, or the person might have a preference for self-managed care as a primary choice. The majority of SMAs are completed safely with misoprostol, either alone or with mifepristone. Rare medical complications should be managed as they would be in any case of spontaneous pregnancy loss. For many people, the greatest risk of harm related to SMA comes from the threat of criminalization. Many U.S. states have at least one law in place that could be misused to prosecute people attempting or assisting with SMA. Criminalization makes people less safe and harms the confidential patient–practitioner relationship. Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should provide all people with compassionate, nonjudgmental medical care, including those presenting before, during, or after self-managing an abortion. 
Recommendations and Conclusions 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should educate themselves on self-managed abortion (SMA), including its history in the United States and globally, the reasons people may use SMA and the methods that can be involved, the safety of SMA, and the ways the medical field can better support patients who need and choose abortion care. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should apply a harm-reduction approach to reduce potential negative consequences associated with SMA while creating an open and nonjudgmental patient–clinician relationship. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should recognize that the greatest risk of harm related to SMA often is the harm resulting from potential criminalization. 
The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) strongly opposes the prosecution of pregnant people for conduct alleged to have harmed the fetus, including the criminalization of SMA. 
The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists strongly opposes legislation requiring obstetrician–gynecologists to report pregnancy outcomes to law enforcement or other governmental agencies. Obstetrician-gynecologists should not report pregnancy outcomes unless legally compelled to do so and should seek confirmation that such reporting is legally required before any reporting occurs under employer policies or procedures. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should provide all people with compassionate, nonjudgmental medical care, including those presenting before, during, or after self-managing an abortion. Health care professionals should be aware that, if a patient voluntarily discloses an SMA, documenting and reporting of the information can cause harm to the patient as well as the health care professionals involved in the patient's care. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals have an obligation to both understand the legal environment in which they practice and how laws in various states affect care and to protect patient autonomy and confidentiality and the integrity of the patient-clinician relationship. Professional and legal advocacy organizations should work to make resources available to health care professionals to help them navigate these complexities. 
Background 
Self-managed abortion (SMA) refers to actions people take to end a pregnancy outside the formal health care system. Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals may encounter patients who have attempted or completed SMA using misoprostol with or without mifepristone, other medications, herbs (eg, aloe, rue, sage, black and blue cohosh, savin, myrrh, mugwort, ergot, parsley, pait), vitamins, supplements, or uterine extraction. People may use over-the-counter medications such as pain relievers, drugs, alcohol, toxic substances, and physical methods (ie, physical trauma) 1 2 3 . Currently, physical methods, such as the introduction of instruments into the uterus, are less commonly used. One cross-sectional survey study of 7,022 individuals found that, among those who reported attempting SMA, 20% used misoprostol, 29% used another medication or drug, 38% used herbs, and 20% used physical methods 1 . Another prospective cohort study found that herbs, supplements, and vitamins (52% of respondents) were the most common method used by those who reported an SMA attempt 3 . In that study, 18% of the participants reported using mifepristone or misoprostol or both. Abortion medications can be obtained in a variety of ways, including through international nongovernmental organizations, online pharmacies, and international retail pharmacies near border communities. 
History and Prevalence of Self-Managed Abortion Globally and in the United States 
Although this is the first ACOG publication describing SMA, it is important to understand that SMA is a long-standing practice in many communities in the United States and globally 4 5 . Historical evidence from around the world shows that people have used a variety of pharmacologic and mechanical methods to attempt to end their pregnancies 4 5 . Although it can be challenging to know the current prevalence of SMA, a 2020 study estimated that 7% of women in the United States will attempt to self-manage an abortion during their lifetime 1 . Another study examined the number of requests for SMA medication to the online nonprofit group Aid Access before and after the 
Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision and found that the most dramatic increases in requests came from states that moved quickly to ban abortion 6 . 
Effects of the Pre-Roe History of Self-Managed Abortion on Current Biases and Assumptions 
Although SMA is not new, it is important to note that it often looks different in the United States now compared with the time before the legalization of abortion in 1973. Before the legalization of abortion in the United States, community organizations and individuals existed to help provide safe abortion care outside the health care system. However, people also commonly turned to less safe or invasive methods of self-managing abortion, and some relied on unskilled or untrained providers of abortion. Some people were afraid to seek care when complications arose. These factors contributed to high levels of morbidity and mortality from SMA, leading to the widespread use of phrases such as “coat-hanger” and “back-alley” abortion 7 . Since that time, the availability of medications, particularly misoprostol, has improved the safety of SMA notably in places with limited access to safe, facility-based abortion care 8 . 
There are a variety of reasons people choose to self-manage their abortions, and these reasons may vary based on regional contexts Box 1 . For example, for some people, medically delivered abortion care is no longer, or has never been, available in their community. Available options might be inaccessible or unacceptable 9 , or the person might have a preference for self-managed care as a primary choice 10 .

Recommendations and Conclusions 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should educate themselves on SMA, including its history in the United States and globally, the reasons people may use SMA and the methods that can be involved, the safety of SMA, and the ways the medical field can better support patients who need and choose abortion care . 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals may encounter patients who have attempted or completed SMA using misoprostol with or without mifepristone, other medications, herbs, vitamins, supplements, or uterine extraction. People may use over-the-counter medications such as pain relievers, drugs, alcohol, toxic substances, and physical methods (ie, physical trauma) 1 2 3 . It is essential that health care professionals understand why individuals may choose to self-manage their abortions and what they can do to support patients they may encounter. See 
Table 1 for resources for both health care professionals and patients. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should apply a harm-reduction approach to reduce potential negative consequences associated with SMA while creating an open and nonjudgmental patient–clinician relationship. 
Health care professionals from many different practice settings, including obstetrician–gynecologists and primary care and emergency medicine professionals, likely will interact with people who may have self-managed their abortions. Data from multiple settings in which abortion access is limited demonstrate that health care professionals can use a harm-reduction approach to improve patient outcomes and decrease morbidity and mortality from less safe abortion methods 11 12 13 14 15 16 17 18 . A harm-reduction approach aims to reduce the negative consequences of a behavior when complete elimination of that behavior is not a realistic goal 11 . Importantly, this approach aims to center patient autonomy by focusing on reducing potential negative consequences associated with a behavior instead of pretending that the behavior does not occur at all. 
A harm-reduction approach has been used successfully in many parts of the world to reduce maternal morbidity and mortality related to SMA in places where safe, legal abortion was not accessible. For example, the nongovernmental organization Inciativas Sanitarias in Uruguay created a program that could support people planning to have an abortion outside of the formal health care system when abortion was criminalized in the country. Through this program, health care professionals talked with people who could not access abortion in the formal health care system about other options, including SMA. They also discussed the risks of SMA related to the patient's weeks of gestation and abortion method and talked to patients about the World Health Organization–recommended regimen for safe and effective termination of pregnancy in the first trimester with misoprostol. Health care professionals made sure to provide people with support after the abortion and to assist with any concerns that came up. Data demonstrate that this harm-reduction program in Uruguay was associated with reduced abortion-related maternal mortality in the country, with a decrease from 37.5% to 8.1% over the course of a decade 12 18 19 . 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should recognize that the greatest risk of harm related to SMA often is the harm resulting from potential criminalization. 
In the United States, for many people, the greatest risk of harm related to SMA comes from the threat of criminalization. At the time of this publication, many U.S. states have at least one law in place that could be misused to prosecute people attempting or assisting with SMA 20 21 . Among these policies are legislation criminalizing harm to the fetus, criminalizing performing an abortion without a medical license, and criminalizing abortion entirely; child endangerment laws; and tissue-disposal regulations 21 . One investigation identified 61 cases of individuals who faced criminal investigation or arrest between 2000 and 2020 for allegedly self-managing or assisting with SMA 21 . More than 50% of these individuals were prosecuted under legislation not directly related to abortion. Many of the individuals who have been targeted by criminalization for SMA came into contact with law enforcement after interactions with health care professionals. According to this study, Black, Hispanic, and Asian individuals were disproportionately reported for SMA 21 . 
The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists strongly opposes the prosecution of pregnant people for conduct alleged to have harmed the fetus, including the criminalization of SMA. 
As detailed in ACOG's Statement of Policy 22 , criminalization of pregnant people for actions allegedly aimed at harming their fetus poses serious threats to people's health and the health system itself. Threatening patients with criminal punishment destroys trust in the medical system, which in turn leads people to be less likely to seek needed help. Criminalization makes people less safe and harms the confidential patient–practitioner relationship. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists is committed to the elimination of policies or practices that criminalize people during pregnancy and the postpartum period for conduct alleged to be harmful to a pregnancy. 
The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists strongly opposes legislation requiring obstetrician–gynecologists to report pregnancy outcomes to law enforcement or other governmental agencies. Obstetrician-gynecologists should not report pregnancy outcomes unless legally compelled to do so and should seek confirmation that such reporting is legally required before any reporting occurs under employer policies or procedures. 
It is important for health care professionals to understand that reporting suspicions of SMA can cause harm; breach a patient's rights to privacy, constituting Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) violations; and deter people from seeking care that they need. Applying a harm-reduction approach to SMA includes talking to patients about potential risks and how to minimize those risks, as well as educating oneself and fellow health care professionals on how to protect patients from potential criminalization. It is essential for health care professionals to understand the laws in their states, because many laws are not based on current medical evidence or best practices. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should engage with local-, state-, and federal-level legislative advocacy against any mandated reporting for pregnancy-related outcomes, such as SMA. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals should provide all people with compassionate, nonjudgmental medical care, including those presenting before, during, or after self-managing an abortion. Health care professionals should be aware that, if a patient voluntarily discloses an SMA, documenting and reporting of the information can cause harm to the patient as well as the 
health care professionals involved in the patient's care. 
Currently, available data do not indicate how many patients interact with the formal health care system before, during, or after self-managing their abortion. Likely, patients may ask questions about SMA if they engage with the health care system. Pregnancy-options counseling should not only be patient-centered and reinforce trust and patient autonomy, but also should include an acknowledgement of the existence of SMA as a part of the spectrum of abortion-related options, including that SMA using standard medication abortion regimens is incredibly safe and effective. Patients should be encouraged to reach out for help from the medical community if they experience adverse effects or complications from an SMA or if they have questions or concerns about the process. Health care professionals should advocate within their own health systems and communities for the protection of patient's privacy, regardless of their circumstances. This includes advocating among policymakers, other health care professionals, and clinical and administrative staff. 
There are several scenarios in which people might present to a health care professional for supportive care for SMA. It is important to note that the approach to caring for someone who is self-managing an abortion is the same as the management of spontaneous pregnancy loss. Some patients may present for care related to their pregnancy before SMA. This could be either with the intention of self-managing an abortion or to get routine information about SMA as part of pregnancy-options counseling. Patients also may present after SMA for care, both to evaluate for successful termination of pregnancy and with concerns about symptoms. Patients should be able to access routine pregnancy care, including ultrasonography as well as Rh status testing and Rhogam, as indicated. 
Some individuals may enter the health care system during an SMA because of adverse effects or complications. Regardless of their presenting symptoms, patients should be treated as any patient presenting for care for spontaneous pregnancy loss would be treated. Questions and evaluation should be based on the patient’s presenting symptoms in the context of a recent pregnancy but do not need to delve into the circumstances that lead them to seek care. For example, a patient may present with signs and symptoms of an early pregnancy loss, and it is unclear whether the process has been completed. The patient should undergo routine evaluation to make sure that the individual is clinically stable and that the process has completed. There is not a clinical need for the health care professional to probe further to ask whether this is the result of an SMA. Furthermore, health care professionals should be aware that, if a patient voluntarily discloses an SMA, documenting and reporting the information can cause harm to the patient as well as the health care professionals involved in the patient's care. Many cases in which patients have faced criminalization due to SMA are because health care professionals reported their patients 21 . Without any obligation to do so, this is a violation of the patient–health care professional relationship. 
Health care professionals should consider how the information they request and subsequently document may be putting their patients and themselves at risk of criminalization. It is recommended to document only information that is necessary for the ongoing care of a patient. Health care professionals should consider whether it is imperative to document items such as the last menstrual period, which later could be used to corroborate a pregnancy. Other information, such as ultrasonographic findings, also could be used in attempts at criminalization. Therefore, health care professionals should be thoughtful in determining what tests are indicated in their evaluation of patients presenting for care. Tests that may be part of a “routine” evaluation may not actually be necessary and could prove harmful; thus, it is important to approach an evaluation with the risk of criminalization in mind. Overall, health care professionals should be intentional about the information they seek from patients and whether that information is clinically necessary. They should not seek or document information that may put a patient at legal risk while not being clinically relevant to the care plan. 
Health care professionals should consider that some patients may choose to undergo SMA by less safe methods, such as self-instrumentation or trauma. These patients can present with signs of uterine perforation and sepsis. Others may present with signs of overdose of certain herbal medications if used improperly. As with any other patient presenting for emergency care, patients who may have started or completed an SMA should receive prompt and compassionate care. It is usually possible to ask about potential exposure to toxic substances or uterine trauma without delving further into the circumstances that led to their presentation. Resources such as Poison Control can be used if there are concerns about rare but potential serious toxicity from substances, and uterine trauma should be treated using standard medical or surgical responses. Health care professionals should be thoughtful about what information they need in order to provide appropriate care to the patient without increasing the patient's risk of criminalization. Health care professionals may consider the following approach to patient care in this setting: 1) be aware of the presence or absence of reporting requirements based on institutional policies and federal, state, and local laws; 2) if practicing in a place where there are restrictions, preface any conversation with a patient by informing them of relevant restrictions; and 3) inform patients that, regardless of the legality of the patient's actions, the health care professional is committed to providing the patient with safe care or referring the patient if the health care professional is unable to provide that care. 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals have an obligation to both understand the legal environment in which they practice and how laws in various states affect care and to protect patient autonomy and confidentiality and the integrity of the patient-clinician relationship. Professional and legal advocacy organizations should work to make resources available to health care professionals to help them navigate these complexities . 
Health care professionals also should be cognizant of their own potential legal risks when discussing SMA with patients and should consider seeking legal advice to help make appropriate risk assessments specific to their regions and practice environments. Although information sharing is legal in most circumstances, offering medical advice or directing patients to specific places where they can access medications for SMA could expose health care professionals to medical–legal liability 23 . 
Conclusion 
Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals are likely to encounter patients who have managed or attempted to manage their own abortions, particularly as more jurisdictions move to ban or restrict abortion. The majority of SMAs are completed safely with misoprostol, either alone or with mifepristone. Rare medical complications should be managed as they would be in any case of spontaneous pregnancy loss. The greatest risk to patients is from criminalization, the risk of which increases on contact with the formal medical system. Obstetrician–gynecologists and other health care professionals have an obligation to provide compassionate care that does not expose patients to additional criminalization risk.
