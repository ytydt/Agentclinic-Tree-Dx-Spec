This Practice Advisory was developed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists. The Society of Family Planning endorses this Practice Advisory. 
Current American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guidance addressing prelabor rupture of membranes (PROM) is available in Practice Bulletin No. 217, Prelabor Rupture of Membranes   1 , and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine Consult Series #71: Management of previable and periviable preterm prelabor rupture of membranes   2 , which ACOG endorsed* in September 2024. 
In Practice Bulletin 217, the risks and management considerations for patients with preterm PROM before viability are addressed. Previable and periviable PROM are associated with a significant risk of maternal complications, including intraamniotic infection, endometritis, abruptio placentae, and retained placenta  1 3 . Prior studies referenced in this Practice Bulletin noted a 14% risk of significant maternal morbidity, including life-threatening infection in approximately 1–5% of cases, complicating expectant management  3 4 5 . Maternal mortality due to infection in this setting has also been reported  1 2 . In Practice Bulletin 217, it is currently recommended that patients with previable and periviable PROM be counseled regarding the risks and benefits of both expectant management and immediate delivery. Both options should be offered. Immediate delivery may involve termination of the pregnancy through induction of labor or by dilation and evacuation. 
More recently published data, summarized in SMFM Consult #71, demonstrate a higher rate of maternal morbidity associated with expectant management than that previously cited. In one study from Texas  6 , patients were included if they presented at less than 22 weeks of gestation, without preterm labor, and had a medical indication for delivery (preterm premature rupture of membranes, preeclampsia with severe features, and/or vaginal bleeding); preterm premature rupture of membranes was the most common presentation (93% of included patients). All patients were expectantly managed based on state laws. Maternal morbidity, including clinical chorioamnionitis and hemorrhage, occurred in 43% of patients, and 32% of these patients required intensive care unit admission, dilation and curettage, or readmission. The rate of composite maternal morbidity was 57%. 
A separate study considered patients with PROM between 14 0/7 and 23 6/7 weeks of gestation and compared outcomes in patients who underwent expectant management versus termination of the pregnancy with induction of labor or dilation and curettage/evacuation 7 . In this study, patients undergoing expectant management had significantly higher rates of chorioamnionitis and postpartum hemorrhage, and although not reaching statistical significance, higher rates of sepsis (4.6% versus 1%). The rates of composite maternal morbidity (60.2% versus 33%) and severe maternal morbidity (12% versus 5%) were also significantly higher in the expectantly managed group.  
It is critically important that patients are counseled on the higher rates of morbidity associated with expectant management, which may be greater than previously reported, to ensure they can make a fully informed decision regarding their care. Although current recommendations support offering both expectant management and immediate delivery—including termination of pregnancy—after counseling, patients need to be informed of the greater risks associated with expectant management.  
Clinicians must be supported in using patient-centered shared decision-making approaches when caring for patients with medical complications. It is essential for clinicians to be able to recognize signs of deterioration early and act promptly to prevent acute decompensation. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists asserts that physicians and other health care professionals must be able to intervene and, in cases of previable and periviable preterm PROM, provide abortion care before the patient becomes critically ill.
