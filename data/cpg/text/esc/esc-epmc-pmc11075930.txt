Abstract 
The sex disparity in outcomes of patients with cardiovascular disease is well-described and has persisted across recent decades. While there have been several proposed mechanisms to explain this disparity, there are limited data on female patient–physician sex concordance and its association with outcomes. The authors review the existing literature on the relationship between patient–physician sex concordance and clinical outcomes in patients with cardiovascular disease, the evidence of a benefit in clinical outcomes with female patient–physician sex concordance, and the possible drivers of such a benefit and highlight directions for future study. 
Keywords: Women’s health, Cardiovascular disease, Sex disparity, Patient–physician sex concordance, Sex concordance, Clinical outcomes

Introduction 
Female patients with cardiovascular disease have well-described poorer outcomes in many aspects compared with male patients, a disparity that has remained relatively unchanged over time despite advancements in medical and invasive management. 1 , 2 The aetiology of this sex disparity is likely multifactorial. Differences in baseline anatomic, biologic, and functional characteristics, 1–3 in timing and acuity of clinical presentation, 4 and in treatments 5 , 6 have been proposed as possible explanatory mechanisms, among others. Yet, there has been relatively little study on what role, if any, physician characteristics may play. Emerging data suggest an association between patient–physician sex concordance, risk factor management, and clinical outcomes in female patients. In this systematic review, we summarize the evidence on an association between clinical outcomes and female patient–physician sex concordance, describe possible drivers of this association, and delineate future directions for further study. To inform the review, a systematic review of the literature was performed by a medical librarian (see online supplementary material ). 
Current evidence on patient–physician sex concordance in cardiovascular disease 
Medical therapy 
The majority of the existing literature on medical management of cardiovascular disease and its risk factors suggests a benefit with female patient–physician sex concordance. In a cross-sectional study of Medical Expenditure Panel Survey data 7 including 9686 patient–primary care physician (PCP) sex-concordant pairs (53% female patient–physician pairs), overweight female patients seeing female PCPs were more likely to receive exercise and lifestyle modification advice for weight loss than female patients seeing male PCPs [odds ratio (OR) 1.44, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.10–1.89]. Notably, overweight male patients were also more likely to receive weight loss advice from female physicians than from male physicians (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.04–1.69), perhaps reflecting a greater emphasis on preventive primary care in female physicians than in their male counterparts. 8 , 9 A 2009 study evaluated the relationship between patient–physician sex concordance and cardiovascular disease risk factor management in 157 458 adult diabetic patients (48% female) and 1750 PCPs (43% female) and found that female patient–female PCP pairs had the highest adjusted rates of haemoglobin A1c control (70%), defined as haemoglobin A1c < 8.0%, compared with all other patient–PCP dyads (female PCP–male patient, 66%; male PCP–male patient, 66%; male PCP–female patient, 68%; P < .05 for all comparisons). Female patients of female PCPs were slightly more likely to receive appropriate treatment intensification for the management of hypertension [intensification for systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130: 58% of female patients] and hyperlipidaemia (intensification for LDL ≥ 100: 45%) than female patients of male PCPs (intensification for SBP ≥ 130, 55%; intensification for LDL ≥ 100, 43%). 10 A cross-sectional study of 4152 patients with diabetes found that on multivariable linear mixed regression modelling, female patient–physician sex concordance was independently associated with lower haemoglobin A1c (−0.84, 95% CI −1.01 to −0.63; P < .001; reference: male patient–male physician). 11 The evidence of improved outcomes with female patient–physician sex concordance among patients with cardiovascular disease has not only been shown in the outpatient or longitudinal setting but also in the acute care setting. A 2018 analysis of 581 845 patients (10.7% female) admitted to Florida (USA) hospitals with acute myocardial infarction from 1991 to 2010 12 found that patient–emergency department physician sex discordance was particularly harmful to female patients. The baseline mortality rate was 11.9%, and the coefficient of patient–physician sex concordance reduced the probability of death by 5.4% from baseline in both sexes. Yet, after matching each female patient to a male patient treated by the same emergency department physician in the same emergency department in the same year, female patients treated by male emergency department physicians were the least likely of any patient–physician sex dyad to survive an episode of care: female patients with male emergency department physicians had 1.52% (standard error ± 0.43%) lower probability of survival, representing an approximate 12% decline from the baseline mortality rate of 11.9%, while male patients with male physicians had only a 0.38% lower probability of survival, and female patients with female physicians had a 0.27% greater probability of survival, all compared with male patients with female emergency department physicians ( P < .01). Interventional cardiology and surgery 
The evidence on the effect of patient–operator sex concordance in the invasive management of cardiovascular disease is limited by the paucity of available data. In the only study evaluating patient–operator sex concordance in percutaneous coronary intervention (the Does Interventionalists’ Sex impact Coronary Outcomes [DISCO] study), Yelavarthy et al. 13 evaluated the outcomes of percutaneous coronary intervention performed by 18 female (6362 cases) and 385 male operators (233 058 cases) from 2010 to 2017 at 48 Michigan hospitals. There was no difference in risk-adjusted hospital mortality between female patients treated by female operators and female patients treated by male operators (1.54% vs. 1.59%; P = .48). The same was true for acute kidney injury (3.55% vs. 3.42%; P = .98), transfusion requirement (2.76% vs. 3.06%; P = .22), and major bleeding (0.97% vs. 1.07%; P = .89). 13 Wallis et al. 14 conducted the only large-scale analysis of patient–surgeon sex concordance. In a population-based, retrospective cohort study including 1 320 108 adult patients who underwent 1 of 21 common elective or emergent surgical procedures from 2007 to 2019 in Ontario (Canada), 38.6% of patients were male patients with male surgeons, 50.5% were female patients with male surgeons, 3.8% were male patients with female surgeons, and 7.0% were female patients with female surgeons. Patient–surgeon sex discordance was associated with a significantly higher risk of the primary outcome of the composite of mortality, readmission, or procedure-specific complication (adjusted OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.04–1.09), mortality (adjusted OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.13), and complications (adjusted OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.07–1.11) when compared with patient–surgeon sex concordance. Patient sex was a significant effect modifier ( P for interaction = .004), with worse outcomes for female patients treated by male surgeons (adjusted OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.10–1.20) but not for male patients treated by female surgeons (adjusted OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.95–1.03), again suggesting that patient–surgeon sex discordance may only be detrimental to female patients ( Table 1 ). On pre-specified subgroup analysis, patient–surgeon sex discordance in cardiothoracic surgery was associated with a higher risk of the composite of death, readmission, and complications (adjusted OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.06–1.13). Female patient–surgeon sex concordance in cardiothoracic surgery was associated with a non-significant trend towards better outcomes (adjusted OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.74–0.80; P = .08); the lack of significance may be due to the small number of female patient–surgeon sex-concordant pairs in cardiothoracic surgery. Limitations of the available evidence include a limited ability to adjust for case mix or complexity, which may not be fully captured with traditional risk adjustment, and the loss of power in subgroup analyses due to the small number of female surgeons in some surgical subspecialties such as cardiothoracic surgery or neurosurgery.

Aetiologies of the female patient–physician sex concordance benefit 
The reasons female patient-physician sex concordance may be beneficial in cardiovascular disease are unclear. In order to understand potential mechanisms of these findings in cardiovascular disease, review of the general medical and surgical fields suggests that better overall outcomes by female physicians, an emphasis on preventive care among female physicians, better communication from female physicians, improved patient adherence in sex-concordant physician–patient pairs, or implicit physician biases may play a role. 
Clinical outcomes of female physicians 
Several studies 12 , 14–16 have found superior clinical outcomes of female physicians irrespective of patient sex. For example, a large retrospective study of Medicare beneficiaries including 1 583 028 hospitalizations and 58 344 internal medicine physicians (32.1% female) from January 2011 to December 2014 16 found that after adjustment for patient, physician, and hospital characteristics, patients treated by female physicians had significantly lower 30-day mortality (adjusted risk difference −0.43%, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.28%, number needed to treat to prevent one death: 233) and lower incidence of readmission at 30 days (adjusted risk difference −0.55%, 95% CI −0.71 to −0.39%; number needed to treat to prevent one readmission: 182) when compared with patients treated by male physicians. When patients were stratified by severity of illness, patients treated by female physicians had significantly lower mortality in all but the second-healthiest strata [fifth stratum (highest severity of illness) adjusted risk difference, female–male: −1.03%, 95% CI −1.51 to −0.56; fourth stratum adjusted risk difference: −0.62%, 95% CI −0.95 to −0.29; third stratum adjusted risk difference: −0.33%, 95% CI −0.95 to −0.29; second stratum adjusted risk difference: −0.10%; −0.27 to +0.10%; first stratum (lowest severity of illness): −0.10%, 95% CI −0.20 to −0.01], and the interaction between physician sex and expected mortality of patients was statistically significant ( P for interaction < .001). 16 These severity of illness–stratified results were derived from a subgroup analysis, but they nevertheless provide a counterpoint to the suggestion that observed better outcomes among female physicians may be due to their treatment of less complex, lower acuity patients. 16 
Greater emphasis on preventive care among female physicians 
Numerous studies have shown that female physicians place a greater emphasis on preventive care than do their male counterparts. 8 , 9 , 17–20 A study of 92 389 patient visits from the National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey, a nationally representative survey of outpatient physicians, found that female physicians were more likely than male physicians to perform preventive procedures, including Papanicolaou testing (11% vs. 4.7%; P < .001) and mammograms (9% vs. 4%, P < .01). 17 In a study of 3256 (64% female) consecutive adult patient visits to 138 family physicians (28% female) observed by research nurses, patients of female physicians were more likely to receive evidence-based preventive services, including blood pressure screening, cholesterol screening, and weight screening, counseling services including tobacco and alcohol cessation, and immunizations than patients of male physicians. 19 This focus on prevention is congruent with professional society guidelines on cardiovascular disease management, including the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on cardiovascular disease prevention, which recommend physician discussion of cardiovascular risk, risk assessment, screening, and necessary lifestyle and medical modifications with all patients. 21 
Communication skills and patient adherence 
Physician communication skills have previously been associated with higher levels of patient adherence as well as better clinical outcomes. 22 , 23 Female physicians have consistently been associated with superior communication skills compared with male physicians. 9 , 24 A meta-analysis of 26 studies (23 observational, 3 physician reports) by Roter et al. 24 found that while there were no sex differences in the amount of biomedical information given by physicians during patient visits, female physicians engaged in significantly more patient-centred communication, with female physicians exhibiting significantly more active partnership behaviours, positive talk, psychosocial counseling, psychosocial question-asking, and emotionally-focused talk. This type of open, emotionally-focused communication between the physician and patient that prioritizes connection with the individual patient is recommended by professional society guidelines. 21 While differences in communication strategy and skills have been generally demonstrated between the sexes, current evidence is mixed as to whether female physicians are more empathetic than their male counterparts. 25–27 
Patient adherence to physician recommendations has been shown to be higher in female patient–physician sex-concordant pairs. For instance, in an analysis of 22 440 patients from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey database, 28 female patients with female providers were more likely to adhere to recommendation for mammography (average adjusted marginal effect: 3.9%, 95% CI 1.6%–6.2%; P < .01) than those with male physicians. In another study of 32 041 patient–provider pairs from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey database, 29 adherence to physician recommendation for breast (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.76–0.90), cervical (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.76–0.91), and colorectal cancer (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.90) screening was lower in sex-discordant patient–physician pairs than in sex-concordant patient–physician pairs. Interestingly, the improved adherence with patient–physician sex concordance may not be related to patient preference. In numerous studies on patient preference, the majority of patients stated that skill and experience were far more important in their physician selection than sex, although when patients did express a preference, female patients preferred a sex-concordant physician more often than did male patients. 9 , 30 , 31 
Physician bias 
Male physicians treating female patients may have implicit or explicit biases that can impact clinical outcomes. 12 , 16 A 2006 cross-sectional study 32 of the medical management of 1857 chronic heart failure patients found that when the prescribing physician was male, female patients received significantly fewer guideline-directed medications (such as beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers) and received significantly lower drug dosages (despite guideline recommendations) than male patients, yet there was no sex difference in treatment with guideline-directed medications and dosages when the prescribing physician was female. These data suggest a particular detriment with female patient–male physician sex discordance that could possibly be attributed to bias in male physicians. However, a retrospective study of Canadian national database of atrial fibrillation patients including 1035 physicians (9.8% female) and 10 927 patients (41.7% female) found that male physicians underestimated the stroke risk in female patients and overestimated the stroke risk in male patients ( P for interaction between physician and patient sex = .01) and that female physicians, while appropriately estimating the stroke risk in female patients, underestimated the risk in male patients ( P for interaction between physician and patient sex = .01). 33 
Also, in the aforementioned study by Greenwood et al. 12 on acute myocardial infarction survival, the higher mortality in female patient–male physician pairs was attenuated as the number of female physicians working in the emergency department with the treating male physician increased: for every 5% increase in female physicians working in a given emergency department, female patients had a 0.4% higher probability of survival. In addition, they found that female patients treated by a male physician experienced a 0.02% increase in probability of survival for every female patient that said male physician had previously treated. 12 This suggests that transferrable knowledge from colleagues of the opposite sex and ongoing professional education on how to better diagnose and treat patients of the opposite sex might combat existing implicit bias and improve patient outcomes. The questions of how bias in physicians of both sexes influences patient care, how to best combat implicit bias among physicians, and whether sex concordance is beneficial for both female and male patients require further investigation. 
Limitations of the data on patient–physician sex concordance in cardiovascular disease 
The existing data on the benefits of female patient–physician sex concordance are potentially important but have notable limitations. Methodologically, all studies on patient–physician sex concordance are observational and thus at risk of attrition and survival biases, as well as treatment allocation bias. Culture- or country-specific differences in the care of women or in the number or role of female physicians may exist, but there are not enough data for meaningful comparisons of the effect of patient–physician sex concordance. 
The vast majority of the participants 16 in most studies is male, particularly in invasive or surgical subspecialties; 14 , 15 , 34 this is consistent with the small number of female participants in clinical trials on cardiovascular disease. 35 A recent study of seven large, contemporary cardiovascular disease clinical trials found that enrolment of female patients did not exceed 27%. 36 Limited female enrolment presents a barrier to further study on female patient sex concordance in cardiovascular disease. 
In addition, the physicians included in sex concordance studies are overwhelmingly male. 12 , 14–16 , 34 According to the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC), the proportion of female cardiothoracic surgery attendings was 16.1% in 2018. 37 Only 12.6% of cardiology attendings and fellows are female physicians, despite drawing from an internal medicine residency pool that is 50% female. 9 The limited number of female physicians in cardiovascular disease impedes study on female patient–surgeon sex concordance. For example, in a recent retrospective study on the relationship between operator characteristics and transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) outcomes including 5896 elective TAVR procedures performed by 161 operators in New York State from 2012 to 2016, 34 the authors were unable to evaluate the role of operator sex solely due to the small numbers of female operators (5/161, 3.1%). A study on liver transplant outcomes 38 found that 5-year survival in female patients treated by female surgeons was higher compared with those treated by male surgeons (81.3% vs. 68.4%), but the difference did not reach significance ( P = .36), likely due to the small number of female sex-concordant pairs: patient–surgeon sex concordance was present in just 11.5% of female patients and 85.0% of male patients. 
Finally, female leadership may lead to the prioritization of research initiatives for female patients, facilitate entry of female physicians into cardiovascular specialties, and promote education to mitigate bias in the treatment of female patients, both increasing the amount of research on female patient–physician sex concordance and, hopefully, improving outcomes among female patients. 39 Unfortunately, female attendings in cardiovascular specialties rarely advance to academic leadership roles and affect such change. 36 , 39 A 2019 analysis of the top 140 National Institutes of Health-funded institutions found that only 9% (109/1213) of academic attending cardiothoracic positions were held by female physicians. 40 , 41 Whitelaw et al. 42 found that of all heart failure clinical trials (403 randomized trials in heart failure), female physicians comprised just 15.6% (95% CI 12.2%–19.6%) of first authors and 12.9% (95% CI 9.8%–16.6%) of senior authors. 43 Another analysis of 58 cardiac surgery clinical trials found that 13.8% of studies had a female first or last author and just 1.2% of all authors were female cardiac surgeons. 44 Female leadership of cardiovascular clinical trials has been associated with greater female enrolment, 36 , 43 , 45 with one recent analysis of heart failure trials 43 finding that the proportion of female authors per trial was the only independent predictor of female patient enrolment ( Figure 2 ). Whether this is due to female authors studying issues pertaining to female patients, female researchers helping female participants overcome barriers to enrolment, or other factors, 36 it is evident that the paucity of female practitioners, researchers, and leadership in cardiovascular specialties prevents further study of female patient–physician sex concordance and its associated clinical outcomes.

Future directions 
More and higher-quality data on the relationship between clinical outcomes and patient–physician sex concordance are needed. Prospective, randomized clinical trials dedicated only to female patients/participants would provide more definitive answers regarding the benefit of sex concordance. The nascent ROMA:Women ( NCT04124120 ) trial is the first cardiac surgery trial enroling only female patients, and its leadership represents a departure from the status quo: over 75% of the trial leadership is female. Data on sex concordance between operating surgeons and patients will be collected and measured and will provide valuable data on patient–surgeon sex concordance in cardiovascular disease. 
More female cardiologists, interventional cardiologists, and cardiothoracic surgeons are needed. 39 Advocacy to facilitate the entry of female residents and fellows into these fields and to advance female attendings to leadership roles is not just the responsibility of other female physicians but of all physicians. 
Sex is just one factor for which patient–physician concordance may impact clinical outcomes. Early data on patient–physician race concordance in general adult internal medicine have suggested higher patient satisfaction with a race-concordant physician. 28 , 46 Interestingly, the existing data show poorer patient adherence 7 and no improvement in guideline-directed management 28 , 46 with patient–physician race concordance. Yet, the amount of data is limited, and the issue has often been combined with evaluation of sex concordance, 7 , 28 , 46 and therefore, it may merit further study. 
Conclusions 
There is emerging evidence of a positive relationship between patient–physician sex concordance and clinical outcomes, particularly among female patients. Equally important is the finding that patient–physician sex discordance is most detrimental to clinical outcomes in female patients. This has been shown both in the longitudinal outpatient setting and the inpatient hospital setting, as well as in primary care, medical, and surgical specialties. Drivers of a benefit of patient–physician sex concordance in female patients are unclear but may include a greater emphasis on preventive care among female physicians, better and more supportive communication from female physicians possibly leading to improved patient adherence, superior clinical outcomes among female physicians, or the absence of implicit physician bias in sex-concordant patient–physician pairs ( Graphical Abstract ). The initial data are promising, but they are limited by their retrospective design, by the small number of studies on the topic, by the dearth of female practitioners, and by the low enrolment of female patients in cardiovascular studies. While randomized trials on patient–physician sex concordance may be difficult to implement, investigating clinical outcomes with patient–physician sex concordance or whether female leadership increases female patient enrolment, increases female entry into cardiovascular subspecialties, or mitigates implicit biases may help optimize clinical outcomes in cardiovascular disease and improve patient-centred care.
