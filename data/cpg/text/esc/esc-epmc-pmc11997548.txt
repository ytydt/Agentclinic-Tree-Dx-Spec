Abstract 
Genome-wide association studies have revealed hundreds of genetic variants associated with cardiovascular diseases (CVD). Polygenic risk scores (PRS) can capture this information in a single metric and hold promise for use in CVD risk prediction. Importantly, PRS information can reflect the causally mediated risk to which the individual is exposed throughout life. Although European Society of Cardiology guidelines do not currently advocate their use in routine clinical practice, PRS are commercially available and increasingly sought by clinicians, health systems, and members of the public to inform personalized health care decision-making. This clinical consensus statement provides an overview of the scientific basis of PRS and evidence to date on their role in CVD risk prediction for the purposes of disease prevention. It provides the reader with a summary of the opportunities and challenges for implementation and identifies current gaps in supporting evidence. The document also lays out a potential roadmap by which the scientific and clinical community can navigate any future transition of PRS into routine clinical care. Finally, clinical scenarios are presented where information from PRS may hold most value and discuss organizational frameworks to enable responsible use of PRS testing while more evidence is being generated by clinical studies. 
Keywords: Genetics, Risk prediction, Polygenic risk score

Introduction 
Atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are driven by a complex interplay of behavioural, environmental, and genetic risk factors. Accurate identification of individuals at greatest risk of developing an atherosclerotic CVD event (e.g. myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death) can enhance targeted prevention opportunities, through appropriate preventive behavioural changes and pharmacologic therapies. 
Current risk prediction tools, such as SCORE2, 1 estimate the 10-year risk of a CVD event based on multiple conventional risk factors. These models are embedded in guidelines that define thresholds of absolute CVD risk to be used for shared decision-making. 2 These absolute risk thresholds are set, in part, by cost-effectiveness estimations at a population level and to prioritize those at the highest risk. 3 However, to refine individual risk estimates, clinical practice guidelines also advocate consideration of risk modifiers, comorbidities, frailty, lifetime CVD risk estimates, and expected treatment benefits from preventive medications. 2 Along with patient preferences, these refined risk estimates can be used to further guide more personalized treatment decisions. Use of risk modifiers, in particular, has gained considerable interest over the last decade to complement a 10-year CVD risk score. 2 , 4 A risk modifier can be considered to have utility if it improves upon performance measures of risk prediction (such as discrimination or reclassification), if its public health impact is clear, and if it is feasible to measure in daily practice. 2 , 4 Assessment of potential risk modifiers is most relevant if the individual’s 10-year CVD risk is close to a decision threshold or where a patient decision to follow treatment advice may be influenced. 
In this context, polygenic risk scores (PRS) have emerged as potentially valuable risk modifiers, informing on genetic predisposition to CVD beyond a recorded family history. Enthusiasm for PRS use has grown rapidly but has arguably outpaced rigorous evaluation of clinical utility. Indeed, PRS are commercially available at present and being used by some clinicians and patients without clear guidance when they may be helpful or in whom. The ‘do not use’ classification given for PRS in the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) prevention guidelines 2 has not hampered their application and uncertainty remains about their use and application. 5 
The aim of this clinical consensus statement is to: (i) provide the reader with a concise overview of PRS basics and the evidence to date for their role in CVD risk prediction; (ii) highlight research gaps and explore major current challenges to implementation that need be addressed before PRS can enter mainstream clinical use in CVD prediction; and (iii) suggest a way forwards by proposing clinical scenarios where PRS may currently offer some utility in CVD prediction and support a research framework for use in other clinical settings. 
Basics of polygenic risk scores 
The genetic basis of CVD has been the subject of research for decades, amplified by the success of the human genome project and the development of genomic technologies. Since the first breakthrough genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in 2007, 6 a significant association was found at over 300 loci for clinically manifest coronary artery disease (CAD) and at 89—partially overlapping—loci for stroke, 7–9 confirming that these diseases have a polygenic architecture with many alleles distributed widely across the genome, each with moderate or weak effects. 
Combining the effects of all of these alleles captures a large part of the genetic heritability for a given individual and can be used to build risk prediction models. A simple approach is to count the risk alleles a person carries and weight each by its GWAS effect size. A weighted sum of risk alleles gives a single number which estimates the polygenic predisposition of a given individual for a target phenotype. This single number has various names in the literature, including polygenic score, genetic risk score, or PRS. The accuracy of GWAS in predicting risk associated with individual variants has improved over time, such that the aggregated risk estimates of the PRS have likewise improved (in part for this reason the various PRS used in the literature often differ slightly; Table 1 ).

Modern PRS include millions of common genetic variants. Taking also into account the correlation structure of these markers, i.e. the information provided by genetic variants in proximity (linkage disequilibrium), their joint predictive value is being optimized by these PRS. 31 Open repositories offer standards and software tools for applying PRS. 32–34 For complex traits, recent GWAS comprising millions of individuals are now starting to fully capture the heritability explainable by common genetic variants; 35 therefore, they also provide the opportunity to estimate the near full genetic risk for individuals with genome-wide data. It has to be considered, however, that such large-scale GWAS are largely build on prevalent—rather than incident—CAD cases, which may hamper their power to predict future events. Moreover, current GWAS are typically not yet representative of global genetic diversity and have been dominated by studies with individuals of European ancestries. As expected, the corresponding PRS frequently perform maximally in European populations and less well in non-European ancestries. 36 But even within European ancestries the predictive performance may vary since many genetic markers used in PRS are only in linkage disequilibrium and thus indirect representatives of the actual causal genetic variants. 37 
Importantly, (aside from the reweighing for improvements of genetic information) a PRS is a stable measure unaffected by age or other biological perturbations and so can be calculated at any point during an individual's lifetime, including at birth. It only needs assessment once and as such overcomes many hurdles associated with other biomarkers or risk modifiers. 
Polygenic risk scores for cardiovascular disease risk prediction 
Polygenic risk score in primary prevention 
PRS have been evaluated for CAD and stroke outcomes in several large-scale prospective cohort studies ( Table 1 ). These studies typically demonstrate modest to moderate improvements in risk discrimination metrics over and above conventional risk assessment models such as Framingham 38 or SCORE2. 1 , 10 For example, a UK Biobank analysis showed that a CAD PRS of 1.7 million genetic variants had higher predictive capacity than nearly any other conventional risk factor ( Figure 1A ). 13 Another CAD PRS of 6.6 million variants identified individuals at approximately three-fold increased relative risk, similar to those carrying a familial hypercholesterolaemia mutation. 11 , 40 Various studies have shown the efficiency of targeted deployment of PRS for persons at low/intermediate CVD risk by traditional risk scores in European and Chinese populations. 12 , 15–18 , 41 , 42 Similarly, in a Finnish study, prediction of early onset incident CAD was improved using PRS compared to conventional risk factors and PRS were shown to be the strongest individual risk factor when added to age and sex. 19 , 20

Notably, CAD PRS have been shown to add predictive value to family history of premature CVD. 12 , 13 , 18–21 , 28 , 41 Polygenic scores have also improved the discrimination of risk for myocardial infarction in individuals affected by a monogenic disease, e.g. familial hypercholesterolaemia. 40 Moreover, it was shown that CAD PRS confer a similar relative increase in CAD risk with or without addition of other risk factors ( Figure 2 ), 10 , 12 , 13 , 15–21 , 28 , 43 with the exception that CAD PRS show increased predictive performance for younger age groups, both in terms of odds ratio and net reclassification index. 15 , 44 Overall, PRS have been shown to improve CVD risk reclassification statistics over conventional CVD risk factor models in some studies, 10 , 12 , 15–28 others have found no or relatively modest improvements. 29 , 30 One public health model has shown that targeting PRS assessment in individuals at intermediate CVD risk—according to conventional risk factors—can modestly enhance prediction and has the potential to prevent ∼7% more events than usual care. 12 Indeed, recent studies conducted in a primary care setting showed that PRS add to clinical risk assessment, particularly in those with intermediate risk. 10 , 25

The seeming paradox that PRS offer only modest risk discrimination—despite a substantial overall burden of genetic CVD risk—is best explained by the rather uniform distribution of risk alleles in the population. 39 As an example, studying the strongest predictive variants in UK Biobank, each person carried ±190 risk alleles; the first and tenth decile of the distribution curve being only about 20 risk alleles (or 10%) apart ( Figure 1B ). 39 Because the effect sizes of risk alleles multiply each other, a sharp increase in genetic risk was nevertheless observed ( Figure 1B ). 39 Therefore, individuals at the outer ends of the PRS are characterized by substantially lower or higher risk and thus may benefit most from the respective information. By contrast, the PRS is rather similar in about 3/5 of the population (grey-shaded in Figure 1B ), which is reflected by a relatively low measure of the c-statistics. In this respect, the predictive power of a PRS is similar to that of other ‘risk modifiers’ such as LDL cholesterol or carotid plaques. 45 , 46 
Polygenic risk score in cardiovascular disease patients 
Outside of risk prediction for first events, there has been interest in predicting secondary or recurrent events in survivors with CVD. Although patients with a clinical disease manifestation are at high risk overall, this risk is not uniform, and better stratification within this group could allow more targeted allocation of resources matched to risk, such as closer clinical follow-up or use of more intensive drug therapies such as proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin Type 9 inhibitors, as described below. Of note, however, studies of genetic risk in atherosclerotic CVD survivors observed that the effect size related to the PRS was notably attenuated compared to apparently healthy individuals experiencing a first myocardial infarction. 47–49 These studies highlight the challenges in explaining disease progression genetically, with methodological difficulties around selection or collider biases (e.g. a priori genetic effects in patients who already had developed CAD). 47 , 50 Despite this, some post hoc analyses from randomized clinical trials recruiting people with established CVD, reveal a greater subsequent event rate in those with a PRS in the >90th centile, and also show a greater absolute CVD risk reduction with LDL cholesterol lowering compared to those with low PRS ( Figure 3 ), 51–53 indicating potential for clinical utility in this group.

Polygenic risk score for prediction of stroke risk 
GWAS for stroke are smaller than those for CAD, are confounded by aetiologic heterogeneity (i.e. not all strokes are solely atherosclerotic in nature), and have correspondingly lower power. 54 A recent study showed a PRS for ischaemic stroke was similarly or more predictive than other risk factors and identified 0.25% of the population who are at three-fold increased stroke risk. 14 A PRS for stroke generated in a Chinese population also showed potential for risk stratification and for identifying those who may disproportionately benefit from ideal maintenance of conventional CVD risk factors. 55 The GIGASTROKE Consortium constructed a PRS for ischaemic and all-cause stroke which improved predictive capacity in diverse ancestries, confirming that ancestry-specific PRS for stroke may be necessary for equitable performance. 9 In five clinical trials of patients with cardiometabolic disease, ischaemic stroke PRS improved risk prediction beyond clinical risk factors in both European and East Asian ancestries. 9 
The role of polygenic risk score in risk-reducing behaviours 
Currently, the evidence base regarding the behavioural impact of providing patients with their CVD PRS results is limited. Data from research on inherited cancer syndromes suggests that predictive genetic testing and communication of genetic disease risk results may improve adherence to cancer screening protocols and large-scale studies testing potential benefits are ongoing. 56–59 In the case of CAD, data suggest a potential benefit of giving people PRS results and the related implications for CAD prevention, even though such knowledge may be similar to that of other risk factors. 60 , 61 A small randomized study reported that disclosure of a CAD PRS increased the participants’ perception of personal control, information seeking, and sharing, and led to statins more often initiated as the result of the shared decision-making process between the physician and the participant. 62–64 In contrast, in an open randomized trial of blood donors, disclosure of CAD risk information, either based on clinical or polygenic risk factors, followed by web-based lifestyle advice did not affect physical activity or other factors impacting on CAD risk, e.g. serum lipids, weight, or diet. 65 It will be critical to learn from future studies whether the knowledge of a PRS test result—e.g. in Scenario 2 of Box 1 —has implications for clinicians’ recommendations and patients’ own decision-making about lifestyle behaviour changes and treatment options. One important design consideration for such studies is that only some patients will receive PRS results that will substantially alter their risk profile. This reflects one of the conceptual principles of PRS, namely that their predictive value may be substantial only in a small segment of the overall population, e.g. those with PRS above the 80th percentile, and that the effects of such polygenic disposition on absolute risk may vary dependent on the overall CVD risk a person carries ( Figure 2 ). 11 , 39

Research gaps, key challenges, and potential solutions 
Current ESC guidelines on prevention do not advocate PRS for routine use in CVD risk prediction. 2 This is partly due to the current uncertainty in published studies (as described above) as well as to unresolved challenges with the PRS itself (see below) and the lack of clarity on how, when and where they should be used ( Figure 4 ). Some key areas where further research or consensus will help advance the use of PRS in clinical practice include the following:

Research gaps 
Given the lack of evidence and somewhat contradictory results, larger and more definitive studies including randomized trials are needed to quantify the potential benefits of PRS in relevant clinical scenarios (e.g. Scenario 2 in Box 1 ). 10 For reasons outlined above, the small improvement in risk prediction provided by PRS does not necessarily mean that some patients (e.g. those at the extremes of the PRS distribution) may not benefit from clinical care informed by their PRS. Therefore, it is possible that significant clinical benefit could be seen in PRS outcomes trials, despite the underwhelming impact of PRS on current risk prediction metrics (such as discrimination and reclassification). 
The utility of CVD risk assessment tools, including those that incorporate PRS results, depends in part on the behavioural actions of the patient in response to their results, e.g. diet or use of medications (e.g. statin), and other ‘lifestyle’ behaviours. More research is needed on how disclosure of personal PRS results specifically impact on health behaviour. 
Little is known about the behavioural, or psychological, impacts of PRS testing on younger adults when few other risk factors may be evident. 66 Research generating evidence on the implications of PRS testing at a young age with the aim of informing measures that modify lifetime risks, is needed (Scenario 5 in Box 1 ). This may include an optimized PRS for younger individuals, since current information on respective genetic variants is largely derived from on middle-aged or older subjects. 
Evidence bases that are appropriately tailored to specific populations are needed to define optimal thresholds and reference standards for PRS testing. 
Standards for clinical reporting of PRS test results need to be agreed. 
Mechanisms for quality assurance, e.g. device certification, need to be in place to minimize some of the potential risks of PRS testing. Within this process, analytic validity, clinical validity, and clinical utility as well as compliance with other ethical, legal and social issues need to be continuously monitored and communicated. 
Adaptive frameworks for refinement of polygenic risk score 
Since PRS have variable performance across diverse ancestries, the fairness (i.e. equal performance in the full spectrum of ethnic and social groups) of a PRS test must be updatable so that it can be optimized. 
Compatibility with clinical risk assessment tools needs to be improved through increasing GWAS and PRS studies beyond CAD with a focus on less-studied cardiovascular phenotypes (e.g. stroke, peripheral arterial disease, age of first/recurrent event, etc.). 
The best-performing PRS (or the set of PRS with effectively equivalent performance) in a particular clinical use case needs to be determined. 
The extent to which other demographic differences, such as those from sex and age, affect PRS performance requires further investigation 15 , 44 , 67 ( Figure 2 ). 
Communication, actioning, and impact of test results 
Personalized risk assessment tools need to be developed that integrate PRS and conventional risk factors. Such tools need to be user-friendly and understandable to patients and the public. 
Education of healthcare practitioners and genetic counsellors is needed to facilitate appropriate counselling of patients what PRS are and when they may be informative. 
In communicating PRS test results to the clinician and via them to the patient, consensus, and guidelines are needed regarding what risk metrics are appropriate, e.g. as a continuous numerical value and/or a stratified risk category (e.g. low, intermediate, or high) 68 and how PRS results should be combined with other risk factors for treatment recommendations ( Graphical Abstract ). Digital communication tools are needed to provide information pre-testing, and to communicate PRS findings post-testing (including the handling of incidental findings). 
The extent to which familial risk of CVD is influenced by PRS also requires further research, which will inform patient decision-making around whether family members should be informed of potential risk. 
The evidence base and medical consequences for a PRS test need to be transparent. Informed consent for genetic testing is key for participatory and autonomous decision-making of patients and to avoid wrongful expectations or lack of autonomy. 
By current legal standards in many EU countries, genetic data obtained for medical prediction requires genetic counselling by a qualified person, discussing the possible medical, psychological, and social questions. Handling of any incidental genetic findings also needs to be specified. 
Regulatory and financial considerations of polygenic risk score testing in clinical practice 
Arrays allowing genotyping for research purposes need to be certified as medical devices and reimbursement for PRS testing needs to be defined in the context of a focused diagnostic tool or a broader screening test for genetic disorders. 
The corresponding costs of clinical PRS testing also need further clarity. While arrays are a relatively inexpensive one-time cost and existing research bioinformatic pipelines for calculating PRS for multiple conditions only add mildly to this cost, the costs of commercial providers of PRS and the corresponding tools are less clear. 
Further studies are needed to model PRS cost effectiveness in the different settings of its potential application. 69 
Navigating the current transitional phase from theory to practical application 
Despite these challenges, enthusiasm to use PRS has grown rapidly, with both clinicians and patients seeking this information from commercial providers without clear understanding of their place in the evaluation of or management of CVD risk. Here we identify the task to manage a transitional phase where PRS data are being generated without a clear framework for their understanding or potential value. 
We propose in practical terms, pending formal clinical utility evaluation, that the most meaningful use for PRS will be as an adjunct to prediction tools recommended by current guidelines on prevention. 2 , 10 
The target group(s) of individuals who can benefit from a PRS test needs to be defined more clearly. Clinically, a predictive test may have the greatest relevance when a person is close to a threshold for initiation or intensification of treatment based on conventional CVD risk assessment tools ( Graphical Abstract ). Given that the therapeutic implications for preventive measures are greatest in subjects with intermediate risk, counselling regarding PRS may be reasonable for these individuals. Other groups that may benefit are young persons with elevated long-term CVD risk, i.e. those with a very strong family history or other lifelong risk factors [lipoprotein(a)], to inform primordial prevention efforts. Such use requires an integrated approach involving appropriate pre-assessment information, conventional risk assessment, accurate PRS calculation, delivery and communication of the test result, and risk information in ways that support understanding and post-assessment decision support. 
Patient-driven use of polygenic risk score 
Consensus principles are needed to assist physicians asked to interpret PRS test results from products obtained by patients themselves (commercially or otherwise). 
A set of approaches for accommodating any potential PRS-based patient concerns and utilizing this information to guide clinical practice should be defined. 
Potential clinical scenarios for the use of polygenic risk score 
To better define the current and short-term clinical challenges of PRS use, we conceive several clinical scenarios which may help to guide when PRS information for CVD risk prediction may or may not be helpful ( Box 1 ). 
Requirements for future application of polygenic risk scores for cardiovascular disease 
Before a broader utilization of PRS for CVD in clinical settings can be advised, it is necessary to minimize the probability of negative consequences from PRS test results, such as inefficient use of financial resources or unnecessary anxiety. Cardiovascular disease risk prediction based solely on PRS genetic testing alone is not currently justified and should be discouraged. 10 However, ignoring the promise of genetic testing as an adjunct to clinical risk assessment may also impede the development of potentially effective approaches for very early identification of individuals at high CVD risk. 
There are currently research efforts to integrate and understand the implications of incorporating PRS for CVD into clinical pathways around the globe, including but not limited to research programmes in the USA, 70 Finland, 71 and the UK. 72 The evidence from these research programmes will be vital for the future applications of PRS for CVD in practice. 
The current state can be anticipated as a transitional period which needs to balance the harms of premature application with missed opportunities of a potentially disruptive risk prediction technology in a field that has seen little advancement in the last 60 years. In this phase, it seems advisable to foster both the development of user-friendly tools for genetic testing and the evaluation of their clinical utility. 
This process needs ( Figure 4 ): 
1. While the scenarios in Box 1 are plausible and, to some extent, obvious, we would clarify that these are not case studies and specific recommendations. Expanded research programmes to optimize PRS, to develop translational tools for communication and decision support, to assess clinical utility, and to define clinical indications for PRS are thus needed to support such reasoning by study evidence. 
2. Guidance from academic societies and health policy makers for setting standards of care. Indeed, the current position statement is motivated to address this need in Europe. 
3. Support from payers, since without financial support PRS will not enter clinical use under the guidance of academic medicine. 
In the interim, biotechnology companies will increasingly offer private options for genetic testing, including polygenic information about CVD risk, a business which is already growing at an astounding rate. 73 Advertising ‘direct to consumer tests’ may increase the gap between technological possibilities and true medical need. Indeed, this development may lead to a ‘data drain’ from the clinical academic towards the commercial sector. If clinical utility is established it may worsen inequality by giving the opportunity to those who can afford genetic testing. 
Alternatively, an evidence-generating care concept should be supported to inform clinical guidelines on how PRS testing might be applied and adapted for optimal guidance of preventive measures. 
One platform for achieving such goals may be specialized preventive care centres, which could offer individualized cross-sectoral applications for genome-driven medicine informing personalized therapeutic strategies for common diseases, not only for CVD but also common tumours. Indeed, PRS for breast cancer are under intense assessment to determine the clinical utility and their potential role in (genetic) risk counselling and prevention. 56–59 
Implementation of such innovative health-screening programmes should be supported by both scientific societies and health policy makers throughout Europe. 74 Scientific societies may develop criteria that guide preventive centres employing genomic prediction tools and health policy makers may consider funding instruments for novel structures of preventive health care as well as calls for prospective studies generating scientific evidence for new preventive standards involving PRS as has been shown for the utilization of PRS in counselling for breast cancer. 56 
Finally, lawmakers should bring the legal framework into the 21st century, including the definition of standards for data protection in order to facilitate a sensible and co-ordinated growth of technologies involving genetic information from PRS for improving a predictive, preventive, personalized, and participatory medicine. 
Summary 
Genetic testing with a PRS holds the potential to improve CVD risk prediction and informed decision-making for preventive treatment. Specifically, individuals who are close to a threshold of absolute 10-year CVD risk that warrants intensified treatment may refine their decision-making based on a PRS test result. Thus, the use of genetically fine-tuned individual risk estimates may be most reasonable in the context of established clinical risk assessment tools, which already provide guidance for the medical consequences of test results. In addition, younger adults with known risk factors but the low 10-year risk may also benefit from knowledge of their genetic and therefore long-term risk using PRS. However, prospective studies and trials with appropriate funding are warranted to investigate the implementation of PRS in CVD risk prediction and their role in guiding preventive interventions in order to answer open questions on the clinical utility and patient´s perception of test results. In a transitional phase like the present, it seems reasonable—beyond the commercial consumer applications which currently dominate—to use the opportunity to generate more scientific data on the use of PRS testing and to consider reimbursement of PRS-refined risk prediction in specific academic clinical settings, where evaluation and utility can be formally assessed in practice. In this sense, the authors would appreciate that future guidelines clinically define patient groups in whom the information from a PRS is anticipated to add significantly to clinical decision-making for the prevention of cardiovascular events.
