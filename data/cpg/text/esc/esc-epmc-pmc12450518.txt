Abstract 
Background and Aims 
Implementing societal recommendations for primary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) in cardiac sarcoidosis requires an accurate diagnosis. However, cardiac sarcoidosis diagnostic schemes are inconsistent and often produce conflicting results. This study aimed to compare the discriminative accuracy of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) phenotyping with the societal recommendations for predicting long-term ventricular arrhythmic outcomes in patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis, regardless of their diagnostic status. 
Methods 
This multicentre study included patients with histology-supported sarcoidosis who underwent CMR for suspected cardiac involvement and were ineligible for secondary prevention ICDs. The study outcome was a composite of fatal or life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias. Outcomes were compared based on eligibility for ICDs by societal recommendations and CMR phenotyping. 
Results 
Among 1514 patients, 84 experienced the study outcome during a median follow-up of 4.5 years and a maximum follow-up of 10 years. The high-risk CMR phenotype was associated with higher 5- and 10-year incidences of the outcome (24.0% and 35.0%, respectively) compared with those who met societal recommendations. Patients with low-risk phenotypes had lower incidences (0.7% and 2.6%). Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging phenotyping outperformed societal recommendations in discriminative accuracy, with areas under the curve of 0.861 and 0.776 for 5- and 10-year outcomes, respectively. Additionally, CMR phenotyping had the highest adjusted subdistribution hazard ratio (19.8) for the study outcome. 
Conclusions 
In patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis, CMR phenotyping showed greater discriminative accuracy than societal recommendations for predicting fatal or life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, suggesting that it may be more effective at identifying candidates for primary prevention ICDs. 
Keywords: Cardiac sarcoidosis, Risk stratification, ICD decision-making, Primary prevention, Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction 
Cardiac sarcoidosis is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. 1 Life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias are common, and sudden cardiac death (SCD) is a major concern. 1 Thus, risk stratification for SCD and identifying patients who may benefit from an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) are key elements in the management of cardiac sarcoidosis. 1 
Societal recommendations for ICD placement for the primary prevention of SCD 2–4 in cardiac sarcoidosis ( Table 1 ) differ from those for other cardiomyopathies. 2 , 4 Effective implementation of these recommendations necessitates an accurate diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis. However, diagnostic schemes (see Supplementary data online , Table S1 ) 3 , 5–10 show significant variability. Studies comparing some of these diagnostic schemes have reported substantial disagreement in diagnosing cardiac sarcoidosis 11–13 and its prognostic implications. 13 Risk profiles of patients diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis vary across institutions, even when using the same diagnostic scheme. This, in turn, results in inconsistent use of ICDs. Therefore, we need a strategy to identify patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis who would benefit from primary prevention ICDs, overcoming the limitations of the diagnostic schemes.

We recently demonstrated that cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) phenotyping can effectively prognosticate patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis. 14 In this multicentre study, we compared the accuracy of CMR phenotyping with societal recommendations for ICD placement in predicting long-term arrhythmic outcomes in patients with histology-supported sarcoidosis and suspected cardiac involvement. 
Methods 
Study cohort 
We studied consecutive patients with histology-supported sarcoidosis who underwent CMR with late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging for the evaluation of suspected cardiac sarcoidosis at three centres: St. Antonius Hospital in Nieuwegein, The Netherlands; University of Minnesota M Health Fairview in Minneapolis, MN, USA; and Montefiore Medical Center in Bronx, New York, USA, between 2004 and 2022. The University of Minnesota was the data co-ordinating centre and the core laboratory for CMR analyses. Institutional review board approval with a waiver of informed consent was obtained from all institutions. 
Cardiac involvement of sarcoidosis was suspected based on symptoms (e.g. palpitations, presyncope/syncope, dyspnoea unexplained by sarcoidosis-associated pulmonary disease or pulmonary hypertension, and chest pain) or electrocardiographic abnormalities [e.g. left or right bundle branch block, pathological Q waves, atrioventricular block, and ventricular tachycardia (VT)]. 3 Demographic data, medical history, comorbidities, medications, and outcomes were uniformly defined across all centres and collected in a de-identified manner at the data co-ordinating centre. Similarly, de-identified CMR images were electronically transferred to the core laboratory, where they were analysed using standard protocols and blinded to non-CMR data, as previously described. 14–17 Patients from our prior studies from the University of Minnesota 14–17 and St. Antonius Hospital 18–20 were included with additional follow-up data. 
Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging acquisition 
Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging was performed on clinical 1.5 T (98.7%) or 3.0 T (1.3%) scanners from Philips (49.6%), Siemens (47.8%), or GE Medical Systems (2.6%) using phased-array receiver coils following standard recommendations. 21 , 22 All CMRs included localizers for cardiac positioning, cine CMR images in multiple short-axis and long-axis views using a steady-state free-precession sequence, and LGE imaging with a segmented inversion-recovery gradient-echo sequence in the same views as cine CMR. 
Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging analyses 
Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging analyses were performed in the core laboratory, blinded to all other patient information, by the consensus of two CMR experts, with disagreements resolved with discussion and consensus, as previously described. 14 , 17 , 23 Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was determined by quantitative analysis according to standard recommendations. 24 Abnormal LVEF was defined as <52% in men and <54% in women, and abnormal right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF) was defined as <47% in men and <49% in women, based on pooled normative reference ranges from a Bayesian hierarchical meta-analysis of 52 studies. 25 
Late gadolinium enhancement was visually identified. The extent of left ventricular LGE was semi-quantitatively assessed on a 17-segmental basis from the area of hyperenhanced myocardium on a 5-point scale as previously described: 0, no hyper-enhancement; 1, 1%–25%; 2, 26%–50%; 3, 51%–75%; and 4, 76%–100%. 14 , 23 The global LGE extent was assessed as a percentage of the left ventricular myocardium by adding the segmental scores, weighted by the midpoint of the range of hyper-enhancement, and dividing by 17 for the total number of segments. 14 , 23 
At the time of the CMR, 31 (2.0%) patients had a cardiovascular implantable electronic device (28 had a permanent pacemaker, and 3 had an ICD). In all 31 patients, the image quality was optimal without significant compromise by device-related artefacts for assessing cardiac function, the presence and extent of LGE, and the CMR phenotype. 
Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging phenotyping 
Following our framework from Athwal et al ., 14 we classified patients into four CMR phenotypes based on LGE features and LVEF: no LGE and normal LVEF, no LGE and abnormal LVEF, pathology-frequent LGE, and pathology-rare LGE. Pathology-frequent LGE included at least one of four features: subepicardial, multifocal, septal, and right ventricular free wall involvement, based on our findings from Okasha et al . 26 a systematic analysis of published gross pathology photographs of the hearts of patients with histologically confirmed cardiac sarcoidosis who underwent autopsy or cardiac transplantation. Patients with LGE not meeting these four features were classified as pathology-rare LGE. 14 We have previously shown excellent reproducibility in identifying LGE features. 14 
Based on our results from Athwal et al ., 14 we dichotomized CMR phenotypes into high-risk (pathology-frequent LGE) and low-risk (no LGE and normal LVEF, no LGE and abnormal LVEF, or pathology-rare LGE) phenotypes. 
Clinical follow-up and study outcome 
The study outcome was a composite of sudden (arrhythmic) cardiac death, resuscitated cardiac arrest with documented VT or ventricular fibrillation (VF), and significant ventricular arrhythmia, defined as sustained VT (duration > 30 s), or appropriate ICD therapy (shock or anti-tachycardia pacing). The appropriateness of ICD therapies was adjudicated by cardiac electrophysiologists during clinical care using ICD-recorded intracardiac electrograms based on tachycardia rate, onset, stability, atrioventricular association, and QRS morphology. 
Follow-up for the arrhythmic outcome was censored at orthotopic heart transplantation, death, or the end of follow-up. The length of follow-up was defined as the time until the arrhythmic outcome, orthotopic heart transplantation, death, or the end of the study. For patients from St. Antonius Hospital, mortality status and death dates from the electronic medical records were independently cross-verified with the Netherlands Nationaal Register Overledenen (national register of deceased persons). For patients from the University of Minnesota, mortality status and death dates from the electronic medical records were independently cross-verified with the Minnesota State Department of Health’s Office of Vital Records. For patients who died outside the hospital, death certificates were reviewed to determine the cause of death. The cause of death was classified using standard criteria. 27 Data on events were obtained and classified, blinded to CMR findings. 
Assessment of eligibility for primary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement based on societal recommendations 
We assessed the recommendations for primary prevention ICD placement from the 2014 Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) Expert Consensus Statement on the Diagnosis and Management of Arrhythmias Associated With Cardiac Sarcoidosis, 3 the 2017 American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology (ACC)/HRS Guideline for management of patients with ventricular arrhythmias and the prevention of SCD, 2 and the 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines for the management of patients with ventricular arrhythmias and the prevention of SCD 4 ( Table 1 ). 
This study focused on primary prevention because societal recommendations for ICD placement in this setting are unique to cardiac sarcoidosis. In contrast, secondary prevention recommendations for ICD placement in cardiac sarcoidosis are the same as for other cardiomyopathies. Thus, decisions regarding secondary prevention ICDs do not depend on an accurate diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis. 
The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS Guidelines include a Class IIa recommendation for primary prevention ICD for patients with cardiac sarcoidosis, LVEF > 35%, and ‘evidence of myocardial scar by CMR.’ 2 In Figure 8 of the guidelines, ‘extensive scar’ is noted; however, this is not defined. Therefore, we first assessed the recommendations accounting for any LGE. 2 Then, we used extensive scar defined as LGE > 5.7%, a threshold we identified previously as optimal for predicting the arrhythmic outcome in patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis. 15 The 2022 ESC Guidelines include a Class IIa recommendation for primary prevention ICD for patients with LVEF > 35% and ‘significant LGE.’ The authors acknowledge that ‘a widely accepted definition of significant LGE is not available’ but also note that ‘LGE affecting ≥22% of the left ventricular mass has been associated with arrhythmic endpoints.’ 4 Therefore, we first assessed the recommendations using ≥22% as the threshold to define ‘significant LGE.’ Second, we used the threshold of >5.7% within the framework of the 2022 ESC Guidelines. 
Thus, we assessed the societal recommendations for primary prevention ICD placement as follows: (i) 2014 HRS Statement; (ii) 2017 AHA/ACC/HRS Guidelines—LGE > 0%; (iii) 2017 AHA/ACC/HRS Guidelines—LGE > 5.7%; (iv) 2022 ESC Guidelines—LGE ≥ 22%; and (v) 2022 ESC Guidelines—LGE > 5.7%. 
In all instances, we used Class I and IIa recommendations as indications for primary prevention ICD placement. 
With CMR phenotyping, the high-risk CMR phenotype indicated the need for primary prevention ICD placement, whereas the low-risk phenotypes indicated it was unnecessary. 
Statistical analyses 
Categorical variables were reported as counts with percentages, and continuous variables were presented as medians with an interquartile range (IQR; 25th–75th percentile) because the data were not normally distributed based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. 
The incidence of the arrhythmic outcome in patients with or without an indication for primary prevention ICD based on societal recommendations or CMR phenotyping was assessed using a Fine–Gray competing risk framework to estimate the cumulative incidence function, considering heart transplantation and non-SCD death as competing risks. 28 , 29 Comparisons were made using Gray’s test. 
Discriminative accuracy of the societal recommendations and CMR phenotyping in predicting the arrhythmic outcome was determined using the inverse probability of censoring weighting approach to estimate and compare time-dependent areas under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC) curves for censored event times with competing risks. 30 
The association between an indication for primary prevention ICD, based on societal recommendations or CMR phenotyping, and the arrhythmic outcome was tested using univariable and multivariable Fine–Gray proportional subdistribution hazard modelling. Covariates for inclusion in the multivariable models were selected a priori , informed by previous studies and our clinical expertise. 
Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P < .05. Analyses were performed using RStudio 2024.04.2 Build 764. 
Results 
Overall cohort 
The cohort included 1574 patients with histology-supported sarcoidosis and suspected cardiac involvement investigated by CMR. Of these, 60 (3.8%) presented with sustained VT ( n = 42), aborted SCD ( n = 10), or both ( n = 8), making them eligible for a secondary prevention ICD.

Cardiac rhythm monitoring data from implantable loop recorders 
Among patients who did not receive an ICD soon after the initial presentation, 119 (7.9% of the cohort) received implantable loop recorders (ILRs) for long-term cardiac rhythm monitoring at the discretion of the treating cardiologists (see Supplementary data online , Table S3 ). Of these, 38 (31.9%) had the high-risk CMR phenotype, and 81 (68.1%) had a low-risk CMR phenotype. 
Among the patients with the high-risk CMR phenotype, at a median follow-up—defined as the time until sustained VT or VF, end of battery life, or ILR explantation—of 3.1 years (IQR 1.5, 3.7), four had sustained VT. Thus, in patients without an ICD, the high-risk CMR phenotype was associated with an annualized ventricular arrhythmic event rate of 3.78%, based on 106 patient-years of follow-up. 
Among the patients with a low-risk phenotype, at a median follow-up of 3.6 years (IQR 2.7, 4.0), one had sustained VT. Thus, in patients without an ICD, a low-risk CMR phenotype was associated with an annualized ventricular arrhythmic event rate of 0.34%, based on 298 patient-years of follow-up. 
Discussion 
Key findings 
In a large multicentre cohort of patients with histology-supported sarcoidosis who had CMR for suspected cardiac involvement, CMR phenotyping had high discriminative accuracy and outperformed all societal recommendations in predicting long-term arrhythmic outcomes ( Structured Graphical Abstract ). 
Strengths of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging phenotyping for determining eligibility for a primary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator 
Societal recommendations for ICD placement for the primary prevention of SCD 2–4 in cardiac sarcoidosis ( Table 1 ) differ from those for other cardiomyopathies. 2 , 4 Appropriate use of these recommendations depends on an accurate diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis. Seven diagnostic schemes have been proposed from 1993 to 2016 for diagnosing cardiac sarcoidosis: the Japanese Ministry of Health and Welfare (JMHW) criteria in 1993, 5 the A Case Control Etiologic Study of Sarcoidosis (ACCESS) criteria in 1999, 6 the Japan Society of Sarcoidosis and Other Granulomatous Disorders (JSSOG) criteria in 2007, 7 the World Association of Sarcoidosis and other Granulomatous Disorders (WASOG) criteria in 2014, 8 the HRS Expert Consensus recommendations criteria in 2014, 3 the Genomic Research in Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency and Sarcoidosis (GRADS) study criteria in 2015, 9 and the Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) criteria in 2016 10 (see Supplementary data online , Table S1 ). None of these schemes have been validated in patients with cardiac histology-supported sarcoidosis. 
Recent studies comparing some of these schemes have found substantial disagreement in diagnosing cardiac sarcoidosis. 11–13 In a multicentre cohort of 587 patients meeting at least one of four (JMHW, JSSOG, HRS, JCS) schemes, only 10% met all four. The risk of adverse outcomes varied across the four schemes. 13 Notably, there was no significant difference in the incidence of adverse outcomes (death, left ventricular assist device placement, heart transplantation, or appropriate ICD therapy) between patients who met at least one diagnostic scheme (168/587; 28.6%) and those with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis who met none (22/99; 22.2%; P = .19). 13 
Recognizing the difficulty in diagnosing cardiac sarcoidosis, experts in a recent scientific statement from the AHA on the diagnosis and management of cardiac sarcoidosis recommended considering the diagnosis using four categories of likelihood: definite, highly probable, probable, and possible/low probability, rather than in a binary fashion of present or absent. 1 However, societal recommendations for ICD placement, including those in the same AHA statement, are based on a binary diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis. 1–4 
Risk profiles of patients diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis vary substantially between institutions using the same diagnostic scheme. In 398 patients with cardiac sarcoidosis diagnosed using the HRS diagnostic scheme in the Myocardial Inflammatory Diseases in Finland (MIDFIN) Registry, the 5-year rate of sustained VT or SCD was 24%. 31 In comparison, in 252 patients with cardiac sarcoidosis diagnosed using the HRS diagnostic scheme (in 83%) at Johns Hopkins Hospital, the 5-year rate of sustained VT or VF was 18%, 32 and in 273 patients with cardiac sarcoidosis diagnosed using the HRS diagnostic scheme at Cleveland Clinic, the 5-year rate of sustained VT or VF was 11%. 33 This wide variation in the incidence of ventricular arrhythmias suggests heterogeneity in the application of the HRS diagnostic scheme, reflected in the prevalence of LGE: 98% in the MIDFIN Registry, 31 72% in the Johns Hopkins cohort, 32 and 40% in the Cleveland Clinic cohort. 33 
The 2017 AHA/ACC/HRS Guidelines and the 2022 ESC Guidelines for ICD placement in cardiac sarcoidosis ( Table 1 ) are challenging to implement in clinical practice because of the undefined terms ‘extensive scar on CMR or PET’ and ‘significant LGE,’ respectively. 
Heterogeneity in applying diagnostic schemes—such as the HRS scheme across different institutions—and challenges in implementing societal recommendations for ICD placement because of undefined terms lead to variations in the use of ICDs; the rates of ICD placement for patients diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis were 74% in the MIDFIN Registry, 31 57% in the Johns Hopkins Hospital cohort, and 58% in the Cleveland Clinic cohort. 32 , 33 CMR phenotyping avoids these issues, as it does not require patients to meet any diagnostic scheme for cardiac sarcoidosis or the societal recommendations for ICD placement. The prevalence of the high-risk CMR phenotype among the three centres in our cohort varied by <1%. 
Our approach involves CMR, widely recognized as the pivotal imaging technique for assessing patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis. 1 , 34–36 Among CMR techniques, it exclusively uses LGE, which has been a cornerstone of CMR for over 25 years. 37 
Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging phenotyping relies on quickly recognizable LGE features and does not need quantitative analysis or specialized software. We previously showed that CMR phenotyping was independent of—and superior to—LGE extent for predicting ventricular arrhythmic events in patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis. 14 Our approach does not require online risk calculators, such as HCM Risk-SCD for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy 38 and ARVC Risk Calculator for arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy. 39 , 40 Thus, it is easily applicable in clinical practice. 
Comparing cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging phenotyping with recent findings from the MIDFIN Registry 
In 2022, Nordenswan et al . 31 demonstrated that societal recommendations for ICD eligibility failed to identify a truly low-risk group of patients in the MIDFIN Registry. They concluded that all patients with clinically manifest cardiac sarcoidosis should be candidates for ICD placement. 31 Patients in the MIDFIN Registry were diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis using the HRS diagnostic scheme. 31 
Nordenswan et al .’s recommendation may not be valid in all patients diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis using the HRS diagnostic scheme for two reasons. First, as discussed earlier, there is variability in how different institutions apply the HRS diagnostic scheme. Second, 48% of patients in the MIDFIN Registry had cardiac histology-supported cardiac sarcoidosis, 31 compared with only 6% of patients in the Johns Hopkins cohort 32 and 12% in the Cleveland Clinic cohort. 33 In the MIDFIN Registry, patients with a cardiac histology-supported diagnosis had more than twice the risk of SCD or sustained VT compared with those without a cardiac histology-supported diagnosis. 31 Thus, the clinical implications of Nordenswan et al .’s study and their recommendation may not be valid at institutions where cardiac sarcoidosis is mostly diagnosed without histology support from cardiac tissue. The possibility of such a spectrum bias 41 is not a concern with our approach using CMR phenotyping to select patients for primary prevention ICDs. 
In their study of patients with diagnosed cardiac sarcoidosis, Nordenswan et al . did not report outcomes for patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis who were ultimately not diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis. As a result, the sensitivity and negative predictive value of their recommendation to consider all patients with clinically manifest cardiac sarcoidosis for ICD placement are unknown. In contrast, we have shown that our approach using CMR phenotyping for selecting patients for primary prevention ICDs not only has high specificity and positive predictive value but also has high sensitivity and negative predictive value. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging phenotyping accurately identifies patients who would benefit from a primary prevention ICD and those who would not. 
Validity of testing societal recommendations in patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis 
Although societal recommendations for ICD placement in cardiac sarcoidosis have been proposed for patients with diagnosed cardiac sarcoidosis, we tested these recommendations against CMR phenotyping in a cohort of patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis, regardless of whether the diagnosis was made. Nevertheless, our comparisons are methodologically valid because the primary measure in our study, AUC, is independent of disease prevalence. 42 Here, the disease prevalence is represented by the cumulative incidence of the study outcome, which would be lower in a cohort of patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis than diagnosed cardiac sarcoidosis. 
Prognosis vs. diagnosis and why the cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging phenotypes predict ventricular arrhythmic outcomes 
Clinical practice focused on patient prognosis and the likelihood of future outcomes may help patients more than focusing on a binary diagnostic label. 43 Although our risk-centred approach using CMR phenotyping avoids the need for a binary diagnosis, the phenotypes may also provide insights into the diagnosis. The high-risk CMR phenotype includes features of cardiac damage we identified by systematically examining gross pathology photographs of the hearts of patients with cardiac histology-supported cardiac sarcoidosis who underwent heart transplantation or had SCD. 26 Therefore, we infer that this phenotype identifies patients with a high likelihood of cardiac sarcoidosis, a highly arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy. 14 , 20 , 31 In contrast, the low-risk phenotypes identify patients with a low likelihood of cardiac sarcoidosis and, consequently, a low risk of ventricular arrhythmias. Patients with the low-risk CMR phenotypes either have no cardiomyopathy (those with no LGE and normal LVEF) or likely have a non-cardiac sarcoidosis cardiomyopathy (those with no LGE and abnormal LVEF, 44 or those with isolated subendocardial or midmyocardial LGE), rather than a benign form of cardiac sarcoidosis. This inference needs to be confirmed in future studies. 
Strengths of our study 
Our study has notable strengths. First, our cohort was binational and multicentre and included a large number of patients. Compared with 1514 patients in our cohort, a 2023 meta-analysis of 13 studies of sarcoidosis patients evaluated by CMR had a total of 1318 patients, including patients eligible for a secondary prevention ICD. 45 The societal recommendations for ICD placement in cardiac sarcoidosis are typically based on small, single-centre observational or registry studies (level of evidence B or C). 2–4 
Second, unlike many previous studies on cardiac sarcoidosis, all patients in our study had histology-supported sarcoidosis. 
Third, our follow-up spanned 10 years after the CMR, allowing us to assess the value of the societal recommendations and CMR phenotyping for ICD placement over a decade. 
Fourth, all CMR analyses in our study were performed centrally in a core lab, blinded to non-CMR data. 
Finally, we had 298 patient-years of cardiac rhythm monitoring data from ILRs in patients with low-risk CMR phenotypes, confirming a very low incidence of sustained VT or VF in these patients. 
Limitations of our study 
This study has several limitations. First, appropriate ICD therapy is an imperfect surrogate for SCD, potentially influenced by non-uniform ICD programming, and may overestimate risk; some ventricular arrhythmias may terminate spontaneously without resulting in death. 46 Nonetheless, appropriate ICD therapies have been considered equivalent to SCD when developing risk-prediction models for selecting patients for primary prevention ICDs in other cardiomyopathies, such as HCM Risk-SCD 38 and ARVC Risk Calculator, 39 , 40 both recommended in societal guidelines. 47 , 48 
Second, sustained VT or VF that terminates spontaneously without resulting in death may go undetected in patients without ICDs. A greater proportion of patients with the high-risk CMR phenotype in our cohort received ICDs than those with a low-risk CMR phenotype; this difference could bias our results. However, our analysis of 298 patient-years of cardiac rhythm monitoring data from ILRs in patients with low-risk CMR phenotypes confirmed a very low incidence of sustained VT or VF in these patients. 
Third, although the 10-year risk of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias for the low-risk CMR phenotypes (2.6%) is less than half the 5-year threshold of 6% for recommending ICD placement for primary prevention in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, 47 , 48 it is still higher than the 5-year risk (0.7%). Some of this increased risk could result from the new development of the high-risk CMR phenotype during follow-up, suggesting that it may be reasonable to reassess the CMR phenotype after 5 years from the initial risk stratification until future research using longitudinal data clarifies the optimal surveillance interval. 
Fourth, only a few patients in our cohort had programmed ventricular stimulation, precluding an evaluation of the discriminatory power of inducible sustained ventricular arrhythmias in identifying candidates for primary prevention ICDs. 
Fifth, we did not collect data on the doses or duration of immunosuppressive therapy. Although clinical practice guidelines recommend immunosuppression for patients with active myocardial inflammation, its impact on long-term arrhythmic outcomes in cardiac sarcoidosis remains unknown. 49 
Sixth, 80% of the cohort was white; thus, our findings need replication in non-white patients. 
Finally, although our approach does not require a diagnosis of cardiac sarcoidosis using a diagnostic scheme, we only studied patients with histology-supported sarcoidosis. Thus, our findings may not be valid in patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis but no histology support for sarcoidosis. 
Conclusions 
In a large cohort of patients with histology-supported sarcoidosis who had CMR for suspected cardiac involvement, a high-risk CMR phenotype defined by at least one of four LGE features accurately identified those at a high risk of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias and, thus, candidates for primary prevention ICDs. The performance of the high-risk phenotype was independent of whether patients met diagnostic schemes for cardiac sarcoidosis. It was also independent of—and superior to—all societal recommendations for primary prevention ICD placement. These findings suggest that CMR phenotyping of patients with suspected cardiac sarcoidosis may be more effective than societal recommendations for patients with diagnosed cardiac sarcoidosis in identifying patients who would benefit from primary prevention ICD placement.
