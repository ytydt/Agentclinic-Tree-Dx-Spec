Abstract 
Background and Aims 
Whether index testing using coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) improves outcomes in stable chest pain is debated. The risk factor weighted clinical likelihood (RF-CL) model provides likelihood estimation of obstructive coronary artery disease. This study investigated the prognostic effect of coronary CTA vs. usual care by RF-CL estimates. 
Methods 
Large-scale studies randomized patients ( N = 13 748) with stable chest pain to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up in addition to or instead of usual care including functional testing. Patients were stratified according to RF-CL estimates [RF-CL: very-low (≤5%), low (>5%–15%), and moderate/high (>15%)]. The primary endpoint was myocardial infarction or death at 3 years. 
Results 
The primary endpoint occurred in 313 (2.3%) patients. Event rates were similar in patients allocated to coronary CTA vs. usual care [risk difference (RD) 0.3%, hazard ratio (HR) 0.84 (95% CI 0.67–1.05)]. Overall, 33%, 44%, and 23% patients had very-low, low, and moderate/high RF-CL. Risk was similar in patients with very low and moderate/high RF-CL allocated to coronary CTA vs. usual care [very low: RD 0.3%, HR 1.27 (0.74–2.16); moderate/high: RD 0.5%, HR 0.88 (0.63–1.23)]. Conversely, patients with low RF-CL undergoing coronary CTA had lower event rates [RD 0.7%, HR 0.67 (95% CI 0.47–0.97)]. The number needed to test using coronary CTA to prevent one event within 3 years was 143. 
Conclusions 
Despite an overall good prognosis, low RF-CL patients have reduced risk of myocardial infarction or death when allocated to coronary CTA vs. usual care. Risk is similar in patients with very-low and moderate/high likelihood.

Introduction 
In patients with stable chest pain, large-scale randomized studies testing the effect of anatomy-based non-invasive diagnostic testing on clinical outcome yield divergent conclusions. The prospective multicenter imaging study for evaluation of chest pain (PROMISE) trial reported that anatomy-based testing by coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) does not improve outcomes compared with an initial test-strategy of usual care including functional testing. 1 In contrast, the Scottish computed tomography of the heart (SCOT-HEART) investigators found substantial changes in patient management as a consequence of additional coronary CTA compared with standard of care including exercise electrocardiography alone but the secondary endpoint of short-term outcomes was similar between allocation groups. 2 In the long-term follow-up study, however, outcomes were improved by coronary CTA. 3 In general, whether coronary CTA should be the initial test in suspected angina is debated. 4 
Overall, patients with stable de novo chest pain have favourable outcomes, but both European and North-American guidelines recommend initial pre-test assessment of the likelihood of obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) to guide decisions regarding referral to downstream diagnostic testing. 5 , 6 Compared with traditional, basic pre-test probability models including sex, age, and symptoms, the novel risk factor-weighted clinical likelihood (RF-CL) model improves the prediction of obstructive CAD and down-reclassifies patients to lower probability categories for whom testing is not indicated. 7 In addition, the RF-CL model has superior discrimination of cardiovascular risk compared with basic models. 8 However, it is unknown whether patient-specific pre-test likelihood of obstructive CAD identify subgroups of patients with improved outcomes from coronary CTA testing as part of the initial work-up. 
In a pooled analysis of the PROMISE 1 and SCOT-HEART 2 trials, the aim was to test the effect on clinical outcome of using coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up compared with usual care including functional testing. In addition, we wished to test whether stratification of patients according to the clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD reveals subgroups of patients with improved outcomes from coronary CTA compared with usual care. 
Methods 
Patients and study design 
This study included individual patient data from the PROMISE 1 and SCOT-HEART 2 trials. Overall, both studies randomly allocated patients with stable de novo chest pain to coronary CTA testing as part of the initial work-up in addition to or instead of usual care most often including functional testing. 
In SCOT-HEART, patients with stable chest pain ( N = 4146) were investigated with standard of care including exercise electrocardiography as the first performed test in 85% of the patients at the initial clinical attendance, before randomization (see Supplementary data online , Figure S1 ). Additional non-invasive stress imaging could be performed in either group at the discretion of the attending clinician. Patients were randomly allocated (1:1) to either additional coronary CTA or continued standard of care without coronary CTA. In total, 401 (9.8%) patients were excluded in the current analysis from SCOT-HEART due to previously documented CAD (see Supplementary data online , Figure S1 ). 
In PROMISE, patients with stable chest pain ( N = 10 003) with indication for non-urgent, non-invasive cardiovascular testing were randomly allocated (1:1) to a strategy of usual care including initial functional testing (stress electrocardiography, stress echocardiography, nuclear stress testing at the discretion of the investigating physician) or anatomy-based testing using coronary CTA. 
For the primary analysis, patients were stratified according to study randomisation coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up (denoted ‘coronary CTA’) vs. usual care most often including functional testing (collectively denoted ‘usual care’). Secondary analyses included further stratification by clinical likelihood categories according to guideline-recommended thresholds, 5 with tertiary analyses stratifying patients according to non-invasive index test abnormality, initiation of lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapy, and coronary revascularisation. 
Definition of variables and calculation of the clinical likelihood model 
The RF-CL model was calculated from sex, age, and type of chest pain with additional implementation of the number of risk factors ranging from 0 to 5. 7 The calculation is described in detail in the original RF-CL deviation and validation paper, and plugin statistical software packages were developed for STATA and R software. 7 Variables were defined as reported in the Supplementary data online, material , ‘ Definition of variables ’. The RF-CL model was divided into groups of very-low (≤5%), low (>5%–≤15%), and moderate/high (>15%) clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD according to guideline recommendations on thresholds for clinical likelihood-based test referral/deferral. 5 
Diagnostic testing, initiation of lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapy, and revascularisation 
The first-performed non-invasive index test results were stratified into three categories: (i) normal, (ii) inconclusive for functional tests and non-obstructive for coronary CTA, and (iii) abnormal. Thresholds for test abnormality were based on the original definitions in each study (see Supplementary data online , Table S1 ). 2 , 9 Initiation of lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapy was defined as receiving no therapy at baseline and receiving the respective therapies after 6-week follow-up in SCOT-HEART and after 8-week follow-up in PROMISE. 2 , 3 , 9 , 10 Early revascularisation included percutaneous coronary intervention and coronary artery bypass grafting within 90 days of baseline. 2 , 3 , 9 , 10 
Endpoint of outcomes 
The primary endpoint was non-fatal myocardial infarction or all-cause death at 3 years. The secondary endpoint included non-fatal myocardial infarction or death at 5 years. Follow-up data were obtained as described in the Supplementary data online, Material , ‘ Follow-up data ’. The definition of myocardial infarction followed the third international definition. 11 
The primary analysis was performed to assess the overall clinical outcome by study randomisation (coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up vs. standard of care including potential functional testing). Secondary analyses included clinical outcomes among patients stratified by RF-CL. Finally, the post-test management was investigated according to the index test abnormality, initiation of lipid-lowering therapy and antiplatelet therapy, and revascularisation. 
Comparative analysis 
Comparative analyses included patient stratification by the currently recommended 2019 European Society of Cardiology pre-test probability (ESC PTP) model, endorsed by US guidelines in 2021. 5 , 6 
Ethical considerations 
The studies were conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. The respective health committees approved the studies. 1 , 2 All patients signed a written informed consent form. 
Statistical analyses 
Tests and presentation of the present analysis of heterogeneous treatment effects of coronary CTA follows the recommendation in the predictive approaches to treatment effect heterogeneity statement, 12 and test of heterogeneity between RF-CL groups was performed using the likelihood-ratio test. Overall statistical comparisons were performed according to randomisation assignment, and stratification by test abnormality was performed using the first-performed index test. Clinical outcomes were analysed using univariate Cox proportional hazard regression models, and relative risks were expressed as adjusted hazard ratios with associated 95% confidence intervals (CI) and were derived from the Cox models. Cumulative event rates across RF-CL categories were compared using the Kaplan–Meier method. No adjustments were made for multiple testing. Sub-analyses included stratification by sex and age groups (<50, ≥ 50–60, ≥ 60–70, and >70 years of age). Heterogeneous treatment effects were validated with graphical illustration of the depicted hazard ratios as a function continuous RF-CL and analyses with an interaction term between RF-CL categories and randomisation assignment (see Supplementary data online, material ‘ Heterogenous treatment effects ’). Categorical variables were compared using χ 2 test, and two-sided P -values of <.05 were considered statistically significant.

Overall, 370 (2.7%) patients met the primary endpoint (160 myocardial infarctions and 210 deaths). At the pre-defined 3-year end-of-follow-up, the primary endpoint occurred in 313 (2.3%) patients (see Supplementary data online , Table S2 ). 
Clinical outcome according to randomisation 
For 3-year follow-up, myocardial infarction or death occurred in 145 (2.1%) patients randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up compared with 168 (2.4%) patients randomized for usual care [risk difference 0.3%, hazard ratio 0.84 (95% CI .67–1.05), P = .12] ( Figure 1A ).

For the extended follow-up at 5 years, myocardial infarction or death occurred in 162 (2.4%) patients randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up compared with 198 (2.9%) patients randomized for usual care [risk difference 0.5%, hazard ratio 0.73 (95% CI .54–.97), P = .03] ( Figure 1A ). 
Clinical outcome according to clinical likelihood 
Information for calculation of a RF-CL estimate was available in 13 711 (99.7%) patients. Median RF-CL was 7.8% (IQR 3.9%–14.2%), and 4483 (32.7%) patients were categorized as very-low (≤5%), 6093 (44.4%) patients as low (>5%–≤15%), and 3135 (22.9%) patients as moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL, respectively. The proportion of patients with CAD by RF-CL categories, overall and by trial, for patients randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up is shown in Supplementary data online , Table S3 . 
For 3-year follow-up, myocardial infarction or death occurred in 313 (2.3%) patients; 55 (1.2%) in very-low (≤5%) RF-CL patients, 119 (2.0%) in low (>5%–≤15%) RF-CL patients, and 139 (4.4%) in moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL patients. Using very-low (≤5%) RF-CL as reference, patients with low (>5%–≤15%) and moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL had higher event rates: risk difference 0.8%, hazard ratio 1.68 (95% CI 1.23–2.3), P = .001; and risk difference 3.2%, hazard ratio 3.74 (95% CI 2.74–4.11), P < .001, respectively ( Figure 1B ). 
For 3-year follow-up, myocardial infarction or death occurred in 31/2229 (1.4%), 49/3064 (1.6%), and 65/1556 (4.2%) patients randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up with very-low (≤5%), low (>5%–≤15%), and moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL, respectively, with corresponding event rates of 24/2253 (1.1%), 70/3029 (2.3%), and 74/1579 (4.7%) in patients randomized to usual care ( Structured Graphical abstract ). In patients with very-low (≤5%) and moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL, event rates were similar between randomisation groups [coronary CTA vs. usual care; risk difference 0.3%, hazard ratios 1.27 (95% CI .74–2.16), P = .38; and risk difference 0.5%, hazard ratio 0.88 (95% CI .63–1.23), P = .45, respectively] ( Figure 1C and 1E ). In patients with low (>5%–≤15%) RF-CL, event rates were lower in patients randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up compared with usual care [coronary CTA vs. usual care; risk difference 0.7%, hazard ratio 0.67 (95% CI .47–.97), P = .03] ( Figure 1D ). Numbers needed to test using coronary CTA to prevent 1 myocardial infarction or death of −333.3, 142.9, and 200 for patients with very-low (≤5%), low (>5%–≤15%), and moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up compared with usual care. Findings were consistent in both SCOT-HEART and PROMISE, but only statistically significant in SCOT-HEART [risk difference 1.9%, hazard ratio 0.41 (95% CI .20–.87), P = .02; and risk difference 0.3%, hazard ratio 0.75 (95% CI .50–1.14), P = .18, respectively] (see Supplementary data online , Figures S2 and S3 ). 
Sex- and age-stratified analyses are shown in Supplementary data online , Figure S4 . In men aged >70 years with moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL, and women aged 60–70 years with low (>5%–≤15%) RF-CL, event rates were lower in patients randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up compared with usual care. 
Lipid-lowering therapy 
Information on lipid-lowering therapy and abnormality on the first-performed non-invasive test was available in 11 465/13 711 (83.6%) patients. Overall, 5830 (50.9%) patients received lipid-lowering therapy at baseline, with increasing proportions of low (>5%–≤15%) and moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL patients receiving lipid-lowering therapy compared with very-low (≤5%) RF-CL patients ( Figure 2A ).

In patients randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up, there were no difference in the proportion of patients treated with lipid-lowering therapy at baseline compared with patients randomized for usual care [2514 (44.7%) vs. 2550 (43.6%) patients, P = .23]. However, at 6–8 weeks of follow-up, a larger proportion of patients randomized to coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up were treated with lipid-lowering therapy compared with patients randomized for usual care [3124 (55.6%) vs. 2783 (47.6%) patients, P < .001)]. The increase in initiation of lipid-lowering therapy in patients randomized to coronary CTA was based on a high number of abnormal and non-obstructive test results in which more patients were commenced on lipid-lowering therapy compared with abnormal and inconclusive functional test results in patients randomized for usual care. The finding was most pronounced in patients with low (>5%–≤15%) RF-CL ( Figure 2B ). 
Overall, regardless of randomisation and non-invasive test result, few [ n = 201 (1.8%)] patients stopped lipid-lowering therapy at 6–8 weeks of follow-up. 
Antiplatelet therapy 
Information on antiplatelet therapy and abnormality on the first-performed non-invasive index test was available in 10 996/13 711 (80.2%) patients. Overall, 5011 (45.6%) patients received antiplatelet therapy at baseline, with larger proportions of low (>5%–≤15%) and moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL patients compared with very-low (≤5%) RF-CL patients ( Figure 3A ).

Revascularisation 
Information on revascularisation and abnormality on first-performed non-invasive index test was available in 12 979/13 711 (94.7%) patients. In patients randomized for coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up, more patients underwent revascularisation within 90 days compared with patients randomized for usual care [387 (6.5%) vs. 255 (4.0%) patients, P = .02]. The prevalence of abnormal index tests and the proportion of patients undergoing revascularisation and increased with increasing RF-CL ( Figure 4 ).

Discussion 
In this pooled analysis of individual data from the PROMISE and SCOT-HEART trials, which compared coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up to usual care, outcomes were not improved by coronary CTA testing within 3 years of follow-up. In general, outcomes were good with low rates of deaths and myocardial infarctions. However, the analyses found a subgroup of patients with low (>5%–≤15%) clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD, accounting for 44% of patients, with improved outcomes if tested with coronary CTA, while outcomes were similar between allocation arms in patients with very-low (≤5%) and moderate/high (>15%) clinical likelihood. In patients with low (>5%–≤15%) clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD, coronary CTA facilitated increased initiation of lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapy in patients with both non-obstructive and obstructive CAD and increased rates of revascularisation. The findings are particularly important as guidelines only recommend that diagnostic testing may be considered based on clinical judgment in patients with <15% likelihood of obstructive CAD, 5 , 6 and provide no clear guidance on the preferred non-invasive test modality. 
Clinical likelihood models 
At present, the traditional, basic ESC PTP model includes only age, sex, and type of chest pain, and is currently recommended to guide referral of patients with new-onset chest pain for downstream testing including coronary CTA and functional tests. 5 , 6 Overall, previous studies report that coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up is beneficial on outcomes in patients with moderate/high (>15%) ESC PTP, 13 which is consistent with our comparative findings (see Supplementary data online , Figure S5 ). Against a reference standard of invasively assessed coronary stenosis and including risk factors, the RF-CL model was introduced as an alternative to the ESC PTP for obstructive CAD discrimination, yielding superior diagnostic performance and also improved prognostication. 7 , 8 Importantly, by the RF-CL model, a large proportion of patients are down-classified to lower CAD likelihood categories compared with the ESC PTP model, 7 and presently, we demonstrate that the RF-CL model enable evidence-based choice of index test as specifically low (>5%–≤15%) RF-CL patients show improved outcomes from undergoing coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up ( Figure 2 ). 
Overall, the ESC PTP model finds prognostic benefit of coronary CTA in patients [ n = 6541 (47.6%)] with moderate/high (>15%) likelihood of obstructive CAD (see Supplementary data online , Figure S5 ) whereas the benefit of coronary CTA by the RF-CL model is depicted in low (>5%–≤15%) likelihood patients [ n = 6093 (44.4%)] ( Figure 2 ). Notably, outcomes in chronic coronary syndrome are in general good with low rates of deaths and myocardial infarctions ( Figure 2 ). Currently, the 2019 ESC guidelines on chronic coronary syndrome recommend index testing by coronary CTA in patients with new-onset chest pain and lower likelihood of obstructive CAD, whereas patients at higher CAD likelihood ranges should undergo index testing by functional modalities. 4 However, our comparative analyses demonstrated improved outcomes using coronary CTA in moderate/high (>15%) likelihood patients by the ESC PTP model (see Supplementary data online , Figure S5 ). Our RF-CL model down-classifies a substantial proportion of patients to lower CAD likelihood categories, 7 and hence the improved outcomes from coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up compared with usual care in the subset of lower likelihood patients is anticipated. Importantly, the primary evidence behind the current recommendations of index testing by coronary CTA in lower CAD likelihood patients are based on diagnostic accuracy, not prognosis. 14 Further, in patients having multiple risk factors, thus having higher RF-CL estimates compared with ESC PTP estimates, primary medical treatment including statin therapy is often initiated before diagnostic testing, thus explaining that patient management is not improved by coronary CTA as part on the initial diagnostic testing compared with usual care including functional testing in patients with moderate/high (>15%) RF-CL in general. Importantly, patients with fewer risk factors yielding low (>5%–≤15%) RF-CL estimates have lower outcome risk from coronary CTA as specifically non-obstructive CAD is identified ( Figures 2 – 4 ), and non-obstructive CAD account for one-in-two of later myocardial infarctions. 15 
Age alone has a high impact on the probability of obstructive CAD, 7 and younger patients will need to have several risk factors to have a RF-CL measure >15% and often have an indication for preventive medical therapy anyway. In contrast, older patients without risk factors may not receive preventive medical therapy, and identification of non-obstructive and obstructive CAD may change this decision. 
In line with our findings ( Figures 2 and 3 ), studies suggest that identification of non-obstructive CAD by coronary CTA and initiation of preventive medical therapy reduce event rates in stable chest pain patients. 3 , 16 However, clinical likelihood models without utilization of a coronary artery calcification score (i.e. the CACS-weighted clinical likelihood model 7 ) potentially defer testing in patients with non-obstructive coronary lesions (see Supplementary data online , Table S3 ). An alternative is the PROMISE Minimal Risk Score, which improves discrimination of non-atherosclerotic coronary arteries and safely guides testing deferral. 17–19 Whether a strategy to identify those patients estimated to be at minimal risk is preferable compared with a risk factor-based clinical likelihood estimation of obstructive CAD is unknown. 
Impact of coronary CTA 
Coronary CTA is an established first-line diagnostic test with overall similar 3 years risk of major adverse cardiac events compared with initial testing with both functional testing and invasive angiography. 1 , 2 , 20 Nonetheless, whether coronary CTA should be the initial test in suspected angina in general is debated. 4 
In contrast to usual care, using coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up facilitates identification of both non-obstructive and obstructive CAD which enable initiation of preventive treatments including lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapy ( Figures 2 and 3 ). 16 In general, optimized medical treatment improves clinical outcomes in patients with CAD. Thus, substantial evidence supports the benefit of lipid-lowering therapy with an expected ≈22% proportional reduction in risk of major adverse cardiac events per millimole per litre reduction in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol for 5-year follow-up. 21 In our study, the overall commitment to lipid-lowering therapy (initiation + continuation) between allocation arms in patients with low clinical likelihood were similar (60% vs. 52% for coronary CTA vs. usual care; Figure 2 ), while the prevalence of myocardial infarctions specifically was nearly three-fold reduced (see Supplementary data online , Table S4 ), hypothesizing that lipid-lowering therapy as a consequence of CTA is more appropriate. 
Less evidence supports the benefit from antiplatelet therapy in patients who do not undergo revascularisation or suffer a myocardial infarction. Early meta-analyses suggest a ≈12% proportional reduction in risk of major adverse cardiac events from aspirin treatment but recent randomized studies do not demonstrate such benefit. 22 , 23 
Compared with functional testing, coronary CTA improves identification of obstructive CAD with subsequently increased revascularisation rates, which may also prevent cardiovascular events. 1 , 2 A large meta-analysis suggested a ≈20% proportional reduction in MACE risk from revascularisation for 4-year follow-up. 24 However, this effect has not been demonstrated in large randomized trials. 25–27 Overall, the observed effect of improved outcomes by an index strategy using coronary CTA was mainly driven by fewer myocardial infarctions (see Supplementary data online , Tables S2 , S4 , and S5 , Supplementary data online , Figure S8 ), but whether the observed risk reduction is influenced by even lower rates of spontaneous events that are partly neutralized by increased rates of periprocedural events in an index strategy using coronary CTA compared with usual care warrants further investigation. 
In general, the present study was not designed to evaluate potential causes for improved outcomes by coronary CTA testing of de novo chest pain patients with low (>5%–≤15%) clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD. However, it is reasonable to believe that coronary CTA appropriately guide initiation of lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapy and additionally revascularisation, which all contributed to the observed favourable outcomes compared with usual care in low (>5%–≤15%) RF-CL patients ( Figure 1 ). Importantly, we acknowledge that the effect of dedicated prevention in patients with non-obstructive CAD was largely unknown during enrolment of PROMISE and SCOT-HEART, and patients, particularly those with very low clinical likelihood, may have been undertreated compared with present day. Similarly, study enrolment precedes findings of the International Study of Comparative Health Effectiveness With Medical and Invasive Approaches (ISCHEMIA) trial, 25 and chances are that patients in the present analysis underwent inappropriate invasive angiographies. 
Clinical implications 
The RF-CL model improves discrimination of obstructive CAD and also prognostication, 7 , 8 with presently also a reported ability to guide decisions on index test strategy, specifically in patients with low (5%–≤15%) clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD ( Figure 1 ). Across various study cohorts, low (5%–≤15%) RF-CL patients account for two in five patients with de novo chest pain, 7 , 28 who based on a coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up would be relatively risk reduced by ∼30% over 3 years compared usual care ( Structured Graphical Abstract ). Equally as important, outcomes of death and myocardial infarction are similar whether patients undergo index testing using coronary CTA or usual care when the clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD is very-low (≤5%) and moderate/high (>15%) ( Figure 1 ), and the general consensus that very-low (≤5%) RF-CL patients should be deferred from downstream testing, and moderate/high (>15%) clinical likelihood patients should be referred for downstream testing using either anatomical or functional testing depending of the magnitude of clinical likelihood, remain in force. 5 , 6 Currently, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence recommend index testing by coronary CTA in all patients with possible angina that will include patients with moderate/high (>15%) clinical likelihood, 29 but our findings support that both anatomical and functional testing is prognostically appropriate in higher likelihood patients. In general, the number needed to test using coronary CTA in patients with low clinical likelihood to prevent one event over a period of 3 years is 143, which could question whether differing of tests, or even testing at all, is appropriate in this subset of patients. However, this study was not designed to investigate this question as almost all patients underwent testing. 
Limitations 
Some limitations apply for this analysis; selection bias cannot be excluded as all patients had indication for diagnostic evaluation. Second, patients with known CAD were excluded from the present analysis, and results only apply to de novo chest pain patients. Similarly, patients with severe kidney failure and severe obesity, which are not good candidates for coronary CTA, were excluded. Additionally, the cohorts consisted primarily of Caucasian patients, limiting the external validity of study results to this group. Third, this is a post hoc analysis and results are primarily hypothesis-generating and need confirmation in a randomized design with appropriate power. Fourth, it could be argued that a large proportion of patients undergoing usual care, especially the patients participating SCOT-HEART, were examined using diagnostically inferior functional modalities, including e.g. exercise electrocardiography, compared with coronary CTA. However, since the intended choice of functional test was used to stratify randomisation in the PROMISE study, we were not able to compare different functional tests against each other. Fifth, inclusion criteria, baseline characteristics ( Table 1 ), and the primary endpoints were different between the SCOT-HEART and PROMISE trials, and neither study applied a composite of only myocardial infarction or all-cause death as a primary endpoint. However, both studies randomized for coronary CTA, and there was an overall tendency of improved outcomes by coronary CTA and specifically in patients with low (>5%–≤15%) clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD across both studies (see Supplementary data online , Figures S2 and S3 ). Notably, our decision to include only myocardial infarction and all-cause death as the primary endpoint was likely to favour coronary CTA in the present pooled analysis since both trials showed clear trends in favour of the coronary-CTA arm for myocardial infarction though non-significant. 1 , 2 Additionally, our findings were primarily a consequence of lower rates of myocardial infarction in patients allocated to coronary CTA (see Supplementary data online , Table S2 ), and though follow-up data can be biased, our endpoint consisted of hard events with high validity (see Supplementary data online, Material , ‘ Follow-up data ’). 1 , 30 However, it can be argued that event rates of myocardial infarction were higher in SCOT-HEART compared with PROMISE (see Supplementary data online , Table S4 ), and as this did not translate into higher mortality rates at 3-years follow-up, the lack of event adjudication in SCOT-HEART may have impact on the results. Sixth, overall follow-up time was different between SCOT-HEART [4.7 (IQR 4.0–5.6) years] and PROMISE [2.1 (IQR 1.5–2.8) years], and the pre-defined endpoint of 3 years was chosen to preclude our observed effects of index testing by coronary CTA from 3 to 5 years to solely be based on SCOT-HEART data. However, in the PROMISE study, end of follow-up was < 3 years in many patients, which could impact the overall results, but our findings were consistent, though not significant, at both 1 and 2 years of follow-up (see Supplementary data online , Table S6 ). Seventh, both the PROMISE and SCOT-HEART trials were conducted almost a decade ago, and advancements in coronary CTA, functional testing, risk factor therapy, and interventional cardiology procedures could potentially impact the applicability of our findings in a contemporary setting. Finally, we acknowledge that other considerations than outcomes should be taken into consideration when choosing the appropriate index test of a patient with new-onset chest pain, e.g. establishing a diagnosis, resource utilization, accessibility, and symptom control. 
Conclusion 
Patients with new-onset chest pain have a favourable prognosis, and there is generally no outcome difference whether an index strategy of coronary CTA or functional testing is applied. However, in the subset of patients with low clinical likelihood of obstructive CAD, which account for 44% of all patients, risk is lower among patients allocated to coronary CTA vs. usual care, primarily driven by fewer myocardial infarctions in the SCOT-HEART cohort, with an absolute risk reduction of 0.7% over 3-year follow-up. In patients with very-low (≤5%) and moderate/high (>15%) clinical likelihood, coronary CTA as part of the initial work-up did not improve outcomes.
