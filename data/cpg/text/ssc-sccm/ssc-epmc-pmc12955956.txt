Abstract 
Objectives: 
The clinical efficacy of short-acting β-blockers in the management of sepsis remains uncertain. In particular, the comparative effects of two commonly used agents—esmolol and landiolol—have not been clearly established. This network meta-analysis aims to systematically evaluate and compare the effects of esmolol, landiolol, and standard of care (SOC) on mortality in patients with sepsis. 
Data Sources: 
A systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, MEDLINE, CENTRAL, ClinicalTrials.gov , preprints, and citation searching was conducted before April 15, 2025. 
Study Selection: 
Randomized controlled trials that enrolled adult patients (≥ 18 yr) diagnosed with sepsis or septic shock and treated with β-blockers and conducted in ICUs. 
Data Extraction: 
Data were extracted on study characteristics, enrolled patients’ characteristics, administration strategies of drugs, and key clinical outcomes (including 28-d mortality, ICU length of stay, and other relevant endpoints). 
Data Synthesis: 
A total of 1165 records were identified through searches of five databases, registries, and relevant references up to April 15, 2025. Ten studies involving 1035 patients were included, after screening and eligibility assessment. Compared with esmolol, landiolol was associated with increased 28-day mortality (relative risk [RR], 1.57; 95% CI, 1.08–2.30; low certainty) and higher norepinephrine requirements (mean difference [MD], 0.17 μg/kg/min; 95% CI, 0.02–0.32; low certainty). Esmolol significantly reduced 28-day mortality (RR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.56–0.85; moderate certainty) and 24-hour heart rate (MD, –16.92 beats/min; 95% CI, –23.49 to –10.36; moderate certainty) compared with SOC. In contrast, landiolol increased norepinephrine use compared with SOC (MD, 0.09 μg/kg/min; 95% CI, 0.01–0.18; moderate certainty). 
Conclusions: 
Among patients with sepsis treated with β-blockers, esmolol probably improves clinical outcomes compared with SOC. However, the effect of landiolol remains uncertain due to the low certainty of evidence. Esmolol may confer a relative clinical advantage over landiolol, but further studies are needed to confirm this finding and elucidate the underlying mechanisms. 
Keywords: beta-blockers, esmolol, landiolol, network meta-analysis, sepsis 
KEY POINTS. 
Question : Among adults with sepsis, is esmolol superior to landiolol in reducing mortality, given both are short-acting β-blockers? 
Findings : This network meta-analysis found esmolol significantly reduced 28-day mortality vs. standard care, whereas landiolol did not. Indirect comparisons indicated esmolol lowered mortality relative to landiolol, achieved greater heart rate reduction, and was associated with lower norepinephrine requirement than landiolol. 
Meaning : Esmolol may offer a potential clinical advantage over landiolol in reducing mortality among patients with sepsis, although direct comparative evidence is needed. 
Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a major global health issue, causing 48.9 million cases and 11 million deaths annually—19.7% of global mortality ( 1 – 3 ). Current sepsis management primarily relies on two key components: early administration of antimicrobial therapy and organ support ( 4 ). Although these strategies have substantially reduced sepsis-related mortality over the past century, mortality rates have plateaued in the last decade ( 5 ). This highlights the need for new therapies, with β-blockers emerging as a promising option in recent years. 
Initially developed in the 1960s for angina, β-blockers are now widely used for various cardiovascular diseases ( 6 ). While the 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommends catecholamines like norepinephrine as first-line agents, their overuse can lead to sympathetic overactivation and tachycardia, which is linked to poor outcomes ( 7 , 8 ). Persistent tachycardia is an independent risk factor for increased mortality in sepsis ( 9 ). By blunting excessive adrenergic stimulation, β-blockers may improve outcomes in patients with sepsis ( 10 ). They may also modulate immunity by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and altering immune cell function, potentially reversing sepsis-induced immunosuppression ( 11 , 12 ). 
Esmolol and landiolol are the main short-acting β-blockers used in sepsis treatment. Multiple clinical studies have investigated the efficacy of esmolol and landiolol in this context. For example, Morelli et al ( 13 ) conducted a randomized controlled trial (RCT) involving 154 patients to evaluate esmolol, while several large-scale RCTs of landiolol have been conducted, including two in Europe and one in Japan ( 14 – 16 ). Landiolol exhibits greater β1-receptor selectivity than esmolol (255-fold vs. 33-fold selectivity for β1 over β2 receptors) and is associated with lower toxicity ( 17 , 18 ). In addition, landiolol has a shorter elimination half-life (4 min) than esmolol (9 min) ( 19 , 20 ). Additional studies have also demonstrated differences between the two agents in heart rate (HR) control and negative inotropic effects ( 21 , 22 ). However, prior meta-analyses either grouped both drugs as short-acting β-blockers or focused solely on esmolol, lacking direct comparisons. We hypothesize that, although both are short-acting β 1 -selective blockers, esmolol may be superior to landiolol in reducing mortality and improving hemodynamic outcomes. Thus, we conducted this network meta-analysis (NMA) of RCTs to assess differential effects between esmolol and landiolol in sepsis. 
Our NMA used direct comparisons to evaluate esmolol vs. standard of care (SOC) and landiolol vs. SOC, and applied indirect comparison methods to explore potential clinical differences between esmolol and landiolol. 
METHODS 
This systematic review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251033640) and reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines for NMA ( 23 , 24 ). 
Search Strategy 
We searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, MEDLINE, CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov up to April 15, 2025, using the terms “sepsis”/“septic shock” and “β-blockers” (full strategy in eTable 1 , https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). We also searched medRxiv and preprints with The Lancet for additional records and manually screened references from prior reviews and eligible studies. 
Study Selection and Inclusion Criteria 
We included only RCTs involving adult ICU patients (≥ 18 yr) with sepsis or septic shock. Sepsis and septic shock were defined as reported by the investigators in the included RCTs. We compared the outcomes of IV esmolol, landiolol, and SOC (without β-blockers). In the SOC group, patients received usual care without the administration of β-blockers or placebo. We excluded non-RCTs, studies on non-ICU patients, β-blocker use before or after ICU admission, and studies that did not clearly distinguish between esmolol and landiolol or assessed them together. 
Outcomes 
To evaluate clinical benefits, we predefined six outcome measures, comprising one primary and five secondary outcomes. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality, a patient-centered outcome. Secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay (LOS), reduction in HR, lactate level, mean arterial pressure at 24 hours, and 24-hour norepinephrine requirement. 
Data Extraction and Risk of Bias Assessment 
Two independent reviewers (Z.T., F.P.) screened titles, abstracts, and full texts during the first and second rounds of selection using EndNote (X9, Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA) to manage references and facilitate the screening process. Data extraction, including patient characteristics, interventions, and outcomes, was conducted independently by two reviewers (Q.S., J.X.), and each study was assessed for Risk of Bias (RoB) using the RoB 2.0 tool (Cochrane Collaboration, London, United Kingdom) ( 25 ). Following data extraction, a verification process was conducted. For any unclear data, the corresponding authors were contacted. Reviewers resolved discrepancies by discussion or, if necessary, by adjudication with a third reviewer (Y.Y.). 
Statistical Analysis 
Data Synthesis 
For binary outcomes, we used relative risk (RR) with 95% CIs, while mean differences (MDs) with 95% CI were used for continuous outcomes. For binary outcomes, we extracted the number of events and total cases in both the experimental and control groups. For continuous outcomes, we recorded the original data, and where medians (with ranges/interquartile ranges) were reported, we converted them to means with sd s for subsequent analysis ( 26 , 27 ). 
We performed NMA using both frequentist and Bayesian approaches. For outcomes with significant differences in the network analysis, we also conducted standard pairwise meta-analyses. A frequentist NMA was conducted using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX) with the network package, which was also employed for standard pairwise meta-analyses. A graph-theoretical approach was applied using frequentist NMA ( 28 ). R (Version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the gemtc and rjags packages was used to build a Bayesian model, integrating clinical trial data via the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method. A random-effects model was used for standard pairwise meta-analyses. Additionally, we used Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) to analyze the primary outcome (TSA software, Version 0.9.5.10; Copenhagen Trial Unit, Copenhagen, Denmark). The effect size was the odds ratio, with information size and adjusted boundaries at α risk 5% and β error 20%. 
To assess heterogeneity across studies, we used visual inspection of the forest plots, supplemented by evaluation of the I 2 statistic ( 29 ). Given the limited number of included studies (≤ 10), the statistical power for detecting publication bias is low ( 30 ). Accordingly, Egger’s test was conducted for outcomes involving 10 studies, supplemented by visual inspection using funnel plots ( 31 , 32 ). 
Sensitivity Analysis and Subgroup Analysis 
Given the limited number of included studies, to ensure that no single trial disproportionately influenced the results, we performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis by excluding each study in turn and assessing effect size changes. When greater than or equal to 2 trials contributed data to a subgroup, we carried out the prespecified subgroup analyses by language (English vs. non-English), sepsis definition (Sepsis-2 vs. Sepsis-3), initial dose (> 200 vs. ≤ 200 mg/hr), and study setting (single-center vs. multicenter). We also ran meta-regressions for each prespecified variable (R, Version 4.4.3; gemtc package). For variables that showed evidence of effect modification, we assessed the credibility of the observed modification using the Instrument to assess the Credibility of Effect Modification Analyses (ICEMAN) instrument ( 33 ). 
Certainty of Evidence 
To assess the certainty of evidence for each outcome, we applied the Grading of Recommendations, Assessments, Developments, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach for NMA ( 34 ). For direct comparisons, we assessed RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias according to guidelines to obtain a preliminary certainty rating ( 29 , 35 – 38 ), followed by assessment of imprecision to reach the final certainty rating ( 39 ). For indirect comparisons, the initial certainty was the lowest preliminary rating among contributing direct comparisons, subsequently downgraded for intransitivity and imprecision. Notably, to assess intransitivity, we performed network meta-regression as recommended and examined distributions of potential effect modifiers across groups ( 40 ). Due to methodological limitations in assessing intransitivity, we routinely downgraded by one level regardless of the presence of effect modification and further downgraded if modification was identified. The detailed assessment process and results are presented in eTables 8 and 10 ( https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). 
RESULTS 
Study Selection 
We identified 1133 articles ( eFig. 1 , https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ), and after screening, ten RCTs (2013–2023) from seven countries met inclusion criteria ( 13 – 16 , 41 – 46 ) ( eTable 2 , http://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). Due to the limited number of studies on esmolol and landiolol in sepsis, no language restrictions were applied. Ultimately, three Chinese studies were included in the esmolol vs. SOC arm ( 43 , 45 , 46 ). Seven studies compared esmolol with SOC ( 13 , 41 – 46 ), while the remaining three compared landiolol with SOC ( 14 – 16 ). No direct comparative evidence between esmolol and landiolol was available. 
Characteristics of Included Studies 
Our review included ten RCTs involving 1035 sepsis patients: 562 in the esmolol vs. SOC arm and 473 in the landiolol vs. SOC arm ( Table 1 ). Sample sizes ranged from 40 to 196; patient age from 58 to 69; and male proportion from 51.0% to 76.6%. Notably, eight studies enrolled patients with sepsis and tachycardia (HR > 95 or > 100 beats/min [bpm]); the remaining two lacked explicit criteria but reported baseline HRs consistent with tachycardia. Network plots are shown in Figure 1 ; and eFigure 2 ( https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ); demographics and treatment details are in eTables 3–5 ( https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ).

Outcomes 
Primary Outcome 
A total of nine studies reported 28-day mortality data ( eTable 6 , https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ) ( 13 – 16 , 41 , 43 – 46 ), involving 993 patients. 
In esmolol vs. SOC arm, esmolol was associated with a reduction in patient mortality (RR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.56–0.85), with consistent findings in Bayesian analysis ( I 2 = 31.3%; moderate certainty) ( Fig. 2 and Table 2 ). The standard pairwise meta-analysis also revealed a significant difference between esmolol and SOC (RR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.57–0.84) ( eFig. 6 , https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ).

In landiolol vs. SOC arm, landiolol did not reduce mortality compared with SOC (RR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.79–1.50), with consistent results in the Bayesian analysis ( I 2 = 48.7%; low certainty) (Fig. 2 and Table 2 ). The standard pairwise meta-analysis also revealed no significant difference (RR, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.72–1.58) (eFig. 6, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). 
Indirect comparison revealed that esmolol reduced mortality compared with landiolol (RR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.18–2.17; low certainty), with consistent findings across both analytical approaches (Fig. 2 and Table 2 ). 
We further evaluated the ranking probabilities of the three treatment options using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) plots. Esmolol showed the highest probability of being the most effective treatment (99.5%), while SOC and landiolol had second-rank probabilities of 62.5% and 37.0%, respectively, suggesting that landiolol may be the least effective among the three ( eFig. 5 , https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). 
To further assess the robustness of these findings, TSA was conducted. For the esmolol group, the cumulative Z curve crossed both the conventional and trial sequential monitoring boundaries, indicating a statistically significant difference compared with SOC; however, only 76% of the required information size (RIS) was reached (684 patients). In contrast, TSA could not be performed for the landiolol group due to the limited number of included trials ( eFig. 10 , https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). 
Other Secondary Outcomes 
A total of eight studies reported overall LOS in ICU ( 13 – 15 , 41 – 43 , 45 , 46 ), while one study provided ICU LOS only for survivors ( 16 ) (eTable 6, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). To address this, we contacted the corresponding author to obtain the original dataset and subsequently recalculated the relevant outcomes. No statistically significant differences were identified among esmolol vs. SOC, landiolol vs. SOC, or esmolol vs. landiolol comparisons (very low certainty; Fig. 2 ; Table 2 ). 
Nine studies contributed data on HR at 24 hours post-treatment ( 13 – 16 , 41 , 43 – 46 ) (eTable 6, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). NMA demonstrated a notable reduction in 24-hour HR with esmolol compared with SOC (MD, –16.92; 95% CI, –23.49 to –10.36; moderate certainty) (Fig. 2 ; and eTable 8, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ), consistent with findings from conventional pairwise meta-analysis (eFig. 6, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). Conversely, comparisons involving landiolol revealed no significant changes in HR vs. either SOC (low certainty) or esmolol (very low certainty) (Fig. 2 ; and eTable 8, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). 
Six studies evaluated norepinephrine requirements 24 hours after intervention ( 13 – 16 , 42 , 44 ) (eTable 6, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). No significant difference was observed for esmolol vs. SOC (low certainty). Results for landiolol vs. SOC were inconsistent between the frequentist (significant difference: MD, 0.09; 95% CI, 0.01–0.18) and Bayesian analyses (no significant difference: MD, 0.09; 95% CI, –0.03 to 0.21; moderate certainty). For landiolol vs. esmolol, the frequentist and Bayesian analyses also showed inconsistent results (frequentist MD, 0.17; 95% CI, 0.02–0.32; Bayesian MD, 0.18; 95% CI, –0.01 to 0.37; low certainty) (eFig. 2 and eTable 8, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). 
Sensitivity Analysis and Subgroup Analysis 
Most outcomes remained stable in the sensitivity analyses, indicating limited influence from individual trials, except for the 24-hour norepinephrine requirement in the landiolol vs. esmolol comparison, which was less robust ( eFig. 8 , https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). Notably, because no prespecified subgroup showed statistical evidence of effect modification (all interaction p ≥ 0.10), we did not apply the ICEMAN instrument, and the treatment effect was deemed consistent across all subgroups ( eTable 9 and eFig. 9 , https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). 
DISCUSSION 
Main Findings 
In our NMA of RCTs in adults with sepsis, esmolol probably reduced the risk of 28-day mortality compared with SOC, whereas landiolol may increase the risk compared with esmolol. Esmolol also probably reduced 24-hour HR by 16.92 bpm vs. SOC. Landiolol was associated with potentially higher norepinephrine requirements compared with both SOC and esmolol, whereas esmolol had no such effect. 
Our findings differ from those of a recent meta-analysis that pooled all short-acting β-blockers together ( 47 ) and reported a possible mortality reduction with β-blockers as a class. We separated esmolol and landiolol and found that only esmolol showed a significant mortality benefit while landiolol did not. This divergence suggests that the class effect observed previously might have been driven predominantly by esmolol. By pooling the drugs, important differences may have been obscured. SUCRA results similarly indicated that esmolol exhibited superior efficacy in reducing 28-day mortality, shortening LOS in ICU, achieving better HR control, and lowering norepinephrine requirements (eFig. 5, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H853 ). These findings highlight the need to evaluate β-blockers individually rather than as a class. The TSA for the esmolol group did not reach the RIS, suggesting that further evidence—particularly from large multicenter trials—is needed; for landiolol, the limited data highlight an even greater need for future research. 
Several mechanisms may underlie this result: 1) Early sepsis features elevated catecholamines, excess catecholamines precipitate tachycardia, contributing to myocardial dysfunction and hemodynamic deterioration. Selective β-blockers may partially attenuate these downstream effects through HR reduction ( 48 ). 2) selective β 1 -blockers can antagonize adrenergic effects, thereby countering the excessive sympathetic activation that occurs during the stress response ( 49 ). 3) Emerging evidence also suggests that β-blockers may exert immunomodulatory effects, which could improve outcomes in sepsis independently of their cardiac actions ( 11 ). 4) Esmolol is less β 1 -selective than landiolol (33:1 vs. 255:1). Its relatively greater β 2 blockade may promote peripheral vasoconstriction, thereby helping to sustain arterial pressure and reducing the need for vasopressors—findings consistent with our study. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, and we are conducting subsequent RCT to further validate this hypothesis. 
Esmolol is more effective than landiolol in reducing HR in septic tachycardia patients within 24 hours. However, this finding contradicts theoretical expectations, as landiolol, a highly selective β-1 receptor antagonist, primarily targets cardiac β-1 receptors to inhibit catecholaminergic stimulation. Although landiolol exhibits greater β-1 selectivity, it did not significantly reduce HR in our analysis. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. 1) The dosages of landiolol were substantially lower than those used for esmolol, both for initial and maximum allowable doses, which may have contributed to the lack of a significant HR-lowering effect. 2) Due to variations in the time points at which HR was reported across different studies, our outcome focused exclusively on HR control at 24 hours. This restriction in time point selection may explain why landiolol group failed to demonstrate a significant effect within that timeframe. Among the three studies in the landiolol group, two reported differences in HR compared with controls at later time points ( 15 , 16 ), and one study found a significant difference in the proportion of patients achieving a composite endpoint (HR within the range of 80–94 bpm) ( 14 ). 
Based on the above analysis, we propose that early and timely HR control can effectively reduce mortality in patients with sepsis complicated by tachycardia. In our NMA, this is reflected by esmolol demonstrating better HR control within the first 24 hours compared with landiolol, which results in a significant reduction in 28-day mortality. This conclusion is also supported by the study by Kakihana et al ( 15 ), although landiolol did not show a significant difference in mortality among the overall patient population, a subgroup of patients who achieved the primary endpoint (HR of 60–94 bpm at 24 hr) showed a significant reduction in 28-day mortality. Earlier studies have also shown that the trend of HR changes (particularly a decrease) during the first 24 hours serves as one of the strongest early indicators of patient survival ( 50 ). Future studies should further explore the impact of specific time points for HR control on prognosis and investigate the optimal timing of drug administration. Additionally, attention should be given to the differences in clinical outcomes based on varying baseline HRs and dosing strategies. 
Notably, we applied GRADE rigorously to all outcomes and found that most were rated low certainty, indicating that conclusions in this field remain uncertain. Accordingly, our conclusions are exploratory and offered as possible directions for future research, which will require further validation. 
Strength and Limitations 
Previous meta-analyses have frequently grouped esmolol and landiolol together as short-acting β-blockers ( 47 , 51 – 53 ), without adequately addressing the pharmacological and clinical differences between them. This NMA is the first to systematically examine pairwise comparisons among esmolol, landiolol, and SOC, and provides, via indirect comparison, the first evidence-based comparison between esmolol and landiolol, thereby addressing a critical gap in clinical research. To minimize between-study heterogeneity, we conducted a systematic and rigorous study selection process, with transparent eligibility criteria and reporting. Additionally, Multiple analytical approaches were employed to enhance the robustness and validity of the findings. This study has several limitations. First, the small number of available trials reduced the overall reliability of the findings. Some sensitivity analyses were influenced by single studies, reflecting the fragility of evidence in this field. Additionally, notable heterogeneity exists between the esmolol vs. SOC and landiolol vs. SOC arms in study settings, dosing, sepsis definitions, and other factors. Although no effect modification was detected, these differences may compromise transitivity. Further research is needed to strengthen the evidence base, particularly direct comparisons between esmolol and landiolol in large, multicenter trials. 
CONCLUSIONS 
With moderate certainty of evidence, esmolol probably reduces 28-day mortality in patients with sepsis and achieves 24-hour HR control. However, its effects on norepinephrine requirements remain uncertain due to the low certainty of the evidence. For landiolol, the effect on mortality is uncertain. Esmolol indicates potential clinical advantages over landiolol in reducing 28-day mortality and norepinephrine requirements, but direct comparative trials are needed to confirm these results.
