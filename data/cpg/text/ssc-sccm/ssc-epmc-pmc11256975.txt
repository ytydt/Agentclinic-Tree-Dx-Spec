Abstract 
Objective: 
The Society of Critical Care Medicine last published an intensivist definition in 1992. Subsequently, there have been many publications relating to intensivists. Our purpose is to assess how contemporary studies define intensivist physicians. 
Design: 
Systematic search of PubMed, EMBASE and Web of Science (2010–2020) of publication titles with the terms intensivist, and critical care or intensive care physician, specialist, or consultant. We included studies focusing upon adult US intensivists and excluded non-data driven reports, non-US publications, and pediatric or neonatal ICU reports. We aggregated the study title intensivist nomenclatures and parsed the introduction and method sections to discern the text used to define intensivists. 14 parameters were found and grouped into five definitional categories: (A) No definition, (B) Background training and certification, (C) Works in ICU, (D) Staffing, and (E) Database related. Each study was re-evaluated against these parameters and grouped into 3 definitional classes (single, multiple, or no definition). The prevalence of each parameter is compared between groups using Fisher’s exact test. 
Setting: 
US adult ICUs and databases. 
Interventions: 
None. 
Measurements and Main Results: 
105 of 657 studies (16%) met inclusion criteria. Within the study titles, 17 phrases were used to describe an Intensivist; these were categorized as Intensivist in 61 (58%), Specialty Intensivist in 30 (29%) and ICU/Critical Care Physician in 14 (13%). 31 (30%) studies used a single parameter (B-E) as their definition, 63 (60%) used more than one parameter (B-E) as their definition and 11 (10%) had no definition (A). The most common parameter “Works in ICU” (C) in 52 (50%) was more likely to be used in conjunction with other parameters rather than as a standalone parameter (multiple vs single-parameter studies; 73% vs 17%; p<0.0001). 
Conclusions: 
There was no consistency of intensivist nomenclature or definitions in contemporary adult intensivist studies in the US. 
Keywords: Intensivist, pulmonary critical care, intensive care physician, critical care specialist, neurointensivist, neurocritical care physicians, critical care cardiologist, surgical intensivist, surgical critical care, trauma intensivist, critical care trained emergency medicine physician, definition 
Critical Care Medicine was first advanced as a new subspecialty in the United States (US) in the late 1970s ( 1 ). By 2015, there were approximately 28,800 privileged intensivists in the US ( 2 ). The Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) Guidelines Committee published its only definition of an intensivist in 1992 ( 3 ). At its core, the SCCM definition focused upon two elements, the successful completion of a critical care medicine (CCM) fellowship training program approved by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) and CCM board certification. The SCCM definition also addressed the many roles expected of an intensivist (i.e., time devoted to intensive care unit (ICU) practice, procedural competencies, and participation in ICU administration, education, and research). In 2001, two taskforces of the American College of CCM used the 1992 SCCM intensivist definition in their deliberations of the intensivist’s role in ascertaining the best ICU practice models ( 4 ). In 2016, the World Federation of Societies of Intensive and CCM, a group that also includes the SCCM, defined an intensivist (also termed an ICU specialist) as a medical professional trained in intensive or critical care according to standards set by a certifying body ( 5 ). This definition addressed the intensivist’s patient care responsibilities, time in the ICU and clinical decision making. Currently, the SCCM Statistics website defines an intensivist very concisely as a physician with CCM training and credentialing ( 6 ). 
Proprietary national organizations, healthcare databases, and global insurers also define intensivists and include codes to identify or locate intensivists. For example, the Leapfrog Group ICU Physician Factsheet defines an intensivist as a board-certified physician with added certification in a subspecialty of CCM ( 7 ). The American Hospital Association (AHA) defines an intensivist as a “physician with special training to work with critically ill patients” ( 8 ). The on-line Merriam-Webster dictionary added the terms intensivist and critical care specialist to its lexicon in April 2020 defining them similarly as a physician who specializes in the care and treatment of patients in intensive care ( 9 ). The American Medical Association (AMA) Physician Masterfile uses eight intensivist “self-designation” codes to identify intensivists ( 10 ). The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) also utilizes multiple taxonomy codes from the National Uniform Claim Committee (NUCC) to identify healthcare providers including intensivists and suggests that all physicians self-designate themselves ( 11 ). Cigna healthcare, a global health company, defines a critical care medicine specialist, or intensivist, as medical doctors who can be certified, and specialize in the care of people who are in an ICU ( 12 ). 
The administrative, clinical, educational and research ramifications that result from the absence of a current comprehensive SCCM based intensivist definition in conjunction with the diversity in intensivist definitions and identification codes noted above are unknown. Herein, we sought to determine and analyze how contemporary studies that involve intensivist physicians caring for patients in US adult ICUs are defining and identifying or finding intensivists. We then suggest potential implications of our findings. 
Materials and Methods 
Literature Search 
Three electronic databases (PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science) were systematically searched by the hospital librarian (LB) for articles written in English that focused on intensivists and were published between January 2010 and December 2020. Keywords related to intensivists, including various sub-specialty intensivists (i.e., Critical Care Cardiologists, Critical Care Anesthesiologists, Anesthesiologist Intensivists, Neurointensivists, Neurocritical Care Physicians, Critical Care Trained Emergency Medicine Physicians, and Surgical Critical Care, Surgical or Trauma Intensivists) and critical care or intensive care physicians, specialists, or consultants were sought using proximity searching with title field codes that were combined with the Boolean operator OR in each database. Keywords for pediatric and neonatal were then excluded from results using the NOT operator. 
Screening 
A total of 657 unique citations were identified and screened for patient population, publication type, and geographic location. Of all citations screened for study relevance, a total of 105 were included in the final analysis. ( Figure 1 , Supplementary Appendix 1. Search Strategy ).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria 
For analysis, we included only studies performed in US adult ICUs or using US physician databases primarily dealing with adult intensivists. At the outset of this study, we assumed that the adult intensivists as listed in the study titles of the included studies would at a minimum be CCM fellowship trained and certified attending physicians. We excluded perspective pieces, commentaries, case reports, literature reviews, guidelines, consensus statements, round table discussions, editorials, correspondences, and letters to the editor or studies that included pediatric or neonatal ICU data or contained non-US data ( Figure 1 ). 
Definitions for intensivists and database mining strategies 
After identifying the studies for inclusion, we parsed their introduction and methods sections to determine the parameters that we perceived were used by the study authors to define, describe, or identify and/or locate intensivists. Finally, in conjunction with the librarian, we examined the citations of each included study to determine whether the 1992 SCCM or 2016 World Federation intensivist definitions were cited ( 3 , 5 ). 
Overall, we found 14 parameters and grouped them into five definitional categories ( Table 1 ): (A) No definition (1 parameter), (B) Background training and certification (2 parameters), (C) Works in an ICU (1 parameter), (D) Staffing (4 parameters), and (E) Database-related (6 parameters). Upon completion of this exercise, we re-evaluated the included studies to determine the exact parameters used within the introduction and methods texts of each study. Based upon our findings, we then divided the studies into 3 definitional classes: single (including one parameter only from B-E), multiple (including more than one parameter from B-E), or none (A). Each study was individually reviewed by two authors (AD, NH) and then adjudicated as necessary with the other authors.

Intensivist nomenclature in study titles 
We tabulated the intensivist nomenclature used in the study titles. For simplicity of presentation, we elected to classify these studies based upon the terminology that the authors used in their titles. The first is Intensivist (includes non-medical adjectives, i.e., academic, adult, night); the second is Specialty Intensivist (includes medical type adjectives, i.e., Neurointensivist, Surgical or Trauma Intensivist, Critical Care Cardiologist, Critical Care Trained Emergency Medicine Physician, Medical, Pulmonary Critical Care); and the third is ICU and Critical Care Physician . 
Study types 
To understand the framework of the studies at a high level, we analyzed the overall types of studies that were performed and their data sources and identified four study types. The first was a hospital based “patient” analysis (HBPA) where the authors use data from a hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) or from proprietary ICU data systems (i.e., APACHE, Project Impact) and determine patient outcomes. The second was a hospital-based “staff” analysis (HBSA) where interventions in ICU staffing models are linked to patient outcomes. The third included survey studies (i.e., surveys of intensivists and/or other clinicians associated with ICUs from a variety of datasets) and the last was others (i.e., literature reviews, miscellaneous). 
Journal of publication 
We determined the number of individual journals that comprised the included studies and relate the intensivist nomenclature in the titles with the journals. 
Statistical analysis 
Studies were stratified based upon the number of intensivist parameters ( Table 1 ) used: single-definition (included one parameter from categories B-E), multi-definition (included two or more parameters from categories B-E) and no definition (the one parameter from definitional category A). Study-level characteristics were summarized as frequency (percentage) or median (interquartile range, IQR). The relationship between the number of studies selected per year and publication year was quantified using Spearman correlation coefficient. Shifts in the distribution of nomenclature, study types, and the number of parameters used in definitions (single parameter, multi-parameter or none) over time were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests. The proportion of studies that used each parameter was compared between nomenclature, study types, and the number of parameters using Fisher’s exact test. The distribution of nomenclature was compared between study types using Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were two-sided and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX). 
Results 
One hundred five studies of 657 articles (16%) identified by date and keyword criteria search met inclusion criteria for analysis ( Figure 1 ) ( 2 , 13 - 116 ) and were published in 55 different journals ( Supplement Table 4 ). Notably, seventy (67%) of the included studies were published in only four journals (Critical Care Medicine, Journal of Critical Care, Journal of Intensive Care, and Neurocritical Care). The number of studies selected per year did not significantly differ by year of publication (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.13, p=0.7). 
Intensivist nomenclature in study titles 
Intensivists were identified in the study titles using 17 different phrases. We have categorized them as Intensivist (61/105, 58%), followed by Specialty Intensivist (30/105, 29%), and ICU and Critical Care Physician (14/105, 13%) ( Table 2 and Supplement Tables 1 - 3 ). Neuro-Critical Care intensivists were the most common of the Specialty Intensivists (11/30) followed by Surgical Critical Care intensivists (6/30). Interestingly, we could find no studies that met our inclusion criteria for Anesthesiology Intensivists or Critical Care Anesthesiologists.

The proportion of studies that used nomenclature categorized as “Intensivist” were similar across the year of publication (56% [27/48] among those published before 2015 vs 60% [34/57] after 2015). In contrast, we found that nomenclatures related to ICU or Critical Care Physician were more common before 2015 (21% vs 7%) and nomenclatures related to Specialty Intensivist were more common after 2015 (33% vs 23%). Intensivist definitions and database searching strategies 
Of the 13 active parameters (B-E) (excludes parameter A1 - no definition) that we identified ( Table 1 ), an intensivist was defined using one single parameter in 31 (30%) studies ( Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 2 ). In contrast, 63 (60%) studies utilized multiple (2, 3, or 4) parameters (B-E) resulting in 34 unique parameter combinations ( Figure 2 , Supplemental Tables 3 and 8 ). No active parameter (A1), thus no definition was found in the remaining 11 (10%) studies ( Figure 2 , Supplemental Table 1 ). Neither the 1992 nor 2016 definitions published by the two major CCM societies were cited by any of the 105 included studies ( 3 , 5 ).

The number of parameters utilized per study did not significantly differ over time (based upon publication year) (p=0.6). Similarly, the use of each parameter did not significantly shift over time (p-values >0.05 across all 14 parameters). As a percent of total studies, publications with no definition (Parameter A1) increased over the years (pre-2015 – 4.2% (2/48) of studies vs 16% (9/57) in or after 2015) but without statistical significance (p=0.2). 
Staffing by Non-Intensivists and study titles 
We also found that a wide variety of clinicians who were not CCM trained and certified attending physician intensivists (Parameters D2–4) appeared in 16 studies ( Table 1 and Supplement Tables 1 - 3 ). CCM Fellows (Parameter D2) appeared alone in 10 studies; five of these used the nomenclature Intensivist in the title, while the other five used a combination of Critical Care Physician ( 3 ), Pulmonary Critical Care Physician ( 1 ), and Critical Care Specialist ( 1 ). Two studies combined Parameters D2 and D3; both had Intensivist in the title. Three studies used parameter D3 alone with intensivist in title. One study included non-CCM attendings (Parameter D4) and had Critical Care Physician in the title. 
Study types 
There were four main study types ( Supplement Tables 1 - 3 ): hospital-based patient analyses (46/105, 44%), hospital-based staff analyses (13/105, 12%), surveys (29/105, 28%), and others (17/105, 16%) ( Supplemental Table 6 ). The distribution of study types significantly shifted over the years (p=0.022). The earlier years were mostly hospital-based patient analyses (median [IQR] year of publication: 2014 [2012,2017]) and surveys (2014 [2013,2016]); in contrast, the latter years were hospital-based staff analyses (2017 [2015,2019]) and other study types (2019 [2016,2020]). 
Relationships between study title nomenclature, intensivist parameters, and study type 
The nomenclature used in the study titles was not significantly associated with any of the individual parameters (all p-values approximated p=0.1) ( Supplemental Figure 1 , Supplemental Table 7 ). Intensivist was the most frequently used nomenclature across all study types. Specialty Intensivists was most commonly observed in the hospital base patient analysis (HBPA) studies. Intensive Care and Critical Care Physician was most commonly used in surveys. However, there were no significant relationships between nomenclature and study type (p=0.2). There were also no significant relationships between study types and single vs multi parameter ( Supplemental Table 6 ). 
Identifying or defining intensivists through databases 
A variety of databases, lists and sampling techniques were used to define by proxy, or to locate, or identify intensivists primarily for the 29 survey studies ( Table 1 , Parameters E1–6). Critical care society membership lists were accessed in 12 studies (Parameter E1); of these, two studies included non-Intensivists (Parameters D2 and D3). National federal or proprietary databases (Parameter E2) were accessed in five studies using the self-designation codes or physician identifiers of the AMA Masterfile (2 studies) and Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) dataset (3 studies) respectively. Faculty listings primarily within a CCM faculty research database of ACGME approved CCM fellowships were used in 5 studies (Parameter E3). Finally, intensivists were identified or defined through statewide hospital databases (Parameter E4), proprietary ICU data systems with intensivist definitions (Parameters E5) and local hospital lists, or sampling techniques (Parameter E6) in 13 studies. 
Discussion 
We found great variability in the terminology used in the study titles to refer to an intensivist. This finding may have resulted from the evolution of critical care as a specialty. First, CCM has been known internationally, since its inception, by two interchangeable and perhaps confusing phrases, Intensive Care and Critical Care ( 117 ). Second, the approach adopted by the American Boards of Anesthesiology, Internal Medicine, Pediatrics and Surgery in the 1980s to mandate distinct CCM fellowship pathways and examinations generated multiple subspecialty adjectives to describe an intensivist ( 1 , 118 ). Moreover, the variability in Intensivist nomenclature has been further amplified in the last decades with the addition of Neurological Critical Care and Emergency Medicine Critical Care training tracks and board certifications ( 118 , 119 ). And more recently with the development of subspecialty intensivist tracks including Cardiology Intensivists ( 120 – 122 ), Critical Care Nephrologists ( 123 , 124 ), Cardiac Anesthesiology Intensivists ( 125 ) and the offering of Surgical Critical Care training to include a diverse group of Surgical, Anesthesiology and Emergency Medicine applicants ( 126 , 127 ). Finally, the diversity of Intensivist nomenclature may also reflect the increasing number of specialty ICUs where intensivists primarily practice especially in large academic medical centers ( 1 , 117 ). 
Similarly, we found great variability in the intensivist definitions within the studies themselves. Thirty percent defined an intensivist using a selection of 11 single parameters. Sixty percent used multi-parameters (12 parameters in 34 unique combinations) to define an intensivist. We believe that the multi-parameter approach found in the majority of studies and illustrated in the intensivist definitions used in 1992 by SCCM ( 3 ) and 2016 by the World Federation of Societies of Intensive and CCM taskforce ( 5 ) best captures the core elements included within an Intensivist’s job definition and is superior to a single parameter definition. From our perspective, the nomenclature, and definitional variabilities we note may be mitigated if future investigators of Intensivist based studies would be able to refer to a current and comprehensive Intensivist definition. 
We do not have an adequate explanation for the absence of a definition in 11 studies. Perhaps the authors assumed that the phrase intensivist, used most commonly in the study titles in this group, is so well understood that no definition is required. Whether that is true or not is unknown; however, the absence of a definition for a primary study variable, in this case the intensivist, is certainly perplexing because definitions of key elements are integral to the methodological design of design peer reviewed studies and are critical to understanding a study’s findings. 
Across the included studies, the most common parameter used was “Works in ICU” (Parameter C1). This parameter was a composite that we developed ( Table 1 ) in order to capture a wide variety of ICU based work activities. However, even though Works in ICU is a well-established definitional component for intensivists ( 3 , 5 ), it does not completely encapsulate the nuances of intensivist work activities (i.e., time spent and assigned duties in the ICU, the number or type of procedures that intensivists are privileged to perform). The second most frequently used parameter was “Attending ICU physician” (Parameter D1). This phrase is well known and consistently used in hospitals and implies that the physician has been assigned critical care or ICU medical staff privileges. Board certification (Parameter B2), the third most frequently used parameter, lacks important detail because the studies using this parameter did not address certification status at the time of their study. Fellowship trained (Parameter B1), the fourth most commonly used parameter, may also associated with nuance especially in the complexities associated with fellowship training and certification of Neurointensivists and Emergency Medicine Intensivists ( 98 , 119 , 128 , 129 ). 
Our initial study design assumed that peer-reviewed studies using the phrase intensivist, specialized intensivist, ICU or critical care physician or consultant in their titles ( Table 2 and Supplemental Tables 1 - 3 ) referred to CCM fellowship trained and board-certified attending intensivist physicians. However, we encountered 16 studies that included clinicians who were not attending intensivists. In five of these studies, CCM fellows were included, and the Critical Care Physician or Specialist wording of their study titles appeared to be concordant with their inclusion. However, the remaining eleven studies, included fellows, residents, APPs, nurses and non-ICU attendings despite the wording Intensivist in their titles, thus suggesting discordance between the titles and study subjects. 
This discordance between titles and study participants, in combination with inconsistencies in intensivist definitions, may be confusing to the reader if these subtleties are even recognized. It is possible that findings generated within these studies were generalized into practice. We suggest that investigators within the intensivist space better synchronize the wording in their study titles, the study subjects and definitions within their methods. Additionally, we believe that peer reviewers of intensivist based studies should be more attentive to these issues. Interestingly, we came across several reports that used “providers” rather than intensivists or physician representatives in their titles ( 130 – 132 ). These studies encompassed all types of CCM or ICU staff members (ie, nurses, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, critical care pharmacists, ICU social workers, case managers, and respiratory therapists) in concordance with the inclusive nature of the word “providers.” 
A wide variety of datasets were used by authors to define and identify or locate intensivists. We want to address two elements related to the large datasets. First, the usage of CCM society membership lists to proxy intensivists may also capture non-intensivists members in the net ( 25 , 48 ). Therefore, societal membership searches must be filtered to identify the exact target population for study. Second, major national datasets also contain an assortment of arcane nuances that may limit their usefulness or reproducibility in defining or identifying intensivists. For example, studies that used the AMA Physician Masterfile ( 14 , 22 ) and CMS datasets (Medicare Physician/Supplier Claims Files ( 80 ) relied upon “self-designated” codes; other studies used complex mergers of CCM billing codes ( 80 ) and National Provider Identifier (NPI) numbers that do not incorporate physician specialties, or combinations of data from the American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM) or the United Council for Neurological Subspecialites (UCNS) CCM certifications ( 20 , 69 ). Future studies using major datasets, alone or in combination, may need to better clarify the challenges and restrictions of these approaches. 
There are several limitations in our study. First, although our search strategies were very broad, we were not able identify data-driven studies that included critical care-anesthesiologists; thus, we do not know how such studies would have defined intensivists. Moreover, Acute Care Anesthesiology ( 133 )and Acute Care Surgery ( 134 ), two evolving subspecialties with CCM training, were not captured in our search as their nomenclature does not include intensivist terminology. Second, our study is premised upon our conviction that we could discern the terminologies used by authors in their introductions and methods sections to define intensivists and then we could construct a definitional model. We believe this approach to defining intensivists is unique and thus may require external validation. Third, we focused our study on “physician” intensivists; it was beyond our scope to encompass non-physician providers (i.e., APPs and pharmacists) that may currently be referred to as intensivists. Lastly, outside high-level relationships between study titles, definitions and study types, it was also beyond our scope to explore in depth the subject matter of the 105 studies themselves. 
Conclusion 
We believe that our primary findings of inconsistencies in intensivist nomenclature in the titles of the studies and in defining intensivists, discordance between study title nomenclature and the subjects, and the challenges associated with using major databases to find intensivists are relevant to the CCM community. Further studies would be required to ascertain the administrative, clinical, education, and research ramifications of our findings. We are optimistic however, that our findings will help better inform readers of intensivist centered studies and encourage the future authors of such studies to optimally align intensivist nomenclature, definitions and databases for the sake of scientific clarity, accuracy and reproducibility. Additionally, we hope that our findings of both the common parameters used to define an Intensivist and their combinations will be useful as a starting point if SCCM elects to update its comprehensive 1992 Intensivist definition. We think this task is especially important given the evolution of CCM that has occurred in the last three decades. Dr. Frank Cerra was prescient in his editorial accompanying the 1992 SCCM definition ( 3 ); “As the Society has grown, so has the demand of its members for the Society to define who practices critical care and to describe the modes of that practice. Also, as the practice of critical care has grown, so has the demand for these definitions from hospitals, regulatory agencies, reimbursement systems, communications media, and from patients, families, and the public.” ( 135 ).

KEY POINTS. 
Question: 
Are there consistent terms used to describe or define an adult intensivist in contemporary (2010–2020) US based data-driven studies? 
Findings: 
In this review of 105 studies, we could not identify a consistent descriptive phrase or definition for an adult intensivist. 
Meaning: 
There is no consistent intensivist nomenclature or definition in contemporary studies involving intensivists.
