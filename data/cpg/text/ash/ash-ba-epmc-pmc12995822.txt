Abstract 
Systems-based hematology (SBH) is an emerging discipline focused on optimizing care delivery for patients with hematologic disorders within complex health care systems. Despite its growing importance, the field lacks a formal definition, standardized curriculum, and clear career pathway, hindering widespread adoption and training of future SBH experts. We sought to define core principles, competencies, challenges, and future directions of SBH to identify foundational components for a potential educational curriculum. Semistructured interviews were conducted with 8 SBH experts. Expert participants were identified through their committee involvement with the American Society of Hematology. Five overarching themes emerged: (1) The accidental expert: SBH careers have developed organically and reactively in response to pressing institutional needs, not through formal training; (2) Defining the unseen: experts define the field pragmatically by its function and roles; (3) The SBH toolkit: success requires a hybrid skillset combining clinical hematology expertise with health systems science (HSS) principles; (4) The architect's dilemma: the field's growth is hindered by interdependent challenges of building a curriculum, awareness, and securing resources; and (5) Charting the future: the discipline is poised for growth, driven by increasing health care complexity and specialization. SBH represents the nexus of HSS principles and hematology care. Formalizing SBH requires a multipronged approach, including developing a standardized curriculum that equips learners with a hybrid skillset, creating clear value for health systems and patients, and fostering a community of practice through professional societies. These findings provide a foundational framework for developing educational resources and institutional support necessary to continue advancing SBH. 
Introduction 
Modern medicine is characterized by increasing complexity. Although basic and clinical sciences provide the foundation for diagnosing and treating diseases, a third pillar of medical education has emerged as equally critical: Health Systems Science (HSS). 1 , 2 , 3 HSS is the study of how health care is delivered, how health professionals collaborate to provide that care, and how the health system can be improved to enhance patient care and outcomes. It encompasses a wide range of domains and topics, such as quality improvement (QI), patient safety, value-based care, clinical informatics, population health, and health care policy. At its core, HSS employs systems thinking to understand and optimize the intricate, interconnected components of health care delivery often occurring within learning health systems. 4 , 5 The American Medical Association (AMA) has greatly advanced HSS education efforts, including publication of textbooks outlining curricular domains and relevant topic material ( Figure 1 ). 6

Within the specialty of hematology, the principles of HSS have found a crucial application in the emerging discipline of Systems-Based Hematology (SBH). There have been several examples of successful SBH efforts using various principles of HSS ( Figure 1 ). 7 First formally described by the American Society of Hematology (ASH) in 2015, SBH focuses on the health care systems responsible for delivering care to patients with hematologic conditions. 8 The need for more SBH experts is increasing. For example, The Joint Commission (NPSG.03.05.01) requires accredited hospitals to have protocols and guidelines for anticoagulant use, including medication selection, dosing, monitoring, and reversal strategies to reduce the likelihood of patient harm associated with anticoagulant therapy. In addition, there are increasing needs for patient blood management programs, improving access to hematologic care (eg, electronic consultations and care pathways), and more. Hematology is a highly consultative specialty that intersects with nearly every other field of medicine. Challenges such as managing high-cost diagnostics and high-risk therapies, ensuring appropriate use of blood products, standardizing care across subspecialties and institutions, and improving access for patients with hematologic disorders represent complex systems-level problems that cannot be solved by individuals or isolated clinical encounters. An SBH approach seeks to address challenges like these by designing and implementing standardized, evidence-based, and high-value approaches that advance transdisciplinary care while minimizing iatrogenic harm for patients with hematologic disorders. 7 , 8 
Despite its importance, SBH remains a fledgling field facing significant barriers to growth. There is not a single, formal definition that is widely understood by trainees, faculty, and/or institutional leaders. Consequently, there is no standardized curriculum or training for future SBH experts and no clearly defined career pathway for those who wish to pursue this work. Individuals practicing SBH have largely been self-taught and are often tasked to solve complex problems without a formal position or dedicated resources. This lack of formal training and institutional structure creates a vicious cycle: health systems are hesitant to invest in creating formal SBH positions and dedicating resources to these efforts without trained SBH experts who can demonstrate their value; simultaneously, without a clear definition and curriculum, training programs cannot produce graduates with SBH skills. 
These gaps represent a critical barrier to training individuals in SBH and improving the quality, safety, and value of hematologic care at a systems level. To address this, we undertook a qualitative needs assessment through semistructured interviews with national SBH experts, aiming to understand the core principles, competencies, challenges, and future directions of SBH. In addition, we sought to identify foundational components for a potential curriculum and standardized training. By synthesizing the collective input of SBH experts, this study provides a foundational framework for defining, teaching, and advancing SBH. 
Methods 
Study design 
A qualitative study design for the needs assessment using semistructured interviews with experts in SBH was chosen to facilitate an in-depth exploration of the participants' experiences, perspectives, and insights, allowing for both consistency in questioning and the flexibility to probe emergent themes. The study was conducted as part of the ASH Medical Educators Institute. 
Participant selection and recruitment 
A purposive sampling strategy was used to identify and recruit hematologists with SBH expertise through practical experiences, scientific publications, and/or service on related ASH committees. Potential participants were identified based on their active involvement and leadership roles within the ASH committee on quality and its subcommittee on implementation and SBH. This strategy ensured that participants had a deep, firsthand understanding of the field's conceptual foundations, practical challenges, and educational needs. An initial list of potential interviewees was generated, and individuals were contacted via email with an invitation to participate voluntarily. In total, 8 experts agreed to be virtually interviewed and recorded. 
Data collection 
Semistructured interviews were conducted between May and June 2024. All interviews were conducted virtually via Microsoft Teams by a single member of the research team (R.C.G.). An interview guide was developed to provide structure and ensure key topics were covered, including participants' career paths, their definition of SBH, essential skills, experiences with specific projects, challenges in the field, and recommendations for curriculum development. The interviewer maintained the flexibility to ask follow-up questions and explore topics as they arose organically during the conversation. All interviews were audio-recorded with automatic generation of verbatim text transcripts. 
Data analysis 
We performed a thematic analysis of the de-identified interview transcripts incorporating the use of a large language model (LLM; Google Gemini Pro 2.5, August 2025). This represents an emerging approach in qualitative research that has shown substantial agreement in intercoder reliability with human analysis, though further refinement and standardization are warranted. 9 , 10 To construct objective and effective prompts, they were developed with the assistance of the LLM through a description of the available data and goal of a grounded, comprehensive thematic analysis. The analysis used a systematic process employing noniterative, role-based, zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting as follows. 
Prompt 1 
“You are a qualitative researcher specializing in thematic analysis. I have 8 separate interview transcripts, each with a hematologist who is an expert in Systems-Based Hematology. In total, these interviews consisted of over 400 minutes of conversation, generating over 200 pages of transcript text and containing over 63,000 words. My goal is to identify and analyze the key themes and recurring patterns across all of these interviews. I need your help to perform a comprehensive thematic analysis. I have uploaded all 8 transcripts as word documents that I will ask you to analyze individually and then comprehensively. Please confirm that you understand the task and are ready to begin the analysis. Do not start the analysis yet." 
Prompt 2 
“Please perform a line-by-line coding of the following transcript of the semi-structed interview with Systems-Based Hematology Expert 1 that is uploaded as a word document. For each section of the text, generate a concise, descriptive code. Explain the rationale behind each code. Structure your output clearly with a table or a list, including the coded text, the code itself, and a brief justification for the code. Please be thorough and identify all relevant codes within this single transcript before moving on.” 
Prompt 2 through prompt 9 were identical for the individual expert interviews. 
Prompt 10 
“Please comprehensively review the entire list of codes generated from the 8 separate semi-structured interviews. Look for similarities, overlaps, and connections between the codes across all interviews. Group related codes into broader, overarching themes. For each theme, provide a clear, descriptive name and a definition. List the codes that fall under each theme.” 
Prompt 11 
“Based on the themes you have identified and defined, please write a summary of the key findings from the interviews. For each theme, provide a detailed analysis that includes: an explanation of the theme’s significance; a discussion of how the theme manifests in the interviews with specific examples or direct quotes from the transcripts to support your points; any notable variations or nuances of the theme across different interviews; anything else that you think is relevant to the analysis. The final output should be a well-structured report, ready for a research team to review and incorporate into their final manuscript.” 
The final thematic structure was reviewed and validated by the research team, including those SBH experts who were interviewed, to ensure it was a coherent and accurate representation of the interview data, personal experiences, and current state of SBH. 
Results 
The thematic analysis of the 8 expert interviews yielded 5 major themes that collectively define the current state, challenges, and future of SBH. 
Theme 1: the accidental expert: an unstructured and problem-driven field 
A universal theme was that the interviewed experts entered the field of SBH not through a structured pathway, but accidentally ( Table 1 ; Figure 2 ). SBH careers predominantly arose from necessity, often in response to specific, high-stakes institutional problems that demanded solutions. This reactive origin has shaped the field's pragmatic ethos but also underscores its foundational lack of a formal training pipeline. This narrative of being a "fixer" for systemic issues was common. This problem-driven entry meant that expertise was almost entirely self-taught and honed through experience. The learning process was described as one of trial and error. Lacking formal curricula, these SBH experts relied heavily on informal mentorship, often from colleagues outside of hematology such as pathologists or pharmacists, who had experience navigating hospital systems.

Theme 1: the accidental expert: an unstructured and problem-driven field 
This theme describes how SBH experts have historically entered the field not through a formal, structured pathway, but organically, often in response to a pressing institutional need. 
Subtheme 1.1: problem-driven and organic career origins 
Experts' careers in SBH were not planned but were initiated by specific, high-stakes institutional problems. They often found themselves practicing SBH before it was a formally recognized term. 
"My career in systems based hematology really started when the hospital managed to spend $1,000,000 on Novo Seven in the ER one year and I got told to please fix that." 
"I was one of the first people to be doing systems based technology without really realize I was doing it." 
"I guess I've become a system based hematologist without meaning to do it, but it's been great. I really love it." 
Subtheme 1.2: experiential and self-taught expertise 
In the absence of a formal curriculum, expertise was developed through hands-on, experiential learning, mentorship, and a process of trial and error. 
"Basically, I've done it myself. I mean this is this is self taught… there is no training. There has been no training." 
"(My experience has been) falling on face, getting back up again and trying to do it all over again and at some point figuring it out, that's what I would say is my learnings." 
"I didn't really have any formal training, so my interest in systems based, hematology really started in my fellowship… and from there really got a lot of my not really education, but mentorship." 
Theme 2: defining the unseen: articulating the role and value of SBH 
This theme encapsulates the ongoing challenge and various strategies for defining SBH in a way that is clear and compelling to colleagues, trainees, and institutional leaders. 
Subtheme 2.1: functional and relational definitions 
Experts define the field pragmatically by what it does: standardizing care, improving quality, and serving as a crucial link between siloed medical specialties. 
"Basically systems based hematology is how can you manage hematological issues across the system without needing a hematologist to be directly involved with each patient. So how can you standardize the work? How can you make it easy for everybody to do the right thing?" 
"I think that what doesn't change is sort of being that bridge between the, you know, the neurologists, the cardiologists, the and the hematologist." 
Subtheme 2.2: communication through analogy 
A key strategy for communicating the value of SBH is to use analogies to well-understood concepts, particularly antimicrobial stewardship, reframing the work as antithrombotic stewardship or hematology stewardship. 
"The way that I think I've been able to communicate most effectively what I'd like to do is by equating it to other kind of systems based efforts that people are familiar with. So things like antimicrobial stewardship." 
"I really think as systems based hematology is kind of like an ID stewardship." 
"…can we just say instead of antimicrobial stewardship, can we just say Hematology stewardship?" 
Theme 3: the SBH toolkit: essential skills for driving systemic change 
This theme outlines core competencies required for SBH experts, which combine deep clinical knowledge with a specific set of nonclinical, systems-level skills. 
Subtheme 3.1: foundational technical skills 
SBH experts must be proficient in the methodologies of QI and possess a sophisticated understanding of health informatics and the electronic medical record as a primary tool for change. 
"Quality training…understanding the quality improvement language is really important…" 
"Anything you wanna do now making change is gonna involve your electronic medical record and the more familiar the systems based hematologist is… they're gonna be just that much more effective." 
Subtheme 3.2: interpersonal and political acumen 
Success depends less on authority and more on the ability to build relationships, negotiate with diverse stakeholders, and align projects with the specific strategic and financial priorities of the institution. 
"You've gotta negotiate. What you're doing, you've gotta understand their practices." 
"(My) HIT e-consult service decreased the length of stay was the selling point. Thrombophilia testing, they don't care about right? It's not in alignment with the values of the institution." 
Theme 4: the architect's dilemma: building a field without a blueprint 
This theme addresses the interdependent structural challenges hindering the formalization of SBH: developing a curriculum, securing institutional resources, and establishing avenues for academic dissemination. 
Subtheme 4.1: need for curriculum and training 
Experts universally agree that a formal curriculum is needed to create a sustainable pipeline of SBH experts, but debate its ideal format, audience, and implementation. 
"I think the average fellow at a heme-onc program which produces 80% oncologists… this would, you know, not interest them… I think a true dedicated educational intervention should be focused on only those people that express interest in it." 
"I think what we need to do is really develop toolkits for doing different tasks." 
Subtheme 4.2: the struggle for institutional buy-in 
A central and profound challenge is securing protected time and resources from health system leadership, who often do not understand the field and prioritize revenue generation over cost savings. 
"If you're not making them money, they don't see the worth in cost savings." 
"Creating a business plan and it's the Holy Grail but I don't think any of us have done it to the (level of success we need)." 
"… I had to get to the person one under the top leader of my institution to get any traction." 
Subtheme 4.3: barriers to academic dissemination 
The field's growth is hampered by a lack of receptive, high-impact publication venues and a misalignment of incentives, as SBH experts funded by the health system are often not motivated by traditional academic metrics. 
"The Journal editors also do not have the level of awareness for this kind of work, so it does not ring the bell at the same intensity." 
"For a systems based hematologist is publication valuable or not? Because particularly if you're funding is coming from your health system, they don't really care if you publish a paper or not." 
Theme 5: charting the future: evolution, expansion, and awareness 
This theme captures the experts' vision for the future of SBH, focusing on opportunities for growth, expansion of scope, and the critical need to raise awareness to create a sustainable discipline. 
Subtheme 5.1: expansion of scope and integration of technology 
The future of the field lies in broadening its application to underserved areas (malignant hematology, pediatric hematology, community practice, etc.) and leveraging new technologies such as artificial intelligence. 
"We have not reached malignant Hematology very well… we need to broaden the appeal to… malignant hematology and pediatric hematology." 
"The use of AI is going to increase in systems based hematology…" 
Subtheme 5.2: the virtuous cycle of formalization and awareness 
The ultimate path to sustainability is a self-reinforcing cycle where formal training (e.g., a curriculum) raises awareness, which in turn helps SBH experts secure institutional investment, leading to greater impact and further growth. 
"If it's a formalized curriculum, it's recognized. Absolutely. You're gonna get more more traction." 
"I think there just needs to be more awareness at the health systems level and more kind of promotion of it."

Theme 2: defining the unseen: articulating the role and value of SBH 
A central challenge highlighted in every interview was the difficulty of defining SBH in a way that resonates with colleagues, trainees, and institutional leaders ( Table 1 ; Figure 3 ). Because the term lacks widespread recognition, experts have developed pragmatic, functional ways to describe their roles as integrators in an increasingly siloed medical environment, capturing the essence of SBH as creating robust systems that enable consistent, evidence-based care at scale. Given the ambiguity of the term, experts have found success using analogies to more established concepts, such as antimicrobial stewardship, which embody HSS principles. As a participant explained, reframing their work as "anticoagulation stewardship" allows them to leverage a familiar model.

Theme 3: the SBH toolkit: essential skills for driving systemic change 
The interviews revealed core competencies for SBH, describing an individual who combines clinical hematology expertise with a diverse, hybrid skillset ( Table 1 ; Figure 4 ). This requires a strong grounding in the principles of quality improvement (QI) and implementation science, understanding methodologies like Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles and the science of behavior change. In addition, proficiency in health informatics and the electronic health record was noted by multiple experts as integral to large scale success. However, the most critical and frequently discussed skills were interpersonal. The ability to build relationships, understand different departmental cultures, and negotiate solutions was paramount. Finally, a sophisticated understanding of the institution's priorities was described as a necessary skill. A project's clinical value is often secondary to its alignment with the institutional priorities and strategic goals. This necessity of speaking the language of administration and making a compelling business case was a recurring sentiment.

Theme 4: the architect's dilemma: building a field without a blueprint 
This theme encapsulates the structural challenges inherent in formalizing a new discipline such as SBH. The experts' discussions highlighted interdependent barriers, including the lack of a curriculum, low familiarity with the field, the struggle to secure resources, and the difficulty of academic dissemination leading to a vicious cycle ( Table 1 ; Figure 5 ). The need for a formal curriculum was undisputed, but opinions varied on its ideal form and audience. Suggestions ranged from a series of practical, task-specific toolkits to more formal, ASH-sponsored training. There was also debate about whether such training should be mandatory for all hematology trainees to ensure baseline competency or an opt-in track for those interested. The most significant barrier identified was securing institutional buy-in and resources. The experts described a constant struggle to justify the importance of SBH and obtain protected resources, such as time, money, and/or additional support. The core of these challenges was a fundamental disconnect from institutional financial perspectives, which can prioritize revenue generating ventures over cost savings of nonbillable work. Finally, the lack of clear avenues for academic dissemination poses a threat to the field's growth and recognition. Experts noted that mainstream journals have been slow to embrace QI and implementation-focused work.

Theme 5: charting the future: evolution, expansion, and awareness 
Despite the challenges, the interviews were imbued with a sense of purpose and a clear vision for the future. The experts unanimously believe that the need for SBH is growing, driven by the increasing complexity and subspecialization of medicine ( Table 1 ; Figure 6 ). The experts see several key avenues for the field's evolution. A primary opportunity is the expansion of scope. Although currently centered in classical hematology, there is a strong interest in applying SBH principles more broadly, welcoming those who practice pediatric hematology, malignant hematology, and community-based hematology. Another key area of evolution is the integration of technology, such as artificial intelligence and predictive analytics. Ultimately, the future of SBH is seen as a self-reinforcing, virtuous cycle. By developing a formal curriculum and raising awareness, the field can generate a pipeline of trained SBH experts better equipped to demonstrate their value, which will, in turn, encourage greater institutional investment, leading to broader impacts on patient care.

Discussion 
This qualitative study of 8 SBH experts provides a foundational and nuanced understanding of the emerging discipline. The findings suggest that SBH is an application of HSS principles to the complex clinical, operational, and financial challenges inherent in hematologic care ( Figure 1 ). 7 The experts describe a field that has evolved out of clinical necessity and is now at an inflection point, requiring a deliberate effort to move from an informal network of experts to a formalized discipline with a clear identity, a robust training pathway, and a sustainable career model that is broadly recognized and valued. 
These findings underscore the need for a formal SBH curriculum. The accidental and problem-driven career paths described are not a sustainable model for developing a workforce with nuanced expertise ( Figure 2 ). The identification of a core toolkit that combines clinical acumen with hybrid skillsets in HSS, provides insights into what such a curriculum should contain. The tenets of SBH align with the core domains of HSS, suggesting that a successful SBH curriculum could borrow heavily from established HSS curriculum frameworks, using hematology-specific examples and toolkits to teach the application of these principles ( Figures 1 , 3 , and 4 ). 6 
The most significant barrier to the growth of SBH identified in this study is mitigation of a vicious cycle that requires ongoing demonstration of value to health system leadership with limited resources invested in the underlying work ( Figure 5 ). Value is a multifaceted metric that simultaneously represents diverse interests influenced by perspective. In health care, value often represents improving outcomes such as patient safety and well-being while minimizing costs. There are several published examples of SBH improving value through both targeted and comprehensive initiatives. 7 From improved outcomes and safety to decreased use and cost savings, diverse SBH initiatives have demonstrated value. Moreover, a systems approach improves patient and service outcomes, increasing value across various settings. 11 Still, physician leaders are increasingly tasked to develop a business case for quality and process improvement initiatives, focusing on only a single aspect of value. However, physicians often lack formal training in finance and management to do so effectively, especially with a deficiency of standard financial benefit measurements in health care. 12 Our findings suggest that any SBH curriculum should include practical training in all of these to equip future SBH experts with the language and skills to advocate for systems-level positions and resources required to improve outcomes while minimizing costs. The strategy of using antimicrobial stewardship as an analogue leverages a successful, preexisting model of systems-level leadership applying HSS principles that has demonstrated broadly recognized value. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 
Furthermore, this study emphasizes the critical need for professional societies like ASH to play a central role in championing SBH. By creating dedicated training resources, ASH can help to provide the foundational education and professional validation that has yet to fully form, similar to other initiatives like the ASH Medical Educators Institute. 19 For example, an idea discussed involved the creation of a widely available, asynchronous video curriculum teaching SBH by utilizing established HSS principles and hematology-specific examples as a framework for core competencies, similar to delivery of the ASH Hematology Review Series ( Figure 1 ). This would be available to the larger hematology community interested in SBH and could incorporate preassessments and postassessments to ensure transfer of knowledge, serving as an adjunct to other in-person ASH training opportunities, such as the Quality Improvement Training Institute, which incorporate projects to demonstrate impact through behavior change. Once equipped with more formal training, future SBH experts could more aptly lead initiatives with progressively broader and more recognized impacts. In conjunction with formal education efforts, journals can create more visible and receptive avenues for publishing SBH research, helping to grow the evidence base and create more viable career pathways for positions requiring academic recognition. 20 , 21 , 22 These initiatives should embrace evolution in the field, including an expanding scope that involves malignant hematology, pediatric hematology, transfusion medicine, blood banking, hematopathology, and community hematology as well as pioneering novel uses for technologies like artificial intelligence and predictive analytics. Together, these approaches were anticipated to promote a virtuous cycle for advancing SBH ( Figure 6 ). 
There are several limitations to this study. The sample size of 8 experts is small, although the depth of their experience and the strong consistency of themes suggest that data saturation was approached. The purposive sampling strategy, which targeted ASH committee members, ensured a high level of expertise but may have introduced a selection bias toward individuals with an academic orientation who are already highly engaged in professional service. Future studies and initiatives should incorporate SBH experts in varied practice settings. The thematic analysis of the interview transcripts used an LLM, representing an emerging approach in qualitative research that has shown substantial agreement in intercoder reliability with human analysis, though further refinement and standardization are warranted. 9 , 10 The final thematic structure was reviewed and validated by the research team, including those SBH experts who were interviewed, to ensure it was a coherent and accurate representation of the interview data, personal experiences, and current state of SBH. To that end, this study also highlights the roles of qualitative research, LLMs, and their intersectionality as emerging tools for future SBH initiatives. Future work with experts in qualitative methodologies and artificial intelligence are warranted to develop approaches and prompt engineering strategies to further standardize the use of LLMs in qualitative research and SBH. 
SBH is a fledgling discipline critical to addressing some of the most complex challenges in hematologic care throughout health systems. Expert insights described in this study provide a foundational framework for bolstering and advancing the field of SBH. They suggest defining the field not just by its title, but by its function to build and manage systems that make evidence-based, high-value, and equitable care the default for every patient using HSS principles. To this end, building a curriculum based in HSS principles that fosters and trains SBH experts is necessary to support the hematology community advocating for the necessary resources and creating a more recognized professional identity for SBH.
