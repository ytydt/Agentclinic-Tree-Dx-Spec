Does age matter? Optimizing AML treatment in older adults

In this issue of Blood Advances , Sekeres et al 1 have undertaken a herculean effort culminating in the updated American Society of Hematology (ASH) 2025 guidelines for treating newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in older adults. Since the publication of the last ASH guidelines for older adults, much has changed in terms of AML classification, prognostication, and treatment. The World Health Organization classification was updated, 2 formation of the International Consensus Classification has occurred, 3 European Leukemia Network (ELN) 2022 for newly diagnosed patients with AML eligible for chemotherapy was updated, 4 and ELN 2024 for newly diagnosed patients with AML ineligible for intensive chemotherapy has been created. 5 The former 2 emphasize morphologic and molecular parameters for diagnosis, and the latter 2 are used for risk stratification and prognosis. These comprehensive guidelines for the general population of patients with AML were created by an international panel with >20 expert opinions. 
The prior ASH guidelines for older adults with AML were published in 2020, 6 and since then, a few major changes have been made. The authors performed a comprehensive literature review and interpretation of the evidence, as described in the article, with recommendations characterized as strong (described as for or against) or conditional (described as suggests or suggests against) based on the certainty of evidence, which culminated into a 4-point rating scale. Importantly, the terms “intensive” and “nonintensive” have been abandoned and replaced with “conventional induction and postremission therapy” and “hypomethylating agents (HMA) or low-dose cytarabine (LDAC) in combination with venetoclax,” reflecting the reality of clinical practice that combination therapy with venetoclax is not “nonintensive” for many patients. 
In total, there are 9 recommendations, summarized as follows: 
1. 
For newly diagnosed patients with AML able to tolerate therapy, the recommendation is for treatment over best supportive care (BSC). 
2. 
For patients able to tolerate intensive induction, conventional chemotherapy should be given, especially in patients with favorable-risk cytogenetics. In patients with certain poor-risk molecular abnormalities, HMA- or LDAC-based treatment may be preferred. 
3. 
For patients in remission after conventional chemotherapy and unable to undergo stem cell transplantation, postremission treatment should be given. 
4. 
For patients unable to tolerate conventional chemotherapy, treatment with HMA plus venetoclax is preferred over HMA only. If unable to use HMA combined with venetoclax, LDAC combined with venetoclax should be used over LDAC only. Preference should be for HMA over BSC, and if using decitabine, a 5-day schedule should be preferred over 10. 
5. 
For older adults with IDH1 mutations who are unable to tolerate conventional chemotherapy, recommendation is for azacitidine plus ivosidenib or HMA plus venetoclax. For more frail patients, recommendation is for azacitidine plus ivosidenib over azacitidine alone. For IDH2-mutated patients unable to tolerate conventional chemotherapy, the recommendation is for HMA plus venetoclax. If venetoclax is not being used, the recommendation is for azacitidine alone over azacitidine plus enasidenib. 
6. 
For patients who receive HMA combined with venetoclax and are in remission, therapy should be continued indefinitely. 
7. 
For FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3)-mutated patients who are able to tolerate conventional chemotherapy, addition of an FLT3 inhibitor is recommended. 
8. 
For patients in remission with a high risk of relapse, recommendation is stem cell transplant if eligible and can tolerate. 
9. 
For patients who have no further treatment options, transfusion support should be an option. 
In addition to the recommendations, the authors identified areas of unmet need requiring further research. These include treatment options for patients with TP53 -mutated AML, incorporation of targeted agents into frontline treatments, and which treatment backbone is the most appropriate for different patient populations. A significant limitation of these guidelines and AML therapy in general is the continued use of age as a surrogate for fitness for conventional induction chemotherapy, as well as the ongoing question of whether there are subtypes of AML that are better treated by conventional induction and postremission therapies or venetoclax-based therapies, regardless of age or fitness for “intensive” treatment. Ongoing clinical trials including the National Cancer Institute–sponsored MyeloMATCH trials for intermediate- and high-risk younger patients with AML are designed to help answer this question, with randomization to conventional induction and venetoclax-based induction regimens. The guidelines do not address the question that often comes up in clinic: when can patients stop treatment with venetoclax-based regimens? Ongoing trials with measurable residual disease monitoring and other risk stratification strategies may help answer this question. As a leukemia community, we must be willing to enroll all patients irrespective of age to multicenter, sufficiently powered randomized phase 3 trials to further improve quality and quality of life. 
The authors are to be commended for these comprehensive guidelines. 
However, guidelines also have limitations, especially when faced with individual clinical scenarios. Application of guidelines must be tailored to the individual patient, taking into consideration the patient’s short- and long-term goals and social support. 
In our current era, it is well recognized that there are patients chronically older but physiologically younger and vice versa. However, this article generalizes the definition to those aged >55 years. 7 With patients living longer, it is imperative that we consider the appropriate sequencing of regimens for our patients. The balance of efficacy and toxicity is complicated, especially in patients who are borderline in their fitness status. However, if “younger” patients are starting with less-intensive treatment options, are we removing intensive chemotherapy from the armamentarium of options for these patients? Or are we allowing less toxicity and transitioning more patients to potentially curative stem cell transplantation? 
Practically speaking, we need guidelines as a starting point to initiate discussion among our leukemia colleagues, the oncology community, and with our patients; recognizing that each patient with leukemia is heterogenous and does not always fit within these guidelines. Striking a balance between the 2 is challenging and is a gap that still needs to be filled.
