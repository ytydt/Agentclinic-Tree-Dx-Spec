Abstract 
The American Society of Hematology (ASH) proposed the median time to first emergency department (ED) administration of pain medication for patients with sickle cell disease (SCD) vaso-occlusive pain episodes (VOE) as a site-level quality measure. Generalizable studies assessing current guideline adherence recommending pain medications within 60 minutes are lacking. We leveraged multisite electronic health record data from Epic’s Cosmos research platform to analyze ED encounters for SCD VOE from 1 January 2019 to 31 December 2024, with administration of at least 1 pain medication. We calculated the quality measure (median time to first pain medication) and ranked sites based on their timeliness. Lower values indicate more efficient pain management. We stratified the measure by medication type (opioid vs nonopioid) and administration route (oral vs parenteral) and compared the values across site-level characteristics (pediatric vs adult and encounter volumes). There were 474 409 SCD VOE encounters from 225 sites. Site encounter volumes ranged from <10 to 17 192. Overall, the quality measure ranged from 30 to 250 minutes; 86% of sites had values >60 minutes. Stratification by type and route revealed consistent median times of 81 to 82.5 minutes across 50th percentile–ranked sites. Median times from pediatric sites were 25 minutes (95% confidence interval, 19.5-86.5) lower than those from adult sites (58 vs 83 minutes; P = .001); encounter volume did not consistently influence site performance. Most sites performed poorly on the measure. These baseline data suggest that implementing the ASH-proposed quality measure could stimulate improvements in ED pain management. 
Introduction 
Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited blood disorder in which the hemoglobin assumes an abnormal “sickle” shape, resulting in numerous complications, including recurrent vaso-occlusive pain episodes (VOE). These VOE often lead to frequent emergency department (ED) visits. 1 , 2 , 3 High-quality treatment of VOE in the ED setting requires timely evaluation and administration of pain medications. 4 , 5 Guidelines from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) and the American Society of Hematology (ASH) both recommend timely administration of the first pain medication within 60 minutes of ED arrival for SCD VOE, with parenteral opioids as first line therapy for moderate to severe pain. 6 , 7 However, despite these recommendations, evidence from several small studies, predominantly in pediatric patients, suggests poor compliance with the guidelines, with patients with SCD presenting to EDs for acute pain experiencing delays in receiving pain medications. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 
A technical expert panel from ASH and the Health Services Advisory Group (HSAG), Inc, proposed a clinical quality measure targeting the timeliness of pain medication administration for patients with SCD with VOE who present to the ED. 13 This site-specific quality measure is defined as the median time (in minutes) from ED arrival to administration of first pain medication for patients with SCD with VOE. 13 Shorter times indicate better quality of pain management. Implementing this measure will facilitate nationwide tracking of timeliness in initial pain treatment for SCD VOE, potentially leading to interventions that improve pain management, reduce hospitalizations, and result in better patient experience. However, a concern about a measure assessing timeliness of any pain medication is that a site could improve timeliness by switching from a parenteral opioid to an oral nonopioid, thereby improving timeliness but not necessarily improving patient care. Baseline knowledge of medication types and routes is therefore essential. 
A significant gap in our understanding of the implications of the measure is the lack of any comprehensive analysis of the timeliness of pain medication across multiple EDs. Past studies reporting pain medication in the ED for patients with SCD are either focused only on opioid pain medications, 9 , 11 exclusive to children, 8 , 10 , 11 or conducted at single sites. 9 , 11 The unavailability of comprehensive studies results in a lack of baseline data for future evaluation of the measure. We addressed these gaps by conducting a comprehensive analysis of the timeliness of pain medication administration across multiple sites, including both opioids and nonopioids, for patients with SCD VOE. Our primary objective was to evaluate the median time to first pain medication, providing baseline data on the current timeliness of analgesia administration and allowing for future evaluation of changes in treatment that may occur with the implementation of the proposed ASH clinical quality measure. We also aimed to evaluate the differences in the median time to first pain medication across ED-level characteristics. 
Methods 
Database and study design 
This cross-sectional study evaluates the proposed clinical quality measure for the timeliness of pain medication administration during ED encounters for SCD VOE. We analyzed data from Epic’s Cosmos research platform, a multisite collaboration between Epic and health systems. 14 The Cosmos platform receives clinical data from >260 health systems, including academic, nonacademic, and community systems in the United States. 14 To participate in the Cosmos research platform, health care organizations must be Epic users, meet the platform’s minimum data quality requirements, and agree to voluntarily transfer patient-level electronic health record information to the database. 15 The platform contains >15 billion clinical encounters from nearly 300 million unique patients (as of this study period). 14 Within the platform, records from unique patients across different participating organizations are linked and deduplicated, enabling the compilation of longitudinal health records for patients across multiple health care systems. 14 
For this analysis, we included records from the 225 organizations with complete data submitted to the database from 1 January 2019, to 31 December 2024. Variables in the data set are deidentified; however, we extracted patient-level demographic information, encounter-level diagnosis codes, ED arrival times, medication types, routes of administration, and medication administration details. Data from Epic’s Cosmos research platform are widely used in health services research. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 This study was determined to be nonhuman subject research and exempted from further ethics review by the Nemours Children’s Health's institutional review board. 
Population and patient identification 
The study included all ED encounters for SCD VOE from 1 January 2019 to 31 December 2024 for which at least 1 dose of pain medication was administered. For inclusion in the analysis and consistent with current guidance for the proposed measure outlined in the methodology report, 13 we required all ED encounters to have a principal International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10) diagnosis code indicating SCD with VOE (D57.0∗, D57.21∗, D57.41∗, D57.43∗, D57.45∗, D57.81∗). Encounters at day hospitals, clinics, and transfusion sites were excluded. The qualifying pain medications in this study included all opioid and nonopioid pain medications administered at any time during the ED encounter. We adopted the list of pain medications provided in the methodology report for the ASH-proposed quality measure. 13 
Supplemental Table 1 contains the list of all pain medications. As a sensitivity analysis for the accuracy of the study population, we repeated the analysis, excluding encounters from patients with <2 qualifying ED encounters for SCD VOE. 
The outcome 
The primary outcome was the median time (in minutes) from ED arrival to the administration of the first pain medication for all eligible ED encounters per site (ASH quality measure). Given the measure’s calculation, lower median times indicate faster administration of pain medication and, therefore, more timely care. 
Site 
We defined a site as the organizational source submitting data to the platform. An organizational source may include >1 ED. Due to privacy requirements, the identity of the sites in the Cosmos platform is masked, but encounters at a site are linked by a distinct blinded ID for differentiation. 
Data analysis 
We used the site as the unit of analysis in this study; individual ED encounters were grouped by site to allow unique patients to have multiple encounters throughout the study. We summarized the overall encounter-level sociodemographic factors (age, sex, and payer) using means, standard deviations (SD), frequencies, and proportions. 
For each ED encounter, we calculated the time (in minutes) between ED arrival and the time of initial pain medication administration as the “first medication time.” Preliminary evaluation of the data revealed that across 11% of eligible ED encounters, different types of medications (opioids and nonopioids) were documented to have the same administration time, suggesting that sometimes multiple pain medications were administered simultaneously or close together and documented as being at the same time. Different routes of medication administration could also be documented to be used simultaneously. Therefore, to provide comprehensive baseline data on pain medication administration, we calculated the proportion of encounters in which the first medication time included an oral or parenteral medication and an opioid or nonopioid pain medication. 
Subsequently, we grouped ED encounters by site, calculated the median time in minutes from ED arrival to the administration of the first pain medication for each site, and created a percentile ranking of the sites based on their performance on the quality measure. We then stratified the encounters according to the type of first pain medication (opioid vs nonopioid) as well as route of administration (parenteral vs oral) and recalculated the measure and percentile rankings of the sites according to their performance. We finally calculated the mean and SD of the measure for all sites in this study. We conducted a sensitivity analysis involving only ED encounters from the subgroup of patients with ≥2 qualifying encounters for SCD VOE. 
Furthermore, because our study follow-up period overlaps the COVID-19 pandemic, 16 , 19 and opioid prescription restrictions implemented across several states, 20 we evaluated their potential impact on the timeliness of analgesia by stratifying the calculated quality measure by the study year. 
Using the Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests, we compared differences in the measure across 2 site-level characteristics: predominant patient type (pediatric vs adult) and volume of SCD VOE encounters in the ED. Using a previously reported method of identifying pediatric and adult EDs from deidentified data sets, 21 we categorized sites as pediatric sites if ≥75% of the patients with SCD who received care at the site (throughout the study duration) were aged ≤19 years; conversely, sites were considered adult sites if ≤10% of their patients with SCD were aged ≤19 years. Sites that did not fit either category were excluded from this comparison analysis. Similarly, we grouped sites into 4 quartiles based on the number of ED encounters for SCD VOE from 1 January 2019 to 31 December 2024 and compared the differences in their calculated ASH quality measure. 
All statistical analyses were conducted using Python version 3.12.2, and P values <.05 were interpreted as indicating significant associations between comparison groups. 
Results 
Study characteristics 
The study included 474 409 encounters from 44 758 unique patients across 225 sites. Table 1 displays the encounter-level characteristics of the study. Females accounted for 54% of the encounters, and the median age at ED encounter was 29 years. There was substantial variability in the volume of eligible ED encounters across the 225 sites; the busiest site recorded 17 192 eligible encounters, whereas the site with the fewest encounters recorded <10. Public insurance (including Medicaid, Medicare, and other publicly provided insurance) was the primary payer during 68% of eligible ED encounters.

ASH quality measure results 
The site-level median times to the administration of first pain medication across the 225 sites are presented in supplemental Table 2 . Table 3 displays the percentile ranking of sites according to their performance on the quality measure. Overall, the variation in the median time to the first pain medication ranged from 30 to 250 minutes, with 86% of the sites having a median time greater than the ASH and NHLBI guideline–recommended 60 minutes. The median time to first pain medication was 81 minutes across sites ranked on the 50th percentile. For the worst-performing 25%, the median time was >95 minutes, regardless of the type of medication or route of administration. The mean (SD) of the measure across the 225 sites was 83.6 (27.3) minutes. Stratification by medication type (opioid vs nonopioid) or route of administration (parenteral vs oral) revealed minimal differences, with sites ranked in the 50th percentile having a median time ranging from 80 minutes to 82.5 minutes.

Discussion 
This study leveraged a multisite, collaborative data infrastructure to evaluate the ASH-proposed clinical quality measure. We found substantial variation in the performance of EDs on the measure, with an overwhelming majority having a median time to first pain medication administration greater than the NHLBI- and ASH-guideline–recommended 60 minutes. Although nonopioid pain medications were administered for one-third of the ED encounters in our study, the performance of the sites on the measure did not substantially change based on the type of first pain medication (opioid vs nonopioid). 
Our study extends the findings from smaller, primarily pediatric studies 8 , 10 , 11 by providing the most comprehensive assessment of site-level timeliness of first pain medication administration and pain management guideline adherence. Significantly, our study differs from previous studies and the ASH quality measure methodology report 13 in key areas, enhancing our understanding of the timeliness of pain medication administration for patients with SCD VOE. First, we leveraged the Cosmos platform, 14 a substantially larger database drawn from diverse sites, reflecting potentially significant variation in treatment practices. The diversity across the sites facilitates a better understanding of the quality of care across multiple settings. Second, our analysis evaluated 6 years of ED-encounter data (2019-2024), providing, to our knowledge, not only the largest sample (nearly half a million ED encounters) to date but also the most recent. These robust and recent data provide novel insights into the current ED practices in treating patients with SCD VOE. Last, our study also provided, to our knowledge, the first baseline data for the ASH quality measure, which will serve as a reference for future evaluations of the measure. 
Only 14% of the sites in our study met the ASH and NHLBI guidelines for administering the first pain medication within 60 minutes. In comparison, 25% of the EDs evaluated in the methodology report met the guidelines. 13 Speculatively, this difference may be due to multiple factors, including variations in patient demographics across our large database and site-specific differences between participants in the Epic Cosmos platform and the ED sites evaluated in the methodology report. Variations in treatment approaches can also lead to significant differences in pain management efficiency across sites. For instance, whereas some centers have established pain pathways that facilitate effective analgesic administration protocols, 22 , 23 , 24 others lack these critical infrastructures, negatively affecting quality of care. 
Comparisons of the time to first pain medication administration by site-level characteristics suggested that patients with SCD at pediatric sites receive pain medications faster than patients at predominantly adult sites. There are many possible explanations for why this difference would exist, such as a reflection of less overall patient acuity at pediatric sites, 25 implicit bias, or more empathy toward children in pain; however, we did not account for other individual and site-level factors, such as pain severity, academic designation, and resource availability, that could affect the sites’ timeliness of pain medication administration. In our study, site-level univariate analysis by SCD encounter volume did not reveal a consistent pattern of change in the quality measure. Future studies could evaluate the effects of site-level factors on the quality measure in multivariate analyses. Additionally, our study reports a wide range of ED encounters for VOE across 225 sites, reflecting an overall annual average 1.5 VOE ED encounters per patient, consistent with findings from previous studies. 4 , 26 
Acute pain management is an integral part of comprehensive SCD care. 27 Although acute SCD pain management has multiple components, a significant focus of various quality initiatives has been the timeliness of pain medication delivery in the ED. 9 , 11 This emphasis indicates the recognition of the timeliness of pain medication delivery as a key element with the potential to improve overall pain management efficiency. Implementing strategies to improve this metric is a crucial step toward enhancing overall pain management practices across EDs. This could be achieved by promoting the best practices, identifying areas for improvement, and monitoring progress. Importantly, improving pain management has the potential to reduce hospitalizations, lower health care expenditures, and enhance patient experience and quality of life. 13 , 28 , 29 
Nevertheless, a significant concern anticipated with implementing the measure is that it would incentivize EDs to alter their practices to improve their performance on the measure without necessarily focusing on improving patient outcomes. Specifically with the implementation of the measure, a scenario could unfold in which ED providers switch the first pain medication from a parenteral opioid to an oral nonopioid to improve their ED’s performance on the measure, with no resulting improvement in patient pain. Findings from our study could be used to track the unintended consequences of the implementation of the quality measure; specifically, the proportion of parenterally administered first pain medication or opioid as the first medication should be of particular interest during future evaluations of the measure. 
Another drawback of the measure is the fact that it focuses exclusively on the first pain medication, implying that subsequent doses are less important, despite previous studies reporting that most patients with SCD VOE receive multiple doses of pain medication for effective treatment. 11 , 12 However, despite these concerns, the ASH-proposed clinical quality measure is a potential first step in improving overall analgesic timeliness, affecting care for patients with SCD VOE. 
Limitations 
This study has several limitations. Our sample is not nationally representative because the Cosmos database consists of only Epic users; however, the data are drawn from a large number of sites with significant variation in demographics and volumes of patients with SCD. Also, data from multiple EDs affiliated with the same organizational source are submitted to the database as a single source, which limits our ability to know whether a site represents a single or multiple EDs. Additionally, our comparisons of the measure according to site-level characteristics did not adjust for potential confounders such as academic designation and urban/rural locations. Furthermore, data on patient genotype are unavailable in the data set, limiting our ability to evaluate the impact of SCD genotype on the timeliness of pain medication for these patients. Nonetheless, our study undertook the first multisite evaluation of the proposed clinical quality measure, providing baseline data for future evaluations. 
Conclusions 
This study demonstrates widespread variability in timeliness and delays in pain medication delivery for acute SCD pain in the ED, highlighted by the poor performance of most EDs on the quality measure. Although implementing the quality measure could potentially motivate practice changes that improve pain management and lead to better outcomes for patients with SCD, providers should not overemphasize performance in the measure at the expense of genuine adherence to guidelines, which is essential for improving patient outcomes.
