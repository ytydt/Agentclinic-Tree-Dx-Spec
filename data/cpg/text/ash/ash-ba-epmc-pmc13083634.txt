Key Points 
• 
RBD4059, a novel siRNA targeting FXI, was well tolerated with a durable, dose-dependent suppression of FXI up to >90%. 
• 
RBD4059 may provide a novel option for preventing thrombosis in patients not adequately treated with current anticoagulation treatments.

Abstract 
Inhibiting coagulation factor XI (FXI) activity has emerged as a promising strategy to prevent intravascular thrombosis. Here, we describe the first-in-human trial with an N-acetylgalactosamine–conjugated small interfering RNA (siRNA), RBD4059, targeting FXI. In this phase 1 single ascending dose study ( NCT05653037 ), healthy volunteers were randomized to receive either active drug or placebo (6 + 2), administered subcutaneously, in 4 consecutive dose cohorts ranging from 50 mg to 600 mg. The primary end point was safety and tolerability up to day 169 after dose. Secondary end points included pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PD) parameters. Single ascending doses of RBD4059 were well tolerated, and no apparent safety concerns were identified. RBD4059 was associated with a dose-dependent and durable suppression of FXI activity up to >90%. Modeling of the available PD data supports clinical dosing schemes of 3 to 6 months to achieve a robust antithrombotic effect. Altogether, these observations support further clinical development of RBD4059 to provide an siRNA-based therapeutic option targeting FXI for patients inadequately treated with or contraindicated for current available thrombosis prophylaxis. This trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as # NCT05653037 . 
Introduction 
Cardiovascular and thromboembolic diseases are leading causes of death, 1 and intravascular thrombosis is a key and direct pathophysiological link to cardiovascular mortality and morbidity. 
Inhibiting activated factor X (FX) or FII in the common coagulation pathway with direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs) is one of the main strategies for preventing intravascular thrombosis and thromboembolic complications in patients with cardiovascular disease at risk of thrombosis. 2 , 3 For many indications, DOACs improve the benefit/risk balance over classical coumarin-based treatments by reducing severe and fatal bleeding events without requiring to monitor anticoagulation therapy. However, bleeding remains a major concern in high-risk patients (including those with prior major or critical bleeding episodes, fragility, or potential drug-drug interactions [DDI]) or when added to antiplatelet treatments of comorbid conditions. DOACs, through their mechanism of action in the common coagulation pathway, also inhibit hemostatic function as a part of their antithrombotic effect, thereby contributing to an increased risk of bleeding. 4 
Inhibiting coagulation FXI activity 5 has emerged as a promising strategy to potentially prevent intravascular thrombosis without significant effects on hemostasis, thereby further improving the benefit/risk balance in clinical treatment strategies for preventing thrombosis. 4 , 6 Clinically, individuals exhibiting genetic loss-of-function FXI variants with reduction in FXI activity (hemophilia C) generally display a lower risk of thrombosis without an overtly increased bleeding risk. 7 Conversely, increased FXI activity is associated with an increased risk of thrombosis. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 
FXI is almost exclusively synthesized in hepatocytes and then secreted into the circulation, in which it serves as an important initiator of the intrinsic coagulation pathway. 12 Inhibition of FXI synthesis in the liver using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) has previously been shown to reduce plasma FXI activity and prevent thrombosis in patients after total knee replacement surgery, with a lower risk of bleeding than enoxaparin. 13 
The concept of RNA silencing of FXI using small interfering RNA (siRNA), which provides an alternative mode of action and pharmacodynamic (PD) profile compared with ASOs, 14 is therefore highly attractive. If feasible, this could potentially provide long-acting, robust inhibition of FXI, alongside high treatment compliance and a lower risk of bleeding than current DOAC-based thrombosis prophylaxis. This would be beneficial to the millions of patients for whom adequate thrombosis prevention is not feasible due to increased risk of bleeding, poor compliance and adherence, or DDI. 
Based on promising preclinical data, 15 we performed a first-in-human study to assess the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetic (PK), and PD profiles of RBD4059, an N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)–conjugated (for delivery to the liver) siRNA targeting FXI, in healthy volunteers and to allow model-based estimation of dosing regimens in patients. 
Methods 
Study design 
We conducted a phase 1 single ascending dose trial ( NCT05653037 ) in healthy volunteers with the primary end point of safety and tolerability up to day 169 after dose, as well as secondary end points of PK and PD parameters (see supplemental Table 1 for protocol). Participants were randomized to receive active substance RBD4059 or placebo (6:2 ratio), administered subcutaneously, in 4 consecutive and incremental dose cohorts ranging from 50 mg to 600 mg, with dose escalations initiated after safety reviews, in agreement with Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study was sponsored and funded by Ribo (Australia) Life Science Pty Ltd and conducted at Q-Pharm Pty Ltd (Nucleus Network Pty Ltd) phase 1 trial unit according to valid Good Clinical Practice regulations and procedures. The protocol conformed with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board. Participants provided written informed consent before participation. 
Study participants 
Healthy volunteers were enrolled in the study based on key inclusion and exclusion criteria as defined in the protocol. Eligible male or female participants were required to be aged 18 to 65 years, have a body mass index between 18 and 32 kg/m 2 , and have activated partial thromboplastin time, prothrombin time, international normalized ratio, thrombin time, and FXI activity in the normal reference range (as per the local laboratory reference range) at screening. Additionally, participants needed a complete blood cell count within the normal range and to be otherwise healthy, as determined by prestudy medical history, vital signs, physical examination, general clinical laboratory assessments, including liver function tests, electrolytes, and creatinine, and have a normal 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG). 
Study drug 
RBD4059 is an siRNA drug formulation formed by hybridization of 2 chemically synthesized complementary RNA single strands; that is, the sense strand oligonucleotide containing 19 nucleotides and the antisense strand oligonucleotide containing 21 nucleotides. A triantennary GalNAc group for liver-targeted delivery is covalently conjugated via a linker to the 3' end of the sense strand. RBD4059 was dissolved in 25mM phosphate buffer to a concentration of 100 mg/mL (1 mL per vial was divided or combined to provide the allotted total dose). The colorless solution was administered as a subcutaneous injection in the abdominal/periumbilical or outer thigh area. The single dose administration could be up to 4 injections per dose and up to 2 mL per injection (matched for placebo injections), at the investigator’s discretion. The placebo-matched solution was without any active ingredient and had the same appearance as the RBD4059-containing solution. 
Study assessments 
The schedule of assessments is provided in supplemental Table 1 . 
Statistical analysis 
Study results were primarily analyzed in a descriptive approach based on the following data sets (n = participants included): full analysis data set (n = 32), safety analysis data set (n = 32), antidrug antibody (ADA) analysis set (n = 32), PD analysis set (n = 32), and PK concentration/parameter analysis set (n = 24). 
Safety and tolerability, PK, PD, and ADA measurements 
Safety and tolerability were assessed throughout the study. Laboratory safety analyses were performed locally and reported for the evaluation of overall safety. ECG recordings were performed at maximum compound plasma concentrations to evaluate potential effects on QTc intervals. Vital signs, including blood pressure, were also recorded. 
Blood and urine PK measurements were performed before dose and during the first 48 hours after dosing (see schedule of assessments in supplemental Table 1 ). Measurements of plasma and urine concentrations of RBD4059 were performed using high-performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry detection at PPD Laboratories, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China. 
PD measurements of FXI antigen and FXI activity were performed before dose and at defined time points throughout the 169 days of the study (see schedule of assessment in supplemental Table 1 ). FXI antigen levels and FXI clotting activity were assessed at the US PPD Central Laboratory (Highland Heights, KY), using standard validated assays. 
Blood samples were collected at specific time points before dose and toward the end of the trial (see supplemental Table 1 ) for measurement of ADAs against RBD4059. Serum ADAs were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay method at Thermo Fisher Scientific PPD Laboratories (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). 
Model-based analysis of FXI activity modeling and clinical dosing strategy 
A PD model (ie, a kinetic-PD model often referred to as a KPD-model) was developed to describe the effects of RBD4059 on FXI activity using data collected in the study. The model was then used to predict the degree of FXI inhibition expected to be achieved in different dosing regimens, supporting the further clinical development of RBD4059 in phase 2a. For detailed methodology of the model, see the supplemental Methods . 13

A total of 207 participants were screened, and 32 healthy volunteer participants were enrolled and randomized 6:2 (active:placebo) in each of the 50-, 150-, 400-, and 600-mg RBD4059/placebo cohorts (n = 8 per cohort). One participant in the 150-mg RBD4059 cohort voluntarily withdrew from the study on day 92. Baseline characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1 .

Safety events, safety laboratory analyses, vital signs, and ADAs 
A total of 40 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred during the study ( Table 2 ). No participants withdrew from the study due to TEAEs. In the RBD4059 group, 29 TEAEs were reported by 12 of 24 participants; 4 TEAEs were judged by the investigator to be related to RBD4059 treatment, including 1 in the 50-mg cohort, 1 in the 150-mg cohort, and 2 in the 600-mg cohort. All study drug–related TEAEs were injection site reactions and occurred within 21 days of administration. For the pooled placebo group, 11 TEAEs were reported by 5 of 8 participants, 6 of which were reported within 21 days after administration.

A total of 4 TEAEs were classified as grade 2, with 3 occurring in the placebo group (contact dermatitis, musculoskeletal chest pain, and tooth ache) and 1 TEAE of back pain occurring in the RBD4059 600 mg dose group, which was judged by the investigator to be unrelated to RBD4059. In the RBD4059 150-mg group, 1 participant experienced 2 grade 3 TEAEs: back pain and cervical radiculopathy (due to heavy lifting), both of which were judged by the investigator to be unrelated to RBD4059. Cervical radiculopathy was reported as a serious adverse event because it required surgical treatment. One participant in the RBD4049 150-mg group experienced thoracic back pain, which started 39 days after RBD4059 administration, after lifting heavy objects. On day 47, the participant went to the emergency department because the pain shifted to the right shoulder, radiating down the arm to the fifth digit. Due to persistent and worsening pain radiation on days 54 and 55 after dosing, computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging disclosed a large acute protrusion at C7/T1 impinging the exiting right C8 nerve root. A neurological review recommended anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, which the patient underwent on day 57 after dosing. According to investigator reports, the participant underwent surgical treatment during study drug exposure without requiring other treatments or reversal treatments during the procedure or recovery. The participant recovered well from surgery. 
Liver function tests, renal function tests, and electrolyte measurements did not detect any dose-dependent or clinically significant laboratory biochemistry or hematology changes during the study. There were no systematic or clinically significant changes in blood pressure, heart rate, or resting ECG. There were no recorded QTc changes related to RBD4059 exposure, and no treatment-emergent ADAs were detected after single dose administration of RBD4059. 
PKs 
RBD4059 exhibited dose-proportional plasma PK ( Figure 1 ) across the 50- to 600-mg dose range. Maximum plasma concentrations were reached at a median of 3 to 4 hours after dosing. Plasma concentrations declined quickly thereafter, with a mean half-life ranging from 3.4 to 4.6 hours, consistent with GalNAc-mediated uptake of the siRNA to the liver. Approximately 30% of the active substance was excreted into the urine as RBD4059 during the first 24 hours after dose administration.

PD 
PD measurements demonstrated a similar sustained dose-dependent reduction in both FXI antigen and activity ( Figure 2 ). The mean maximum percentage changes from baseline in FXI activity in the 50-, 150-, 400-, and 600-mg cohorts were 67.5%, 81.0%, 85.8%, and 91.6%, respectively; this effect was maintained at day 169, with activity reductions of 45.3%, 62.3%, 74.3%, and 83.5%, respectively. A dose-dependent increase in activated partial thromboplastin time was observed, whereas RBD4059 did not affect prothrombin time ( Figure 2 ). Thus, a single dose of RBD4059 at ≥150 mg resulted in >80% inhibition of FXI activity, exceeding the degree of inhibition expected to provide robust antithrombotic therapeutic benefit with enoxaparin. 13

PD modeling 
PD modeling predicted the expected reduction in FXI activity from baseline with different doses and dosing frequencies. Notably, the model predicted that FXI activity inhibition of >80% can be sustained over the dosing interval with RBD4059 dosing of 100 mg every 3 months or 400 mg every 6 months ( Figure 3 ).

Discussion 
Based on the discovery of fundamental RNA interference and silencing characteristics, 16 , 17 , 18 the introduction of siRNA into clinical medicine was initially hindered by difficulty in establishing an optimal chemistry and ligation platform to achieve immune-evasion and chemical stability to reach the intracellular Ago-DICER complex. 19 After decades of further research, siRNA targeting hepatocytes was developed based on GalNac conjugation and ASGPR1-mediated uptake, which has been highly successful, with some licensed therapies now available and more in clinical development. 14 
The observational evidence from genetic loss-of-function variants of FXI (hemophilia C)/genetically proxied FXI deficiency supports the notion of a decreased risk of thrombosis without increased risk of bleeding. 7 Preclinical experimental data supporting this concept, 15 along with the unique ability to leverage exclusive hepatocyte FXI expression and the major unmet need for safer anticoagulation, led us to develop a GalNac-conjugated clinical siRNA molecule targeting FXI. In this phase 1 trial, RBD4059 was well tolerated with no apparent safety concerns and delivered a dose-dependent, robust, and durable suppression of FXI concentration and clotting activity. Single doses of RDB4059 ≥150 mg reached FXI knockdown and inhibition in the expected maximal therapeutic range, potentially adding another important modality to the landscape of FXI inhibitors. 4 , 5 , 12 
To this end, the initial PD model developed from the phase 1 data set on FXI inhibition predicts that treatment with RBD4059 100 mg administered every 3 months or 400 mg every 6 months will be sufficient to maintain a FXI activity reduction of 80% over the selected dosing interval ( Figure 3 ; 80% was chosen as an a priori benchmark based on data from the FXI ASOs showing superiority compared with enoxaparin). 13 These predicted potential dosing regimens indicate that using an siRNA-based approach with RBD4059 to inhibit FXI activity allows for an expected clinical efficacious dose to be administered with a 3- to 6-month dosing interval, providing a novel therapy option for preventing thrombosis with a high degree of compliance or adherence. This model was also used to set doses in the RBD4059 multidose phase 2a trial ( NCT06717074 ) in patients with stable coronary artery disease on concomitant low-dose aspirin therapy. Further evaluation of dosing strategies in patient populations will be needed to assess the optimal approach. 
Furthermore, siRNA-based therapies have a low risk of DDI 20 and a low risk of significant increases in drug exposure with required dose adjustments in individuals with impaired renal or hepatic function, 20 which would benefit patients on multiple other drugs or at risk of organ function deterioration. 
siRNA-based inhibition of FXI activity is designed to mimic the genetic FXI loss-of-function genotype (hemophilia C). Should a need for reversal of FXI inhibition occur, reconstitution with fresh-frozen plasma or recombinant FXI is readily available, 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 in line with present recommendations for hemophilia C. 21 , 22 However, further data are needed regarding bridging or switching between anticoagulation treatments, for example, from DOACs to FXI inhibitors, because clinical experience is very limited at present. 25 It is currently not known whether the bleeding risk related to DOAC therapy is additive or synergistic to FXI inhibition, but the intrinsic pathway is upstream of activated FX inhibition of the common pathway and, therefore, may not directly affect the hemostatic response. 
Finally, increased FXI activity is associated with increased risk of thrombosis and appears to be temporally elevated in association with acute events. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Research into whether this acute phase–mediated rise in FXI is also blunted by siRNA-mediated suppression of hepatic production is lacking. If it is, then siRNA-mediated FXI inhibition may work differently from other FXI inhibitors that act on circulating FXI or activated FXI. Further PD data on FXI levels and coagulation tests during acute phase responses are needed to address these clinical aspects. 
In conclusion, RBD4059 was safe and well tolerated in healthy volunteers, with a dose-dependent reduction of FXI activity up to >90% after a single dose. PD modeling supports the potential to achieve robust antithrombotic effects with a 3- to 6-month dosing schedule. Taken together, these observations support further clinical development of RBD4059 to provide a novel siRNA-based therapeutic option for patients currently inadequately treated or contraindicated for thrombosis prevention with DOACs. RBD4059 is currently being investigated in a placebo-controlled multidose phase 2a trial ( NCT06717074 ) in patients with stable coronary artery disease on concomitant low-dose aspirin therapy. Other FXI inhibitor modalities are being tested in large phase 3 trials, and upcoming data readouts will further guide the clinical potential, benefit/risk balance, and dosing strategies for FXI inhibition in diverse clinical populations requiring prevention of thrombotic and thromboembolic diseases. 
Limitations 
We acknowledge the modeling limitations based on the relatively small sample size of the phase 1 study. However, the model provides a first estimate of the dose range and dosing schemes relevant to carry forward into patient populations. Further evidence generated in patient populations will allow for more accurate modeling, as well as broader safety and efficacy assessments in relation to indication and patient phenotypes.
