Abstract 
Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), including polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and myelofibrosis (MF), are chronic myeloid neoplasms associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Statins are a common group of cholesterol-lowering medications recommended for the primary and secondary prevention of CVD, including arterial thrombotic events. Emerging evidence suggests that statins may reduce the risk of developing MPNs and their use may be associated with improved survival. However, the effect of statins on cardiovascular and hematologic outcomes among patients with MPNs remains uncharacterized. We conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of patients with MPNs who had at least 1 transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) from 2010 to 2024. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) competing-risk regression analysis was performed to assess the association between statin use at time of index TTE on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), MPN disease progression, and all-cause death. Patients with MPN were analyzed as a whole and separately by type (ET or PV and MF). A total of 669 patients were included, 43.9% were on statin use, 50.5% were female, 83.9% were White, 78.8% had JAK2 driver mutation, and 72.9% had class I guideline indication for statin therapy. There were 267 (39.9%) patients with PV, 234 (35.0%) with ET, and 168 (25.1%) with MF. After IPTW, statin use was associated with lower risk of MACE but not MPN disease progression or all-cause death. Among patients with ET or PV, statin use was associated with lower risk of MACE but not MPN progression or all-cause death. Among patients with MF, there was no difference in MACE, leukemia progression, or all-cause death. Among patients with MPNs who underwent TTE, statin use was associated with lower risk of MACE, particularly among patients with ET or PV. However, there was no association between statin use and all-cause death or MPN disease progression. Statin therapy is underused in this patient population. Further studies are needed to explore the utility of statin therapy in patients with MPN and identify patients who would benefit most from statin therapy. 
Introduction 
The Philadelphia chromosome–negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), including polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and myelofibrosis (MF), are a group of chronic myeloid neoplasms associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), including thrombotic events and heart failure (HF). 1 , 2 , 3 Driver mutations leading to constitutive activation of the Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway promote a chronic inflammatory state that is associated with increased CVD risk. 2 , 4 Rates of CVD and thrombosis remain elevated among patients with MPNs. Moreover, treatment guidelines for MPN, such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines, recommend managing cardiovascular risk but offer no concrete guidance for how to apply CVD guidelines for patients with MPN. 
Statins, 3-hydroxy-3-methylgultaryl–coenzyme A inhibitors, are a group of lipid-lowering medications that are used and recommended in cardiovascular society guidelines for primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD). 5 , 6 In addition to lipid-lowering effects, particularly of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels, statins also have anti-inflammatory and atherosclerotic plaque stabilizing effects. 7 In preclinical studies, statins demonstrated antiproliferative effects in myeloid malignancies. 8 A population-based cohort study demonstrated an association between statins and a lower risk of developing MPN. 9 Statin use among patients with PV was associated with a reduction in the number of therapeutic phlebotomies and may enhance the efficacy of pegylated interferon therapy. 10 , 11 Despite these studies, it remains unclear whether statin use is associated with cardiovascular outcomes, including thrombosis and HF, as well as hematologic progression, among patients with MPNs. We therefore leveraged a multicenter, retrospective cohort study with focus on granular cardiovascular data of patients with MPNs to investigate the association between statin use and outcomes. 
Methods 
Patient population 
This was an analysis of a retrospective registry of patients aged ≥18 years meeting World Health Organization 2016 criteria for MPN (ET, PV, or MF), who had at least 1 transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) after their MPN diagnosis from 1 January 2010 to 1 January 2024 at 3 academic hospitals. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Patients who underwent bone marrow transplantation (BMT) before first TTE were excluded. Baseline characteristics at the time of first TTE, including age, comorbidities, MPN type, driver mutation ( JAK2 , CALR , MPL , or triple negative), presence of nonphenotypic driver mutations, and spleen size (as measured by largest craniocaudal dimension on cross-sectional imaging or ultrasound if performed within 3 months of TTE or MPN diagnosis), were captured. Laboratory values, including white blood count, hemoglobin, platelet count, and lipid panel captured. Markers of inflammation, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), were also captured. This study was approved by the New York University, University of Chicago, and Massachusetts General Hospital institutional review boards. Given retrospective nature of the study, inform consent was not required. 
Outcomes 
The primary cardiovascular outcome was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), a composite of cardiovascular death, arterial thrombosis, venous thromboembolism, and HF hospitalization. Cardiovascular death was determined via chart review, and deaths were deemed to be cardiovascular deaths if the primary cause of death was due to MACE or arrhythmias. The primary hematologic outcome was progression of MPN disease to secondary MF or acute leukemia. Secondary outcomes were all-cause death and individual components of MACE and MPN progression. 
Statistical analysis 
In the primary analysis, patients on statin therapy at the time of first TTE were compared with patients not on statin therapy. Statin therapy was assessed via review of the electronic medical record and pharmacy records. Patients were deemed to be on statin therapy if there was documentation of statin use within 3 months of first TTE. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis was performed. Propensity scores (PS) were calculated using a nonparsimonious multivariable logistic regression using age at first TTE, sex, race, body mass index, time from MPN diagnosis to first TTE, MPN phenotype, MPN canonical driver mutation status ( JAK2 , CALR , MPL , or triple negative), presence of nonphenotypic driver mutations including high molecular risk mutations (mutations in ASXL1 , SRSF2 , EZH2 , IDH1/2 , or U2AF1 ), treatment for MPN at time of TTE, previous HF, previous ASCVD including peripheral arterial disease and coronary artery disease, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, smoking status, presence of class I indications for statin therapy per cardiovascular society guidelines, left ventricular ejection fraction, antiplatelet (including aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitor) use, anticoagulation, and treatment with antihypertensive medications as covariables. 5 , 6 , 16 , 17 , 18 Weights for IPTW were calculated with 1/PS being assigned to patients with statin therapy and 1/(1 − PS) for patients not on statin therapy. 19 To estimate risk of MACE and individual components of MACE, and MPN disease progression (including MF progression and leukemia transformation), subhazard ratios (SHR) were estimated using Fine-Gray competing-risk regression modeling before and after IPTW, with noncardiovascular and all-cause death as competing risks, respectively. To estimate risk of all-cause death, hazard ratios (HR) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression before and after IPTW. Patients were censored at time of BMT, if performed. Given that time-to-event modeling only accounts for the first event, risk of total recurrent number of MACE events was modeled using negative binomial regression to estimate incident rate ratio (IRR). 
Patients with ET or PV and patients with MF were analyzed separately. Patients with ET or PV were analyzed in an identical fashion as the entire MPN cohort. Given the small number of patients with MF, multivariable Fine-Gray, Cox proportional hazards, and negative binomial regressions were performed adjusting for age, sex, Mutation-Enhanced International Prognostic Score System 70, previous ASCVD, and antihypertensive use. Patients with ET or PV were also compared with patients with MF and stratified analysis by statin use status was performed using multivariable competing-risk regression adjusting for age, sex, driver mutation, and nonphenotypic driver mutation. 
To assess whether statin intensity affected outcomes among patients on statin treatment, patients treated with high-intensity statins (defined as rosuvastatin ≥20 mg daily or atorvastatin ≥40 mg daily) were compared with those treated with moderate- or low-intensity statins. 6 To assess for risk of outcomes, multivariable Fine-Gray, Cox proportional hazards, and negative binomial regressions were performed adjusting for age, sex, previous ASCVD, coronary artery disease, previous stroke, P2Y12 inhibitor use, and time from MPN to first TTE. 
Categorical variables were compared using χ 2 or Fisher exact tests. Continuous variables were compared using a Student t test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and STATA version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). 
Results 
Baseline patient characteristics of patients with MPN treated with vs without statins before and after IPTW 
A total of 669 patients were included, 294 (43.9%) were on statin therapy at time of first TTE, 338 (50.5%) were female, 561 (83.9%) were White, 488 (72.9%) had a class I indication for statin therapy, 527 (78.8%) had JAK2 mutation, 267 (39.9%) had PV, 234 (35.0%) had ET, and 168 (25.1%) had MF. The mean time from MPN diagnosis to first TTE was 60.9 months, and the mean follow-up time from first TTE was 55.8 months. Patients treated with statins were older (74.5 vs 68.4 years; P < .001), less likely to be female (45.6% vs 54.4%; P = .024), and less likely to have MF (18.0% vs 30.7%; P < .001) compared with patients not on statin therapy. Patients on statin therapy also had higher rates of previous CVD and CV risk factors. Patients on statin therapy were more likely to have a class I indication for statin use (85.0% vs 63.5%; P < .001). Mean levels of LDL (70 vs 86 mg/dL; P < .001) and total cholesterol (140 vs 155 mg/dL; P < .001) were lower among patients treated with statin. Among patients with available ESR (N = 167), CRP (N = 108), and NLR (N = 649) data, patients treated with statins had lower ESR (mean, 10.9 vs 19.0 mm/h; P = .002) but no statistically significant difference in CRP and NLR. Among 488 patients with class I indications for statin therapy, 250 (51.2%) were on statin therapy at time of first TTE. The proportion of patients with statin indication who were on statin therapy was lower among patients with MF (38.5%). After IPTW, there was no difference in baseline characteristics and comorbidities between patients with vs without statin treatment ( Table 1 ).

Outcomes of patients with MPN treated with vs without statins before and after IPTW 
After a mean follow-up time of 55.8 months, 267 (39.9%) patients had at least 1 MACE event. Before IPTW there was no difference in MACE, MPN disease progression, and all-cause death between patients treated with vs without statins. After IPTW, patients with statin use had fewer MACE (36.5% vs 43.7%; P = .008), arterial thrombosis (14.4% vs 20.9%; P = .002), and HF hospitalization (18.1% vs 23.9%; P = .011) compared with patients without statins but no difference in MPN disease progression (13.0% vs 14.1%; P = .58) and all-cause death (37.6% vs 41.5%; P = .16; Table 2 ).

Additionally, statin treatment was associated with lower total number of MACE (IRR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.62-0.94), arterial thrombosis (IRR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.57-0.96), and HF hospitalization (IRR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.52-0.98) events ( Figure 1 ).

Baseline patient characteristics and outcomes of patients with ET or PV treated with vs without statins before and after IPTW 
There were 501 patients in our cohort with either PV (267) or ET (234) at time of first TTE, of whom 241 (48.1%) were treated with statins at time of first TTE. Patients with PV or ET treated with statins were, on average, older (75.2 vs 68.4 years; P < .001), less likely to be female (49.0% vs 58.5%; P = .039) and had higher rates of CVD and cardiovascular risk factors. There was no difference in PV, canonical driver mutation, and nonphenotypic driver mutations between groups. Serum levels of LDL (70 vs 92 mg/dL; P < .001), total cholesterol (142 vs 164 mg/dL; P < .001), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL; 47 vs 51 mg/dL, P = .010) were lower among patients treated with statins. Among the patients with available ESR (N = 138), CRP (N = 74), and NLR (N = 484) data, patients treated with statins had lower ESR (mean, 10.2 vs 17.2 mm/h; P = .004) but no statistically significant difference in CRP and NLR. After IPTW, both groups were well balanced ( supplemental Table 1 ). 
After IPTW, statin treatment was associated with lower risk of MACE (35.6% vs 44.8%; SHR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.64-0.95), arterial thrombosis (17.4% vs 24.1%; SHR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.55-0.96), and HF hospitalization (18.0% vs 25.5%; SHR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.54-0.93) but not MPN progression (13.9% vs 14.4%; SHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.74-1.44) or all-cause death (28.9% vs 38.6%; HR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.68-1.06). Treatment with statins was also associated with lower total number of MACE (IRR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.60-0.96) and arterial thrombosis (IRR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.57-0.99) but not HF hospitalization (IRR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.55-1.10) events ( Figure 2 ).

Baseline patient characteristics and outcomes of patients with MF treated with vs without statins 
There were 168 patients with MF, of whom 53 (31.5%) were treated with statins. Patients with MF treated with statins had similar age at time of first TTE compared with patients not on statins. There was no difference in canonical driver mutation or nonphenotypic driver mutation between groups, as well as Mutation-Enhanced International Prognostic Score System 70 score. Patients with MF treated with statins were less likely to be treated with ruxolitinib (18.9% vs 37.4%; P = .016) or receive any treatment for MPN (47.2% vs 65.2%; P = .027) and had higher rates of previous ASCVD (54.7% vs 23.5%; P < .001), and class I indication for statin use (88.7% vs 65.2%; P = .001). There was no significant difference in serum levels of LDL and total cholesterol in patients treated with vs without statins. Among patients with available ESR (N = 29), CRP (N = 34), and NLR (N = 165) data, there was no difference in ESR, CRP, and NLR ( supplemental Table 2 ). 
There was no difference in rates of MACE, progression to leukemia, or all-cause death in patients with vs without statin use. After multivariable time-to-event regression modeling, there was no difference in MACE (adjusted SHR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.45-1.48), leukemia progression (adjusted SHR, 0.53; 95% CI, 0.15-1.90), or all-cause death (adjusted HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 0.93-2.80) among patients treated with statin compared with no statin. There was also no difference in overall MACE events between groups (adjusted IRR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.44-1.63; Figure 3 ).

Among all patients with MPN treated with statins (N = 294), 79 (26.9%) were treated with high-intensity statins. Patients treated with high-intensity statins were on average younger and less likely to be White compared with patients treated with moderate- or low-intensity statins. Comparison of outcomes and cholesterol levels of patients with ET or PV vs MF with and without statin use 
Patients with MF were younger (median age, 70 vs 74 years; P = .007) at time of first TTE and were less likely to be on statin therapy (31.5% vs 48.1%; P < .001). Nonphenotypic and high molecular risk mutations were more common in patients with MF. Patients with MF had similar rates of CVD. Median total cholesterol (127 vs 150 mg/dL; P < .001), LDL (77 vs 69 mg/dL; P = .002), and HDL (33 vs 46 mg/dL; P < .001) were lower among patients with MF, whereas triglyceride levels (125 vs 100 mg/dL; P = .003) were higher. Rates of MACE were similar although patients with MF had higher rates of acute leukemia progression (10.7% vs 3.4%; P < .001) and death (51.2% vs 33.9%; P < .001; supplemental Table 3 ). 
After stratifying by statin use, there no difference in total cholesterol, LDL, and triglyceride levels among patients with statin use. However, total cholesterol (median, 127 vs 165 mg/dL; P < .001) and LDL levels were lower in patients with MF and triglyceride levels were higher in patients with MF among patients not on statin therapy ( supplemental Table 4 ). 
After multivariable modeling, among all patients, MF was associate with increased risk of death (adjusted HR, 3.53; 95% CI, 2.50-4.99). Among patients treated with statins, MF was associated with increased risk of HF hospitalization (adjusted SHR, 2.77; 95% CI, 1.40-5.46) and death (adjusted HR, 4.78; 95% CI, 2.87-7.98), with no association with MACE (adjusted SHR, 1.26; 95% CI, 0.71-2.22). Among patients not on statin therapy, MF was associated with increased risk of death (adjusted HR, 3.13; 95% CI, 1.93-5.08) and acute leukemia (adjusted SHR, 4.04; 95% CI, 1.35-12.06), but not MACE (adjusted SHR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.60-1.67; supplemental Table 5 ). 
Discussion 
In our multicenter retrospective cohort study of patients with MPN with or without statin use, statin use was associated with a lower risk of MACE, arterial thrombosis, and HF hospitalizations but not all-cause death and hematologic progression. However, among patients with MF, there was no association with statin therapy and MACE, leukemic transformation, or all-cause death. Additionally, among patients with MPN on statin therapy, there was no difference in outcomes among patients treated with high- vs low- or intermediate-intensity statins. 
An important finding in our study is the association between decreased MACE and statin use in patients with MPN, particularly in ET or PV. In our study, among patients treated with statins, there was a 20.5% and 27.8% relative risk reduction of MACE and arterial thrombosis, respectively. The magnitude of relative risk reduction in our patient cohort is similar to data in the general population for primary and secondary prevention. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 A previous study using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results–Medicare database of patients with ET or PV demonstrated an association between statin therapy use in the first year after MPN diagnosis and reduced all-cause mortality and thrombosis. 24 However, this study did not investigate HF, which is an underappreciated but increasingly recognized cardiovascular complication of MPNs. 3 Our study demonstrated an association between statin use and decreased risk of overall and recurrent HF hospitalization, in addition to a decreased risk of arterial thrombosis. Previous studies have similarly suggested an association between statin use and decreased risk of HF. 25 Additionally, statins have been shown to reduce endothelial dysfunction and improve microvascular reactivity, which may explain their potential protective effect from HF. 26 , 27 This is potentially important in MPNs, given that endothelial and coronary microvascular dysfunction may play a role in the development of HF in this patient population. 28 , 29 Unlike previous studies, there was no association between all-cause death and statin use in our cohort. This may have been due to incomplete follow-up in some patients and a smaller sample size that was underpowered for this outcome. 
Statins play an important role in primary and secondary ASCVD prevention in the general public. In our study, among the entire MPN cohort and patients with ET/PV, statin use was associated with lower LDL and total cholesterol levels compared with no statin use. After IPTW, there was no difference in LDL and a trend toward higher total cholesterol levels and, despite this, there was a significant association between statin and reduced risk of MACE. This implies an alternative mechanism of reduced MACE other than LDL reduction. The anti-inflammatory effect of statins may explain the association between statin use and reduced MACE risk. 
In patients with MF, we found no association between statin use and MACE and all-cause death. Additionally, there was no difference in serum total cholesterol, LDL, and inflammatory markers between patients treated with vs without statins. Potential explanations for this include a possible reduced adherence to statin therapy due to polypharmacy or reduced efficacy of statins. This lack of anti-inflammatory and lipid-lowering effect may partially explain the lack of association between statin use and MACE compared with in patients with ET or PV. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated lower levels of serum LDL and total cholesterol in patients with MF compared with those with ET or PV. This may be due to poor nutritional status, increased cell turnover, and a hyperproliferative state and may confound the effect of statins on lipid levels and MACE outcomes although further investigation is needed. 30 Patients with MF may also have higher levels of systemic inflammation and inflammatory cytokines than patients with ET or PV. 31 Whether the degree of inflammation or other factors affect statin efficacy and pleiotropic effects in patients with MF remains to be elucidated, and further studies are needed. Our study demonstrated worse outcomes, including all-cause death and HF hospitalization, among patients with MF compared with those with ET or PV regardless of statin use. Additionally, among patients without statin use, patients with MF had lower levels of LDL and total cholesterol. A perceived or actual risk of decreased life span among patients with MF, coupled with lower LDL levels at baseline, may explain the lower rates of statin use among patients with MF. Additional studies are needed to explore the interplay of lipid levels on outcomes in this patient population. 
Among patients with MPNs on statin therapy, our study suggests that there is no difference in outcomes between high- and low- or moderate-intensity statin therapy after multivariable adjustment. However, there were lower rates of all-cause death among patients with high-intensity statins, although our study is limited by size. Larger studies are needed to evaluate an interaction between statin intensity and MPN outcomes. Our study also showed relatively low statin use among patients with class I indications for statin therapy per cardiology guidelines. This suggests that statins may be underused in this patient population and may be due to a variety of factors including lower LDL levels at baseline, polypharmacy, or anticipated poor long-term prognosis. Further work needs to be done to improve statin use and adherence. 
Our study has several limitations to consider. The first limitation is that our study included only patients who had undergone TTE after MPN diagnosis, which likely introduces indication and selection biases to our cohort and enriches our population with more patients with CVD and cardiovascular risk factors. Additionally, given that we dichotomized patients by statin use at 1 time point, it is likely that some patients crossed over from statin use to nonuse and vice versa. Precise timing of statin initiation was not captured in our study and therefore the impact of timing of statin therapy on outcomes could not be explored in our cohort. Only statin intensity (high intensity or not) was captured; therefore, the impact of specific statins on outcomes could not be determined. Only a minority of our cohort had CRP and ESR measurements, and inflammatory cytokine levels were not captured, therefore further studies are needed to assess the role of the anti-inflammatory effect of statins and its potential impact in MPN outcomes. There were no patients on novel lipid-lowering therapies, including proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors, in our study; therefore, their impact on outcomes among patients with MPN remains unclear. Additionally, patients who underwent BMT before TTE were excluded and patients who received BMT before outcomes were censored at time of BMT therefore the impact of statins on post-BMT outcomes is not known and merits further study. Our patient population cohort was primarily White, thus further research is needed to evaluate whether race-based or socioeconomic disparities exist in the use of statins and outcomes in this patient population. 
Conclusions 
In our multicenter, retrospective study of patients with MPN, statin therapy was associated with decreased risk of MACE and HF hospitalization but not all-cause death. Additionally, after stratifying by type of MPN, patients with MF had worse survival regardless of statin use compared with patients with ET or PV. Additionally, our study suggests that statins may be underused among patients with indications for statin therapy for the primary and secondary prevention of ASCVD, especially among patients with MF. Further studies are needed to explore the effect statin therapy on MPN disease progression and the interaction with MF phenotype. Additionally, studies investigating pleiotropic effects of statin therapy in patients with MPN are needed in order to better understand the role of both serum lipids and inflammation on cardiovascular outcomes.
