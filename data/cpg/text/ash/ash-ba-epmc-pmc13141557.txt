Abstract 
Hemoglobinopathies comprise the most common monogenic diseases worldwide. In Europe, formerly low-prevalence regions have seen increasing numbers of carriers and patients, with important public health implications. Implementation of policies and laboratory methodologies varies across countries, affecting equitable care. This study aimed to provide a snapshot of current screening and diagnostic practices and to identify gaps and priorities. A cross-sectional online survey was conducted from May to October 2024 across the COST (European Cooperation in Science and Technology) Action Haemoglobinopathies in European Liaison of Medicine and Science network participants. The survey, including 21 questions, covered hematological methods, molecular diagnostics, and laboratory practices and policies. Responses were provided directly by centers and summarized descriptively in tables and maps. Responses were received from 38 centers in 20 countries. Hematology remains the diagnostic backbone for hemoglobinopathies, DNA-based testing is widely adopted although advanced genomics are limited, newborn screening is implemented widely for sickle cell disease and thalassemia, carrier reporting practices vary, and genetic modifier testing is emerging. The findings highlight opportunities for cross-country collaboration and targeted capacity building, identifying centers that could benefit from shared expertise and resources. These findings can guide strategies to harmonize protocols, strengthen diagnostic capabilities, and support broader implementation of best practices across Europe. 
Introduction 
Hemoglobinopathies are inherited disorders of hemoglobin that remain prevalent in former malaria regions but are increasingly recognized as a global public health concern due to population movement and migration. 1 , 2 Early assessments by the European Network for Rare and Congenital Anaemias and the Thalassemia International Federation (TIF), 3 , 4 drawing on expert opinion and data from 12 European countries, highlighted differences in health policies, system readiness, and equitable access to care. Although prevention and care systems for hemoglobinopathies have been implemented in some countries with a longstanding disease burden or established migrant populations, such approaches remain the exception rather than the norm. Gaps persist, especially where screening and diagnostic infrastructure is limited, laboratory standards are inconsistent, or hemoglobinopathies are excluded from strategic health planning because they were historically absent from the native population. 5 , 6 
To explore how broader challenges within health care systems, such as infrastructure, funding, and policy, are reflected in the availability, scope, and application of diagnostic services for hemoglobinopathies, we adopted a direct, practice-level approach by reviewing data collected from 38 centers across 20 countries in Europe and beyond. Screening and diagnosis serve complementary roles in the assessment of hemoglobinopathies. Screening identifies asymptomatic individuals at risk of carrying or developing the disease, through population, carrier, prenatal, or newborn programs, whereas diagnosis confirms disease in symptomatic individuals, with molecular testing considered the gold standard. Hemoglobinopathies uniquely allow carrier detection through hematological and biochemical methods, although results remain presumptive unless supported by at least 2 complementary techniques and can be further diagnosed by genotyping to confirm heterozygous status. 7 The complexity and range of diagnostic approaches, and diverse testing indications and practices 8 , 9 highlight the need to understand service delivery across settings with varying income levels and capacities, a critical step toward harmonizing procedures despite the challenges this entails. Against this background, this study aims to map current screening and diagnostic practices for hemoglobinopathies across centers in Europe and beyond, and to identify patterns, gaps, and areas of convergence at the practice level. 
By collecting structured data directly from active centers, our study builds upon prior policy-level analyses to provide a detailed overview of current practices. Conducted through the COST (European Cooperation in Science and Technology) Action Haemoglobinopathies in European Liaison of Medicine and Science (HELIOS), 10 a network of >245 experts from >35 countries, this mapping reveals both diversity and convergence in methodologies across Europe and adjacent regions. These insights are essential for informing targeted improvements, promoting harmonization, and supporting capacity-building initiatives across different health systems. 
Methods 
A cross-sectional survey was conducted to map the current screening and diagnostic methods for hemoglobinopathies across centers in Europe and beyond. The survey, originally developed within the collaborative International Hemoglobinopathy Research Network, 11 was further refined with input from members of HELIOS Working Group 1. It consists of 21 core questions, some with subquestions, covering 3 main areas: hematological methods, molecular methods, and laboratory practices and policies. 
The survey was developed and administered via the HELIOS REDCap 12 and launched on 27 May 2024. Invitations were sent to 57 HELIOS members based on their self-declared specialization in laboratory diagnosis and/or molecular research of hemoglobinopathies, of whom 43 responded (75% response rate). Nonrespondents were professionally diverse (including clinicians, students, and researchers from already represented institutions) and were based in the United Kingdom, Egypt, France, Greece, Italy, Spain, Switzerland, and Cyprus, indicating no apparent systematic bias in participation. The survey remained open for 5 months, and responses were reviewed for completeness. Clarification emails were sent to 33 respondents, with a 75% response rate (25/33). Missing responses were not imputed, and analyses were based on the information provided. Eight responses (from Albania, Greece, Portugal, The Netherlands, Turkey [n = 2], and Serbia [n = 2]) were excluded from center-level analysis because respondents were unable to complete center-level questions on screening and diagnostic practices due to lack of access to relevant institutional information. However, relevant country-level policy information was retained. The final data set was compiled on 17 March 2025. Geographical visualization used MapChart 13 and kepler.gl (Uber Technologies Inc). Country income groups were classified per World Bank 2025 Gross National Income data as high-income countries (HICs), upper-middle-income countries (UMICs), and lower-middle-income countries (LMICs); UMICs and LMICs were combined due to the small number of LMIC centers. Data processing and statistical comparisons with χ 2 tests were performed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA). This was a mapping exercise, not an evaluation of individual centers, and did not involve patient data. All contributors consented to data processing and sharing. All participants in this survey provided written informed consent and a personal declaration for the processing of personal data, in compliance with applicable laws, for the specific purposes they are collected. 
Results 
Composition and capacities of responding centers 
We received 43 responses from 38 centers in 20 countries ( Figure 1 ), with most centers located in Europe and Central Asia. The data set spanned diverse health system capacities and included 12 HICs (Cyprus, Czech Republic, Greece, Malta, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia, Denmark, France, Germany, The Netherlands, and Spain), 6 UMICs (Albania, Armenia, Serbia, Turkey, Kosovo, and Malaysia), and 2 LMICs (Morocco and Nigeria). Table 1 provides an overview of the 30 centers in the final center-level data set. Most (90%) were dedicated hemoglobinopathy laboratories (hematology and/or DNA analysis), and 67% served as national reference or expert facilities for hemoglobinopathy diagnostics. Reference/expert status did not differ by country income. Most European Reference Network EuroBloodNet members/affiliates (n = 8) also identified as national bodies, reflecting strong integration in national and international frameworks. Crossborder services, including diagnostics, training, consultation, and research, were offered by 43% of centers and were more frequent in HICs (12/20) than in UMICs/LMICs (1/10; χ 2 = 6.79; P = .009). Staffing ranged from 3 to 40 individuals (mean, 12; median, 8), with the largest teams (>20) in countries with historically high carrier prevalence linked to former malaria endemicity, such as Cyprus, Malaysia, Morocco, Nigeria, and Turkey. The size of populations served varied widely, from local catchments of hundreds to several million; centers in Germany, France, and Czech Republic reported over one million people per staff member. Public health insurance covered screening (hematology and/or molecular tests) in 80% of centers. Most laboratories (90%) were publicly funded. However, payment mechanisms varied: in Morocco, Slovenia, and Turkey, out-of-pocket contributions were required despite public funding, whereas the laboratory in The Netherlands operated through private insurance billing. Conversely, some privately owned laboratories, such as in Slovakia, were supported by public insurance, whereas those in Kosovo and Armenia relied on private or out-of-pocket payments. Mixed models were reported in Germany and Nigeria, where certain tests were reimbursed publicly and others required private or patient payments. These differences illustrate the centers’ varied capacities and contributions within their regions.

Laboratory policies and quality assurance 
Accreditation (or equivalent) was reported by 43% of centers ( Table 1 ) and was more frequent in HICs than UMICs/LMICs (χ 2 = 6.78; P = .009; supplemental Figure 1 ), reflecting differences in national regulatory frameworks. It was mostly granted by national bodies aligned with international standards (eg, International Organization for Standardization [ISO] 15189, ISO 9001) or their national adaptations, such as Cyprus Organization for Standardization European Norm ISO 15189:2012 (Cyprus), Slovak Technical Standards European Norm ISO 15189:2022 (Slovakia), Entidad Nacional de Acreditación, (Spain), COmité FRançais d’ACcréditation (France), Deutsche Akkreditierungsstelle (Germany), and Czech Institute for Accreditation (Czech Republic). Other certifications included designation as a “Centre de Référence Maladies Rares” (France), and hematological testing accreditation by the Hellenic Hematology Society (Greece), reflecting formal oversight of specific laboratory functions. 
Guideline adherence was reported by 90% of centers (n = 27) for hemoglobinopathy screening and/or diagnosis ( Table 1 ). One-third followed national guidelines and another third international guidelines, with commonly referenced sources including European Molecular Quality Network, 7 TIF, 14 and British Society For Haematology. 8 Additional examples included Sociedad Española de Hematología y Oncología Pediátrica (Spain), Arbeitsgemeinschaft der Wissenschaftlichen Medizinischen Fachgesellschaften (Germany), Vereniging voor Hematologische Laboratoriumdiagnostiek (The Netherlands), the Malaysian Clinical Practice Guideline for Transfusion-Dependent Thalassemia, and the French network for red blood cell disorders. Notably, 22% (n = 6) used in-house protocols based on published guidelines ( supplemental Figure 2 ). Overall, guideline use spanned all income levels, indicating broad alignment with established professional standards. 
A variety of systems were reported for tracking hemoglobinopathy cases, with spreadsheets and electronic health record (EHR)/hospital systems most common (9/30 each [30%]), followed by laboratory information systems (LIS; n = 7). Hybrid setups (n = 4), custom/standalone databases (n = 3), and generic registers (n = 4) were less frequent, whereas manual records were limited to a single center (Nigeria), reflecting a shift away from paper-based methods ( supplemental Figure 3 ). Usage patterns varied by income: HICs (n = 19) more often used LIS and EHRs (32% each) but some still relied on spreadsheets (21%) or custom systems (16%); UMICs (n = 9) favored spreadsheets (44%) and EHRs (33%); LMICs (n = 2) reported the least advanced setups, including Excel-based and manual records. Variation across and within countries highlighted fragmented implementation and uneven integration of advanced systems (eg, EHR/LIS). 
Screening programs and detection strategies at the national level 
Availability of newborn screening (NBS) and carrier detection programs for hemoglobinopathies was reported in a similar proportion of countries (8/20 [40%]), with no apparent association between country income and program implementation. As shown in Figure 2 A, national NBS programs were reported in HICs across Southern/Western Europe, specifically France, Germany, Malta, The Netherlands, Portugal, and Spain. All reported universal NBS for sickle cell disease (SCD). In addition, all, except Germany, also screened for α- and β-thalassemia, and targeted screening in France. Regional NBS was reported in Kosovo (UMIC), and a pilot initiative for SCD was reported in Nigeria (LMIC). National carrier screening programs ( Figure 2 B), were reported in Southern Europe (Cyprus, Greece, Malta, Turkey), Northern Europe (Denmark and The Netherlands), and Southeast Asia (Malaysia). Cyprus and Greece reported universal screening for all hemoglobinopathies, consistent with established national policies. Turkey offered universal screening for α-thalassemia and SCD, with targeted screening for α-thalassemia. The Netherlands screened universally for SCD, reflecting its growing importance. Malta and Denmark applied targeted screening for all hemoglobinopathies, whereas Malaysia targeted α- and β-thalassemia. Regional SCD screening programs existed in Spain, with some regions providing targeted screening and others disclosing carrier status through NBS.

Hematological methods for hemoglobinopathy carrier screening or detection 
Hematological methods were used for hemoglobinopathy carrier screening by 70% of centers (21/30). Adoption varied within countries; for example, 1 of 2 centers in Albania, Germany, and Spain; 1 of 3 in Turkey; and 1 of 4 in Greece did not report applying in-house hematology. Nonusers were also in Cyprus, Malaysia, Slovenia, and Slovakia. Μost centers incorporated hematological methods into anemia investigations, family studies, and prenatal/antenatal screening ( Figure 3 A). Less frequent uses included reproductive planning (premarital/preconceptional screening), policy-based screening, and diabetes screening. Three centers reported use in NBS (France and Spain) and pre–in vitro fertilization testing (Malta), whereas others applied them to screen for hemoglobin variants (n = 4) and hereditary spherocytosis (n = 2).

A similar number of centers reported using hematological methods for patient and carrier testing ( Figure 3 B), with higher sample volumes for carrier testing, possibly reflecting differences in screening strategies or population structure. NBS was less widely implemented but involved large sample volumes (eg, 40 000 in France, 10 000 in Portugal [outsourced from Angola], and 4500 in Malta per year). This variability highlights differences in screening policies and coverage across centers. 
All 21 centers that reported using hematological methods for hemoglobinopathy screening performed both complete blood count and at least 1 form of hemoglobin analysis ( Figure 3 C). Most centers used automated platforms; high-performance liquid chromatography and capillary electrophoresis ( supplemental Figure 4 ). Traditional methods, hemoglobin electrophoresis at pH 8.6 on cellulose acetate, acid gel electrophoresis at pH 6.0, and isoelectric focusing, remain in use at 10 centers across UMICs (Armenia, Albania, and Turkey), HICs (Greece, Spain, Portugal, and France), and LMICs (Morocco and Nigeria). Peripheral blood smears and iron studies (ferritin and transferrin) were widely used, with exceptions in Portugal and Kosovo. Most centers used biochemical markers of hemolysis (bilirubin, lactate dehydrogenase, aspartate aminotransferase, and plasma free hemoglobin), with exceptions in Czech Republic, Greece, Kosovo, Portugal, and The Netherlands. Specialized tests such as hemoglobin H inclusions, osmotic fragility, and sickle solubility were used variably; all 3 were applied in centers from Greece, Turkey, and Morocco, whereas other centers, such as France, Kosovo, Malta, Nigeria, and Portugal, reported none. One-third of centers reported additional assays, including oxygen scan and hemoglobin stability tests (Denmark), eosin-5-maleimide tests and ektacytometry (France, Portugal), cryohemolysis (Portugal), red cell density assays (France), acid glycerol lysis and reticulocyte count (Greece), and coagulation studies (Nigeria). Outsourcing was reported by 10 centers in Albania, Armenia, Czech Republic, Greece, Malta, Portugal, Turkey, and France. 
Molecular methods in hemoglobinopathy diagnosis 
Most centers (25/30 [83%]) used DNA-based methods to genotype hemoglobinopathy carriers and patients. The 5 centers not using DNA-based methods (2 in Greece; 1 each in France, Nigeria, and Turkey), relied on hematological screening. As seen in Figure 4 A, most centers (n = 18) tested for all 3 major hemoglobinopathies (α-thalassemia, β-thalassemia, and SCD), whereas a few others focused on a subset of these conditions; for example, α- and β-thalassemia (Morocco), and β-thalassemia and SCD (Albania, The Netherlands, and Turkey). Seventeen centers also diagnosed other conditions, mainly hemoglobin variants and other forms of thalassemia, such as γ- and δβ-thalassemia. One center (Slovenia) used DNA exclusively for erythrocytosis. SCD and β-thalassemia were diagnosed early in life, most before 6 months, whereas α-thalassemia showed a broader age range, often identified during adolescence ( supplemental Table 1 ). Molecular testing practices did not vary significantly by country income, indicating variability across centers.

Figure 4 B shows highest uptake of molecular testing for patient genotyping (n = 23), followed by carrier genotyping (n = 19) and prenatal genetic diagnosis of the fetus (n = 16). Six centers also reported DNA use for preimplantation genetic testing (Cyprus and Greece), noninvasive prenatal diagnosis (Cyprus), genetic counseling (France), NBS (Spain and Malta), and national programs (Malaysia). Annual sample volumes varied widely. Carrier genotyping volumes were highest in countries with established screening programs (Cyprus, Greece, Spain, and The Netherlands; ≥1000 samples per year), and lowest in Albania, Portugal, and Morocco, countries without formal programs. Patient genotyping volumes ranged from 2000 in Germany and 1800 in Malaysia to <50 per year in 9 centers (Albania, Armenia, Turkey, Cyprus, Czech Republic, Greece, Malta, Slovenia, and Slovakia). Notable within-country variation (eg, Germany, Greece, and Turkey) suggests sample volumes reflect local demand and infrastructure more than national income. Prenatal genetic diagnosis was less commonly performed, being more specialized and resource intensive, with stark differences even between same-country centers, for example, Portugal (n = 200 vs 5) and Greece (n = 120 vs 10), whereas both centers in Spain reported the lowest volumes (as low as 1 sample per year). 
Among 25 centers with DNA-based diagnostic capacity ( Figure 4 C), most performed DNA extraction from the blood, buffy coat, or saliva (n = 22), with some also using dried blood spots (n = 9), followed by DNA quantification (n = 22). Cryostorage (−20°C/−80°C in 72%) and long-term storage (>3 months in 80%) were also commonly available. α - and β -genotyping was conducted by 72% (n = 18), mainly in HICs (Cyprus, Czech Republic, Greece, Malta, and Portugal), and absent in lower-resource settings (Armenia, Kosovo, and Morocco; supplemental Figure 5 ). Sanger sequencing, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–based techniques were the most reported for variant detection ( Figure 4 C). Capacity was limited in some UMICs and LMICs (eg, PCR-based approaches in Armenia and Morocco, none in Kosovo). Although HICs reported broader use, within-country differences (eg, France, Germany, Greece, and Spain) suggest variation in capacity or priorities. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) use was limited with targeted gene panels reported in 12 centers mainly across HICs and some UMICs (Malaysia and Turkey), whole-exome sequencing in 5 centers (Cyprus, France, The Netherlands, Portugal, and Turkey), and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) only in Denmark and The Netherlands. Devyser Thalassemia NGS kits were reported in 4 centers (Cyprus, Czech Republic, Malaysia, and The Netherlands), indicating growing uptake of commercial NGS solutions in well-resourced settings. 
Notably, 12 centers reported using genetic modifier analysis in diagnosis, either routinely or selectively based on phenotype ( supplemental Figure 6 ). Most targeted fetal hemoglobin–related modifiers (n = 7) and assessed α-thalassemia in β-hemoglobinopathies (n = 5). Others reported testing HBD (Germany), β- haplotype (Portugal, in SCD), SUPT5H (The Netherlands, in β-thalassemia), and HFE (Spain). Broader approaches were noted in France (targeted NGS) and Denmark (WGS for unexplained phenotypes). 
Discussion 
This survey presents, to our knowledge, the most extensive comparative mapping to date of hemoglobinopathy diagnostic and screening services, covering 38 centers in 20 countries across diverse geographic and economic contexts, conducted as part of HELIOS. 
Our findings reveal key aspects of service capacity. Staffing and population coverage, including crossborder services, generally align with local disease burden, with larger teams in regions with historically high prevalence, across the Mediterranean. Several HICs in which hemoglobinopathies are not endemic have high population-to-staff ratios, likely reflecting referral-based models or migration-driven demand. 15 , 16 Many laboratories operate as specialized hemoglobinopathy centers within national health systems and networks, providing a basis for collaboration to standardize and improve laboratory practices. 2 
Most centers receive public funding, but varying reimbursement models, from public insurance to private payments or direct patient costs, may limit equitable access. Laboratory accreditation <50% highlights disparities in infrastructure, regulation, and/or resources. Although many cited ISO or national equivalents, these are not universally mandated, and uneven adoption underscores the need for coordinated efforts toward harmonization. 
Encouragingly, most centers follow guidelines; however, variation in sources and specificity reveals ongoing gaps in standardization. For example, many centers use in-house protocols based on international recommendations, which, although practical and tailored to local needs, may lack formal validation or consistent application across settings. Progress toward digital tracking systems for hemoglobinopathy cases is evident, especially in wealthier settings. Continued use of spreadsheets and paper records reveals fragmentation but also opportunities for developing interoperable systems essential for robust surveillance and seamless continuity of care. 
Screening strategies range from universal national programs to targeted or institution-based efforts. NBS is traditionally designed to facilitate early clinical management, more crucial for SCD than for thalassemia, and provides a cost-effective approach given the high long-term clinical and economic burden of managing undiagnosed hemoglobinopathies. 17 , 18 , 19 Nearly all centers conduct universal NBS for SCD; most also screen for thalassemia, except Germany (none) and France (targeted), reflecting debate over immediate newborn benefits despite the value of early family counseling. 20 In some countries, NBS is the sole preventive measure, whereas others, mostly HICs (eg, Spain and The Netherlands), combine newborn and carrier detection. Beyond identifying affected newborns, NBS can reveal carriers, helping to identify a percentage of at-risk families and, with genetic counseling, support informed reproductive family planning 21 ; however, carrier reporting practices vary. In some regions of Spain, carrier status is reported during the NBS program, whereas 1 center in France lists NBS among the contexts for SCD carrier testing using hematology. Although advocacy emphasizes the preventive value, particularly when antenatal screening is unavailable, carrier reporting remains debated due to administrative and ethical considerations. In low-resource settings such as Nigeria, hemoglobinopathy screening is often fragmented and institution-based due to limited resources and uneven infrastructure, despite high public health needs. Pilot NBS programs in Nigeria, which has a high SCD burden, 22 show potential for broader implementation, highlighting the importance of regional collaboration, advocacy, awareness, and sustainable funding. Conversely, countries around the Mediterranean and parts of Southeast Asia with high carrier rates, such as Cyprus, Greece, and Malaysia, integrate broad carrier screening with genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis, markedly reducing affected births. 23 Overall, these findings underscore the need to expand equitable carrier screening and integrate it with broader hemoglobinopathy prevention efforts to reduce global disease burden. 
Across participating centers, routine hematological tests (complete blood count) and standard hemoglobin separation methods provide a common diagnostic foundation. However, variation in the range and complexity of techniques reflects differences in laboratory capacity, funding, and infrastructure. Although most centers incorporate differential diagnosis for iron deficiency, HICs generally offer more specialized testing, and some centers, across different settings, occasionally rely on outsourcing of specific analyses. These data provide a snapshot of current practices to inform future efforts in capacity building and harmonization. 
The broad use of DNA-based methods shows strong uptake of molecular diagnostics, although scope and application vary. Most centers offer broad testing, whereas a few focus on specific disorders. In some laboratories, such as in Greece without in-house hematology, full hematology is requested before reporting molecular results. A subset continues to rely solely on hematological methods, underscoring persistent gaps in molecular diagnostics. Disparities are most evident in LMICs, in which capacity remains minimal or absent. In Morocco and Nigeria, testing was limited to basic procedures (DNA extraction, PCR–restriction fragment length polymorphism) or unavailable, highlighting inequities. Early diagnosis is common for SCD and β-thalassemia, whereas α-thalassemia diagnoses occur over a wider time range, likely reflecting milder presentation or lower screening priority. Conventional molecular techniques (PCR-based assays, Sanger sequencing, and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification) remain the diagnostic backbone, whereas advanced genomic approaches, including generic whole-exome sequencing, WGS, or more tailored commercial NGS platforms, are limited to centers with infrastructure and investment; some UMICs, such as Malaysia and Turkey, demonstrate substantial NGS implementation. In the absence of validated evidence and thus currently not recommended in hemoglobinopathy guidelines, several centers recognize the potential clinical impact of genetic modifiers and incorporate related testing into their workflows, highlighting a gap between emerging practice and current guidance. TIF guidelines for β-thalassemia note modifiers such as α-thalassemia coinheritance and γ-globin expression. 24 Wider adoption, however, requires evidence of clinical utility to justify routine use, and further research and consensus for standardized integration into diagnosis. Overall, although molecular diagnostics are increasingly embedded in hemoglobinopathy testing, marked inequalities persist, particularly in low-resource settings, emphasizing the need for targeted investment, technology transfer, and regional collaboration to support harmonized, scalable services. 
Practical considerations and implications 
This mapping highlights priorities for strengthening hemoglobinopathy services across diverse settings. Greater alignment around core diagnostic workflows, including minimum hematological and molecular standards and consistent reporting practices, could support quality and comparability while allowing flexibility for local capacity. In parallel, heterogeneity in registration and tracking of hemoglobinopathy cases underscores the need for interoperable digital tracking systems linking screening, diagnosis, and care. Finally, uneven access to molecular diagnostics and quality frameworks points to the importance of targeted capacity building through training, infrastructure support, and cross-center collaboration. 
Limitations 
Our study offers a valuable snapshot of current screening and diagnostic practices across 38 HELIOS-affiliated centers in 20 countries. However, participation was voluntary and focused on self-identified experts in hemoglobinopathy diagnostics and molecular research, potentially favoring specialized centers and limiting representativeness. Notably, Italy, an early adopter of hemoglobinopathy prevention policies, has no representation. Although 43 responses were received, this coverage may not fully capture intracountry variability or the broader diversity of diagnostic capacities, especially in underrepresented regions. Data were self-reported and not independently verified, introducing potential variability due to differences in terminology and protocols across centers/countries. To mitigate this, the survey was iteratively refined with HELIOS Working Group 1 members, and responses were cleaned and clarified as necessary; however, some inconsistencies may remain. Despite these limitations, our study provides important insights into the current diagnostic landscape and establishes a foundation for identifying gaps, harmonization needs, and opportunities for future collaboration and capacity building within the HELIOS network.
