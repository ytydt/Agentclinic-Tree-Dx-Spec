TO THE EDITOR: 
B-lymphoblastic lymphoma (B-LLy) accounts for <5% of all pediatric non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Despite its identical cellular morphology and immunophenotype to B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), B-LLy clinically presents as a solid lymphoid tumor with <25% bone marrow involvement. Because of its relative rarity, there is a knowledge gap in B-LLy biology and treatment compared to its more common disease counterparts, T-lymphoblastic lymphoma (T-LLy) and B-ALL. Depending on the treatment era, patients with B-LLy have been grouped either with children with B-ALL or T-LLy by Children’s Oncology Group (COG), European Intergroup for Childhood Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma, and Berlin-Frankfurt-Munster group protocols. Although this consolidated approach to clinical trial enrollment has facilitated collection of clinical outcome data for B-LLy, it has prevented comprehensive tissue collection for molecular characterization and direct study of B-LLy-specific therapies. As therapeutic advances involving targeted pathway inhibitors 1 and immunotherapies like the bispecific T-cell engager blinatumomab 2 continue to improve outcomes for children with other premature lymphoid cancers like T-LLy and B-ALL, respectively ( Table 1 ), a critical question remains for children with B-LLy: what is the optimal treatment approach and role of B-cell–directed immunotherapy?

Blinatumomab, antibody-drug conjugates like inotuzumab ozogamicin, and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy have shown promising efficacy in B-ALL, but evidence for their use in patients with B-LLy remains anecdotal. Blinatumomab, which is currently US Food and Drug Administration–approved as an IV formulation but under investigation in adult B-ALL as a subcutaneous formulation (ClinicalTrilas.gov identifier: NCT07223190 ), has shown survival benefit in both pediatric and adult B-ALL as part of relapse, bridging, and upfront therapy. 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 Blinatumomab acts by engaging both CD3 on T cells and CD19 on B cells, leading to T-cell activation, perforin and granzyme release, and ultimately B-cell apoptosis 7 ( Figure 1 ). Recently, data from the COG B-ALL/B-LLy clinical trial AALL1731 revealed that the addition of 2 nonsequential cycles of blinatumomab to upfront chemotherapy significantly improved disease-free survival by preventing medullary relapse in children with newly diagnosed standard risk B-ALL, although isolated central nervous system relapse was not prevented by blinatumomab. 2 This benefit was pervasive across subjects regardless of prognosticators like minimal residual disease status or cytogenetic subtype, as well as across patient race and ethnicity. 2 Thus, National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines now recommend the inclusion of blinatumomab as part of upfront B-ALL therapy. 8 However, despite children with localized B-LLy having been enrolled on this groundbreaking B-ALL/LLy protocol, they were ineligible for blinatumomab randomization due to a paucity of preliminary B-LLy-specific efficacy data.

Patients with B-LLy have a generally favorable prognosis when treated on a B-ALL chemotherapy backbone, but an absence of established biomarkers for relapse prediction and limited salvageability after relapse has prevented exploration of risk-directed treatment modifications. Currently, treatment intensity is directed only by clinical stage as determined by Murphy Staging System or International Pediatric Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma Staging System. Patients with localized disease have a 3 to 5 year event free survival (EFS) in the range of >90% to 95% and are treated on a standard risk protocol, whereas those with disseminated disease warrant treatment with high-risk therapy and have an analogous EFS of 80% to 85%. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Survival after B-LLy relapse is historically extremely poor at around 20% to 30%. 11 , 13 , 15 , 16 Patients with disseminated B-LLy are at higher risk of relapse than those with localized disease. Regardless of initial stage, most relapses occur within the first 2 years and—in addition to localized, primary site relapses—commonly involve the bone marrow rather than the CNS. 16 Thus, it is logically presumed that most B-LLy relapses arise from persistent minimal marrow disease (MMD), underscoring the importance of defining better strategies to eradicate MMD as part of frontline B-LLy therapy. 
New methods for preventing medullary relapse for children with B-LLy are sorely needed. Efforts to intensify early treatment with increased cytotoxic chemotherapy have been ineffective due to intolerable toxicity. 11 In the modern era, B-cell–directed immunotherapy offers a valuable new opportunity to optimize disease eradication in B-LLy, as observed in B-ALL. Although the role of blinatumomab in treating B-LLy has not been directly tested, the propensity for B-LLy to relapse systemically suggests that data from COG AALL1731 showing blinatumomab’s efficacy in preventing B-ALL marrow relapse can likely be extrapolated to B-LLy. Both diseases share near universal expression of CD19, the target antigen of blinatumomab. 
Molecular characterization initiatives have yet to reveal whether B-LLy is truly a distinct entity from B-ALL entirely or merely a clinical subtype on a heterogeneous disease spectrum. As illustrated in patients with T lymphoblastic disease, the distinct therapeutic benefit of agents like nelarabine and bortezomib between T-ALL and T-LLy underscore the importance of independently testing new modalities across disease types, 1 , 17 rather than assuming analogous efficacy. Despite some evidence that B-LLy may derive from a more mature B-cell precursor stage than B-ALL, its comparable immunophenotype and at least partially overlapping molecular landscape 18 , 19 suggest enough similarity to justify formal testing of blinatumomab in children with B-LLy. Opportunities may exist for collaborative B-LLy–focused initiatives across trial consortia. In the meantime, analysis of real-world data pertaining to clinical use of blinatumomab in children with B-LLy could provide valuable preliminary insights, as this agent is often incorporated by clinicians in the relapsed/refractory setting or as part of upfront treatment, with justification extrapolated from B-ALL. 
In summary, the efficacy of blinatumomab as a means of improving B-LLy disease-free survival cannot be directly inferred from existing B-ALL data, as these diseases are clinically and biologically distinct, but there is strong justification in support of explicitly testing blinatumomab in a B-LLy trial setting. The current 5-year EFS of 80% to 85% among patients with disseminated B-LLy remains unacceptably inferior to B-ALL and other childhood non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Specific B-LLy-directed trial initiatives will be critical to improving outcomes. Thus, we pose 2 critical questions: (1) can incorporation of blinatumomab prevent medullary relapse among all children with B-LLy, particularly those with disseminated disease? and (2) can the addition of blinatumomab facilitate directed deintensification efforts to minimize treatment toxicity for children with localized B-ALL, while preserving their excellent survival outcomes? Because B-LLy relapse is predominantly medullary, and because blinatumomab is most effective in eliminating marrow disease, 2 , 4 we postulate that blinatumomab will offer therapeutic benefit in B-LLy by eradicating MMD and reducing bone marrow relapse. We support trial efforts to explicitly test this in pediatric B-LLy.
