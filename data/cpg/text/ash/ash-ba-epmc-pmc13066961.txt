Abstract 
When advising patients on the optimal duration of anticoagulation for venous thromboembolism (VTE), current management often relies on the intuition of treating physicians and guideline panels, an approach that remains opaque to outside observers. We present a decision-analytical framework adopted in our practice. This approach formalizes the intuition of physicians and guideline panels, providing an explicit and transparent integration of the best available evidence on benefits and harms. It also incorporates a systematic method for eliciting patient values and preferences to determine the optimal duration of anticoagulation. To demonstrate the practical utility of the decision-analytical approach, we apply it to 3 clinical scenarios: unprovoked VTE, VTE related to long-haul travel, and portal vein thrombosis in cirrhosis. By transparently synthesizing evidence and patient values, this method supports individualized, evidence-based, patient-centered recommendations for the duration of anticoagulation, and it is applicable to solving similar clinical problems in hematology and medicine. 
Introduction 
The existing literature offers extensive guidance on the optimal duration of anticoagulation (AC) therapy for patients with venous thromboembolism (VTE), which is widely recognized as a leading hematological cause of morbidity and mortality. VTE consists of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism (PE). 1 , 2 , 3 Conceptually, management of VTE is straightforward: in patients with VTE, AC should be continued until the risk of recurrence declines to the baseline level observed in the general population (1-2 per 1000 per year). 4 , 5 This forms the basis of the risk-oriented approach, commonly expressed as the management of “provoked” (ie, VTE that occurs in the presence of a transient or persistent risk factor) vs “unprovoked” (ie, VTE that occurs in the absence of any identifiable transient or persistent provoking risk factor). 1 , 6 , 7 
Evolutionarily, a surge in thrombin, triggering the hemostatic cascade, is most often associated with strong provoking factors, such as injury, trauma, or surgery. When VTE arises in these contexts, the hemostatic response typically returns to baseline within 3 to 6 months. 8 Thus, the recommended duration of AC for “provoked” VTE is generally 3 to 6 months. 1 In contrast, in patients with “unprovoked” VTE, the risk of recurrence never drops to baseline levels seen in the general population. 9 Similar considerations apply to situations in which VTE occurs in relation to “weak” (eg, long-haul air travel) or “intermediate” risk factors (eg, nonsurgical hospitalization, active infection, or other significant medical illnesses). 10 These factors can be transient (eg, pregnancy, estrogen therapy, travel) or persistent after the initial VTE event (eg, thrombophilia, active cancer). 1 , 10 However, even in these settings, the risk of recurrence rarely, if ever, returns to the baseline seen in the general population. 3 , 10 , 11 , 12 Therefore, AC for “unprovoked” VTE or VTE related to nonstrong risk factors should generally extend well beyond 3 to 6 months. However, the question of “how long” has not been explicitly addressed in contemporary guidelines. This is surprising because, in everyday practice, hematologists worldwide make these decisions, likely numbering in the hundreds of thousands, each day. In other words, it is unclear how hematologists actually advise their patients on the duration of AC. 
In our practice, we use decision analysis in the management of VTE and in determining the optimal duration of anticoagulation. Decision modeling has a strong scientific foundation, and has been widely used in economics and clinical medicine, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 but it has not sufficiently penetrated the hematological literature. Here, we provide practical examples of how decision analysis can be applied in hematology practice, adapted to address the complexities of hematological conditions such as VTE. 
A decision-analytical framework 
From this conceptual framework, the optimal duration of AC can be approached by considering 3 key questions derived from the fundamental concerns that patients have: 
“What happens if nothing is done—that is, if I don’t continue anticoagulants?” vs 
“What good and bad things can happen to me if I continue taking blood thinners?” 
These questions are: 
• 
What is the risk of VTE recurrence without treatment after the initial episode? 
• 
What are the benefits and harms of AC therapy? Specifically, how effective is AC in preventing recurrence, and what is the associated risk of major bleeding? 
• 
How do patient preferences factor in? Do patients value avoiding recurrent VTE more than preventing bleeding events, or vice versa? 
This framework is often illustrated as a balance scale, weighing these factors against each other ( supplemental Figure 1 ). However, even current state-of-the-art, evidence-based guidelines often rely on intuitive or implicit weighting of these key considerations to formulate recommendations for AC duration. 3 , 6 As a result, the process by which guideline panels integrate the factors depicted in supplemental Figure 1 remains opaque, even when using the best available evidence. 11 , 12 , 17 , 18 
Here, we describe a simple and explicit decision-analytic approach to provide transparent and easily understandable recommendations for the optimal duration of AC. Because it is widely accepted that the risk of VTE recurrence without anticoagulation is highest immediately after the initial event, ∼40% during the first month, and 10% during the second and third months, respectively, 19 we assume that all patients receive initial (primary) AC therapy for 3 to 6 months. The primary therapy aims to prevent recurrent VTE during the acute and subacute phases after the first event. 1 In this article, we exclude patients with active bleeding, because bleeding management takes clinical priority. We focus on determining the optimal duration of anticoagulation for secondary prophylaxis, preventing recurrent VTE after the acute phase, using a threshold decision model informed by the best available clinical evidence. 
Evidence and key inputs for the decision model 
The risk of VTE recurrence without treatment 
To assess the risk of VTE recurrence without treatment, we rely on Khan et al’s 9 systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 randomized controlled trials and prospective observational cohort studies involving 7515 patients. In this analysis, the authors assessed VTE recurrence after discontinuation of anticoagulation in patients with unprovoked VTE. They estimated cumulative recurrence risk as follows: 10.3% (95% confidence interval [CI], 8.6-12.1) in the first year, 16% (95% CI, 13.3-18.8) at 2 years, 25.2% (95% CI, 21.3-29.3) at 5 years, and 36.1% (95% CI, 27.8-45) at 10 years. 9 Khan et al 9 judged that all studies included in their meta-analysis were of high quality, indicating that these assessments are close to the true outcomes observed in this patient population. 20 
Because ACs are often recommended indefinitely, 1 , 7 we are also interested in outcomes beyond 10 years. Unfortunately, the data with the longest follow-up reported in the literature are up to 20 years, described in a prospective cohort of 839 patients with first unprovoked VTE by Kyrle et al. 21 The reported 10-year cumulative recurrence risk was 32% (95% CI, 29-36), and the 20-year cumulative recurrence risk was 44% (95% CI, 38-49). We judged this study of moderate quality and used it to extend calculation of cumulative risk by 20 years. The data from the studies by both Khan et al 9 and Kyrle et al 21 confirmed the persistent elevation above baseline risk after discontinuation of anticoagulation. 
Benefits and harms of anticoagulant therapy 
Benefits 
The best evidence for the benefits (efficacy and effectiveness) of anticoagulant treatment comes from the American Society of Hematology (ASH) panel on the management of VTE. 1 The panel estimated relative risk (RR) reduction (RRR) with indefinite anticoagulation as 0.85 (95% CI, 0.77-0.90), judged as high certainty. The ASH thrombophilia panel adopted these values to develop their recommendations, 3 and we also use these values as starting points in our discussion of AC benefits. 
The ASH panel adhered to the widely recommended statistical practice of assuming constant RRR over the predicted risk range when estimating treatment effects for individual patients. 22 , 23 , 24 When calculating the absolute risk of VTE recurrence on anticoagulants (VTEriskRx), we apply this RRR to the risk of VTE recurrence without treatment (VTEriskNoRx) using the formula: 
VTEriskRx = VTEriskNoRx × (1 − RRR) (Equation 1) 
Thus, if RRR = 1, the risk of VTE recurrence becomes 0 (ie, completely preventable); if treatment is ineffective, the risk remains unchanged. For example, in the first year, patients with unprovoked VTE have a 10% recurrence risk 3 , 9 ; with RRR of 0.85, this is reduced to 1.5% (95% CI, 1-2.3). (Note that RRR = 1 − RR.) 
Harms 
The ASH panel also judged the certainty of evidence for major bleeding as high. 1 They estimated RR for major bleeding on indefinite anticoagulant treatment at 2.17 (95% CI, 1.40-3.35), with baseline (off ACs) major bleeding rates of 0.5% (low risk) and 1.5% (high risk) per year. These values were also adopted by the ASH thrombophilia panel 3 and are our defaults for initial decision-making. 
Because long-term data on bleeding are not available, to project the cumulative probability of major bleeding at least once during N years of follow-up, we apply the standard formula 25 : 
Cumulative risk (year N) = 1 − (1 − annual risk_bleed) N (Equation 2) 
in which annual risk_bleed is the annual probability of major bleeding, and N is the number of years. 
In our practice, we predominantly use direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), mostly apixaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran. Although the approach is applicable to all anticoagulants (and, generally, all treatments), unless otherwise stated, the reader should assume we are referring to DOACs. 
Patient V&P 
Management of VTE necessitates trade-offs between benefit (prevention of VTE recurrence) and harm (bleeding; see visual abstract and supplemental Figure 1 ). These trade-offs are shaped by patient values and emotions, reflected in the normative ethical principle: “no decision about me, without me,” that is, physicians are repeatedly told to use shared decision-making to elicit patients’ values and preferences (V&P) in the management of VTE and other disorders. 26 Unfortunately, learning a patient’s true V&P is not easy, and physicians are simply not well trained in how to elicit V&P, even though it is considered one of the most essential aspects of effective and ethical medical practice. 26 , 27 To complicate matters further, it should be noted that there is no universally accepted method for eliciting V&P. 
Here, we provide a method that we have successfully used in our practice. Because optimal decisions should integrate both analytical and emotional considerations, we use a regret-based approach to elicit V&P. 28 Regret is technically defined as a “cognitive emotion,” which we are motivated to regulate to achieve our desired goals. 28 , 29 This makes regret theory especially suitable for clinical decisions, 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 that is, regret taps into the affect-based aspect of cognition and, by prompting the imagination of counterfactuals, guides our deliberative and analytical reasoning. 28 , 34 
In our model, V&P are represented as the relative value (RV) parameter: 
• 
RV < 1: the patient values avoiding the consequences of VTE more than (major) bleeding; 
• 
RV > 1: the patient values avoiding bleeding more than VTE; and 
• 
RV = 1: the patient values both equally. 
RV is defined as the ratio of regret associated with commission (giving unnecessary/harmful treatment) to regret associated with omission (failing to provide effective treatment): 
RV = regret of commission/regret of omission 
We elicit these values using 2 methods. First, by asking direct questions, such as: 
• 
How many more times would you regret receiving unnecessary (harmful) anticoagulant treatment that may not have benefited you at all, compared to not receiving treatment that could have prevented a serious event?” 
• 
How many more times would you regret missing out on the benefits of anticoagulant treatment, compared to receiving unnecessary (and potentially harmful) anticoagulant treatment?” 
Alternatively, we use the dual visual analog scale: 33 , 35 , 36 
• 
Regret of commission: “on a scale from 0 to 100, how much regret would you feel if you received unnecessary treatment, that is, treatment that, in hindsight, should not have been given?” 
• 
Regret of omission: “on a scale from 0 to 100, how much regret would you feel if you did not receive necessary treatment, that is, treatment that, in hindsight, should have been given?” 
Once we elicit RV, we use it in our decision model (see “The threshold decision model”). Although the literature suggests that most patients consider a bleeding event to be about three-quarters as bad as a VTE event, 37 , 38 in our experience, patients often cannot decide which outcome is worse. Thus, our default is to set RV = 1, individualizing as needed. We have developed a digital tool to help elicit V&P (accessible at VTE Anticoagulation Duration Decision Tool ). 
The threshold decision model 
The foundations and methodology of threshold models are well described, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 including their applicability to VTE management 11 , 12 and other diseases. 13 Briefly, threshold models link scientific evidence (on a continuum) to categorical clinical decisions (yes/no), for example, prescribe or withhold AC. 13 , 34 , 39 The threshold model simply formalizes the process depicted in the visual abstract and in supplemental Figure 1 . By explicitly and transparently integrating each key component of benefits and harms for a given health intervention, while also accounting for V&P, the model arrives at the most optimal resolution of the inherent trade-offs in managing VTE. It directly addresses the central question all physicians face on a daily basis: at what probability, “how high is high?” of VTE recurrence should we recommend anticoagulation to justify its benefits over its harms, namely major bleeding? 
Formally, this can be expressed by the following simple threshold equation 11 , 13 , 14 : 
T rx = RV × [absolute risk of (major) bleeding]/RRR (Equation 3) 
Here, T rx is the risk of VTE recurrence without treatment (VTEriskNoRx) at which we are undecided whether we should give AC. 
• 
If VTEriskNoRx >T rx , AC is justified. 
• 
If VTEriskNoRx <T rx , AC is not justified. 
The model nicely combines regret-based elicitation of RV and standard decision analysis. 11 , 13 
When considering thrombophilia testing in addition to treatment, we also calculate a testing threshold (T test ). 11 , 12 Thrombophilia testing should only be done for T test < VTEriskNoRx < T rx . For these cases, we use our threshold-based thrombophilia management calculator ( Thrombophilia Management Calculator , also available at Thrombophilia Calculator ). 11 , 12 
General practice and default assumptions 
Our estimates of VTEriskNoRx primarily draw on meta-analytic data (see “Evidence and key inputs for the decision model”). We do not routinely monitor clot resolution by ultrasound, 40 as the results do not affect our decision thresholds. Because of their poor performance to date, we also do not use D-dimer 41 or predictive models to individualize VTE or bleeding risk. 42 , 43 When we do use these, it is as a sensitivity check when evaluating the stability of our decisions, not as the main basis for our recommendation. 
Given that the RRR is relatively fixed (typically ∼0.85, though it may be as low as 0.5), our clinical assessment focuses on bleeding risk and eliciting patient V&P. We reassess our recommendations on an annual basis. Because anticoagulants are highly effective and thresholds are typically very low, when in doubt we err on the side of continuing AC. 
We also developed an interactive web application that allows bedside adjustment of threshold equation variables (accessible at: https://mak-djulbegovic.github.io/vte-duration-calculator/ and https://mak-djulbegovic.github.io/vte-anticoagulation-advisor/ ). 
Application to actual patient care: case discussions 
Case 1: a 38-year-old man with unprovoked VTE 
A 38-year-old African American man, otherwise healthy, developed severe shortness of breath during his daily run. He was diagnosed with bilateral PE and started on apixaban, which he had tolerated with no adverse effects. Six months later, after negative thrombophilia evaluation, he was referred for advice on duration and dose of AC. He tolerated apixaban well, experienced no bleeding, and strongly preferred to avoid recurrent PE. 
This is a classic case of unprovoked PE with a high risk of recurrence. Based on our model, the patient should be offered indefinite anticoagulation ( Figure 1 A), unless the bleeding risk outweighs the benefit of treatment or his preferences change ( Figure 1 B). Although a recent trial found that reduced dose of DOACs (eg, apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily) did not meet noninferiority criteria compared with the standard dose, 44 a decision-analytic perspective, factoring in long-term bleeding risk and patient values, supports the use of the reduced dose for long-term prevention ( supplemental Figure 2 ). Standard noninferiority testing does not account for patient V&P; decision analysis does. 17 We recommended apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily with annual reassessment, consistent with the patient’s preferences.

Case 2: a 52-year-old woman with VTE due to long-haul travel 
A 52-year-old woman developed PE 3 weeks after a flight of duration >8 hours. She has no personal/family history of VTE, and is otherwise healthy. She completed 3 months of apixaban and sought advice on whether to continue AC. She was not a frequent flyer, but flies to Asia and back to the United States twice a year. She was undecided whether she values the consequences of bleeding more than those of VTE (RV = 1). The patient was deemed to be at low bleeding risk. 
Management of VTE related to long-haul travel is debated. 45 Some experts consider long-haul travel a transient, episodic, weak risk factor (similar to hormonal therapy or minor injury), believing recurrence risk returns to baseline after 3 to 6 months if the trigger is removed. Others contend that air travel-associated VTE may unmask underlying thrombophilia or other risk factors, resulting in persistent risk, especially if travel continues. 10 
From a decision-analytic perspective, the key is to assess whether the risk of VTE recurrence falls below the treatment threshold T rx , the point at which AC can be discontinued. Unfortunately, high-quality evidence of the accurate assessment of VTE recurrence after long-haul air travel is lacking. A case-control study indicates that VTE risk returns to baseline within ∼12 weeks after air travel. 46 This supports classifying travel-related VTE as “provoked,” for which 3 months of AC may suffice. However, the low-quality (certainty) evidence may underestimate the true risk by 4 to 5 times. 47 The incidence of air travel-related VTE is low (1 in 4600-6000 flights), 48 , 49 suggesting that travel alone rarely causes VTE and likely interacts with other risk factors. 
When high-quality data are lacking, problems like this are typically modeled using an exponential decay equation 13 , 50 to represent how recurrence risk declines after the trigger is removed: 
VTErisk_(after_travel) = 100 × VTErisk_year1 × exp ˆ (−k × (years − 1)) (Equation 4) 
in which VTErisk_year1 is the first-year recurrence risk (estimated at 7.5%, ∼25% lower than unprovoked VTE). 3 , 11 , 12 For our patient (occasional traveler, 1-2 flights per year), we set the decay constant (k = 0.10-0.15). For frequent travelers (4+ flights per year), k was set between 0.05 and 0.1; monthly flights, k ranged from 0.01 to 0.03; and for those with no future flights, k ranged from 0.15 to 0.20. The higher the value of k, the faster the risk drops to baseline. Although the approach is consistent with evidence-based practices, 13 , 50 patients should, of course, be informed that the estimates are based on low-quality evidence. 
Figure 2 shows our analysis. Patient V&Ps remain central to these decisions. Because our patient is an occasional traveler, deemed at low bleeding risk with undecided values about avoiding VTE vs bleeding outcomes (RV = 1), we recommended continuing apixaban for a total of ∼3 to 4 years, with annual reassessment ( Figure 2 A). If our patient had a high bleeding risk, we would recommend a duration of anticoagulation of ∼1.5 to 2 years ( Figure 2 B). Supplemental Figures 3 and 4 show the analyses for RV of 0.5 and 2, respectively.

Case 3: a 56-year-old woman with cirrhotic PVT 
A 56-year-old woman with a 4-year history of primary biliary cirrhosis developed extensive portal vein thrombosis (PVT), confirmed by computed tomography. Her platelet count was 50 × 10 3 /μL. She had been on apixaban for the past 3 months, but 2 months before the PVT diagnosis, she experienced severe variceal bleeding requiring banding. The patient was referred for a recommendation regarding the duration of anticoagulation. She was uncertain whether she values the consequences of bleeding more than those of VTE (RV = 1). 
This case is complicated by a persistently elevated risk of VTE (due to cirrhosis) and a high risk of bleeding (from portal hypertension and recent major bleeding). Long-term data on recurrence in this setting are limited. A pooled estimate of the natural history recurrence rate for PVT suggests a risk of ∼24% (95% CI, 14.7-33.4) at a median follow-up of 6 to 48 months, 51 which is comparable to the recurrence rates reported by Khan et al 9 for unprovoked VTE. 
The annual risk of major bleeding is estimated at 1.5% to 2.7%, rising to 3% to 4% in advanced liver disease or when platelet counts fall below 50 × 10 3 /μL. 52 , 53 For decision modeling to inform our patient care, we used a baseline bleeding risk of 4% and a RR increase of 3.5. 
Figure 3 shows that only patients with RV ≤0.5 (ie, those who value avoiding VTE at least twice as much as bleeding) meet the threshold for long-term AC. For all others, long-term AC is not justified. In this patient, we recommended reduced dose of apixaban for 12 months, with monitoring for recanalization (see supplemental Figure 5 for details).

The duration of treatment of PVT reported in the literature varies from 5 to 13 months. 54 Guidelines vary, recommending 6 months to indefinite therapy. 55 Most recommend re-imaging at 3, 6, and 12 months. 55 We also tend to obtain ultrasound imaging to monitor the rate of recanalization. However, although anticoagulants may more than double the rate of regression noted in the natural history studies, 51 , 55 this does not affect thresholds as significantly as consideration of bleeding rates, and the patient’s V&P (as reflected in Figure 3 ; supplemental Figure 5 ). Discussion 
The cornerstone of expertise is judgmental competence, 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 the ability to integrate data, evidence, and patient context into sound clinical inferences and decisions. Historically, this integration has been largely intuitive and opaque. The evidence-based medicine (EBM) movement emerged to make clinical reasoning more transparent, yet EBM is still criticized for functioning as a “black box,” an operation with well-defined inputs and outputs, but unclear integration of key elements such as patient preferences and benefit-harm trade-offs. 11 , 60 The only approach to address these “black box” and “integration” problems is by embedding EBM within a transparent decision-analytical framework. 11 , 17 , 20 
In this article, we illustrate how this framework can be applied to VTE management. The threshold equation we present simply codifies the clinical process by which physicians integrate evidence and patients’ V&P into treatment recommendations. For optimal decision-making, the model should be populated with the best available evidence for each parameter; if such evidence is lacking, clinicians may still use our threshold model based on their best estimates, with the reasoning and thresholds made explicit to patients and readers regardless of the sources of evidence. 
From an EBM point of view, the problem of VTE management includes additional issues not usually encountered when managing other medical problems, recommendations about the optimal duration of anticoagulation. These recommendations often involve projections beyond available data, but practice guidelines 17 have repeatedly made recommendations for “indefinite anticoagulation” without attempting to explicitly explain the methods for extrapolating their recommendations into the future. Presumably, guideline panels make these projections in their heads, but, as argued, such assessments remain a “black box” to outside observers. The guideline panels do recommend that these decisions be reassessed annually. We agree with this recommendation. Nevertheless, physicians should be aware that the risk at the start of each year is different than at time 1 ( Figure 1. , Figure 2. , Figure 3. ). Although the graphs created in this article are a simple extension of the threshold model (based on Equations 1-4), a busy clinician likely will not take the time to manually calculate VTE and bleeding risk 5 or 10 years into the future. For this reason, we have developed user-friendly applications for easy use of our methods at the bedside. Alternatively, clinicians can refer to our graphs if their patients resemble those described in the vignettes. Although our model is broadly applicable to clinical problems resembling those presented here, it naturally requires different data inputs for each specific context. 
It should be emphasized that the model is only a starting point. Clinical intuition and experience remain invaluable. The best decisions achieve coherence between formal, explicit models and intuitive judgment. 13 , 34 Discrepancies between model output and clinical intuition should be explored. The explicit nature of the model makes it easy to detect any mismatch between the formal model and clinical judgment, which often reflects limitations in the available data. Digital tools and artificial intelligence applications can help clinicians vary their assumptions until they are satisfied with their recommendations. 
It is virtually impossible to empirically verify which approach, decision-analytical vs intuitive, is superior. However, structured decision models are likely more accurate than intuition alone, as they reduce cognitive biases, enhance consistency, and transparently integrate both empirical evidence and patient preferences. Intuition, nevertheless, remains essential for applying clinical nuance, managing exceptions, and recognizing the limitations of available data. The optimal strategy, therefore, combines explicit decision-analytic frameworks with clinical intuition, using structured models as the primary tool and intuition as a vital complementary check. 
Conclusion 
We believe the approach illustrated here has improved our decision-making and, in turn, our patients’ care. The same framework may also benefit the wider hematology community. Supplementing clinicians’ judgment with the decision-making principles outlined in this article has the potential to improve care in VTE management and other hematological and clinical conditions.
