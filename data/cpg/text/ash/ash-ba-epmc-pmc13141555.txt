In this issue of Blood Advances , Stephanou et al 1 provide a timely snapshot of current screening and diagnostic practices for hemoglobinopathies, highlighting both progress and persistent gaps in equity. Their study, conducted through Hemoglobinopathies in European Liaison of Medicine and Science (HELIOS) Action, draws on a large international collaborative network spanning 36 countries across Europe, Africa, Asia, North America, and Australia. 2 
Using a cross-sectional online survey distributed in 2024 to HELIOS members with expertise in laboratory diagnosis and molecular research, the authors examined hematologic screening, molecular diagnostics, and laboratory practices and policies. Responses from 43 participants (75%) representing 38 centers in 20 countries, predominantly from Europe and Central Asia, form the basis of the analysis. Although the data set is necessarily selective, the survey offers valuable insight into real-world diagnostic capacity across diverse health systems. 
One of the most striking findings is the degree of uniformity among responding centers. Most are dedicated hemoglobinopathy laboratories, many serving as national reference or expert facilities, and the majority are publicly funded, with reimbursement through public and private health insurance. Although formal laboratory accreditation was not universal, adherence to national, international, or guideline-derived protocols was common, and nearly all centers reported systems for case reporting and tracking. Together, these findings suggest a solid foundational infrastructure across participating laboratories. 
As with any survey-based study, interpretation of the findings is shaped by who was invited and who responded. Several HELIOS Action member countries with well-established hemoglobinopathy programs were not represented, most notably the United Kingdom, Belgium, and Italy. Their absence may skew perceptions of diagnostic capacity and technical sophistication because these countries have long-standing experience with newborn and carrier screening initiatives. Italy, in particular, has been at the forefront of implementing next-generation sequencing (NGS)–based molecular testing for hemoglobinopathy diagnosis. 3 Nevertheless, the survey identifies meaningful patterns that remain highly relevant to ongoing discussions about diagnostic standardization and equity. 
Hematologic screening practices were largely consistent across centers. All relied on a core framework of complete blood counts and hemoglobin (Hb) analysis, most commonly using high-performance liquid chromatography and/or capillary electrophoresis. Peripheral blood smear review and iron studies were frequently incorporated, whereas more specialized assays, such as Hb H inclusion testing or sickle solubility, were available in a minority of settings. These approaches remain the backbone of hemoglobinopathy screening worldwide and continue to provide cost-effective, scalable presumptive diagnoses. 
However, definitive diagnosis depends on molecular testing of the globin genes. Molecular diagnostics for patient and carrier genotyping as well as prenatal diagnosis were widely available in well-resourced countries and present, although less consistently, in lower-middle-income settings. Given the relatively small size of the globin genes and the extensive catalog of known pathogenic variants, many centers appropriately rely on targeted, affordable assays such as allele-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for common hemoglobin variants, Sanger sequencing for β-thalassemia alleles, and gap PCR or multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification for deletional α-thalassemia to confirm hematologic findings. 
Against this backdrop, the relatively slow uptake of NGS-based diagnostics stands out. Only 10 countries reported the use of NGS platforms, primarily in high-income settings. Even where NGS was available, its role in diagnostic algorithms, whether as first-line testing or as a second-tier approach, was often unclear. This lag contrasts with other areas of genetic medicine and may reflect lingering concerns regarding cost, infrastructure, data interpretation, and workforce expertise. 4 
Yet the case for broader adoption of NGS in hemoglobinopathy diagnostics is increasingly compelling. Targeted NGS platforms capable of detecting both sequence and copy number variants across the α- and β-globin gene clusters are well suited for high-throughput workflows and standardized reporting. Integration with curated variant databases and bioinformatics pipelines offers the potential to harmonize interpretation across laboratories. As experience accumulates, shared implementation lessons may help lower barriers and accelerate the transition away from fragmented, assay-by-assay molecular testing. 3 , 4 , 5 
Laboratory practices surrounding prevention further underscore global inequities. Newborn screening (NBS), particularly for sickle cell disease (SCD), is one of the most effective interventions for reducing early childhood morbidity and mortality; however, only 6 of the 20 responding countries reported established NBS programs for hemoglobinopathies. In addition, Nigeria has a pilot NBS initiative through the Consortium on NBS in Africa, representing an important step forward in a region with a high burden of SCD. 6 , 7 Carrier screening programs, which have dramatically reduced affected births in several countries, were similarly concentrated in high-income settings. 
These disparities should not be surprising. Screening and diagnostic priorities are necessarily shaped by disease prevalence, health care infrastructure, available resources, and societal values. In sub-Saharan Africa, where SCD is highly prevalent and clinically significant thalassemia syndromes are relatively uncommon, NBS for SCD offers immediate life-saving benefit, whereas carrier screening and prenatal diagnosis primarily influence disease incidence. In such settings, focused molecular strategies targeting Hb S, Hb C, and prevalent β-thalassemia mutations may be both appropriate and pragmatic. By contrast, ethnically diverse populations with heterogeneous disease spectra require more comprehensive diagnostic approaches. 
Therefore, Stephanou et al are to be commended for advancing a nuanced discussion of diagnostic equity that resists one-size-fits-all solutions. Their work aligns closely with the broader goals of HELIOS Working Group 1, which seeks to harmonize diagnostic standards, improve variant interpretation, and identify actionable molecular targets. 2 Together with complementary HELIOS efforts addressing care pathways, access to therapies, workforce development, and stakeholder engagement, this study highlights both how far the field has come and how much coordinated effort will be required to ensure equitable prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of hemoglobinopathies worldwide.
