Key Points 
• 
TTR V122I coinheritance in patients with SCD is associated with earlier death and worse cardiac function than those without the variant. 
• 
Screening for TTR V122I in SCD is important, helps guide high-risk therapies, and supports comprehensive patient care.

Abstract 
The amyloidogenic V122I variant of the transthyretin ( TTR ) gene is found in ∼3% of African American individuals and increases cardiovascular mortality risk after the age of 65 years. Sickle cell disease (SCD) primarily affects individuals of African descent, leading to multiorgan damage and premature mortality, with cardiopulmonary issues being a major cause of death. We assessed the impact of TTR V122I on cardiac phenotype and survival in a study of 584 patients with SCD (mean age, 35.9 years; 50.7% women). The prevalence was 3.1% (18/584), mainly female (72.2%). Age, blood pressure, body mass index, and liver/renal markers were similar between carriers and noncarriers, except for higher blood urea nitrogen levels in carriers. Echocardiography showed that carriers had increased septal thickness and left ventricular mass index and lower diastolic function indices. Over a median follow-up of 6.5 years, 219 patients died. The coinheritance of TTR V122I with SCD was associated with increased mortality (hazard ratio, 2.82; 95% confidence interval, 1.57-5.06). At 5 years, the cumulative incidence of death was 52.9% among carriers compared with 14.5% among noncarriers, corresponding to an approximate relative risk of 3.6. TTR protein levels were significantly lower in carriers. In conclusion, TTR V122I prevalence in patients with SCD mirrors that of the general African American population but affects cardiovascular function much earlier, and a contributing factor may be the underlying oxidative stress and chronic anemia. Genetic screening for TTR V122I is important and should be considered for patients with SCD. This trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as NCT00011648 . 
Introduction 
Transthyretin (TTR) is a homotetrameric transport protein predominantly synthesized in the liver and is responsible for carrying thyroxine and retinol-binding protein–bound vitamin A. 1 Destabilization of the TTR tetramer, leading to dissociation and misfolding of monomers, can lead to the aggregation of amyloid fibril deposits, causing TTR amyloidosis (ATTR), which notably affecting the heart and peripheral nerves. 2 , 3 , 4 Among the numerous pathogenic variants, the valine-to-isoleucine substitution at position 122 (V122I), also annotated as V142I (rs76992529), is the most common and has emerged as one of the most clinically relevant. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 
TTR V122I is prevalent among individuals of African descent with a frequency of ∼3.4% compared to 0.3% in the general population. 9 
TTR V122I is recognized as a major genetic risk factor for hereditary TTR amyloid cardiomyopathy, 10 , 11 , 12 a condition marked by diastolic dysfunction, arrhythmias, and eventual cardiac failure. 11 , 13 Several population-based studies further highlight the variant’s role in accelerating age-dependent cardiac dysfunction and elevating the risk of heart failure and death. 5 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 14 
Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited blood disorder that predominantly affects individuals of African descent. In the United States, SCD is considered “rare,” but nonetheless affects over 100 000 Americans primarily of African descent and 1 in 365 African American births ( https://www.cdc.gov/sickle-cell/data/index.html ). 15 The disorder is caused by an abnormal hemoglobin variant, hemoglobin S (HbS; βGlu6Val), as a consequence of a single-nucleotide substitution in the β-globin gene. Under hypoxic conditions, HbS polymerizes within the red blood cells to alter their function and structure (“sickling”), which underly the 2 key features of SCD: recurrent acute vaso-occlusive episodes and chronic hemolytic anemia. 16 , 17 Improved childhood survival has shifted SCD toward a chronic condition in adults, with ongoing inflammation and vasculopathic processes resulting in progressive multiorgan damage and a reduction in life expectancy by 20 to 30 years compared to the general population. 18 , 19 Among the emerging complications, cardiopulmonary manifestations, including restrictive cardiomyopathy, diastolic dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, and sudden cardiac death, are key contributors to morbidity and the leading cause of death in the SCD population. 20 , 21 
A common recurring theme in SCD populations is the extreme variability in disease severity. 17 Both acquired and inherited factors contribute to this clinical complexity of SCD. 22 Given the high prevalence of the TTR V122I variant in individuals of African descent, it is expected to be similarly prevalent among patients with SCD. We performed a candidate gene association study using a well-characterized cohort of patients with SCD to evaluate the impact of TTR V122I on cardiac phenotypes and all-cause mortality in individuals with SCD, with the aim of informing genetic screening and risk assessment strategies for this high-risk group. Plasma profiling was performed in a subset of the cohort to evaluate the correlation of TTR levels with the TTR V122I variant. 
Methods 
Study population and outcomes 
For this study, we leveraged data from a prospective observational cohort of 584 adult (ageed ≥18 years, all SCD genotypes) patients with SCD, enrolled at the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute under protocol NCT00011648 between September 2006 and February 2015. The protocol was approved by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. The primary objective of the protocol was to assess the prevalence of secondary pulmonary hypertension in SCD compared with that in controls. Although the cohort was not selected for disease severity, it likely includes more severe cases due to the tertiary referral setting. The cohort had previously been used to develop a disease severity risk score for the prediction of mortality. 23 Of the initial cohort of 598 patients, 14 individuals were excluded (13 lacked biological samples for genotyping and 1 individual did not have confirmed SCD status; Figure 1 ). All patients were in steady state at enrollment and underwent a baseline echocardiogram (echo), which was centrally interpreted by readers blinded to SCD and other genotypes. Standard laboratory tests and hemoglobin electrophoresis were performed on the same date or within 1 week of the echo.

National Institutes of Health (NIH) disease severity risk scores were compared between TTR V122I carriers and noncarriers. The score was based on 9 independent risk factors reflecting the summation of cardiopulmonary, renal, and liver damage. 23 All available cardiac phenotypes from the echos were analyzed for cardiac dysfunction. The date of the initial echo and the date of death or last available contact was used for all-cause mortality. The survival status was ascertained by online obituary search, patient’s family reports, National Death Index, and Social Security Death Index searches with a final follow-up in June 2024. 
Genotyping of TTR V122I and measurement of TTR levels 
TTR V122I (rs76992529) carrier status was derived from the analysis of whole-genome sequence data in 505 of the 584 patient, and polymerase chain reaction direct sequencing for 79 patients ( supplemental Methods - Target detection of the TTR V122I genetic variant using PCR and Sanger sequencing). 
Plasma profiling was performed in 336 of the 584 patients using the Olink Explore HT platform, which uses an antibody-based proximity extension assay. 24 Plasma samples, separated from freshly collected blood in EDTA tubes and stored at −80°C, were thawed, plated, and shipped on dry ice to Psomagen for analysis. The platform measures 5400 unique proteins across multiple panels, with extensive quality control steps and normalization. TTR levels were quantified using Normalized Protein eXpression values, Olink relative protein measurement unit on a log 2 scale. 
Statistical analysis 
Baseline characteristics were compared between TTR V122I carriers and noncarriers in the full genetic cohort (N = 584). In the subset with available plasma TTR protein measurements (n = 336), baseline characteristics were compared between patients with high and low TTR protein levels. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD) or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Group comparisons were performed using the Student t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann-Whitney U ) tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 tests or Fisher exact tests. Normality of the variable distributions was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk tests. 
Phenotypic risk scores and echocardiographic parameter comparisons were stratified by carrier status in the full cohort (N = 584). Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier methods and compared using the log-rank tests for carrier vs noncarrier with mortality rates and median survival times summarized descriptively by group. Kaplan-Meier estimates at prespecified time points were used to report cumulative mortality, with 5-year absolute risks presented due to sparse numbers at risk at later follow-up times. 25 The Cox proportional hazards (PH) models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for time-to-mortality outcomes. The PH assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding early deaths (≤1 year after baseline) to assess the robustness of the mortality findings. In the full cohort (N = 584), multivariable Cox models were fitted using 2 predefined adjustment strategies: (1) adjustment for blood urea nitrogen (BUN; log-transformed) level and (2) adjustment for BUN (log-transformed) level and age. Cox models restricted to the TTR protein subset (n = 336) included (1) an unadjusted model evaluating the association between circulating TTR protein levels and mortality and (2) a joint model including both TTR protein levels and TTR V122I carrier status. Mortality incidence rates were calculated as deaths per 100 person-years, and incidence rate ratios were estimated using Poisson regression with follow-up time included as an offset. Analyses were additionally stratified by sex to assess sex-specific genotype and phenotype differences and to compare echocardiographic parameters and all-cause mortality. All hypothesis tests were 2-sided with a significance level of .05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 
A classification and regression tree–derived cutoff (−0.89) was applied to plasma TTR protein levels to classify the 336 patients into low (TTR at or below −0.89) or high (TTR greater than −0.89) TTR groups. This cutoff was selected because it showed the largest survival difference between the groups ( supplemental Figure 3 ). Echocardiographic parameters and Kaplan-Meier survival probabilities were compared between individuals (n = 336), stratified by high and low TTR protein levels. 
Results 
Baseline characteristics 
Of the 584 adult participants (mean [SD] age, 35.9 [12.9] years; 50.7% female), 18 were heterozygous for the TTR V122I variant (minor allele frequency, 1.54%; carrier frequency, 3.1%; Table 1 ). Unlike the distribution observed in the general population, carriers in this SCD cohort were predominantly female (72.2% [13/18]) compared to 27.8% (5/18) in males. Carriers and noncarriers have similar ages (mean ± SD, 42.2 ± 17.2 vs 35.7 ± 12.7 years; P = .129), baseline demographics, and serologic markers, with the exception of higher BUN levels in carriers (BUN, 2.6 ± 0.7 vs 2.2 ± 0.7 mg/dL; P = .032). Among the 336 patients (49.4% females) with analyzed plasma profiles, 9 individuals (6 females), were carriers of TTR V122I, and the remaining 327 were noncarriers ( supplemental Table 1 ). The median plasma TTR levels were significantly lower in TTR V122I carriers than in noncarriers (median, −0.553 [IQR, −0.679 to −0.417] vs 0.006 [IQR, −0.263 to 0.285]; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P = .0001; Figure 2 ; supplemental Table 1 ).

Association of TTR V122I carrier status with cardiac function 
To further investigate the impact of TTR V122I on cardiac function, a detailed comparison of echo parameters was conducted ( Table 2 ). Carriers showed increased cardiac dysfunction, with higher interventricular septum (IVS) diastolic thickness (mean ± SD, 10.7 ± 1.4 vs 9.7 ± 1.7 mm; P = .004), higher left ventricular (LV) mass index (110.7 ± 25.3 vs 99.1 ± 31.0 g/m 2 ; P = .029), and higher LV posterior wall diastolic thickness (10.3 ± 1.9 vs 9.6 ± 1.7 mm; P = .048). Diastolic dysfunction was also more evident, with lower mitral E:A ratio (ratio of early (E) to late (A) ventricular filling velocities) (1.3 ± 0.5 vs 1.7 ± 0.6; P = .020) and lower septal e′ velocity (8.8 ± 2.0 vs 10.3 ± 2.9 cm/s; P = .019).

Association of TTR V122I carrier status with all-cause mortality 
TTR V122I carriers had a significantly greater risk of all-cause mortality than noncarriers (HR, 2.82; 95% CI, 1.57-5.06; P < .001) and a shorter survival time (median, 4.43 years [95% CI, 4.43 to not estimable] vs 11.96 years [95% CI, 11.36-13.5]; Tables 3 and 4 ; Figure 3 ). Over a median follow-up of 6.5 years, 37.5% (219/584) participants died, with a higher mortality rate among carriers than among noncarriers (66.7% [12/18] vs 36.6% [207/566], respectively; Table 4 ). At 5 years, cumulative mortality was 52.9% among TTR V122I carriers compared with that of 14.5% among noncarriers, corresponding to an approximate relative risk of 3.6.

The Cox PH model was further applied to assess the risk of variant while controlling for potential risk factors. The significantly increased hazard of having the variant was sustained after controlling for BUN level (HR, 2.43; 95% CI, 1.35-4.37; P = .003) and controlling for both BUN level and age (HR, 2.12; 95% CI, 1.17-3.84; P = .013; Table 3 ). In these multivariable models 2 and 3, BUN level (HR, 1.87 and 1.60, respectively) and, in model 3, age (HR, 1.03) at baseline were also significant predictors of mortality. For patients with SCD with the same BUN level and age at baseline, those with TTR V122I had more than a twofold risk of mortality compared with that of those without the variant ( Table 3 ). No violations of the PH assumption were observed. Results were consistent in sensitivity analyses excluding early deaths (≤1 year after baseline), although attenuated after adjustment for renal function and age ( supplemental Table 8 ). Mortality incidence rates were 12.6 deaths per 100 person-years among TTR V122I carriers and 5.08 deaths per 100 person-years among noncarriers, corresponding to an incidence rate ratio of 2.48 (95% CI, 1.31-4.24). These findings complement the Cox PH analyses. 
Association of TTR V122I carrier status with the NIH phenotypic risk score 
An NIH disease severity risk score composed of 9 risk factors had previously been developed to predict mortality risks in SCD, which was studied using the same patient cohort. 23 
TTR V122I carriers had a significantly higher NIH severity risk score (mean ± SD, 2.7 ± 1.9 vs 1.8 ± 3.0; P = .002), indicating increased mortality risk ( Table 4 ). Besides BUN and alkaline phosphatase levels, the difference was contributed mainly by cardiac parameters, including higher IVS diastolic thickness (10.7 ± 1.4 vs 9.7 ± 1.7 mm; P = .004), tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity (2.8 ± 0.5 vs 2.6 ± 0.5 mm; P = .088), and IVS diastolic thickness (10.7 ± 1.4 vs 9.7 ± 1.7 mm; P = .004; Tables 1 and 2 ). 
Sex difference of TTR V122I carrier risk 
The TTR V122I variant was more prevalent in females, with a rate of 4.39% (13/296) compared to 1.74% (5/288) in males, although this difference was not statistically significant ( P = .106; Table 1 ). The mortality rates were similar between females and males (37.8% vs 37.2%, respectively; Table 5 ). However, within the female group, carriers had a significantly higher risk of mortality than noncarriers (HR, 3.48; 95% CI, 1.81-6.68; P < .001; supplemental Table 2 ; supplemental Figure 1 ). The number of male carriers was insufficient to compare mortality risks. Among female carriers, a larger left atrial volume was observed in carriers than in noncarriers (76.4 [SD, 20.4] mL vs 64.5 [SD, 23.0] mL; P = .033), but there was no difference in LV end-diastolic or end-systolic diameters (data not shown; supplemental Table 3 ). Additional echocardiographic differences included a higher estimated right ventricular systolic pressure (40.5 [SD 17.0] vs 32.7 [SD 12.1] mm Hg), although the latter difference did not reach significance ( supplemental Table 3 ).

TTR levels: baseline characteristics, association with TTR V122I, echocardiographic characteristics, and mortality 
Among the 336 participants with Olink protein profiles, the median age was 34 years (IQR, 26-45), and 164 (48.8%) were female ( supplemental Table 4 ). TTR levels were significantly lower in TTR V122I carriers (n = 9) than in noncarriers (median, −0.553 [IQR, −0.679 to −0.417] vs 0.006 [IQR, −0.263 to 0.285]; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P = .0001; Figure 2 ). The distribution of plasma TTR protein levels was shifted toward lower values in females compared with those in males ( supplemental Figure 2 ). Based on the first split point from the classification and regression tree survival analysis, using survival time and death status, participants were categorized as having low TTR (at or below −0.89) or high TTR (greater than −0.89) levels ( supplemental Figure 3 ). Using the cutoff value of −0.89, 15 patients were classified as low TTR and 321 as high TTR. The TTR V122I variant was more frequent in the low TTR group than in the high TTR group (2/15 [13.3%] vs 7/321 [2.2%]; P = .056; supplemental Table 4 ). Participants with low TTR level had significantly higher LV mass ( P = .027) and higher LV mass index ( P = .016) than participants with high TTR level ( supplemental Table 5 ). During the study period, there were 124 deaths (36.9%) among the 336 participants. Mortality was significantly higher in the low TTR group (8/15 [53.3%]) than in the high TTR group (116/321 [36.1%]), and this difference was significant ( P < .001; supplemental Table 6 ). Patients in the low TTR group have 3.63 times higher risk of death than those in the high TTR group (HR, 3.63; 95% CI, 1.75-7.52; P < .001) and a shorter median survival time than those in the high TTR (median, 6.53 years [95% CI, 2.83 to not estimable] vs 13.5 years [95% CI, 11.76-14.9]; supplemental Tables 6 and 7 ; supplemental Figure 4 ). In the joint Cox PH model including TTR V122I carrier status and TTR protein levels, low circulating TTR levels remained significantly associated with increased mortality (HR, 2.87; 95% CI, 1.20-6.91), whereas the association between TTR V122I carrier status and mortality was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 0.62-5.49; supplemental Table 7 ). 
Discussion 
To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the prevalence and clinical implications of the pathogenic TTR V122I variant within a SCD population. TTR V122I was identified in 3.1% of the 584 patients with SCD. Individuals in the general population who carry the TTR V122I variant have an increased risk of heart failure and mortality. 7 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 26 , 27 Individuals with SCD already face a higher risk of early mortality and cardiac complications. 20 , 28 , 29 Our findings indicate that the presence TTR V122I further elevates this risk. 
Notably, TTR V122I carriers experienced significantly worse survival outcomes, with an HR for all-cause mortality nearly threefold higher than that of noncarriers, and median survival was substantially shorter. At 5 years, the cumulative incidence of death was 52.9% among carriers compared with 14.5% among noncarriers, corresponding to an approximate relative risk of 3.6. Specifically, mortality rates were 12.6 deaths per 100 person-years among TTR V122I carriers and 5.08 deaths per 100 person-years among noncarriers, yielding an incidence rate ratio of 2.48 (95% CI, 1.31-4.24). These results highlight the potential prognostic implications of TTR genotyping in the SCD population. Our study found a higher prevalence of the TTR V122I variant in females than in males, although current evidence showed no significant sex difference in prevalence. Notably, female carriers exhibited higher mortality risk and greater hemodynamic stress, as indicated by increased tricuspid regurgitation velocity, suggesting possible sex-based vulnerability in ATTR-related cardiac pathology. However, these findings are exploratory and based on a limited sample size. TTR V122I coinheritance was also associated with significantly elevated NIH severity risk scores, suggesting an increased multisystem disease burden including higher BUN levels. Echocardiographic and laboratory findings further indicated subclinical cardiac involvement, increased septal thickness, elevated LV mass index, and impaired diastolic function. 
This study is also concordant with previous literature, 3 showing that TTR V122I carriers have lower TTR levels than noncarriers. The attenuation of TTR V122I carrier status and mortality association in the joint model suggest that TTR levels reflect underlying disease severity rather than mediating the effect of genotype on mortality risk. 
Although the prevalence of the variant in patients with SCD is similar to that in the general African American population 9 and individuals of African descent in the UK Biobank, 6 it appears to affect cardiovascular function at a much younger age (mean, ∼36 years) compared with after the age of 65 years in African American individuals. 5 We propose that the high oxidative stress environment, chronic anemia, inflammatory processes, and widespread systemic vasculopathy in SCD contribute to the earlier clinical penetrance of the TTR V122I phenotype. Another example of the sickle environment accelerating health decline is the coinheritance of APOL1 risk variants, which is associated with renal function deterioration and progression to end-stage renal failure. 30 , 31 The prevalence of the high-risk APOL1 genotype in patients with SCD is comparable to that in self-identified African Americans, yet end-stage renal disease appears much earlier in patients with SCD. 32 , 33 , 34 
APOL1 risk variants (G1 and G2) are primarily associated with an increased risk of nondiabetic kidney diseases, especially among individuals of African ancestry. The presence of 2 risk alleles greatly increases disease risk but penetrance is incomplete, and most carriers do not develop kidney disease. 30 , 35 No APOL1-specific therapies currently exist; management focuses on monitoring and addressing modifiable risk factors. In contrast, pathogenic TTR variants, such as V122I, typically exhibit higher penetrance and are primarily linked to hereditary TTR amyloidosis, which can manifest as cardiomyopathy and/or neuropathy. Both APOL1 and TTR variants have important clinical implications, but TTR mutations have a broader impact on therapeutic decision-making due to the availability of targeted treatments and typically higher penetrance. 8 , 36 
APOL1 testing is more focused on risk stratification, preventive care, and donor selection, and although highly relevant in nephrology, currently lacks targeted therapies. 
This study has several limitations. Although our cohort is sufficiently large with adequate follow-up and baseline echocardiography, the number of individuals with the TTR V122I variant is relatively small; therefore, causality is not supported and analysis in male patients is limited. The sample size also restricted correlation studies of TTR protein levels with survival, thereby we can only suggest association rather than causality. SCD genotype is an established determinant of disease severity and mortality; however, the small number of TTR V122I carriers within individual genotype strata precluded reliable genotype-stratified survival analyses in this cohort ( supplemental Table 9 ). In addition, our findings are based on a single-center cohort and have not been validated in another SCD population. 
Overall, our study contributes to the growing body of evidence that genetic modifiers such as TTR V122I may contribute to the phenotypic heterogeneity and adverse clinical outcomes in individuals with SCD. This study draws attention to the considerable number of patients with SCD at risk of developing ATTR, adding to the burden of sickle-related cardiovascular poor outcomes. We suggest that genetic testing for TTR V122I or the measurement of TTR protein levels should be considered to help assess individual risk and inform clinical management. Identifying TTR V122I carriers among patients with SCD could prompt closer monitoring for the early presentation of cardiac dysfunction, which may otherwise be overlooked as SCD-related cardiomyopathy. Early detection can guide more tailored surveillance strategies, such as periodic imaging or biomarker assessments, potentially enabling earlier intervention if cardiac involvement develops. In terms of relevance to existing TTR-targeted therapies, the availability of disease-modifying treatments for TTR amyloidosis, such as tafamidis, underscores the value of identifying at-risk individuals. Although data on the efficacy of these therapies specifically in patients with SCD with TTR mutations are limited, knowledge of TTR V122I status could inform future therapeutic decisions as evidence emerges. Screening does carry the potential for identifying TTR V122I carriers who may never develop clinically significant amyloidosis, which could lead to anxiety, unnecessary testing, or overtreatment. It is important to balance the benefits of early detection with the risk of unintended consequences and to ensure that screening is accompanied by clear counseling and management guidelines. 
Future prospective studies should further explore the mechanistic pathways linking TTR protein levels with cardiac dysfunction in SCD and assess whether targeted interventions may mitigate risk of cardiac dysfunction in genetically susceptible individuals.
