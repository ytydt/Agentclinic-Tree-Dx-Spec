Abstract 
Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a complex condition that results in low oxygen affinity and oxidative damage within red blood cells (RBCs), which contributes to serious complications such as vaso-occlusive episodes (VOEs). Given the well-established theoretical and experimental evidence that oxygenated hemoglobin is diamagnetic, whereas deoxygenated hemoglobin and methemoglobin are paramagnetic, this study aims to investigate whether RBCs from patients with SCD exhibit progressively increased magnetic susceptibility in correlation with disease severity. In this study, we employed cell tracking velocimetry (CTV) to perform single-cell analysis of RBC magnetic properties under both oxygenated and deoxygenated states. CTV quantifies the oxygen saturation of each cell by measuring their magnetically induced cell velocities in conjunction with known cell properties. Subsequent statistical analysis revealed that although the mean oxygen saturation of the RBCs from patients with SCD did not differ significantly from that of healthy donors, the magnetic velocities at the 1st and 99th percentile of RBC samples from patients with SCD were significantly reduced and elevated, respectively. These elevated and reduced velocities correlate directly to the magnetic susceptibility of the cell, which corresponds to the hemoglobin state. Moreover, we identified a correlation ( P < .01) between magnetic heterogeneity in the top and bottom 1% and pain severity, particularly in patients with SCD experiencing acute VOEs. This study underscores the potential of RBC magnetic properties as biophysical markers for identifying the pain severity in patients with SCD and opening avenues for personalized therapeutic interventions. 
Introduction 
Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited disorder of red blood cells (RBCs) that affects up to 100 000 people in the United States. 1 First identified as a molecular disease by Pauling et al in 1949, SCD is characterized by the presence of sickle hemoglobin (HbS) in RBCs. 2 HbS results from a single amino acid mutation in the β-globin chain of the Hb molecule, specifically the substitution of valine for glutamic acid. Hb is a protein composed of various combinations of 4 globin subunits, each of which contains a prosthetic heme group capable of binding an oxygen molecule. 3 Before characterizing SCD as a molecular disease, Pauling et al reported on the magnetic properties of deoxygenated Hb (deoxy state) and the nonmagnetic properties of oxygenated Hb (oxy state). 4 It has also been observed that Hb in the methemoglobin state (met state) is also magnetic, with a magnetic susceptibility similar to that of deoxygenated Hb. Methemoglobin occurs when the iron atom in Hb is oxidized from its ferrous state (Fe 2+ ) to its ferric state (Fe 3+ ), making it unable to release oxygen. Methemoglobin-containing RBCs account for 1% of total RBCs present in a healthy blood sample. 5 , 6 
Typically, patients with SCD have chronic hemolytic anemia and intermittent vaso-occlusive episodes (VOEs), causing severe acute pain that often results in emergency department visits and hospitalizations. 7 VOEs are triggered initially by the polymerization of HbS inside RBCs under deoxygenated conditions, which deforms the shape of RBCs from the well-known biconcave disk to a deformed shape, sometimes referred to as a sickle shape. 7 , 8 This polymerization and shape change is believed to be the primary pathophysiological event in SCD. 7 Deformed RBCs activate platelets and leukocytes, promoting adhesion to capillary walls and resulting in hemolytic anemia, vascular obstruction, and ultimately VOEs. 8 VOEs typically manifest as acute complications, such as ischemic tissue damage, resulting in intense pain or organ dysfunction. 
Further complicating the pathology of SCD, pain in SCD can be complex and multidimensional. Although the physical manifestation of pain is considered to be a single symptom, pain itself can arise from a multitude of etiologies. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 For example, patients with SCD may present with acute vaso-occlusive crises, but many also experience chronic baseline pain, often because of avascular necrosis (AVN) of the joints. AVN is a common complication in SCD, particularly affecting the hips and shoulders, and can lead to persistent pain, impaired mobility, and long-term disability. 14 Pain can manifest as early as at the age of 6 months in patients with severe SCD with dactylitis, where digits become swollen and painful because of microvascular occlusion of the blood vessels. 15 , 16 Patients can develop acute and chronic bone changes with bone infarcts and AVN in multiple joints and H shaped compression fractures of the spine causing chronic back pain. 17 Recurrent pain can modify the nervous system and lead to neuropathic changes in both the spine and the brain. Therefore, the combination of individualized pain experiences and the multifactorial nature of SCD pain complicates treatment. To manage both acute and chronic pain, patients frequently rely on opioid therapy. 18 However, prolonged opioid use may contribute to the development of neuropathic pain and opioid-induced hyperalgesia, complicating the pain management landscape. 19 , 20 Importantly, even after curative treatment with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, some patients continue to experience pain episodes resembling sickle crises, despite the absence of HbS. 21 Studies have shown that persistent pain after transplant may be influenced by pretransplant pain history, age, and psychological factors, and may require ongoing pain management strategies. 21 , 22 Thus, this poses significant challenges for both patients and providers, particularly as patients age and develop complex pain syndromes. 
Various techniques and assays such as complete blood count, 23 peripheral blood smear, 24 Hb electrophoresis, 25 and genetic tests 26 have been used for SCD detection and monitoring. Unfortunately, there is currently no approved test that can be correlated to a VOE. Magnetophoresis is the movement of an entity (in this case a suspended RBC) in response to a magnetic field gradient. Magnetophoresis is a theoretically and experimentally validated technique that can quantify the presence of deoxygenated, oxygenated, and methemoglobin Hb in measured RBCs. 27 This highly accurate quantification is the logical outgrowth of the pioneering work of Pauling et al, mentioned earlier. Over the subsequent decades, this magnetic property of Hb has been applied to develop several approaches to characterize and separate RBCs. 28 , 29 , 30 Cell tracking velocimetry (CTV) is an instrument which quantifies magnetophoresis, and is capable of capturing RBC movement on a single-cell level in a well-characterized magnetic field. 31 From the computer-recorded movement by the CTV, the quantification of the magnetic susceptibility of the cell is determined, which can be subsequently translated into a measure of the oxygen saturation of Hb within the RBC. It can also enable the calculation of the shape or size of the RBC since the magnetically induced velocity is resisted by hydrodynamic drag, which is directly proportional to the diameter of the cell. 32 The CTV instrument has been used to demonstrate that the measured magnetic velocity of oxygenated and deoxygenated RBCs is consistent with theoretical predictions based on the physical properties of RBCs. 33 
In this work, we sought to test the capabilities of our CTV technology to characterize the magnetic properties of RBCs from patients with SCD across various pain categories. Steady state was defined as no acute healthcare use for 4 weeks, as reported by the patients. As mentioned earlier, SCD-related pain is multifactorial, encompassing both acute and chronic manifestations. Chronic pain in patients with SCD is increasingly recognized to involve both nociceptive and neuropathic components. Neuropathic pain arises from maladaptive changes in the somatosensory nervous system, including central and peripheral sensitization, and is often underdiagnosed in this population. 34 , 35 This subgroup may represent a unique pathophysiological phenotype within the broader SCD population. Recent transcriptomic analyses have identified biomarker signatures associated with pain frequency in SCD, suggesting that chronic pain may be driven by a persistent inflammatory microenvironment. Under our consent protocols, patients with SCD self-reported any chronic pain rated on at least 50% of days over the past 3 months. It is important to note that chronic pain medication usage was not looked into for this study. Although chronic pain can persist independently, acute pain is typically associated with VOEs. In our consent protocols, patients with SCD in crisis were defined as patients admitted to the hospital with a primary diagnosis of VOE. Patients experiencing frequent VOEs (defined as >3 crises per year) exhibit distinct hematologic and inflammatory profiles. These include increased expression of adhesion molecules such as P-selectin, contributing to enhanced RBC–endothelial cell interactions, hemolysis, and systemic inflammation. 36 , 37 
Categorizing patients based on the presence or absence of chronic pain and the frequency of VOEs allows for better control of intrinsic biological variability. This stratification is essential for interpreting data and understanding the underlying mechanisms of pain in SCD. We hypothesized that a detectable difference in the magnetic velocities exists between healthy Hb (HbA) containing RBCs of healthy donors and HbS-containing RBCs from patients in a non-VOE state and those experiencing an active VOE. To test our hypothesis, we conducted tests on RBCs from patients with SCD experiencing various levels of pain and used healthy donors as controls. 
Materials and methods 
Patient population and categories of pain 
Whole blood samples were obtained from 10 healthy donors (HDs) and 21 patients with SCD across different pain categories. Clinical characteristics of the SCD donors are provided in supplemental Table 1 . All RBC analyses of patients with SCD were conducted blinded to participants’ pain categories. After all measurements were completed and pain categories were subsequently provided, correlations were evaluated between the analytical results and the reported pain categories. Blood samples from patients with SCD and healthy donors were obtained by direct venous draw. Approximately 5 to 8 mL of blood was collected into a 10 mL vacutainer tube spray-coated with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulant (Becton Dickinson, 366643). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 
Isolation of RBCs from blood samples 
RBCs were isolated from donor blood samples on the same day of collection. Within 3 hours, RBCs were isolated by 3 successive washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), using centrifugation at 1300 g for 5 minutes. Immediately after isolation, RBCs were suspended in PBS to maintain isotonic osmolarity and preserve physiological conditions. The sample cell concentration was then measured using Coulter counter ( B23005 Multisizer 4e Coulter Counter, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) and diluted to an appropriate concentration (10 9 to 10 11 cells/L) for further analysis. Oxygenated RBCs were exposed to room air conditions for 10 minutes to equilibrate under ambient oxygen pressure (21% oxygen). Deoxygenated RBCs were prepared by treating the washed RBCs with 783μM sodium dithionite without mixing or stirring. Sodium dithionite scavenges oxygen in the extracellular medium, lowering the oxygen concentration outside of RBCs. According to Henry’s law, this causes an equilibrium shift resulting in Hb-bound oxygen diffusing out until the RBCs equilibrate in PBS with 0% dissolved oxygen. To ensure that the oxygenation status of the samples was stable, the partial pressures of oxygen for both the oxygenated and deoxygenated RBCs were measured using a blood gas analyzer (RAPIDLab 248, Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Germany). This protocol was applied to RBCs from both healthy donors and patients with SCD. 
CTV 
The magnetic behavior of RBCs was measured using CTV ( Figure 1 ). 31 Oxygenated RBCs, at a concentration of 10 9 RBCs/L in PBS, were injected into the microfluidic channel. This channel was positioned within a constant magnetic field gradient in horizontal direction, created by permanent rare earth magnets. The cells exhibited diagonal movements because of the combined gravitational force acting on them vertically and the magnetic force acting on them horizontally. A microscope and camera system connected to a computer recorded this motion by capturing images over 30 seconds with a 0.5 second interval between images. ImageJ’s TrackMate plugin was used to identify the cells within the images, and our in-house MATLAB code was used to determine the horizontal or magnetic velocities (u m ) and vertical or settling velocities (u s ). The number of oxygenated and deoxygenated RBCs tracked by ImageJ’s TrackMate plugin are provided in supplemental Tables 4 and 5 , respectively.

Statistical analysis 
All statistical analyses were performed using JMP software (version 18, Systat Software, Inc, Chicago, IL). To compare magnetic velocity distributions across categories, we examined the means of the 1st, 5th, 10th, 90th, 95th, and 99th percentile values of each sample from each pain category. A standard 1-way analysis of variance was first applied to assess overall differences among pain categories. To evaluate the assumption of equal variances, Levene’s test was conducted. Finally, Wilcoxon each-pair comparisons (Kruskal-Wallis U test) were employed as a nonparametric approach for comparing the values between the different pain categories. The confidence level of P <.05 was considered as statistically significant, and significantly different groups were annotated with a star. 
Whole blood from patients with SCD and HDs was collected in strict adherence to the informed consent protocols approved by the institutional review board at The Ohio State University (2021H0075 and 2018H0268 respectively). All experimental procedures were conducted based on relevant guidelines and regulations. 
Results 
Representative dot plots of u s vs u m , along with corresponding histograms showing frequency and cumulative distributions of u m and u s ( Figure 2 ), demonstrated that deoxygenated RBCs from a representative healthy donor and a patient with SCD exhibited higher u m values than the oxygenated RBCs, which is consistent with our previous studies. 27 , 33 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 In addition, a fraction of the oxygenated RBCs from patients with SCD showed elevated u m compared with oxygenated RBCs from healthy donors, suggesting reduced oxygen binding in sickle cells. Although dot plots offer valuable insight into individual cell behavior, histograms offer a more straightforward means of comparing distributions between different populations.

Some notable observations are evident in the histograms depicting frequency as a function of u m for oxygenated and deoxygenated RBCs, contrasting a representative category A healthy donor with a category C patient with SCD (Figure 3 A-B), and with a category F patient with SCD ( Figure 3 C-D). First, the distribution of the histograms around the mean frequency and u m values of healthy donor’s RBCs were consistent with previously published data and theoretical models. 27 , 33 , 40 This was observed for all the data tested. In contrast, the visual distribution around the mean frequency for the patient with SCD in VOE, indicated that there are a small number of RBCs that have a significantly higher and lower velocity than the velocity measured for the RBCs from healthy donor. These RBCs are represented by data in the rectangular regions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that we are observing RBCs with such low or high velocity. Both the fast- and slow-moving cells appear in both the oxygenated and deoxygenated populations. Although the standard deviation (SD) of u m for SCD RBCs in category F was higher than those in other pain categories, the observed magnitude was less prominent than implied by the visual spread in the magnetic velocity profiles ( Figure 3 ). Nevertheless, the category F SCD RBCs contained only a minimal subset of very fast- and slow-moving cells, representing ∼1% of all tracked RBCs ( Figure 3 C-D).

The mean confidence diamonds did not overlap, indicating that the group means were statistically different despite the overlapping SDs, which reflect similar variability between the different pain categories. However, differences in the means primarily capture heterogeneity at the level of the bulk populations. To examine distributional differences at the level of individual samples, we additionally used a quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plot for all magnetic velocity measurements across pain categories in both oxygenated cells ( Figure 5 A) and deoxygenated cells ( Figure 5 B). Q-Q plots were generated using an in-house MATLAB script, comparing the percentiles of u m from patients with SCD with those from healthy donors. A reference line (y = x, dashed brown line) was added to highlight how the 2 distributions align or diverge. Velocities were measured on a cell-by-cell basis, yielding >50 000 pooled observations. Incorporating the full data set, rather than focusing solely on the upper tail, allowed for a more clinically meaningful comparison across pain categories and better characterization of patient-level heterogeneity.

As shown in Figure 6 , there were significant differences observed in the population of the 1st and 99th percentile values between the healthy donors or patients with SCD without chronic pain and patients with SCD in crises based on the Wilcoxon each-pair test. There were no or slight significant differences observed for the 5th, 10th, 90th, and 95th percentile values between all the pain categories as shown in supplemental Figures 1 and 2 . These trends were observed for both fully oxygenated and deoxygenated SCD RBCs.

Discussion 
Biomarkers for measuring VOE are noted as a priority for research as defined by the American Society of Hematology SCD Research Priorities. 42 Moreover, a test that helps determine the number of sickled RBCs in real time could significantly affect clinical care. It could define clinical trial end points and potentially act as an early marker of the severity of a clinical VOE episode over time. It also has the potential to affect the stigma and disparities around SCD pain. A test that measures RBC sickling may allow diagnosis and the treatment of chronic pain earlier, assist in identifying at-risk populations that would need treatment intensification earlier, and scientifically prove and validate the patient’s experiences, which would significantly alter the patient-provider relationship in a disease fraught with significant health disparities. 
Interestingly, similar to phantom limb pain, SCD pain persists even after bone marrow transplantation for many months and patients experience recurrent VOE symptoms without biological laboratory value changes. 21 , 43 More recently, the same phenomenon has been noted in gene therapy patients. In both bone marrow transplant and gene therapy, some of the pain that persists has a chronic component. A test to delineate active RBC sickling after bone marrow transplant and gene therapy would be incredibly meaningful for patients experiencing residual pain. If their RBCs are not sickling during the pain episodes, the targeted long-term treatment of chronic pain may be different and may be able to start sooner. Patient VOE symptoms are biologically complex and multidimensional. Therefore, treatments for VOE may not be effective if the treatment is 1-dimensional. We have observed the failure of multiple clinical studies assessing VOE, because the investigated therapies target a specific component of SCD pain. 12 , 44 
To find effective therapies for VOE, we must first understand and describe with biomarkers for the multidimensional aspects of VOE. For example, a commercial device known as the laser optical rotational red cell analyzer was developed to determine the point of sickling based on the principle of oxygen gradient ektacytometry or measuring RBC elongation under shear stress along an oxygen gradient. 45 , 46 This biomarker could be used for early intervention during acute VOE episodes, help with early identification and intervention for alternative etiologies of pain, and proactively optimizing therapies and patient-directed self-care. 47 Laser optical rotational red cell analyzer determines the point of sickling using the entire population. Even though population-level biomarkers are useful for epidemiology and large-scale clinical classification, single-cell biomarkers have the potential to capture heterogeneity within patients, revealing disease-driving subpopulations and refining precision diagnosis and treatment at the cellular level. Moreover, complications of SCD are dynamic and are always in flux. Understanding single-cell level differences over time may provide insights that are missed when analyzing cells in bulk, especially when measured longitudinally. A point-of-care test that can identify in real time and detect single-cell level differences in RBC shape and Hb changes could be a significant step in understanding VOE and could be applied for developing targeted VOE treatments. 
By using CTV to analyze the magnetic behavior of the RBCs at the single-cell level, this study has determined that the top and bottom 1% percentile values of u m are significantly different for patients with SCD in crises. The presence of rapidly moving RBCs, with velocities higher than 0.002 mm/s for deoxygenated RBCs and 0.0005 mm/s for oxygenated RBCs, is highly suggestive of RBCs that have a smaller, or deformed shape, corresponding to a lower hydrodynamic drag than RBCs from healthy donors, and/or a higher content of paramagnetic Hb forms. We and other laboratories 32 have theoretically and experimentally validated that the velocity of RBCs in a magnetic energy gradient is determined by the following equation: 
v mag = ( χ RBC − χ buffer ) V RBC 3 π D RBC μ S m 
(1) 
where χ RBC , V RBC , and D RBC , are the magnetic susceptibility, volume, and diameter of each RBC, respectively, and χ buffer , μ, and S m are the magnetic susceptibility of the medium, viscosity of the buffer, and magnetic energy gradient, respectively. Equation 1 clearly indicates that the primary way an RBC can have a higher magnetic velocity is either through a higher magnetic susceptibility (more paramagnetic Hb in the cell) or a smaller projected diameter in the direction of movement, when the values of S m, 
χ buffer , and μ are constant. Since Hb is generally the only factor that contributes to the paramagnetic behavior of an RBC, the most logical conclusion is that the rare cells have a smaller diameter and/or more or less paramagnetic Hb. Given that SCD RBCs have been historically characterized by misshapen RBCs, it is highly likely that these are either sickled or cylinders with small diameters. It is expected that a cylinder will present the smallest cross-sectional area as it moves in the direction of the magnetic field gradient. It is also possible that altered Hb allosteric responsiveness is affecting the magnetic mobility of the unique populations of cells, microerythrocytes or microparticles. For example, irreversibly sickled RBCs as well as sickle cells with high levels of paramagnetic heme hexacoordinated hemichrome are present in the blood of patients with VOEs. 48 In addition, several studies suggest that both HbFe 2+ O 2 and HbFe 2+ and higher oxidation state Hb (HbFe 4+ /HbFe 4+ = O) are present in microparticles, which are also more common in the blood of patients experiencing VOEs. 49 , 50 The unique combinations of oxidation states, oxygen binding, and hemi-chromes likely contribute to the physical behavior of the small populations of cells showing both extremely slow and extremely fast mobility. 
Although the present study provides important insights into the magnetic behavior of RBCs across different pain categories, there are some limitations that should be acknowledged. One is the variable time duration between patient admission with VOE and the collection of a sample, as this time is variable both between patients and within individual patients at different instances. Regardless of the sensitivity and specificity of the tests, there are difficulties in determining how to accurately interpret the pain category for each patient. Further research will need to be done to understand intraindividual and interindividual variation of the tests and clinical applicability during specific episodes. For example, if the patient has a splenic or liver sequestration episode and the number of circulating abnormal cells decreases, this may lead to catastrophic misclassification of patients in acute VOE. Moreover, this is a pilot study to correlate the magnetic characteristics to the pain categories at a single cell level. Future studies should also incorporate how patient medication (eg, hydroxyurea) and the hemoglobinopathy of the patients also affect the magnetic characteristics of the RBCs. This underscores the extent to which the test still requires further evaluation, as its performance and limitations must be fully understood before any consideration of clinical application. This is particularly important in the SCD population as the patient community already has a high level of distrust for medical professionals. Identifying those situations where the test may yield a negative result will be essential in providing an accurate diagnosis and maintaining the SCD community’s trust. 
Furthermore, the findings from this study demonstrated that RBCs from SCD donors exhibit significantly elevated u m in the 99th percentile compared with healthy donor RBCs. This means that magnetic separation techniques can be developed to separate sickle RBCs from healthy donor RBCs from waste apheresis bags during an RBC exchange transfusion. This separation has to be done at an optimal partial pressure of oxygen, since the RBCs’ magnetic behavior inversely correlates with the percent oxygenation status. In the end, a potential source of RBCs is generated for patients with SCD undergoing exchange transfusion that reduces the risk of alloimmunization and the number of donor RBC units required for the procedure.
