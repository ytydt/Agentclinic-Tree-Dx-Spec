Toward therapeutic development for ML-DS

In this issue of Blood Advances , Berman et al 1 report the results of the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) trial AAML1531, in which conventional chemotherapy was intensified for children with high-risk myeloid leukemia associated with Down syndrome (ML-DS), defined by flow cytometry (FCM)–based measurable residual disease (MRD) at the end of induction 1 (EOI-1). Strikingly, this strategy failed to improve outcomes compared with the preceding AAML0431 trial, while resulting in an increased incidence of febrile neutropenia and sepsis. This sobering observation raises an important question: how can we move beyond the limits of conventional chemotherapy in ML-DS? 
ML-DS has been recognized as a distinct category of myeloid neoplasms in the 2022 World Health Organization classification, characterized by early onset (typically between >3-6 months and ≤4 years), predominance of megakaryoblastic leukemia, and mutations in the hematopoietic transcription factor GATA1 . ML-DS is uniquely sensitive to cytarabine, and reduced-intensity chemotherapy has been adopted to balance efficacy with safety. This strategy has achieved excellent results in newly diagnosed patients, with 3 to 5 year event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) rates approaching 90% (see table ). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Nevertheless, these favorable outcomes are largely restricted to the frontline setting. Once relapse occurs, survival drops precipitously. In an international collaborative study of relapsed/refractory ML-DS, 3-year EFS and OS were only 20.9% and 22.1%, respectively. 5 This contrast between frontline success and relapse failure highlights an urgent unmet need: preventing relapse must become the central focus of future therapeutic development.

Efforts to identify prognostic markers in ML-DS have yielded mixed results. Cytogenetic abnormalities such as monosomy 7, trisomy 8, loss of 1q, or even a normal karyotype have been associated with relapse in some studies, as have clinical features such as nonmegakaryoblastic morphology, high leukocyte counts, extramedullary disease, older age (≥2 years), or early morphologic response. However, these associations have varied across trials in North America, Europe, and Japan, and none have been universally validated. In contrast, MRD at EOI-1 has emerged consistently as the most powerful prognostic factor, paralleling its importance in other leukemias. In the COG AAML0431 trial, FCM-MRD positivity (>0.01%) at EOI-1 was associated with a 5-year disease-free survival of 76.2%, compared with 92.7% in MRD-negative patients ( P = .011). 1 , 4 Similar results were reported in the Japan Children’s Cancer Group (JCCG) AML-D11 trial (3-year EFS of 93.3% in MRD-negative [<0.05%] patients vs 60.0% in MRD-positive [≥0.05%] patients, P = .01), with comparable findings using polymerase chain reaction–based GATA1 MRD assays. 3 These results have firmly established MRD-based risk stratification in ML-DS, and recent and ongoing trials now incorporate MRD into frontline designs. 
However, AAML1531 demonstrated the limitations of MRD as a sole guide for therapy modulation. In that study, the omission of high-dose cytarabine in MRD-negative (<0.05%) patients resulted in inferior outcomes (2-year EFS of 85.6% vs 91.0% in the AAML0431 counterpart; P = .0002). 4 This disappointing result makes clear that FCM-MRD alone cannot safely identify patients for therapy de-escalation. More sensitive techniques, such as next-generation sequencing (NGS)–based GATA1 MRD, may refine prognostic precision, but ultimately, further improvements will require the incorporation of new therapeutic modalities rather than the further manipulation of conventional agents. 
Recent genomic studies from Japan have shed important light on the biology of ML-DS. Transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM), a self-limiting proliferation of megakaryoblasts, occurs in 10% to 20% of neonates with Down syndrome, and 20% to 30% of TAM cases progress to ML-DS. Yoshida et al demonstrated that TAM is initiated by constitutive trisomy 21 and a single GATA1 mutation, whereas progression to ML-DS requires the acquisition of additional lesions in JAK kinases, RAS pathway genes, cohesin components, or epigenetic regulators. 6 Sato et al extended these findings, showing that ML-DS patients harboring mutations in CDKN2A , TP53 , ZBTB7A , or JAK2 had dramatically poorer outcomes. 7 A similar result was reproduced in the AAML1531 cohort, where relapse occurred in 50% of patients with these markers compared with only 6.7% in those without, irrespective of MRD status. These observations identify molecular lesions with both prognostic and therapeutic significance. 
Among these, CDKN2A and JAK2 stand out as targetable. CDKN2A loss may confer susceptibility to CDK4/6 inhibitors such as palbociclib, whereas JAK2 mutations may be addressable with ruxolitinib. Frequent alterations in epigenetic regulators further suggest a role for epigenetic therapies. Azacitidine, a hypomethylating agent, has shown preclinical efficacy in ML-DS models, inducing cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and differentiation, and retrospective series confirm its safety in relapsed/refractory patients. 8 , 9 Building on this, the JCCG has launched the AML-D24 trial (jRCTs041250031) to evaluate azacitidine-priming chemotherapy in high-risk newly diagnosed ML-DS (age ≥2 years, or any of the following: nonremission or MRD-positivity at EOI-1 or absence of GATA1 mutation). Moreover, a recently published expert guideline recommended azacitidine ± the HDAC inhibitor panobinostat for relapsed/refractory ML-DS with low blast count, raising the possibility that such regimens may one day move into the frontline setting. 10 
Collectively, the report by Berman et al underscores the therapeutic plateau of conventional chemotherapy and provides a crucial reference point for the next era of therapy development. The future for ML-DS will depend on harnessing its unique biology to design targeted interventions and on testing these innovations through international collaboration considering the rarity of this disease. For a disease that has already demonstrated the curability of de novo cases with attenuated therapy, the challenge now is to prevent relapse and to achieve durable cures for all patients. This report may thus represent an important milestone, paving the way for the integration of molecularly guided approaches into the treatment of ML-DS.
