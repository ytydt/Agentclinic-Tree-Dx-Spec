TO THE EDITOR: 
Unrelated umbilical cord blood (UCB) is an alternative source for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) in patients without a fully matched sibling or unrelated donor. UCB offers several advantages, including less stringent HLA-matching requirements, rapid graft acquisition, and excellent antitumor effects. 1 , 2 , 3 The applicability of UCB is further expanded by using reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) protocols in older patients or those with comorbidities and using double UCB units to achieve a higher cell dose. 4 , 5 However, the use of UCB transplant is limited by slow engraftment and the risk of early nonrelapse mortality (NRM) historically. 6 , 7 We conducted a retrospective analysis of the outcomes of 88 consecutive adult patients (median age, 49 years; range, 18-70) with acute leukemia and myeloid malignancies who received their first allogeneic HSCT with UCB as the stem cell source from 2007 to 2022 at The Royal Marsden Hospital. 
The most common diagnosis of the study population was acute myeloid leukemia (61%), followed by acute lymphoblastic leukemia (20%) and myelodysplastic neoplasms (11%). All except 1 patient with myelodysplastic neoplasms had intermediate-risk disease or higher according to the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System, and half had high-risk or very high-risk disease. 8 When considering the classification systems specific to the diagnosis, 8 , 9 , 10 31 patients (35%) exhibited high-risk genetic features ( supplemental Table 1 ). Most transplants were performed at first complete remission (67%). Measurable residual disease (MRD) was assessed before HSCT using multiparametric flow cytometry in 74 patients in complete remission, and an additional 12 patients were evaluated using molecular methods. MRD was positive in 38%, negative in 49%, and undetermined owing to the absence of leukemia-associated immunophenotype or molecular marker in 14% of patients ( supplemental Table 2 ). 
In the study center, HLA-matched siblings were the preferred stem cell sources, followed by matched unrelated donors. For acute leukemia and myeloid malignancies, UCB would be selected in preference to mismatched unrelated or haploidentical donors if suitable UCB units were available. Mismatched UCB units were selected using Anthony Nolan Graft Identification and Advisory Service and adhered to international cord selection guidelines, 11 , 12 prioritizing banks accredited by the Foundation for the Accreditation of Cellular Therapy. Of the patients, 38%, 11%, and 51% were conditioned with myeloablative conditioning (MAC), medium-intensity conditioning (MIDI), and RIC regimens, respectively (details of conditioning in supplemental Table 3 ). Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis included cyclosporine and mycophenolate mofetil. 11 , 12 Eight patients (9%) transplanted in 2009 or before received antithymocyte globulin for T-cell depletion. 
Single-unit UCB HSCT was performed in 29 patients (33%), whereas double-unit UCB HSCT was performed in 59 patients (67%). The median cryopreserved total nucleated cell count of the UCB units was 3.47 × 10 7 /kg (range, 2.36-6.41) for recipients of single-unit UCB HSCT and 2.50 × 10 7 /kg (range, 1.36-4.55) for double-unit recipients. The median CD34 + cells for single-unit UCB HSCT recipients was 1.86 × 10 5 /kg (range, 0.51-6.32), whereas for double-unit recipients it was 1.37 × 10 5 /kg (range, 0.12-4.31). Among the UCB units, 16 (14%) had CD34 + cells of <1 × 10 5 /kg; these were transplanted before the publication of the 2016 United Kingdom recommendations. 11 High-resolution typing at HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, and HLA-DR was available in 140 UCB units, revealing that 32% of UCB units were 5 of 8 matched or less for single-unit transplants compared with 11% for double-unit transplants ( supplemental Table 4 ). 
The overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. The time to neutrophil engraftment, risk of relapse, and NRM were determined using cumulative incidence. The influence of covariates, including age (<60 or ≥60 years), genetic risks, MRD status at HSCT, conditioning intensity, number of UCB units, and period of transplant (2006-2017 vs 2018-2022), on OS was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models, whereas, on engraftment, relapse risk and NRM were assessed using Fine-Grey regressions. 
The median follow-up time after UCB transplantation for the study population was 56 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 46-79). The median OS was 105 months (95% CI, 21 to not reached), and the 3-year OS rate was 59% (95% CI, 47%-68%). The cumulative incidence of relapse at 3 years was 22% (95% CI, 14%-32%) ( Figure 1 ). In multivariable analysis, RIC was associated with worse OS (hazard ratio, 2.13; P = .045) and a higher risk of relapse (hazard ratio, 3.42; P = .03) than MAC; however, no differences in OS and relapse risk were observed between patients who received MIDI and MAC. No other factors were associated with OS or relapse risk ( Tables 1 and 2 ). Moreover, all factors examined were not associated with NRM.

The median time to neutrophil engraftment was 21 days (95% CI, 19-22). Transplant performed after 2017 was the only predictor of earlier engraftment ( P = .03). Primary graft failure was observed in 4 patients (5%). Acute GVHD was reported in 47 patients (53%) by 100 days after UCB transplant, with a grade 3 to 4 acute GVHD rate of 16%. Among the 72 patients who survived at least 100 days after transplant, 16 developed chronic GVHD (22%) by 3 years. Of these, 7 cases were classified as moderate and 1 as severe according to National Institutes of Health grading. 
We hereby report favorable survival outcomes after UCB transplant in our cohort of adult patients with acute leukemia and myeloid malignancies. Notably, many patients exhibited high-risk characteristics: 35% had high-risk genetics, 32% had positive MRD before HSCT, and 2% were not in remission at the time of HSCT. Notwithstanding, the 3-year OS rate of 59% is comparable with many analyses of HSCT using matched donors as stem cell source in the literature. 1 , 3 , 13 , 14 Furthermore, UCB provides secure and rapid donor access, contributing to its increased use at our center during the COVID-19 pandemic. 
There was a trend toward shorter survival in patients with positive MRD at the time of HSCT and harboring high-risk genetic features, with median OS of 70 months and 14 months, respectively. However, their cumulative incidences of relapse were comparable with those without these high-risk features. These findings are in line with other studies and may indicate a stronger graft-versus-leukemic effect of UCB. 1 , 15 Although it is not surprising that patients receiving MAC had a lower relapse rate and superior OS than those receiving RIC, our results indicate that outcomes of patients receiving MIDI and MAC were comparable. It suggests that MIDI should be considered in selected fit older patients, given that it enhances survival without increasing NRM compared with RIC. 11 , 15 
The risk of delayed neutrophil recovery and engraftment failure has been a concern for UCB transplants. However, our study population compared favorably with many earlier analyses of UCB transplants. 6 , 16 Notably, the time to engraftment was shorter in more recent transplants. This may be attributed to optimal UCB unit selection, which incorporated considering CD34 dose alongside total nucleated cell count, 17 choosing units from banks with best practices, 11 , 17 and avoiding units with donor-specific antibodies. 11 , 18 
This study is limited by its retrospective design and the small sample size relative to the heterogeneous patient characteristics. Genetic risk stratification was primarily based on cytogenetics, given that precise molecular diagnostics were not available for patients who received HSCT during the early study period. The sensitivities of MRD evaluation also varied depending on the methods used, which hindered the interpretation of our findings. Furthermore, the current study spans a significant period during which transplant practices have evolved considerably. Nonetheless, our real-life experience demonstrates that UCB HSCT is a viable alternative for patients lacking fully matched donors. With advancements in genetic risk stratification and MRD assessment techniques, future studies are warranted to elucidate whether patients exhibiting high-risk features would derive greater benefit from UCB transplants.
