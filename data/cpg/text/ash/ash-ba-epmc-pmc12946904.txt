TO THE EDITOR: 
We read with interest the viewpoint by Banerjee et al 1 advocating for universal immunoglobulin replacement therapy (RT) following bispecific antibody (bsAb) therapy in multiple myeloma (MM). Although we share concerns regarding the risk of infection, several issues argue against universal immunoglobulin RT. 
Recent guidelines endorse this practice. 2 , 3 , 4 However, infection incidence in earlier studies was likely overestimated because of the COVID-19 pandemic. 5 , 6 , 7 Bacterial infections likely to be prevented by immunoglobulin RT are those caused by encapsulated organisms (eg, Streptococcus pneumoniae ). However, in the largest retrospective series, 7 two-thirds of bacterial infections were caused by Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa , infections unlikely to be prevented by immunoglobulin RT. 8 Moreover, these studies 5 , 6 , 7 were retrospective and suffered from confounding and selection biases, in which major differences existed between patients who received immunoglobulin RT and those who did not. 
We disagree with the claim that a randomized controlled trial (RCT) would be impractical. An RCT is both feasible and urgently needed. Clinical equipoise exists; the control arm can be standard of care (appropriate vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis). A feasibility study comparing antibiotic prophylaxis with immunoglobulin RT showed similar infection rates 9 and lower cost with antibiotics. 10 
The universal adoption of immunoglobulin RT risks becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy. Early observational data derive from heavily pretreated cohorts. As clinician experience improves and bsAbs is introduced earlier in the disease course, infection rates may fall independently of immunoglobulin RT, and future historical comparisons will inaccurately attribute improvements to immunoglobulin RT, precluding rigorous evaluations. 
Costs and availability also deserve emphasis. For example, in Brazil, immunoglobulin RT is often unavailable in the public system. Even in high-income settings, immunoglobulin RT is expensive and resource constrained. A universal immunoglobulin RT strategy risks exacerbating inequities and compromising other high-priority indications. 
Infections remain a major threat in bsAb-treated MM. However, adopting immunoglobulin RT solely based on biological plausibility and nonrandomized studies sets a problematic precedent. 11 Therefore, we advocate conducting RCTs before widespread implementation in all patients with bsAb-treated MM.
