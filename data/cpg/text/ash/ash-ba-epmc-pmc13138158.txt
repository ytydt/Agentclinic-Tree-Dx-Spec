Abstract 
Newly diagnosed mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is commonly treated with rituximab (R), combined with anthracycline-based chemotherapy, with or without autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT). Although R maintenance (RM) in clinical trials has been shown to prolong overall survival (OS), its impact at the population level remains largely unknown. This study evaluated the effect of RM on the outcome of patients with MCL. Patients aged ≥18 years, who were diagnosed with MCL between 1989 and 2020 were identified using the Netherlands Cancer Registry and were categorized into periods that reflected R and RM implementation (1989-2000, 2001-2014, 2015-2020). The treatment strategies were categorized as R-CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone); R-CHOP, followed by high-dose cytarabine (intensive) and ASCT; and other. The primary end point was 5-year OS. A multivariable analysis (MVA) was performed using Cox regression. Among 4751 patients, the 5-year relative survival (RS) improved from 38% (1989-2000) to 47% (2001-2014) and 60% (2015-2020) ( P < .01), irrespective of age (≤65 years: 32% vs >65 years: 20% increase over time). Patients with progression within 12 months had a 2-year OS rate of 25%. Since 2014, RM implementation reached 80% in younger patients and 50% in older patients. RM was associated with improved OS, especially for patients in partial remission (PR) after induction treatment with R-CHOP. In the MVA of patients treated with R-CHOP, RM was independently associated with reduced mortality (hazard ratio, 0.69; 95% confidence interval, 0.53-0.90). The RS in MCL improved by >20% in 30 years. Early disease progression remains associated with poor outcome. RM was associated with improved survival, especially among patients who achieved PR following R-CHOP. 
Introduction 
Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a rare and aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma that is characterized by the chromosomal t(11;14) translocation, which leads to an overexpression of cyclin D1. Although MCL typically demonstrates high response rates to induction chemotherapy, it is associated with a significant risk for relapse. Historically, MCL has been considered an incurable disease with poor long-term outcomes and a median overall survival (OS) of 4 to 5 years. 1 , 2 
Elderly patients (commonly defined as ≥65 years) or those who are considered unfit typically receive rituximab (R) in combination with chemotherapy regimens such as bendamustine or CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone). 3 , 4 In contrast, fit patients, generally younger than 65 years, are treated with R-CHOP combined with a high-dose cytarabine-containing regimen, with or without the addition of cisplatinum, followed by autologous stem-cell transplantation (ASCT). 5 , 6 , 7 
In clinical trials, R maintenance (RM) after first-line treatment with R-CHOP and a high-dose cytarabine-containing regimen, but not FCR (fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab), has demonstrated the potential to prolong survival. 3 , 8 In retrospective cohort studies, RM improved survival after R-bendamustine (BR); however, the analyses of patients who were treated with R-CHOP were limited by small sample sizes. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Among patients who were treated with intensive therapy, retrospective studies have indicated that RM after ASCT was associated with improved progression-free survival (PFS) and OS. 13 , 14 In the randomized study by Le Gouille et al , 8 RM after ASCT improved the 4-year OS by 9% when compared with observation only (89% vs 80%; P = .04). At the 7-year follow-up, the PFS benefit remained significant, although the OS difference was no longer statistically significant (83% vs 72%; P = .09). 15 More recently, studies have investigated the addition of ibrutinib to standard chemoimmunotherapy and demonstrated an improvement in PFS but not in OS to date. 7 , 16 
Whether the benefit of RM observed in the clinical trials, typically conducted in selected patient populations that meet strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, also applies to the real-world MCL population is largely unknown. 17 This study aimed to characterize the clinical features, treatment modalities, and outcome of patients with MCL in a population-based setting in the Netherlands with a specific focus on the impact of RM over time in different treatment groups. 
Material and methods 
The Netherlands Cancer Registry 
Data were obtained from the nationwide, population-based the Netherlands Cancer Registry (NCR). The population-based NCR is maintained and hosted by the Netherlands Comprehensive Cancer Organization and has, since 1989, nationwide coverage of at least 95% of all malignancies. 18 All newly diagnosed malignancies are reported to the NCR via the Nationwide Network of Histopathology and Cytopathology and the National Registry of Hospital Discharges (eg, in- and outpatient discharges). Information on patient characteristics and treatment is routinely recorded in the NCR by trained registrars of the NCR through retrospective medical record reviews. Information on the last known vital status of all patients (eg, alive, deceased, emigrated) is obtained through annual linkage with the Nationwide Population Registries Network, which holds vital statistics on all residents in the Netherlands. Since 1 January 2014, detailed information on the diagnostic and treatment characteristics of all hematologic malignancies diagnosed in the Netherlands has been recorded in the NCR. The data are entered 1 year after diagnosis. According to the Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects, this type of observational study does not require approval from an ethics committee in the Netherlands. The privacy review board of the NCR approved the use of anonymous data for this study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 
Study population 
Patients, ≥18 years, who were diagnosed with histopathologic MCL in the Netherlands between 1 January 1989 and 31 December 2020 were identified in the NCR using the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (World Health Organization), morphology code 9673/3, based on pathology reports. 
For the overall cohort (1989-2020), first-line therapy is presented for 3 calendar periods, namely 1989 to 2000, 2001 to 2014, and 2015 to 2020. The calendar periods were based on the introduction of R in the Netherlands as part of routine clinical practice and, more recently, as maintenance. The period 1989 to 2000 represents the pre-R era, the period 2001 to 2014 represents the R era, and the period 2015 to 2020 represents the era of additional RM implementation ( supplemental Figure 1 ). Because of the COVID-19 pandemic that started in 2020, patients with histopathologically confirmed MCL beyond 2020 were not included in this study. Survival follow-up was available until 1 February 2025. After this date, all patients who were alive were censored in the survival analyses. 
In analysis A, the OS and relative survival (RS) for the whole cohort (eg, patients diagnosed between 1989 and 2020), according to age (18-65 years and ≥66 years) and the 3 calendar periods, were calculated. Detailed information on treatment was available for all patients from 2014. First-line therapy was categorized into 3 groups: (1) R-CHOP; (2) R-CHOP followed by high-dose cytarabine-containing regimens and ASCT (intensive therapy); and (3) other, such as CHOEP (cyclophosphamide, hydroxydaunorubicin [doxorubicin], oncovin [vincristine], etoposide, and prednisone), CVP (cyclophosphamide, oncovin [vincristine], prednisone), R monotherapy, chlorambucil, BR, ibrutinib, or radiotherapy. Patients participating in clinical studies or treated otherwise were excluded from all analyses. 
Progression of disease within 12 months (POD12) following diagnosis was evaluated in analysis B, in which progression of disease (POD) was categorized as <6 months after diagnosis and within 6 to 12 months after diagnosis. Because all relapses were recorded 1 year after diagnosis, information on POD12 only was available ( supplemental Figure 1 ). The impact of RM, depending on remission status after first-line therapy, on the OS for patients who were treated with R-CHOP or intensive therapy was evaluated in analysis C. Patients with stable disease, primary refractory disease, or unknown remission status were excluded from analysis C because these patients did not qualify for RM ( supplemental Figure 2 ). 
End points 
The primary end point was OS. For analysis A, RS was a secondary outcome measure. RS was defined as the ratio of the observed OS of the patient cohort to the expected OS of a similar group from the general population, matched for age and sex. RS reflects the excess mortality associated with MCL, independent of other causes of death, and provides an estimate of disease-specific survival when the cause of death information is unavailable. 
Statistical analysis 
Descriptive statistics were used to present the patient and treatment characteristics. Pearson χ 2 tests were used to compare categorical covariables, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare non-normally distributed continuous covariables between the 2 treatment groups. The OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. A log-rank test was used to evaluate the differences in survival distributions. The RS and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated as the OS divided by the expected survival of an equivalent population using the Ederer II methodology from Dutch population life tables, stratified by age and the 3 calendar periods (1989-2000, 2001-2014, and 2015-2020). The impact of RM, age at diagnosis (per year increment), sex, Ann Arbor stage (1-2, 3-4, and unknown), response (complete remission [CR] vs partial remission [PR]), and Mantle Cell Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (MIPI) score (low, intermediate, and high risk) as categorical variables on the risk for mortality was evaluated using uni- and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses. The results from the Cox regression analyses produce hazard ratios (HRs) with the associated 95% Cls. All covariables with a significant difference in the univariable regression model were included in the multivariable regression model. Covariables were sequentially removed, starting with the highest P value, until all remaining variables had P values ≤.05. The statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE 17.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX). 
Results 
Patient characteristics 
In total, 4751 patients with MCL were identified for the period of 1989 to 2020 ( supplemental Figure 1 ), with a median age of 70 years and a male-to-female ratio of 2.6:1 ( supplemental Table 1 ). From 2014 to 2020, a total of 1396 patients were diagnosed with MCL, of whom 513 (37%) were treated with R-CHOP and 265 (19%) were treated with intensive chemotherapy. The baseline characteristics of these patients are described in Tables 1 and 2 . Patients who were treated with R-CHOP were significantly older than patients who received ASCT (median age: 72 vs 61 years; P < .01).

Outcome over time (analysis A) 
The 5-year OS for the entire cohort was 41% ( Figure 1 ). When evaluating survival for the 3 calendar periods, the 5-year RS improved from 38% in 1989 to 2000 to 47% in 2001 to 2014 and to 60% in the most recent calendar period (2015-2020; P < .01; Figure 2 ). The 5-year RS of patients aged 18 to 65 years was significantly higher than the 5-year RS of patients older than 65 years (66% vs 38%; P < .01; supplemental Figure 4 ). Over time, the RS improved in both age groups, with a 32% increase among young patients and a 20% increase among the elderly ( supplemental Figure 5 A-B).

Outcome among primary refractory patients (analysis B) 
Among the 778 patients diagnosed between 2014 and 2020, 101 patients (13%) experienced POD within 12 months. The OS of patients with POD within 12 months was poor, with 1-year and 2-year OS rates of only 38% and 25%, respectively. There was no significant difference in OS between patients with primary refractory disease and those with an early relapse ( Figure 3 ). In the multivariable analysis, neither age, sex, RM, MIPI score, nor stage of disease at initial diagnosis was associated with risk for POD within 12 months of diagnosis.

Outcome according to treatment and impact of RM (analysis C) 
Between 2014 and 2020, the use of RM increased ( supplemental Figure 6 ), primarily driven by its introduction in patients ≤65 years. Between 2015 and 2017, the proportion of patients who received RM following intensive chemotherapy increased from 0% to 71%. Among patients who were treated with R-CHOP, RM was applied in 40% to 60% of cases. The baseline characteristics of patients who were treated with RM and those who were not were comparable ( Tables 1 and 2 ). 
Among 513 patients who were treated with R-CHOP, the 5-year OS was 43% ( supplemental Figure 7 ). Of the 398 patients who achieved a response (CR or PR), 227 (57%) received RM. The OS was similar between patients who were treated with RM and those who were not ( P = .79). Among the 262 patients with a CR, there was a trend toward an OS benefit among patients who treated with RM when compared with those who were not ( P = .06), with 5-year OS estimates of 50% and 64%, respectively ( Figure 4 ). Among the 140 patients with a PR, the OS was significantly better among those who received RM than among those who did not ( P < .01), with 5-year OS estimates of 44% and 33%, respectively ( Figure 4 ). Among patients who did not receive RM, those with a CR had a superior 5-year OS rate than those with a PR (64% vs 33%; P < .01).

Among the 265 patients who were treated with intensive therapy, the 5-year OS was 74% ( supplemental Figure 8 ). Of the 230 patients who achieved a CR or PR, 114 (50%) received RM. In this group, the 5-year OS was similar between those who received RM and those who did not. For patients with a CR, the 5-year OS was 83% with RM vs 77% without RM ( P = .46); for those with a PR, the 5-year OS was 55% vs 70%, respectively ( P = .29; Figure 5 ).

A multivariable analysis was performed separately for patients who were treated with R-CHOP and those who were treated with intensive therapy. In the R-CHOP group, RM was associated with a reduced risk for mortality (HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.53-0.90). Factors associated with an increased risk for mortality in this group included PR after induction (HR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.11-1.85), older age (HR per year increment, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.04-1.08), and a higher MIPI score (intermediate score: HR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.12-2.2; high score: HR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.45-3.0). Among patients treated with intensive therapy, PR was also associated with increased mortality (HR, 2.26; 95% CI, 1.28-4.01), as was older age (HR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.03-1.11; Figure 6 ). In this group, RM was not significantly associated with survival.

Discussion 
In this nationwide, population-based study, we evaluated the long-term survival outcomes among patients with MCL and assessed the impact of RM in routine clinical practice. RS improved by 22% over the past 3 decades, largely reflecting the introduction of R, improvements in supportive care, diagnostics, and the integration of novel agents ( supplemental Figure 3 ). Our findings also underscore the importance of RM, particularly in a subgroup of patients with a PR following R-CHOP induction. 
The survival benefit of R in induction regimens for MCL has been well documented since the early 2000s, with Coiffier et al 19 and others demonstrating significantly improved response rates and time to treatment failure when R was added to CHOP-like regimens. 20 , 21 In younger, fit patients, the outcomes improved further with high-dose cytarabine-based induction and ASCT consolidation, as shown in the European MCL Network trial. 22 More recently, the TRIANGLE trial highlighted the role of ibrutinib in further augmenting frontline treatment. 7 
Several clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of RM in MCL. The European MCL Elderly trial (Kluin-Nelemans et al 3 ) reported improved PFS and OS with RM following treatment with R-CHOP but not among those who received FCR. Similarly, RM after ASCT was associated with improved survival in the LyMa trial (Le Gouill et al 8 ); however, the OS benefit did not retain significance at long-term follow-up. 15 These trials shaped international guidelines and established RM as a standard approach following first-line treatment. 
Our real-world data largely validate the findings of clinical trials, particularly in supporting the role of RM after R-CHOP induction therapy. In addition, our study provides insight into the value of response-based analyses after induction therapy. In the multivariable analysis, RM following R-CHOP therapy was independently associated with reduced mortality. Notably, this benefit was most pronounced among patients with a PR, with a trend toward an OS advantage observed in patients with CR ( P = .06), possibly limited by the sample size and duration of follow-up. Next, the difference between patients with CR and those with PR could have been more pronounced if response assessments were uniformly conducted using positron emission tomography/computed tomography imaging (PET/CT). In our cohort, data on the method of response evaluation were available from 2017 to 2020, showing that the method of response assessment was PET/CT in 84% of patients who achieved CR, whereas it was 33% in patients with a PR, suggesting that the uniform use of PET/CT could have led to better differentiation of patients with PR. 
The effect of treatment response is not well captured in randomized controlled trials, which often lack the granularity or statistical power to assess subgroups based on depth of response. 23 The findings of this study are somewhat similar to previous observational studies. In a large US cohort study, Martin et al 11 reported a statistically significant improvement in time to next treatment with RM following both BR and R-CHOP. However, an OS benefit was observed only after BR and not R-CHOP, potentially because of a limited sample size in the R-CHOP group. Similarly, a second US-based, real-world analysis by Di et al 12 demonstrated improved outcomes with RM after BR or R-CHOP; however, most patients received BR, and the effect of RM was not evaluated independently in the R-CHOP–treated population. Importantly, neither study stratified by remission status, potentially masking important heterogeneity. Our findings did not align with those from Hoster et al, 24 who reported that minimal risidual disease (MRD)-positive patients benefited less from RM. Differences in the first-line induction regimens, cohort size, information on remission, or progression status, follow-up period, and the inclusion of patients treated as early as 2007 likely contributed to the heterogeneity in the outcomes of real-world studies. Collectively, these results suggest that remission status may be a critical determinant of RM efficacy and survival, and its use may be limited in those with CR or molecular remission. 
Interestingly, in our cohort, RM was not associated with improved OS following intensive chemoimmunotherapy and ASCT. Although retrospective data 13 , 14 and the LYMA 8 trial initially supported RM in this setting, its long-term impact remains debated, especially in the context of evolving frontline strategies that incorporate novel agents. As suggested by Sarkozy et al, 15 RM may not overcome the adverse prognosis of early relapse (POD24), and its benefit may be less apparent in patients who already achieved deep remissions. 
The poor outcomes observed in patients with early progression (POD < 12 months) are consistent with previous studies and highlight the need for better risk stratification and therapeutic strategies. Emerging treatments, including next-generation Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors, B-cell lymphoma 2 inhibitors, and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy, offer hope for this high-risk population. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 
Our study is strengthened by the large, unselected cohort of patients who were uniformly treated with R-CHOP and who underwent comprehensive follow-up; however, it is limited by the lack of data on toxicity, MRD status, response assessment in routine clinical practice, reasons for (non-)initiation of RM, and information on second-line therapy. Therefore, analyses of the impact of salvage strategies (eg, Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors, bendamustine, or chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy) in light of RM on survival could not be performed. Moreover, we acknowledge that real-world decision-making, particularly in elderly patients or those with comorbidities, may reflect unmeasured confounders that are difficult to adjust for. 
In conclusion, our study validates the survival improvement in MCL over recent decades and supports the selective benefit of RM, especially in patients who achieved PR after R-CHOP. These findings emphasize the need for individualized application of RM and support further evaluation of MRD-guided and response-adapted maintenance strategies in prospective studies.
