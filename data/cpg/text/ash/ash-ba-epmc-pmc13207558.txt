Abstract 
Accurate molecular classification is essential for diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment selection in B-cell lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL). In this study, we performed a comprehensive, real-world reclassification of 1015 consecutively diagnosed B-ALL patients using the fifth edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours (WHO-HAEM5) and the International Consensus Classification (ICC). An integrative genomic strategy that combined whole transcriptome sequencing, fusion detection, mutational analysis, and cytogenetics enabled reclassification according to both the WHO-HAEM5 and ICC frameworks, thereby substantially reducing the proportion of unclassifiable B-ALL from 41.9% (2016 WHO revision [WHO-HAEM4R]) to 15.9% (WHO-HAEM5) and 11.9% (ICC). Distinct clinical and prognostic features were identified across newly defined subtypes. Multivariable analysis confirmed that this genomic classification is a robust, independent predictor of survival after adjusting for age, minimal residual disease status, and transplant intervention. Specifically, HLF -rearranged and MEF2D -rearranged B-ALL conferred a persistently poor prognosis across all age groups despite allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, highlighting an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. Gene expression profiling resolved cryptic subtypes, including ETV6 :: RUNX1 -like, ZNF384 -rearranged-like, and BCR :: ABL1 -like B-ALL, and uncovered diagnostic ambiguity in patients with concurrent lesions. In addition, we report emerging high-risk groups, including IDH1 / 2 - and ZEB2 Q1072-mutated B-ALL, that may warrant recognition as distinct molecular entities. Our findings demonstrate the clinical use of integrative transcriptomic profiling in refining B-ALL taxonomy in guiding risk-adapted therapies and informing future revisions of diagnostic standards. This study supports the incorporation of high-throughput molecular diagnostics into routine leukemia classification and precision treatment planning. 
Introduction 
B-cell lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) is a genetically and clinically heterogeneous malignancy in which accurate genomic classification is central to risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making in the era of precision oncology. 1 In 2022, the fifth edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours (WHO-HAEM5) 2 and the International Consensus Classification (ICC) 3 introduced updated frameworks for B-ALL classification, emphasizing the recurrent genetic abnormalities and expression-defined entities. 
When compared with the 2016 WHO revision (WHO-HAEM4R), 4 the WHO-HAEM5 formalized previously provisional entities, including BCR :: ABL1 -like B-ALL and intrachromosomal amplification of chromosome 21 (iAMP21), and introduced 9 new formal subtypes, 7 of them grouped under “B-ALL with other defined genetic alterations.” The ICC adopted a more granular approach, defining 9 new formal and 5 provisional B-ALL subtypes. Although most formal entities overlap between the 2 systems, notable differences exist, namely ETV6 :: RUNX1 -like and PAX5 alt are provisional only in the ICC, whereas UBTF :: ATXN7L3/PAN3 , CDX2 (“ CDX2 / UBTF ”) and IKZF1 N159Y are formal ICC entities not recognized by WHO-HAEM5. The ICC also proposes additional provisional subtypes and further subdivides established categories, such as BCR :: ABL1 -positive, BCR :: ABL1 -like, and hypodiploid B-ALL. 
Despite this increased granularity, the real-world performance, concordance, and clinical use of these updated frameworks remains incompletely defined in large, unselected B-ALL cohorts. In our previous comparative study of WHO-HAEM5 and ICC in acute myeloid leukemia, discrepant classifications were observed in ∼16% of patients, reflecting differences in the disease definitions, hierarchical logic, and recognition of specific genetic alterations. 5 These findings underscore how conceptual distinctions between classification systems can translate into meaningful divergence when applied to real-world data. 
Building on this analytic framework, we performed a comprehensive reclassification of 1015 consecutively diagnosed B-ALL patients from a single institution using integrated genomic profiling, including transcriptome sequencing, fusion detection, mutation analysis, and cytogenetics. We systematically evaluated the diagnostic resolution, concordance, and prognostic implications of the WHO-HAEM5 and ICC classifications. Our study provides real-world validation of the updated B-ALL classification systems and highlights the critical role of integrative genomic diagnostics in contemporary leukemia management. 
Methods 
Patients 
From 1 September 2018 to 30 April 30 2024, a total of 1015 B-ALL patients that were diagnosed at our institution based on the WHO-HAEM4R criteria were included. The cohort comprised 554 children (≤18 years; median age, 8 years) and 461 adults (>18 years; median age, 36 years). All patients underwent conventional chromosomal karyotyping and high-quality whole transcriptome sequencing (WTS), which served as the primary methods for fusion detection and molecular subtype assignment. In addition, 100 healthy donors were included as nondisease controls for gene expression analyses. 
Frontline treatment was administered according to the institutional protocols, which were aligned with national and international standards, and was adapted based on age, disease characteristics, and early treatment response. Pediatric patients were treated according to the Chinese Children’s Cancer Group Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia–based protocols, whereas adult patients received either pediatric-inspired or adult-type regimens. Across both cohorts, minimal residual disease (MRD) assessment was routinely used to guide consolidation intensity and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) decisions. 
Karyotyping and FISH 
Peripheral blood or bone marrow aspirates were analyzed using conventional G-banding, and the karyotypes were interpreted according to the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature. 6 Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analyses were performed selectively based on clinical and cytogenetic indications. FISH for iAMP21 was applied only when karyotyping suggested RUNX1 amplification or complex chromosome 21 abnormalities. 7 Targeted FISH was used to confirm IGH -associated rearrangements (eg, CRLF2 :: IGH or EPOR :: IGH ) when such events were indicated by WTS. In selected BCR :: ABL1 -positive B-ALL patients, supplementary FISH studies were performed to assess potential myeloid lineage involvement when clinically indicated. 
WTS and data analysis 
Total RNA was extracted from bone marrow samples and assessed for quality before library preparation. Paired-end RNA sequencing was performed, and pathogenic fusion genes, intrachromosomal deletions, and intragenic amplifications were identified as previously described. 8 , 9 
DUX4 rearrangements were manually reviewed by inspecting the aligned reads in the Integrative Genomics Viewer when required. 10 Single nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions were called from the WTS data following the Genome Analysis Toolkit best practices. 11 Gene expression was quantified as TPM (transcripts per kilobase million). 
Patient follow-up and outcome definitions 
Among the 425 patients initially diagnosed with B-ALL not otherwise specified (NOS) according to WHO-HAEM4R, 365 with assessable clinical outcomes were included in the prognostic analyses. The median follow-up was estimated by using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was evaluated among patients who achieved complete remission (CR) and was defined as the time from first documented CR to relapse, death, or last follow-up, whichever occurred first. Patients without events were censored at the last follow-up. The follow-up cutoff date was 1 December 2025. 
To identify independent prognostic factors, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to assess the association between molecular subtypes and OS with adjustment for age, sex, presenting white blood cell count, central nervous system involvement at diagnosis, treatment backbone (adult vs pediatric-inspired), postinduction MRD status, and allogeneic HSCT (allo-HSCT). The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals were reported. 
Statistical analysis 
Categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. The OS and RFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and were compared with log-rank tests. All analyses were 2-sided with P < .05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) and R (version 4.2.1). 
The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 
Results 
Identifying subtype-defining aneuploidy and fusion genes 
Among the 1015 B-ALL patients, chromosomal aneuploidy and fusion genes accounted for a substantial proportion of the classifiable subtypes. Aneuploidy was identified in 15.7% of patients (n = 159), most of them being high hyperdiploidy (n = 132; 13%). Hypodiploidy was observed in 27 patients, including 6 near-haploid, 18 low hypodiploid, and 3 high hypodiploid patients. Notably, ICC restricts the hypodiploid category to near-haploid and low hypodiploid B-ALL. Therefore, the 3 patients that exhibited high hypodiploidy (modal chromosome number 43) that were classified as hypodiploid under WHO-HAEM4R/WHO-HAEM5 could not be assigned to this entity under the ICC guidelines. 
Fusion gene analysis revealed recurrent rearrangements that were sufficient for subtype assignment in 56.7% (n = 576) of patients. The most prevalent fusions included BCR :: ABL1 (n = 172; 16.9%), KMT2A -rearranged (n = 71; 7.0%), ETV6 :: RUNX1 (n = 62; 6.1%), ZNF384 -rearranged (n = 61; 6.0%), and DUX4 -rearranged (n = 55; 5.4%), representing the major molecular subtypes across the current classification systems. The distribution and interaction patterns of fusion genes are illustrated in Figure 1 , and age-stratified landscapes are provided in supplemental Figure 1 .

Overall, aneuploidy and fusion gene analyses enabled definitive subtype assignment in 71.8% of patients (n = 729). 
Identifying B-ALL subtypes based on gene mutations 
Both updated classification systems recognize B-ALL subtypes as being defined by recurrent hotspot mutations. The WHO-HAEM5 includes B-ALL with the PAX5 P80R mutation as a distinct entity, whereas the ICC further recognizes B-ALL with the IKZF1 N159Y mutation. 
In our cohort, PAX5 P80R was identified in 12 patients (1.2%), predominantly adults (10 adults, 2 children). Eight patients were homozygous, whereas 4 were heterozygous with a second PAX5 alteration (2 nonsense, 1 frameshift, and 1 deleterious missense). One patient also harbored a concurrent PAX5 intragenic amplification ( PAX5 amp) and was classified as PAX5 P80R positive based on expression clustering (see later sections for details). 
The IKZF1 N159Y mutation was observed in 3 patients (0.3%; 2 adults and 1 child). All were heterozygous and lacked additional subtype-defining lesions. 
The ICC also recognizes B-ALL with the ZEB2 H1038R mutation as a provisional entity. This alteration was detected in 20 patients (2.0%), including 7 with co-occurring subtype-defining lesions ( DUX4 -rearranged, n = 4; ZNF384 -rearranged, n = 3). In addition to H1038R, the ZEB2 Q1072 mutation is associated with poor event-free survival and increased relapse risk. 12 We identified 7 patients that harbored the Q1072 variants (Q1072R, n = 4; Q1072K, n = 3), 5 of which exhibited concurrent alterations, including high hyperdiploidy (n = 3), TCF3 :: PBX1 (n = 1), or PAX5 amp (n = 1). 
Finally, although not currently incorporated into the WHO-HAEM5 or ICC guidelines, IDH1 / 2 mutations have been proposed as adverse-risk alterations in adult B-ALL. 13 , 14 We identified IDH1 / 2 mutations in 15 patients (1.5%), including IDH1 R132C (n = 8), R132G (n = 3), R132S (n = 3), and IDH2 R140Q (n = 1). All patients occurred in adults and lacked other subtype-defining abnormalities. 
Expression analysis of key genes associated with B-ALL subtypes 
In addition to fusion and mutation detection, WTS enabled comprehensive quantification of gene expression. Because our current fusion detection pipeline did not reliably capture rearrangements, such as CRLF2 :: IGH and EPOR :: IGH , expression-based approaches were used to determine the overexpression of these genes. 
Across all B-ALL patients and 100 healthy controls, the CRLF2 expression (log 2 [TPM + 1]) was ranked. In total, 23 patients showed CRLF2 overexpression (above mean + 2 standard deviations; Figure 2 A). All P2RY8 :: CRLF2 -positive patients that were identified by fusion analysis (n = 11) fell within the CRLF2 -high group. Among the remaining 12 patients, 10 harbored CRLF2 :: IGH rearrangements as confirmed by FISH. The other 2 included 1 with a CRLF2 F232C mutation and 1 with concurrent BCR :: ABL1 and PAX5 :: MLLT3 fusions; the latter was classified as BCR :: ABL1 -positive B-ALL. Four CRLF2 -overexpressing patients (3 P2RY8 :: CRLF2 and 1 CRLF2 :: IGH ) also carried PAX5 -related fusions, indicating genetic heterogeneity. Because CRLF2 overexpression is not specific to BCR :: ABL1 -like B-ALL, additional transcriptomic profiling was required for subtype assignment (explored in subsequent sections).

We further examined the expression of NUTM1 , HLF , DUX4 , and CDX2 across the corresponding subtypes, controls, and unrelated B-ALL patients. NUTM1 , HLF , and DUX4 expression was selectively elevated in their respective rearranged subtypes ( Figure 2 C- E ). All UBTF :: ATXN7L3 -positive patients showed marked CDX2 overexpression ( Figure 2 F), consistent with the CDX2 / UBTF -deregulated subtype. 
These results demonstrate that WTS-based expression analysis facilitates the identification of subtype-specific expression signatures and the detection of cryptic genetic alterations in B-ALL. 
Gene expression profiling–based identification of challenging B-ALL subtypes 
Although many B-ALL subtypes can be defined by fusion genes, aneuploidy, or hotspot mutations, a subset of patients required gene expression profiling for accurate classification. To resolve these cases, we performed t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding analysis based on the transcriptomic profiles. 
The analysis included patients with established genetic alterations, including ABL -class fusions ( ABL1 , ABL2 , PDGFRB ), JAK - STAT pathway lesions ( JAK2 fusions and EPOR :: IGH ), FGFR1 fusions, ETV6 :: RUNX1 , ZNF384 -rearranged, KMT2A -rearranged, PAX5 P80R, other PAX5 alterations (rearrangements, non-P80R mutations, and intragenic amplifications), CRLF2 -high expression, and patients initially classified as B-ALL, NOS. 
Clustering reassigned 41 NOS patients to BCR :: ABL1 -like (n = 23), ETV6 :: RUNX1 -like (n = 11), KMT2A -rearranged-like (n = 3), and ZNF384 -rearranged-like (n = 4) ( Figure 3 A- C ).

With the exclusion of PAX5 P80R, PAX5 alterations were identified in 68 patients, including rearrangements ( PAX5 -r; n = 39), sequence mutations ( PAX5 mut; n = 17), and PAX5 amp (n = 13) with 1 patient harboring both a mutation and an amplification. A total of 12 patients co-occurred with other subdefining lesions, and 4 exhibited CRLF2 overexpression. Among the PAX5 mut patients, 12 were homozygous and 5 were compound heterozygous with 3 showing co-occurring, subtype-defining abnormalities. Three PAX5 amp patients also had additional molecular alterations, including 1 with a concurrent PAX5 P80R mutation ( Figure 3 D). 
Clustering assigned 4 patients with both PAX5 -r and CRLF2 -high to PAX5 alt (n = 2) or BCR :: ABL1 -like (n = 2). One PAX5 amp patient with P80R clustered with the PAX5 P80R subtype. In addition, 1 PAX5 -r and 2 PAX5 mut patients clustered into the BCR :: ABL1 -like group. Furthermore, 23 previously unclassified patients clustered into the PAX5 alt subtype ( Figure 3 A-C). 
Among the 23 CRLF2 -high patients, 1 co-occurred with BCR :: ABL1 and 1 with high hyperdiploidy and were classified accordingly. Four patients with concurrent PAX5 -r were classified as mentioned earlier. Of the remaining 17 patients, 12 clustered into the BCR :: ABL1 -like group, whereas 5 were assigned to NOS (n = 3), ETV6 :: RUNX1 -like (n = 1), or PAX5 alt (n = 1), highlighting that CRLF2 -high is not exclusive to BCR :: ABL1 -like B-ALL ( Figure 3 A-C). 
Overall, expression profiling identified 91 BCR :: ABL1 -like, 69 PAX5 alt, 12 ETV6 :: RUNX1 -like, 4 ZNF384 -rearranged-like, and 3 KMT2A -rearranged-like patients. Transcriptomic clustering also resolved subtype assignments in 7 patients with concurrent aneuploidy and fusion genes ( Figure 3 E). 
Comparative analysis of B-ALL classification using WHO-HAEM4R, WHO-HAEM5, and ICC 
All 1015 patients were reclassified according to the WHO-HAEM4R, WHO-HAEM5, and ICC criteria, thereby enabling a systematic comparison across the classification systems. The subtype transitions and overall distributions are summarized in a Sankey diagram ( Figure 4 ). 
Figure 4. 
Open in a new tab 
Comparative reclassification of 1015 B-ALL patients across WHO-HAEM4R, WHO-HAEM5, and ICC guidelines. 
For well-established fusion-defined subtypes, including BCR :: ABL1 , ETV6 :: RUNX1 , TCF3 :: PBX1 , and KMT2A -rearranged B-ALL, high concordance was observed across all 3 systems. The ICC further refines BCR :: ABL1 -positive B-ALL by distinguishing between lymphoid-only and multilineage involvement. Among the 172 BCR :: ABL1 -positive patients, including 39 cases that were diagnosed after ICC release, myeloid involvement was assessed by FISH in 24 patients. Application of ALLCatchR_bcrabl1 15 yielded high-confidence predictions for 74 patients, classifying 45 as lymphoid only and 29 as multilineage with complete concordance between the molecular and cytogenetic classification in 10 patients with available FISH data. Transcriptome-based t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding analysis further separated these groups into distinct clusters ( supplemental Figure 2 ). No significant OS difference was observed between the lymphoid-only and the multilineage patients ( supplemental Figure 3 ), although the limited number of multilineage patients warrants cautious interpretation. 
Within the BCR :: ABL1 -like category, ICC subdivided the 91 patients into ABL -class rearranged (n = 23), JAK - STAT activated (n = 40), and NOS (n = 28), whereas, according to the WHO-HAEM5, this group is retained as a single entity. Survival analysis did not reveal significant OS differences among the ICC-defined subgroups ( supplemental Figure 4 ), likely reflecting limited statistical power. 
Differences were also evident in hypodiploid B-ALL classification. Although the WHO-HAEM4R and WHO-HAEM5 include high hypodiploidy within the hypodiploid category, ICC restricts this entity to near-haploid and low-hypodiploid patients. Three high hypodiploidy patients (modal chromosome number = 43) therefore met the WHO but not the ICC criteria. Two of these harbored subtype-defining fusions ( DUX4 :: IGH and ETV6 :: RUNX1 ) and clustered with their respective fusion-defined subtypes by transcriptomic analysis, supporting reassignment. The remaining fusion-negative patient was classified as B-ALL, NOS under ICC ( supplemental Table 1 ; Figure 3 E). Although limited by sample size, the OS for the low-hypodiploid (n = 17) and near-haploid (n = 6) patients showed no significant difference ( supplemental Figure 5 ). TP53 alterations were detected in all low-hypodiploid patients (18/18), but these were rare in near-haploid (1/6) and absent in high-hypodiploid patients ( supplemental Table 1 ), consistent with established biologic distinctions. 16 , 17 
The greatest intersystem differences involved the NOS category. Under WHO-HAEM4R, 425 patients (41.9%) were classified as NOS. This proportion decreased substantially under WHO-HAEM5 (161 patients, 15.9%) and ICC (121 patients, 11.9%) because of the incorporation of newly defined fusion-, mutation-, and expression-based subtypes, including ZNF384 -, DUX4 -, and MEF2D -rearranged B-ALL, as well as PAX5 alt and PAX5 P80R-mutated entities. 
Collectively, the updated classification frameworks, particularly when integrated with transcriptomic profiling, markedly reduced the NOS assignments and enhanced molecular resolution in B-ALL. 
Age distribution of the newly defined B-ALL subtypes 
We evaluated the age-specific patterns of the newly defined B-ALL subtypes by comparing pediatric (≤18 years) and adult (>18 years) patients. Adult enrichment was observed for ZNF384 -rearranged, CDX2 / UBTF , ZEB2 H1038R, and PAX5 P80R-positive B-ALL, whereas the PAX5 alt, HLF -rearranged, and ETV6 :: RUNX1 -like subtypes were more frequent in children. Other subtypes showed no significant differences between age groups, although DUX4 - and MEF2D -rearranged patients tended to be more common in pediatric group ( Figure 5 A-B).

For the 5 most frequent subtypes ( PAX5 alt, ZNF384 -rearranged, DUX4 -rearranged, MEF2D -rearranged, and CDX2 / UBTF ), we further analyzed 5 age brackets, namely infant (<1 year), pediatric (1-14 years), adolescents and young adults (AYA; 15-39 years), middle-aged adults (40-59 years), and older adults (≥60 years). All 8 infants carried KMT2A rearrangements; none belonged to these 5 subtypes. ZNF384 -rearranged peaked in AYA (10.5%) with lower frequencies observed in children (3.6%) and a progressive decline in middle-aged (4.5%) and older adults (2.0%). The DUX4 - and MEF2D -rearranged mutations were most frequent in children and AYA ( DUX4 : 5.2% and 7.7%, respectively; MEF2D : 3.6% and 6.6%, respectively), decreased among middle-aged adults (2.2% each), and were absent in older adults. PAX5 alt was highest in children (9.4%), decreased in AYA (5.1%) and middle-aged adults (2.2%), and showed a modest rebound in older adults (3.9%). CDX2 / UBTF was absent in children and increased with age, peaking in older adults (5.9%; Figure 5 C). 
Overall, these data demonstrate a distinct age-dependent prevalence across novel B-ALL subtypes, supporting the incorporation of age into subtype-oriented diagnostic and therapeutic considerations. 
Prognostic analysis of novel B-ALL subtypes 
To evaluate the clinical relevance of the newly defined genomic classification, survival analyses were performed for 365 patients with assessable follow-up who were initially diagnosed as B-ALL, NOS under WHO-HAEM4R. The median follow-up was 57.0 months (95% confidence interval, 51.8-62.2). 
Given the distinct biology and treatment strategies of pediatric and adult B-ALL, survival was analyzed in both the overall cohort and in age-stratified subsets across 9 molecular subtypes with sufficient case numbers. In the overall cohort, these subtypes showed significantly heterogeneous survival outcomes ( Figure 6 A-B). Importantly, the relative prognostic hierarchy was largely preserved in pediatric (≤18 years; n = 214) and adult (>18 years; n = 151) patients ( Figure 6 C-F).

(E-F) The corresponding RFS analysis for pediatric (n = 209) and adult (n = 140) patients (note: only molecular subtypes with a sample size of ≥10 within each age-stratified subgroup are visualized to ensure statistical reliability; discrepancies in the patient numbers between the OS and RFS analyses reflect the exclusion of individuals who failed to achieve CR). (G) Multivariable Cox proportional hazards model for OS. The forest plot illustrates the HRs and 95% CIs for molecular subtypes and transplant status. The model was adjusted for key clinical covariates, including age (as a continuous variable), sex, presenting white blood cell count, central nervous system involvement at diagnosis, therapy backbone (adult vs pediatric regimen), and MRD status after induction. The subtypes were compared against the B-ALL, NOS (WHO-HAEM5/ICC) reference group, and transplant impact was evaluated relative to patients who did not undergo transplant. 95% CI, 95% confidence interval. 
To assess the independent prognostic impact of molecular subtype, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model for OS was constructed with adjustment for sex, age (continuous), presenting white blood cell count (log 10 -transformed), central nervous system involvement, treatment backbone, postinduction MRD status, and transplantation as a time-relevant intervention. HSCT status was modeled as a 3-level variable, specifically no HSCT (n = 118), HSCT in first CR (CR1-HSCT; n = 62), and HSCT in ≥CR2 or refractory disease (≥CR2-HSCT; n = 185). 
In the fully adjusted model, molecular subtype remained a highly significant independent predictor of OS ( Figure 6 G; supplemental Table 2 ; overall P < .001). After accounting for the strong protective effect of allo-HSCT (HR, 0.264 for CR1-HSCT; HR, 0.308 for ≥CR2-HSCT; both P < .001) and the adverse impact of increasing age (HR, 1.035 per year; P < .001), HLF -r and MEF2D -r emerged as the strongest molecular predictors of inferior survival with HRs of 3.610 ( P = .006) and 2.992 ( P < .001), respectively. 
Notably, although some subtypes, such as PAX5 alt, showed a survival benefit from allo-HSCT in univariable analyses ( supplemental Figure 6 ), this effect was attenuated after multivariable adjustment ( P = .405), suggesting that their adverse biologic risk can be substantially mitigated by appropriate therapeutic intervention. 
Discussion 
This study provides a large, real-world molecular reclassification of 1015 B-ALL patients using the updated WHO-HAEM5 and ICC frameworks based on integrated genomic profiling, including fusion detection, mutation analysis, cytogenetics, and transcriptomics. Application of these approaches reduced the proportion of unclassified B-ALL from 41.9% (WHO-HAEM4R) to 15.9% and 11.9% under WHO-HAEM5 and ICC, respectively. Beyond the improved diagnostic resolution, the refined classification uncovered distinct clinical, prognostic, therapeutic, and age-related characteristics across the newly defined molecular subtypes. 
Our results highlight the marked clinical heterogeneity and prognostic significance of the newly defined molecular subtypes. Multivariable analyses confirmed genomic subtype as an independent predictor of survival beyond age, MRD status, and transplantation. Notably, HLF -r and MEF2D -r retained high HRs even after adjustment for CR1-HSCT, indicating intrinsic resistance to conventional therapy and graft-versus-leukemia effects and identifying an ultrahigh–risk group that requires early incorporation of targeted or novel therapies. In contrast, PAX5 alt illustrates how therapeutic intervention can modify genetic risk. The survival benefit observed after HSCT, together with its attenuated significance in multivariable analysis, supports PAX5 alt as a transplant-sensitive subtype and suggests that early allo-HSCT should be prioritized in patients with PAX5 alt to optimize outcomes. 
An unexpected observation in our cohort was the relatively poor RFS among pediatric patients, including those with DUX4 -rearranged B-ALL, which is generally associated with favorable outcomes in contemporary pediatric studies. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, the prognostic cohort was restricted to patients initially classified as B-ALL, NOS under WHO-HAEM4R rather than the entire pediatric B-ALL population, thereby excluding several well-established favorable subtypes. Second, as a tertiary referral center for leukemia, our institution may receive a higher proportion of high-risk or complex patients. Third, the limited numbers of individual molecular subtypes, including DUX4 -rearranged B-ALL, may lead to variability in the survival estimates. Consequently, these outcomes should be interpreted primarily in the context of classification refinement rather than as population-level survival estimates. 
The incorporation of gene expression profiling enabled the identification of cryptic or poorly defined subtypes, such as ETV6 :: RUNX1 -like and ZNF384 -rearranged-like B-ALL, which closely resemble their canonical counterparts. These findings reinforce the essential role of transcriptomic analysis in contemporary B-ALL diagnostics by resolving ambiguous cases and refining subtype definitions, supporting routine incorporation of WTS into clinical workflows. 
When compared with conventional cytogenetics or targeted sequencing panels, WTS provides a comprehensive view of the molecular landscape of B-ALL, capturing fusion genes, cryptic rearrangements, and subtype-defining expression signatures in a single assay. This capability is particularly valuable in diagnostically complex patients with overlapping genetic features, such as concurrent aneuploidy and fusion events in which clustering-based approaches enable assignment to the dominant molecular subtype and thus accurate classification and risk stratification. 
The relationship between CRLF2 overexpression and BCR :: ABL1 -like B-ALL further illustrates the molecular heterogeneity of the disease. Among 23 CRLF2 -high patients, 1 was classified as BCR :: ABL1 -positive B-ALL and another as high hyperdiploidy based on cytogenetics. Of the remaining patients, most clustered with the BCR :: ABL1 -like group and frequently harbored JAK - STAT pathway mutations, consistent with kinase-driven signaling as a hallmark of this entity. 18 In contrast, non– BCR :: ABL1 -like CRLF2 -high patients showed a substantially lower rate of kinase alterations. Consistent with previous reports, 19 these data indicate that CRLF2 overexpression alone is insufficient to define BCR :: ABL1 -like B-ALL and point to alternative leukemogenic mechanisms in CRLF2 -high, non– BCR :: ABL1 -like patients. This limits single-marker diagnostics and supports integrated molecular profiling to guide targeted therapeutic strategies. 
Study limitations should be acknowledged. This single-center cohort may limit the detection of extremely rare B-ALL subtypes. Accordingly, entities with a very low population incidence, such as IGH :: IL3 B-ALL, 20 were not identified, likely reflecting both their rarity and the demographic characteristics of the study population. Larger multicenter studies will be required to more fully define the spectrum and biologic features of these uncommon entities. Another limitation is evolving therapeutic standards. Most patients in this cohort received chemotherapy-based regimens. As modern pediatric ALL protocols increasingly incorporate immunotherapies, such as blinatumomab, the prognostic impact of specific genomic subtypes observed here may require reassessment in the context of contemporary immunotherapy. 
The inclusion of provisional entities in the ICC reflects efforts to accommodate emerging subtypes but also raises questions regarding their biologic independence and clinical relevance. The proposed ZEB2 H1038R/ IGH :: CEBPE subtype is based on limited evidence, comprising 31 reported patients across 2 studies. 12 , 21 Notably, many patients in both reports harbored additional subtype-defining alterations, and prognostic data were incomplete. Consistent with these observations, 7 of 20 ZEB2 H1038R-positive patients in our cohort carried concurrent defining lesions ( supplemental Table 3 ), suggesting that H1038R may frequently represent a secondary event. Both studies also described recurrent ZEB2 Q1072 mutations (Q1072R/Q1072K), which were not incorporated into the current classification schemes. In our cohort, all patients with ZEB2 Q1072 mutations relapsed as opposed to a 76% relapse rate among ZEB2 H1038R-positive patients with evaluable follow-up. These findings highlight the adverse prognostic association of ZEB2 Q1072 mutations and raise the question of whether they should be incorporated into future classifications. Larger studies will be required to determine whether ZEB2 Q1072 and H1038R define a unified ZEB2 -mutated category or represent distinct biologic subgroups. 
Some low-frequency subtypes may also merit reevaluation. Although NUTM1 -rearranged B-ALL is generally associated with favorable outcomes, 22 , 23 patients in our cohort showed unexpectedly aggressive behavior. Both a pediatric patient with ZNF618 :: NUTM1 24 and an adult patient with CUX1 :: NUTM1 25 experienced early relapse, suggesting potential biologic heterogeneity. Larger multicenter studies will be required to clarify risk stratification and optimize the management of rare NUTM1 -rearranged patients. 
Several molecularly distinct B-ALL subsets with the potential for independent classification remain unrecognized in the current guidelines. Among them, IDH1 / 2 -mutated B-ALL emerged as a coherent subgroup in our cohort. All patients were adults, harbored clonal IDH1 R132 or IDH2 R140 mutations, and lacked alternative subtype-defining lesions. Consistent with previous mechanistic studies, these mutations are linked to hypermethylation and mitochondrial pathway dysregulation that contribute to leukemogenesis. 13 , 14 This subgroup exhibited the poorest OS, and no clear benefit from allo-HSCT was observed ( supplemental Figure 7 ), although the limited sample size precludes definitive conclusions. Collectively, the molecular uniformity, adult predilection, and adverse prognosis suggest that IDH1 / 2 -mutated B-ALL may represent a candidate independent entity and support the exploration of targeted approaches, such as IDH inhibition. 
In summary, this study demonstrates that updated classification frameworks, integrated with comprehensive molecular diagnostics, are reshaping the classification and clinical management of B-ALL. By reducing the diagnostic ambiguity, refining risk stratification, and enabling subtype-informed therapeutic decisions, our findings support a shift toward transcriptome-integrated precision oncology. The ongoing validation of rare subtypes, refinement of provisional entities, and identification of novel high-risk categories, together with broader implementation of multiomic profiling and targeted therapies, will be essential for advancing personalized care in B-ALL.
