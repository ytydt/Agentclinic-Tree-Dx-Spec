Key Points 
• 
In patients with relapsed/refractory RT, rapid and durable CRs to mosunetuzumab were observed, with potential for long-term treatment-free remission. 
• 
Mosunetuzumab exhibits a favorable safety profile in relapsed/refractory RT.

Abstract 
Richter transformation (RT) to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma is an aggressive lymphoma that arises from underlying chronic lymphocytic leukemia or small lymphocytic lymphoma. RT is often chemorefractory, resulting in poor clinical outcomes with standard chemoimmunotherapy. Mosunetuzumab, a bispecific CD20/CD3 T-cell–engaging antibody, was investigated in a cohort of 20 patients with relapsed/refractory RT. Cytokine release syndrome occurred in 65% of patients, almost exclusively grade 1 (20%) or grade 2 (40%), and occurring during the first treatment cycle. Other adverse events included infections, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, tumor flare, and low-grade neurotoxicity, with no adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation. Mosunetuzumab resulted in an overall response rate of 40% and a complete response (CR) rate of 20%. CRs were durable, with 2 patients experiencing CR for >20 months without further therapy, and 2 were able to proceed to allogeneic stem cell transplant while in CR, with no subsequent relapse. Median progression-free survival and overall survival were 3.4 and 10.2 months, respectively. Given the favorable toxicity profile of mosunetuzumab and rapid and durable CRs observed in this cohort, further investigation of mosunetuzumab for the treatment of RT, as monotherapy and in combination with other novel agents or chemotherapy, is warranted. This trial was registered at www.ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT02500407 . 
Introduction 
Richter transformation (RT) to aggressive lymphoma occurs in 2% to 10% of patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) or small lymphocytic lymphoma, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 with an annual transformation rate of 0.5% to 1%. 1 , 5 Most patients transform to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), whereas a few transform to Hodgkin lymphoma. 6 Clinical outcomes following the development of clonally related RT remain dismal, with a median overall survival (OS) of only 8 to 12 months after RT diagnosis. 4 , 7 , 8 , 9 In the novel agent era, outcomes have improved slightly but remain poor, with median survival still <20 months. 10 , 11 , 12 Despite suboptimal response rates of 40% to 60%, with only 5% to 15% complete response (CR) rates to R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisolone), this regimen remains the most frequently used therapeutic standard, and intensified chemotherapeutic regimens have not yielded superior response rates. 10 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Primary relapsed/refractory (R/R) disease is frequent, and consolidation of CR with autologous or allogeneic stem cell transplant is generally recommended for patients fit to receive this therapy. However, only few patients are suitable for this treatment after receiving first-line therapy due to chemorefractory disease, comorbidities, or advanced age. Response rates to chemotherapy for primary R/R disease are dismal, and effective novel therapies are urgently needed to treat RT in the R/R setting. Investigation of incorporating novel agents into initial therapy is also warranted to improve outcomes following first-line therapy. 
Mosunetuzumab is a first-in-class, humanized immunoglobulin G1–based CD20×CD3 T-cell–engaging bispecific antibody that redirects T cells to engage and eliminate malignant B cells. 17 , 18 , 19 It has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency for the treatment of follicular lymphoma (FL) after ≥2 prior lines of therapies. 20 , 21 
The phase 1/2 dose-escalation study ( ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02500407 ) established that mosunetuzumab, administered IV in a weekly step-up dosing schedule during cycle 1, followed by fixed dosing every 3 weeks, was well tolerated and active, including durable CRs, in a range of R/R non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) histologic subtypes, including RT. 19 Based on the dose-escalation study, a single-arm expansion cohort of patients with R/R RT who had received ≥1 prior line of therapy was enrolled to explore the efficacy and safety of mosunetuzumab in this population with high unmet needs. Here, we report results from the primary analysis of the RT expansion cohort. 
Methods 
Patients 
Patients with DLBCL-type RT who were R/R after ≥1 prior line of therapy were eligible for inclusion in this single-arm phase 2 expansion cohort. Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years, had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 to 1, and had received prior anthracycline and anti-CD20–directed therapy. Patients with a circulating lymphocyte count of ≥5 x10 9 /L were excluded. Documentation of clonal relationship to underlying CLL was not required for inclusion. Full eligibility criteria are provided in the supplemental Methods . 
Study design 
The study design has been described in detail previously. 19 In this ongoing phase 1/2, multicenter, open-label study for patients with R/R NHL, mosunetuzumab is administered IV in 21-day cycles, with weekly step-up dosing during cycle 1 to mitigate the risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS): 1 mg on cycle 1 day 1, 2 mg on cycle 1 day 8, loading doses of 60 mg on cycle 1 day 15 and cycle 2 day 1, and a target dose of 30 mg on day 1 of cycle 3 and subsequent cycles. Patients received premedication with acetaminophen and antihistamine, along with corticosteroid (dexamethasone 20 mg IV or methylprednisolone 80 mg IV) 1 hour before administration of each dose of mosunetuzumab. Use of corticosteroid premedication is optional for cycle 3 and subsequent cycles, provided no CRS was observed at the previous dose. Treatment is administered for a fixed duration, which was 8 cycles for patients in CR after cycle 8, and 17 cycles for patients with partial response (PR) or stable disease after cycle 8. Patients with emergent progressive disease (PD) or unacceptable toxicity discontinued therapy as indicated before these timepoints. 
Hospitalization for administration was at investigator discretion and was not mandated. Antimicrobial prophylaxis and supportive care (eg, immunoglobulin replacement) was permitted per institutional standards. 
The study protocol was reviewed and approved by institutional review boards at each participating center, and the trial was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, International Conference on Harmonization Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and other applicable laws and regulations. Written informed consent was provided by all study participants before any study procedures. 
Study end points and assessments 
The primary end point for the RT expansion cohort was the safety and tolerability of treatment with mosunetuzumab, assessed by the frequency, nature, and severity of adverse events (AE) in all patients who had received at least 1 dose of mosunetuzumab (safety-evaluable population). AE were reported using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0 (2009). CRS was reported according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy criteria. 22 
Efficacy was a secondary end point, assessed for all enrolled patients (efficacy-evaluable population). Efficacy end points included CR rate, objective response rate (ORR; including CR and PR), duration of response (DOR; time from initial PR or CR until PD or death), duration of CR (DOCR; time from first CR to progression of disease or death), progression-free survival (PFS; time from first dose of study treatment until first occurrence of PD or death), and OS (time from first dose of study treatment until death), all assessed by investigators according to the Cheson 2007 criteria. 23 Patients with missing or no response assessments were classified as nonresponders. Tumor assessments were performed with computed tomography (CT) and positron emission tomography–CT scans at screening and during the study treatment phase. During follow-up, CT with or without positron emission tomography was performed. The imaging schedule is described in the supplemental Methods . Documentation of CD20 expression was not mandated for inclusion. In other study cohorts in the NCT02500407 trial, CD20 expression was retrospectively evaluated centrally to determine its potential association with treatment response, 24 but this was not performed in the RT expansion cohort. 
Statistical analysis 
The CR rate was estimated, along with Clopper-Pearson exact 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In total, ∼20 patients were planned for enrollment in this RT expansion cohort. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate PFS, OS, DOR, and DOCR. The Greenwood formula was used to calculate 95% CIs for PFS and OS. Data analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4). 
Results 
Patients 
A total of 20 patients with R/R RT were enrolled between March 2020 and April 2022. Baseline demographics are shown in Table 1 . Of 20 patients, 12 patients (60%) had confirmed marrow involvement by RT, with 8 (40%) having bulky disease. Clonal relationship status with the underlying CLL was not universally tested and is not available. Patients had received a median of 1 prior line of therapy for CLL before transformation (range, 0-9), and a median of 1 line of therapy for RT (range, 0-4), with some patients commencing RT therapy before the date of confirmed transformation. All patients had received prior anthracycline and anti-CD20–directed therapy, 9 patients (45%) received a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor, 10 (50%) venetoclax, and 1 patient (5%) received chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy. Most patients (16/20 [80%] and 15/20 [75%]) were refractory to their most recent prior therapy and prior anti-CD20–directed therapy, respectively. Baseline molecular characteristics of the underlying CLL and RT (eg, TP53 mutation status, del17p status, IGHV mutation status) are not available.

At the data cutoff on 2 May 2023, the median time on study for enrolled patients was 8.7 months (range, 1-37), with 7 patients (35%) remaining in study follow-up. Three patients (15%) had completed study treatment, and 17 (85%) had discontinued therapy; 14 patients (70%) for PD, 2 patients (10%) per physician decision (1 due to COVID-19 infection and 1 for elective consolidative allogeneic stem cell transplant), and 1 (5%) due to patient withdrawal. Patients received a median number of treatment cycles of 4.5 (range, 1-8), with 16 (80%) receiving <8 cycles, and 4 (20%) receiving 8 cycles. 
Safety 
All patients experienced at least 1 AE, with 19 patients (95%) experiencing an AE related to mosunetuzumab. Sixteen patients (80%) experienced at least 1 grade 3/4 AE, with 9 patients (45%) related to mosunetuzumab. Furthermore, 17 patients (85%) experienced a serious AE, with 11 patients (55%) related to mosunetuzumab. Excluding deaths from PD, there were no grade 5 (fatal) AE, and no AE leading to treatment discontinuation. A summary of AE occurring in ≥15% of patients is shown in Figure 1 . The most frequent mosunetuzumab-related AE were CRS (65%), infection (35%), fatigue (25%), hypophosphatemia (25%), pyrexia (25%), abdominal pain (20%), and elevation of liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase and/or alanine aminotransferase; 15% and 20% respectively).

Of 12 patients with confirmed marrow involvement, 10 had CRS. The most frequent occurrence of CRS was after administration of the first 60 mg dose at cycle 1 day 15, occurring in 50% of patients. CRS events were observed almost exclusively during cycle 1, with a single grade 1 CRS event observed at cycle 2 dosing, and none in subsequent cycles. The patient with grade 3 CRS required admission to the intensive care unit for vasopressor and oxygen support, in addition to tocilizumab and corticosteroids. Immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome was reported in 1 patient (grade 1; duration 3 days), and confusional state (grade 1, duration 2 days) was reported in 1 further patient experiencing concurrent CRS. There were no grade 3 or 4 neurological events. Other AE of interest included neutropenia (n = 3 [15%]; all grade 3/4), thrombocytopenia (n = 4 [20%]; n = 2 grade 3/4), tumor flare (n = 2 [10%]; both grade 3 and manifesting as progressive symptomatic lymphadenopathy during cycle 1, later resolved). There were no episodes of febrile neutropenia. In addition, 7 serious infections (n = 1, grade 2; n = 4, grade 3; and n = 1, grade 4) were reported in 5 patients, and included COVID-19/COVID-19 pneumonia (n = 1, grade 2; n = 1, grade 4), cellulitis (n = 1, grade 3), lower respiratory tract infection (n = 1, grade 3), pneumonia (n = 1, grade 3), staphylococcal bacteremia (n = 1, grade 3), and urinary tract infection (n = 1, grade 3). Details of infective prophylaxis administered are not available, but among the 3 patients with neutropenia, 1 patient received granulocyte colony-stimulating factor treatment, with neutropenia resolving. 
Efficacy 
The best ORR was 40% (8/20), with a CR observed in 20% patients (4/20). A further 20% of patients (4/20) exhibited stable disease as their best response, with 40% having PD (8/20). Responses occurred early, with a median time to first response of 1.4 months (range, 1.1-2.8), which corresponds to the first 2 to 4 cycles (6-12 weeks) of treatment. Treatment response and durability for all enrolled patients is shown in Figure 2 . Four patients attained a CR; 2 at the first response assessment and 2 with initial PR and subsequent conversion to CR by 6 months. CRs were durable, with 2 of 4 patients having CR lasting >20 months (DOCR of 21.2 months in 1 patient, and 34.2 months in the second patient with CR ongoing). The remaining 2 patients with CR received an elective consolidative allogeneic stem cell transplant in CR and, at data cutoff, remained alive in CR. Of the 4 patients achieving a CR, 2 had bulky disease at study entry and 2 had nonbulky disease. In addition, 3 patients had received no treatment for CLL before development of RT, and 1 had received 3 CLL-directed therapies before transformation. All had received 1 RT-directed therapy (R-CHOP or dose-adjusted etoposide, prednisolone, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and rituximab).

Discussion 
The development of RT is associated with poor clinical outcomes and has limited available effective treatment options. In this single-arm expansion cohort of the phase 1/2 study investigating treatment with mosunetuzumab in R/R NHL, mosunetuzumab exhibited a favorable risk–benefit profile in a pretreated, refractory cohort of patients with RT. With an ORR of 40%, including 4 patients with durable CR, fixed-duration treatment with mosunetuzumab represents a potentially effective, off-the-shelf therapeutic strategy for some patients with this challenging, chemorefractory disease. 
Time to first response was rapid and no patients experienced a first response beyond 3 months. All patients with PR had a short DOR. Because RT is often an aggressive and rapidly PD, off-the-shelf treatment and rapid disease response are of significant value. With responses occurring early, identification of patients who are not likely to derive benefit from ongoing therapy is possible, providing an opportunity to seek and initiate appropriate alternative therapy at an early time point. Although 2 of 6 patients with initial PR later converted to CR, 4 of 6 experienced subsequent disease progression. The risks and benefits of continuing treatment with mosunetuzumab after initial PR should be carefully weighed, and options for potential alternative therapies explored. 
The subset of patients achieving CR (4/20 [20%]) derived substantial and durable benefit from treatment, with long-term disease-free survival, 1 with response duration of 21.2 months, and 1 ongoing at 34.2 months. Given the typically aggressive biology and refractory behavior of RT, these durable CRs are extremely encouraging. Mosunetuzumab’s fixed-duration dosing schedule means that these patients were both lymphoma-free and treatment-free for many months. 
Notably, 2 patients (10%) were able to receive a consolidative allogeneic stem cell transplant in CR after treatment with mosunetuzumab and remained alive and disease-free at data cutoff. Because of the high rates of RT recurrence and expected poor disease response with subsequent therapies, consolidative allogeneic stem cell transplantation is generally recommended for suitable patients in CR following first-line therapy 25 ; however, many patients are not eligible because of low rates of CR. Despite favorable outcomes for these 2 patients, the role of consolidative therapy in patients who achieve CR on mosunetuzumab therapy remains unclear, because additional 2 patients maintained a durable CR without consolidative allogeneic stem cell transplantation. The role of allograft should ideally be investigated in prospective studies, but the rarity of RT makes this challenging in practice. Other ongoing studies with bispecific antibodies in R/R RT may help to address this question. 
The predominantly low-grade AE profile of mosunetuzumab largely mirrored the safety profile observed in the dose-escalation study and expansion cohorts in other NHL histologies. 24 , 26 , 27 CRS was observed more frequently in this RT cohort (65.0%; 5% grade 3) than in other mosunetuzumab study cohorts (DLBCL expansion cohort CRS, 26.1% 24 ; FL expansion cohort, 44.4% 27 ). Noting the small numbers of patients with RT treated with investigational bispecific antibodies to date, rates of CRS in RT treated with mosunetuzumab (including high grade CRS) appear comparable to epcoritamab (N = 35; CRS, 80%; 3% grade 3) 28 and glofitamab (N = 11; CRS, 73%; 9% grade 3). 29 The higher rate of CRS observed with these agents in RT compared with other histologies likely reflects the high frequency of marrow involvement and bulky disease in this disease entity, which are established risk factors for CRS development. 30 Although the trial populations cannot be directly compared, the overall safety profile in the mosunetuzumab RT cohort also appears favorable compared with the epcoritamab-treated cohort, in which 2 patients discontinued treatment because of AE and 3 patients experienced fatal AE with general deterioration (n = 2) and sepsis (n = 1). 28 This is despite the mosunetuzumab cohort being more heavily pretreated, with a median number of prior therapies of 2.5 (range, 0-4) compared with 51% of epcoritamab patients having received no prior RT-directed therapy. 28 There were no fatal AE related to glofitamab, nor AE leading to drug discontinuation in the glofitamab-treated cohort, whose patients were also heavily pretreated, with a median number of prior therapies of 3 (range, 1-4). 29 
Epcoritamab and glofitamab remain under active investigation in RT and have demonstrated promising efficacy in early studies. 28 , 29 Epcoritamab administered as indefinite therapy is being investigated in 35 patients with RT (treatment naïve and R/R) in the EPCORE-CLL-1 study ( ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04623541 ), with preliminary ORR of 60% and CR rate of 50%, with 53% of these remaining in CR after 9 months. 28 In a cohort of 11 patients with R/R RT in a phase 1 dose-escalation study ( ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03075696 ), glofitamab produced an ORR of 63.6%, with CR observed in 45.5% of patients and 80% of CR being durable beyond 33 months. 31 These 3 agents (mosunetuzumab, epcoritamab, and glofitamab) vary in administration and dosing schedule, with epcoritamab administered subcutaneously as indefinite therapy, and mosunetuzumab and glofitamab administered IV as fixed-duration therapy. Because bispecific antibodies are potentially incorporated into future clinical practice, careful consideration of the balance of potential efficacy, toxicity profile, and dosing schedule will be required for each individual patient. 
Although the efficacy of mosunetuzumab is promising, a larger cohort is required to have a greater understanding of the overall response rates and durability of treatment in RT. Important disease factors that were not collected, which may be relevant to the occurrence of AE and treatment efficacy, including unknown biological risk factors for underlying CLL (eg. TP53 mutation status, del17p status). Clonal relatedness of RT to the underlying CLL is well documented to influence outcomes in RT but was not collected in this study. Patients with lymphocytosis (which is frequently present in those with RT due to underlying CLL) were excluded from enrollment, which may limit the applicability of study results to a broader RT population and may restrict the therapeutic use of bispecific antibodies in RT. The absence of documented CD20 expression may mean that some patients without target antigen expression were included, which may have affected treatment efficacy, although efficacy of CD20-targeting bispecific antibodies have previously observed even at very low levels of CD20 expression. Interpretation of the causative role of mosunetuzumab in the development of infective AE is challenging in the absence of data detailing supportive care, including infective prophylaxis, immunoglobulin levels before, during, and after treatment, and the use of prophylactic immunoglobulin replacement therapy. Infectious AE may be expected to be higher in the RT cohort because of the profound underlying immune dysregulation inherent to CLL, and enhanced infectious prophylaxis may be warranted when administering bispecific antibody treatment in this disease entity. Notably the nonserious infectious AE in this cohort (eg. pharyngitis, conjunctivitis) would have been unlikely to be mitigated by standard antimicrobial prophylaxis. 
Time-limited therapy provides an important opportunity for immune reconstitution, in particular B-cell recovery, and is anticipated to reduce long-term risk of recurrent infections. Although B-cell recovery data following treatment completion or discontinuation were not analyzed in this RT cohort, the trajectory and characteristics of B-cell recovery after mosunetuzumab treatment in 74 patients with FL have been recently reported. 27 For patients receiving 8 cycles of mosunetuzumab, the median time to B-cell recovery was 18.4 months (95% CI, 12.8-25.0), and 8.9% patients received immunoglobulin replacement. Most severe infections in that cohort occurred in the first 4 cycles of mosunetuzumab treatment. For patients with a durable CR to a fixed course of mosunetuzumab, this suggests that long-term immune recovery is achievable without further antilymphoma therapy, although caution should be exercised in extrapolating this expectation to patients with RT, who may have inherent differences in immune reconstitution due to underlying CLL. In light of the anticipated prolonged B-cell recovery and occurrence of infections, prophylactic immunoglobulin replacement may be prudent. 
This analysis of an expansion cohort of 20 patients with R/R RT demonstrates efficacy of mosunetuzumab in a chemorefractory disease with almost universally poor outcomes. Rapid and durable CRs were observed with treatment, with the potential for long-term treatment-free remission and the opportunity to consider curative intent consolidative allogeneic stem cell therapy. CRS and grade 3 and 4 serious infections occurred more frequently than in other previously reported B-NHL subtypes treated with mosunetuzumab, likely reflecting the different disease biology and immune dysregulation inherent to RT and CLL. Further investigation of mosunetuzumab for RT, as monotherapy and in combination with other novel agents or chemotherapy to enhance efficacy, is warranted. Incorporation of mosunetuzumab into first-line therapy with CHOP chemotherapy is currently being investigated in a phase 2 study, with mosunetuzumab with CHOP administered for 6 cycles, followed by mosunetuzumab monotherapy administered as maintenance for up to 17 cycles ( ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06521996). Rational combinations with other agents anticipated to be synergistic and well tolerated, such as Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors, B-cell lymphoma 2 inhibitors, programmed cell death-1 inhibitors, and antibody-drug conjugates, may be effective, and studies investigating a variety of novel combinations with bispecific antibodies, including mosunetuzumab, are ongoing.
