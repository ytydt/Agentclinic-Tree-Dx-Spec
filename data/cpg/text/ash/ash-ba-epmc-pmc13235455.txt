Abstract 
There is currently no consensus on the optimal treatment of early myelofibrosis (MF). Ropeginterferon alfa-2b (ropeg) is a next-generation monopegylated interferon alfa-2b developed specifically to treat myeloproliferative neoplasms. We conducted a phase 2 study in patients with prefibrotic primary MF (pre-PMF) and Dynamic International Prognostic Scoring System low-/intermediate-1–risk fibrotic PMF or secondary MF (SMF) who required cytoreduction. Ongoing treatment with hydroxyurea was substituted with ropeg (week 0, 250 μg; week 2, 350 μg; week 4 onward, 500 μg every 2 weeks). The primary end points were safety and clinicohematologic complete response (CHCR). Seventy-one patients (40 men and 31 women) with a median age of 60 years (range, 31-86) were enrolled and followed up for a median of 119 weeks (range, 10-131). At weeks 24, 48, and 104, CHCR was achieved in 63.8% (pre-PMF, 74%; fibrotic PMF, 20%; fibrotic SMF, 42.9%), 63.5% (pre-PMF, 69.6%; fibrotic PMF, 25%; SMF, 53.9%), and 70% of patients (pre-PMF, 78.4%; fibrotic PMF, 33.3%; SMF, 50%), respectively. Thrombo-hemorrhagic events were not observed after stopping hydroxyurea. At weeks 24, 48, and 104, reduction in JAK2 V617F variant allele frequency (VAF) was observed in 53.1%, 70%, and 67.6% of patients, and reduction in CALR mutant VAF was observed in 52.6%, 42.9%, and 40% of patients, respectively. In 50 evaluable cases at week 104, 13 patients (26%) had reduced bone marrow reticulin fibrosis. There were 3 treatment discontinuations (4.2%) due to adverse events. In conclusion, ropeg was safe and induced CHCR associated with significant molecular responses in patients with early MF. This trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as NCT04988815 . 
Introduction 
Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are neoplasms caused by mutations in hematopoietic stem cells, which result in constitutively activated signal-transduction pathways involved in hematopoiesis. The classical Philadelphia chromosome–negative MPNs include polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF). PMF is characterized by bone marrow reticulin fibrosis, cytokine-mediated systemic symptoms, anemia, hepatosplenomegaly, and a propensity for progression to acute myeloid leukemia (AML). PMF is subclassified into prefibrotic and fibrotic stages. 1 Patients with PV and ET may progress to secondary myelofibrosis (SMF) over time, with manifestations similar to those of fibrotic PMF. The major clinical problems in MF include symptom burden, risk of disease acceleration or blastic transformation, and reduced survivals. Current management of MF targets symptoms and splenomegaly. In this respect, available JAK inhibitors, including ruxolitinib, fedratinib, momelotinib, and pacritinib, alleviate symptoms and reduce splenomegaly but do not significantly improve long-term outcomes. Moreover, they are only indicated for symptomatic and high-risk patients. Other than allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, which may be curative for eligible patients, there is a lack of options that may affect the natural course of MF. 2 
For patients with prefibrotic PMF (pre-PMF) and lower-risk fibrotic PMF and SMF, there is presently no consensus on optimal treatment options. Pre-PMF is associated with a high frequency of thrombo-hemorrhagic complications. 3 Thrombocytosis, associated with thrombo-hemorrhagic complications, is present in ∼84% of patients with pre-PMF at diagnosis. 3 , 4 Furthermore, leukocytosis is significantly associated with increased risk of arterial and venous thrombotic events and portends inferior survivals in pre-PMF. 4 , 5 Finally, ∼15% of pre-PMF progresses to advanced fibrotic MF after a median of 6.7 years. 6 Hence, the median overall survival of pre-PMF is curtailed to 14.7 years, with an accumulative incidence of transformation to AML of 7% at 5 years. 6 , 7 
Hydroxyurea is one of the agents used in MF for cytoreduction to decrease thrombo-hemorrhagic complications. However, it has no specific action on neoplastic hematopoietic stem cells and does not induce durable hematologic responses or improve progression-free survival and event-free survival. 8 , 9 In pre-PMF, the use of hydroxyurea is limited by the associated risks of anemia, leukemogenicity, and nonmelanoma skin cancers. 8 , 9 , 10 
Conventional interferon alfa (IFN-α) is associated with favorable hematologic and molecular responses in PV and ET. 11 , 12 Earlier studies indicated that in ∼39% of patients, pegylated IFN-α might retard the progression of early PMF and achieve complete response and transfusion independence. 13 , 14 In Dynamic International Prognostic Scoring System (DIPSS) low- or intermediate-1–risk PMF, treatment with IFN-α was observed to be effective in preventing or decreasing splenomegaly, anemia, and progression to more advanced MF. 15 IFN-α might also improve bone marrow fibrosis in association with clinical response. 16 In pre-PMF and low-risk fibrotic PMF and SMF, however, there is a lack of prospective clinical trials demonstrating the safety and efficacy of an IFN-α–based strategy. 
Ropeginterferon alfa-2b (ropeg) is a novel pegylated IFN-α with an improved pharmacokinetic profile. 17 , 18 It is approved for the treatment of adult patients with PV and is currently under development for ET. 19 In PV, ropeg induced durable hematologic and molecular responses and improved event-free survival 9 , 20 , 21 without resulting in clinically significant anemia. 22 
Unresolved issues in the treatment of pre-PMF and low/intermediate-1–risk fibrotic PMF and SMF include a lack of prospective studies; uncertainties whether early therapy may improve symptoms, reduce spleen size, and decrease complications; relevance of molecular responses; and impact on survivals. 
To address these issues, we conducted a prospective phase 2 study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of ropeg in pre-PMF and DIPSS low/intermediate-1–risk fibrotic PMF and SMF, specifically in patients requiring cytoreduction. 
Methods 
Trial design 
This open-label phase 2 study enrolled patients aged ≥18 years with pre-PMF or DIPSS low-/intermediate-1–risk fibrotic PMF/SMF. The key inclusion criterion was an indication for cytoreduction, which included disease-related symptoms, thrombocytosis, and/or leukocytosis at the initial diagnosis of MF. Other inclusion and exclusion criteria were stated in the trial protocol ( supplemental File 1 ). Enrolled patients received ropeg subcutaneously every 2 weeks at a starting dose of 250 μg at week 0; 350 μg at week 2; and then 500 μg from week 4 onward until week 104. There was no washout from prior cytoreduction. Low-dose aspirin was continued. In responding patients, treatment was continued from week 104 until disease progression or the development of intolerable toxicities. Dose adjustment was allowed according to changes in blood counts, safety, and tolerability, as assessed by the attending physician ( supplemental File 1 ). The trial protocol and all amendments were approved by the institutional review board of The University of Hong Kong (reference number UW 21-106). The study was conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT04988815 ). 
Procedures and assessments 
The first dose of ropeg was administered on the first day of week 0. Patients were followed up every 2 weeks until week 12, every 4 weeks until week 52, every 8 weeks until week 104, and every 12 weeks thereafter. Assessments during each treatment and follow-up visit included spleen length by palpation (centimeter below left costal margin along the Gardner line); complete blood count with machine and manual differential count; liver and renal function tests; calcium and phosphate; random glucose; lactate dehydrogenase; uric acid; and thyroid function test. Bone marrow aspiration and trephine biopsy was performed at baseline and weeks 24, 48, and 104. The MPN Symptom Assessment Form Total Symptom Score (MPN-SAF TSS) was assessed every 4 weeks until week 52 and every 8 weeks until week 104. Adverse events (AEs) were graded according to the Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse Events (version 5.0) and assessed at each treatment and follow-up visit. Bone marrow samples were obtained at baseline and weeks 24, 48, and 104 for histopathologic evaluation and determination of variant allele frequencies (VAF) of mutated genes with next-generation sequencing targeting a myeloid gene panel 23 ( supplemental File 2 ). Additionally, peripheral blood was obtained every 4 weeks during the first 104 weeks and every 24 weeks thereafter for a more dynamic determination of VAF of specific mutant driver genes, including JAK2 V617F and CALR type 1 or type 2 mutations, with droplet digital polymerase chain reaction developed in-house (experimental details in supplemental File 3 ). Bone marrow fibrosis was assessed by an independent hematopathologist who was blinded to the treatment details. 
End points 
Hematologic responses were defined as hemoglobin (Hb) response (≥10 g/dL); white blood cell count (WBC) response (<10 × 10 9 /L); and platelet count response (<400 × 10 9 /L). The primary end points were clinicohematologic complete response (CHCR), defined as achieving Hb, WBC, and platelet responses associated with absent disease-related symptoms, as well as absent palpable splenomegaly, sustained for ≥12 consecutive weeks, 24 , 25 , 26 and safety. Secondary end points included hematologic responses, mutant VAF in peripheral blood and bone marrow, MPN-SAF TSS, spleen length, thrombotic or hemorrhagic events, and progression to accelerated or blast phase MF. All responses were assessed at weeks 24, 48, and 104. 
Statistical analysis 
Efficacy, safety, and molecular studies were conducted in all enrolled patients. Safety outcomes were analyzed for the entire treatment period. Efficacy outcomes were analyzed at weeks 24, 48, and 104. Continuous variables were enumerated by means, standard deviations, standard errors of means, medians, and ranges. Differences between continuous variables were determined using paired t tests or nonparametric tests. Categorical variables were enumerated by counts and percentages. Differences between categorical variables were determined using Pearson χ 2 test. Data were censored on 30 November 2024. 
Results 
Patients and treatment 
Between 1 December 2021 and 30 November 2023, a total of 71 patients (40 men and 31 women) with a median age of 60 years (range, 31-86) were enrolled ( Figure 1 ; Table 1 ). All patients were on cytoreduction with hydroxyurea before enrollment without washout. The diagnoses were pre-PMF (n = 51 [71.8%]), fibrotic PMF (n = 6 [8.5%]), and SMF (post-PV MF, n = 5 [7%]; post-ET MF, n = 9 [12.7%]). Fourteen patients (19.7%) had palpable splenomegaly at enrollment (≤5 cm below left costal margin, n = 7; >5 cm below left costal margin, n = 7). Mutational profiles of driver genes were JAK2 V617F (n = 48 [67.6%]), CALR mutations (n = 19 [26.8%]; type 1/type 1 like, n = 14 [19.7%]; type 2/type 2 like, n = 4 [5.6%]), and MPL mutation (n = 1 [1.4%]); and triple negative for JAK2 V617F/ CALR / MPL (n = 3 [4.1%]). High-risk mutations were present in 21 patients (29.6%; ASXL1 , n = 16 [22.5%]; EZH2 , n = 2 [2.8%]; IDH2 , n = 3 [4.2%]; SRSF2 , n = 1 [1.4%]; supplemental File 4 ). Three patients had TP53 mutations ( supplemental File 4 ). The median follow-up was 119 weeks (range, 10-131), with 69 patients (97.2%), 63 patients (88.7%), and 50 patients (70.4%) having completed 24, 48, and 104 weeks of treatment, respectively ( Figure 1 ). There were 10 discontinuations (AEs, n = 3; symptom/spleen size progression, n = 6; patient decision, n = 1). No cases of disease acceleration, AML, or thrombosis/hemorrhage were observed during the study period.

Hematologic responses 
At weeks 24, 48, and 104, CHCR was achieved in 63.8% (pre-PMF, 74%; fibrotic PMF, 20%; SMF, 42.9%), 63.5% (pre-PMF, 69.6%; fibrotic PMF, 25%; SMF, 53.9%), and 70% of patients (pre-PMF, 78.4%; fibrotic PMF, 33.3%; SMF, 50%). Hb response was achieved in 73.9% (pre-PMF, 82%; fibrotic PMF, 60%; SMF, 100%), 80.4% (pre-PMF, 80.4%; fibrotic PMF, 50%; SMF, 69.2%), and 82% (pre-PMF, 89.2%; fibrotic PMF, 66.7%; SMF, 60%); WBC response was achieved in 82.6% (pre-PMF, 86%; fibrotic PMF, 60%; SMF, 78.6%), 79.4% (pre-PMF, 80.4%; fibrotic PMF, 50%; SMF, 76.9%) and 82% of patients (pre-PMF, 86.5%; fibrotic PMF, 33.3%; SMF, 80%), respectively; and platelet response was achieved in all patients at all time points. Pre-PMF was significantly associated with CHCR at week 24 ( P = .01) and Hb response at week 24 ( P = .04). 
Hematologic responses according to characteristics at enrollment 
The rates of CHCR were also evaluated based on the characteristics at enrollment ( supplemental File 5 ). Of 69 patients evaluable for responses at week 24, a total of 55 patients were not in CHCR at enrollment, and 34 of them (62%) achieved CHCR. For 14 patients who were in CHCR at enrollment, 10 of them (71%) remained in CHCR. At week 48, a total of 63 patients were evaluable for responses, with 29 of 49 patients (59%) not in CHCR at enrollment achieving CHCR and 11 of 14 patients (79%) in CHCR at enrollment remaining in CHCR. At week 104, a total of 50 patients were evaluable for responses, with 27 of 38 patients (71%) not in CHCR at enrollment achieving CHCR and 8 of 12 patients (67%) in CHCR at enrollment remaining in CHCR. 
Clinical responses 
The mean baseline MPN-SAF TSS was 18.3 (standard deviation, 13.2). At weeks 24, 48, and 104, ≥50% reduction was achieved in 42.9%, 42.1%, and 40% of patients, respectively ( supplemental File 6 ). In patients with palpable splenomegaly below the left costal margin at enrollment, reduction in spleen length was observed in 64%, 64%, and 50% of patients at weeks 24, 48, and 104 ( supplemental File 7 ). 
Pathologic response 
Bone marrow fibrosis was evaluable for 69 patients at week 24, 63 patients at week 48, and 13 patients at week 104, with the gradings of marrow fibrosis reduced in 15%, 22%, and 26% of patients, respectively, and increased in 9%, 10%, and 8% of patients. 
Dynamic changes in mutant driver genes in the peripheral blood 
Treatment with ropeg resulted in significant reduction of JAK2 V617F and CALR mutant VAF in the peripheral blood, as assessed dynamically by 4-weekly droplet digital polymerase chain reaction ( Figure 2 ). JAK2 V617F VAF first began to show significant reductions by week 32. From then onward, at most time points, the JAK2 V617F VAF were significantly reduced. For CALR , the mean VAF was reduced significantly only at week 104.

Molecular responses in the bone marrow 
Ropeg treatment similarly led to reduction in VAF of driver and other mutated genes in the bone marrow in most patients, as assessed by next-generation sequencing at predetermined time points. At weeks 24, 48, and 104, respective reductions in VAF of JAK2 V617F were observed in 53% (≥25% reduction,17%; ≥50% reduction, 4%), 70% (≥25% reduction, 15%; ≥50% reduction, 12%), and 68% of patients (≥25% reduction, 18%; ≥50% reduction, 6%); whereas for CALR mutants, VAF was reduced in 53% (≥25% reduction, 5%), 43% (≥25% reduction, 21%), and 49% of patients (≥25% reduction, 7%; Figure 3 ). For the only patient with MPL W515A mutation, VAF remained unchanged. Reductions in VAF of other mutated genes were also observed ( supplemental Files 8 and 9 ). Notable examples ( supplemental File 9 ) included ASXL1 (5/8 [62.5%]), EZH2 (2/2 [100%]), and TET2 (4/8 [50%]). In 3 patients with TP53 mutations detected before treatment, 1 patient had clearance of the mutant from week 48 onward, whereas the VAF in the other 2 patients remained stable. On the contrary, in 6 patients who had symptom and splenomegaly progression leading to ropeg discontinuation, 2 patients had significant increases in JAK2 V617F VAF at progression, and 2 patients had new mutations at progression ( supplemental File 10 ). One patient had acquisition of ASXL1 , CUX1 , and PDGFRA mutations, and another patient had acquisition of a TP53 p .Arg248Trp mutation ( supplemental File 10 ).

Impact of driver gene molecular responses 
The impacts of changes in driver gene VAF were evaluated ( Figure 4 A-B). In pre-MF, the largest subgroup in our cohort, JAK2 V617F VAF reduction was most prominently related to hematologic responses ( Figure 4 A). At all time points, JAK2 V617F VAF reduction was significantly associated with WBC and platelet responses, as well as CHCR ( Figure 4 B). At weeks 24 and 48, JAK2 V617F VAF reduction was also significantly associated with decreased MPN-SAF TSS scores and marrow fibrosis ( Figure 4 B). There was, however, no spleen size response. JAK2 V617F VAF reduction was not associated with clinicopathologic responses in post-PV and post-ET MF, although the number of cases examined was small. In fibrotic PMF, post-PV MF, and post-ET MF, driver gene mutant VAF reductions were not associated with clinicohematologic or pathologic responses.

Tolerability and safety 
The most common nonhematologic AEs included transaminitis (grade 1-2, n = 35 [49.2%]), malaise (grade 1-2, n = 29 [40.8%]; grade 3-4, n = 1 [1.4%]), hair loss (grade 1-2, n = 24 [33.8%]), muscle pain (grade 1-2, n = 15 [21.1%]), and bone pain (grade 1-2, n = 13 [18.3%]; Table 2 ). Notably, thyroid dysfunction occurred in 8 women (10.4%; grade 1-2, n = 7 [9.9%]; grade 3-4, n = 1 [1.4%]). None of them had a personal or family history of autoimmune thyroid diseases. There was new development of antithyroid peroxidase antibodies in all cases. One patient also developed antithyrotrophin receptor antibody associated with clinical thyrotoxicosis that required carbimazole treatment 8 weeks after starting ropeg treatment. One patient had minor mood change with no overt depression. The most common hematologic AEs were anemia (grade 1-2, n = 15 [21.1%]; grade 3-4, n = 6 [8.5%]), neutropenia (grade 1-2, n = 15 [21.1%]; grade 3-4, n = 4 [5.56%]), and thrombocytopenia (grade 1-2, n = 8 [11.2%]; grade 3-4, n = 3 [4.2%]). Dose interruptions and dose reductions occurred in 13 patients (18.3%) and 58 patients (81.7%), respectively, throughout the study. The median dosage of ropeg during the study period is shown in supplemental File 11 . There were 3 treatment discontinuations (4.2%) due to AEs (pruritus, n = 1; persistent grade 2 transaminitis, n = 1; grade 3 malaise, n = 1).

Discussion 
This is to date, to our knowledge, the first prospective study evaluating ropeg in the treatment of pre-PMF and DIPSS low/intermediate-1–risk fibrotic PMF and SMF, focusing on patients who required cytoreduction. All enrolled patients were previously on hydroxyurea. Ruxolitinib was not used because it is not reimbursed for pre-PMF and DIPSS low/intermediate-1–risk fibrotic PMF and SMF in Hong Kong. Substitution with ropeg was feasible, safe, and well tolerated. Furthermore, there were improvements in all evaluated domains. Hematologically, there was a progressive increase in Hb and WBC responses; resulting in increases in CHCR in 60% to 70% of patients with time. However, in a small proportion of patients, ropeg treatment caused a fall in Hb, and the stopping of hydroxyurea resulted in a rise in WBC. Owing to these changes, in 14 patients who were in CHCR while on hydroxyurea, 3 to 4 of them had an apparent loss of CHCR at various time points ( supplemental File 5 ). Clinically, there was improvement in MPN-SAF TSS scores and reduction in splenomegaly. Pathologically, marrow fibrosis was reduced in 15% to 25% of cases. These results showed that ropeg had significant clinicopathologic advantages over hydroxyurea in prefibrotic and low-risk PMF and SMF. 
Another benefit of ropeg treatment shown in this study was the reduction of driver gene mutant VAF. A continuous reduction of JAK2 V617F VAF was shown in the peripheral blood during treatment. At weeks 48 and 104, JAK2 V617F VAF reduction in the bone marrow was found in ∼70% of patients. For CALR mutants, VAF reduction in the bone marrow was found in ∼40% of patients at weeks 48 and 104. These results were observed in all subtypes of MF. Findings were consistent with the disease-modifying potential of IFN-α shown in previous phase 2 and phase 3 studies. 8 , 12 , 21 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Other than driver genes, ropeg also decreased the VAF of coexisting ASXL1 , EZH2 , TET2 , and TP53 mutants, a phenomenon not previously reported for IFN-α. The efficacy of IFN-α in MPN is based on its antiproliferative, proapoptotic, antiangiogenic, and immunomodulatory properties. 12 , 32 , 33 , 34 IFN-α inhibited the growth and promoted apoptosis of hematopoietic cells by phosphorylation and activation of P 38 mitogen-activated protein kinase. 35 Furthermore, IFN-α suppressed the secretion of interleukins and growth factors from marrow stromal cells. 36 Molecular responses were achieved with IFN-α gradually, with a median time to response of 24 months. 37 
The relevance of reduction of driver gene mutant VAF was further evaluated. Most remarkably, JAK2 V617F VAF reduction in pre-PMF was significantly associated with WBC response, platelet response, and CHCR at all time points. Furthermore, marrow fibrosis was significantly reduced at weeks 24 and 48. These findings showed that in patients with pre-MF requiring cytoreduction, ropeg treatment led to significant improvements in hematologic and pathologic responses by suppressing driver gene mutants, which could not otherwise be achieved by hydroxyurea. However, most patients started with a low symptom burden, and splenomegaly was infrequent, so that ropeg treatment did not have an apparent positive effect on these domains. Although driver gene mutant VAF were also decreased during ropeg treatment in CALR -mutated cases and other subtypes of MF, leading to improvement in hematologic and pathologic responses, these cases were not numerous enough for meaningful statistical correlation to be made. 
Ropeg was well tolerated in MF, with low frequencies of grade 3/4 treatment-related AEs. Autoimmune and psychiatric events were carefully scrutinized. Seven patients had grade 1/2 derangement in thyroid function, and 1 patient had symptomatic thyrotoxicosis requiring treatment. Other autoimmune diseases were not observed. Depression was not observed in our patients, although 1 patient had minor mood change. Notably, although hydroxyurea was regarded to reduce vascular complications in MPN, 38 its cessation and replacement with ropeg did not result in any thrombo-embolic events. In fact, molecular responses reflecting reduction in the neoplastic hematopoietic stem cell pool had been postulated to result in decreased vascular events in MPN. 39 
In conclusion, ropeg offers a new treatment option for patients with pre-PMF or DIPSS low/intermediate-1–risk fibrotic PMF and SMF. Findings suggest that ropeg results in clinicohematologic, pathologic, and molecular responses. These benefits of ropeg will be confirmed in an ongoing randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter phase 3 trial in patients with pre-PMF or DIPSS-plus low- or intermediate-1–risk fibrotic PMF ( ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06468033 ). 40
