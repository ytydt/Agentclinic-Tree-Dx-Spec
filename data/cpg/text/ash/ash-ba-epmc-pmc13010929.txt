Abstract 
Radiotherapy is a definitive treatment for polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal protein, skin changes (POEMS) syndrome with solitary plasmacytoma. However, data on its efficacy and the role of immunochemotherapy are limited. This study evaluated the treatment outcomes of patients with POEMS syndrome receiving radiotherapy as their primary therapy and compared the results of those treated with adjunctive immunochemotherapy with those without. A retrospective analysis of patients with POEMS syndrome referred to the University College London Hospital from 1998 to 2023 was conducted. Among 150 patients with POEMS syndrome, 30 (20%) presented with solitary plasmacytoma. Sixteen patients (53%) received radiotherapy alone, whereas 14 (47%) had combined treatment with radiotherapy and immunochemotherapy, predominantly lenalidomide, and dexamethasone. Treatment responses were greater in the combined treatment group, with clinical, vascular endothelial growth factor, hematological, and radiological responses of 100%, 92%, 100%, and 100%, respectively, compared with 87%, 92%, 85%, and 79% in patients receiving radiotherapy only. The median follow-up for the entire cohort was 124 months, with a 5-year overall survival rate of 100% and a progression-free survival (PFS) rate of 82%. For those receiving radiotherapy alone, the median follow-up was 124 months, with a 3-year PFS rate of 69%. In contrast, no progression was observed among patients treated with combined therapy during the follow-up period (range, 7-54 months). Six patients experienced disease progression, with all having received radiotherapy alone as their first-line treatment. This study demonstrated the high efficacy of radiotherapy in patients with POEMS syndrome and solitary plasmacytoma and highlighted the potential benefits of combining radiotherapy with immunochemotherapy. 
Introduction 
Polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal protein, skin changes (POEMS) syndrome is a rare multisystem disorder characterized by plasma cell dyscrasia and a typical neuropathy. Although most patients require systemic therapy, radiotherapy is typically a definitive treatment option for those with an isolated plasmacytoma. 1 Since 2018, the University College London Hospital has used immunochemotherapy as an adjunctive therapy alongside radiotherapy for these patients, aiming to maximize the response rates and shorten the time to response. Due to the rarity of POEMS syndrome, data on the efficacy of radiotherapy and the role of adjunctive immunochemotherapy are limited. This study aims to evaluate the treatment outcomes of patients with POEMS syndrome receiving radiotherapy as their initial therapy and to compare these outcomes with those of patients undergoing adjunctive immunochemotherapy. 
Methods 
Study design 
This retrospective study involved patients with an established diagnosis of POEMS syndrome who were referred to the national POEMS service at the University College London Hospital in the United Kingdom between 1998 and 2023. Eligible participants had an isolated plasmacytoma identified via fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT) and received radiotherapy as their initial definitive treatment, with or without additional immunochemotherapy. Patients with evidence of bone marrow involvement by clonal plasma cells and Castleman disease were excluded. 
The study was performed using anonymized data. The National Health Service Research Ethics Committee has waived the requirement for formal ethics approval due to the absence of identifiable information. 
Outcomes 
The primary outcomes were clinical, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), hematological, and radiological responses. Secondary outcomes included progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). The criteria for each response category are based on previously reported measures 2 , 3 , 4 and are outlined below. If there was no measurable abnormality at presentation, the response was classified as unevaluable. 
Clinical response was categorized into 4 types: clinical improvement, clinical progression, mixed clinical response, and clinical stability. This assessment relied on qualitative reports from both clinicians and patients regarding symptoms and systemic features. Evaluable symptoms included peripheral neuropathy, organomegaly, papilledema, erythrocytosis, thrombocytosis, endocrinopathy, extravascular fluid overload, and abnormal pulmonary function tests. Peripheral neuropathy was not quantified on a standardized scale because only a few patients had detailed functional statuses recorded both before and after therapy. 
A complete VEGF response (CR VEGF ) was defined as normalization of VEGF levels (<771 pg/mL). A partial VEGF response was indicated by a decrease of >50% (from a baseline of ≥2000 pg/mL). No VEGF response (NR VEGF ) occurred when neither of the previous criteria were met. Progression was characterized by persistent VEGF elevation of ≥771 pg/mL from a previously normal result or a rise of >50% from the posttreatment nadir (if CR VEGF was not achieved). 
Hematological response criteria, adapted from the uniform criteria for multiple myeloma, included: 
• 
Complete response (CR H ): negative bone marrow examination and negative immunofixation of serum and urine. 
• 
Very good partial response (VGPR H ): 90% reduction in M-protein level or immunofixation-positive, provided the baseline was at least 5 g/L. 
• 
Partial response (PR H ): 50% reduction in M-protein or immunofixation-positive, with the baseline M-protein level being at least 10 g/L. 
• 
No response (NR H ): failure to meet the above criteria. 
Progression was defined as the reemergence of M-protein in serum and/or urine or an increase of ≥25% from posttreatment nadir, with M-protein levels exceeding 5 g/L. Patients qualifying for CR H did not require a repeat bone marrow examination if the baseline was negative. 
A complete radiological response (CR R ) was defined as the resolution of all initial foci of FDG avidity. A partial radiological response (PR R ) required >50% improvement in standardized uptake values of all initial FDG-avid foci. No radiological response indicated failure to meet these criteria. Progression was defined as an increase in the size or avidity of the lesion or the emergence of a new FDG-avid lesion. 
PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to disease progression in any of the 4 response categories or death from any cause. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause. 
Statistical analysis 
Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were described using the median and range. The 5-year PFS was calculated for the entire cohort. To address differences in the follow-up duration between the 2 treatment groups, data were censored at 36 months, and the 3-year PFS was calculated and compared. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Median follow-up was calculated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. A P value of <0.05 was deemed statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM; version 28.0). 
Results 
A total of 150 patients with POEMS syndrome were identified, among whom 30 (20%) presented with a solitary plasmacytoma on PET/CT. Among these 30 patients, most (80%) were male, with a median age of 50 years (range, 35-74 years). All patients had an appropriate POEMS syndrome–related polyneuropathy and a λ-restricted monoclonal plasma cell proliferative disorder, evidenced by the presence of paraproteinemia and/or biopsy-proven plasmacytoma. Bone marrow aspiration and trephine biopsy were performed on all patients, and none showed evidence of clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow at baseline. Fifteen patients (50%) had immunoglobulin G-λ (IgG-λ) paraproteinemia, 7 (23%) had IgA-λ paraprotein, and 3 (10%) exhibited 2 monoclonal bands (2 with IgG-λ and IgA-λ paraproteinemia and 1 with IgG-λ and IgG-κ paraproteinemia). The median baseline serum paraprotein level was 3 g/L (range, 0-11 g/L). Five patients (17%) presented with nonsecretory disease, with clonality confirmed by biopsy of the plasmacytoma. 
Baseline VEGF levels were elevated in 28 patients (93%), with a median of 2560 pg/mL (range, 405-15 422 pg/mL), which was slightly lower than that in patients with systemic (bone marrow-involving) disease. Of the 16 patients who received radiotherapy alone, clinical improvement was observed in 13 patients. One patient experienced clinical progression, whereas another attained clinical stability. The clinical response was not documented for 1 patient. In terms of VEGF response, 12 achieved CR VEGF , 1 had NR VEGF , and 3 had nonevaluable disease due to normal baseline VEGF levels. Regarding hematological response, 11 patients attained CR H , 2 had no hematological response, whereas 3 had nonsecretory disease. For radiological response, CR R was observed in 9 patients, PR R in 2, no radiological response in 1, and radiological progression in 2 patients; the outcomes for the remaining patients were undocumented. 
Among the 14 patients who received radiotherapy with adjunctive immunochemotherapy, all demonstrated clinical improvement. CR VEGF and partial VEGF response were seen in 11 patients and 1 patient, respectively. One patient had NR VEGF and 1 had nonevaluable disease. Seven patients achieved CR H, and 2 had a very good partial hematological response. The hematological response was nonevaluable for 3 patients and was not documented for 2 patients. Regarding radiological response, 5 patients attained CR R and 5 had PR R , whereas the response was not documented for the rest. Discussion 
Our study demonstrated that radiotherapy was highly efficacious in patients with POEMS syndrome and solitary plasmacytoma. The overall response rates were significantly higher when compared with those of a previously published cohort from the Mayo Clinic. Our study showed clinical, VEGF, hematological, and radiological response rates of 93%, 92%, 91%, and 88%, respectively, based on evaluable data. In contrast, the Mayo cohort had clinical, VEGF, hematological, and radiological response rates of 47%, 14%, 31%, and 22%, respectively. 6 However, these percentages were derived from all data, including patients with nonevaluable disease or missing information. Excluding nonassessable cases, we recalculated the clinical, VEGF, hematological, and radiological response rates in the Mayo cohort to 47%, 50%, 42%, and 62%, respectively, which are still well below those presented here. 
The differing response rates between the 2 cohorts may be attributed to the differences in disease burden. Our cohort comprised exclusively patients with solitary plasmacytoma, whereas 40% of the Mayo cohort had >1 lesion, with 3 patients having >3 lesions. Although it was shown that there was no difference in the rates of progression and OS in patients with 1 or 3 lesions, patients with >3 lesions were excluded from their analysis. This highlights the importance of patient selection and emphasizes that patients with multifocal plasmacytoma, even in the absence of marrow involvement, are likely to require systemic therapy because radiotherapy alone is likely insufficient for disease control. 
Another possible explanation for the better outcomes in this cohort is the use of PET/CT as part of the diagnostic workup; patients in the earlier Mayo cohort underwent either PET/CT or CT alone. Several studies have demonstrated that PET/CT can reveal additional lesions that then have therapeutic implications in a proportion of patients with previously presumed solitary plasmacytoma. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Current guidelines recommend using either PET/CT or whole-body magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for patients with presumed solitary plasmacytoma to exclude multifocal disease and guide treatment decisions. 11 , 12 , 13 PET/CT is superior to MRI in the assessment of focal lesion viability, whereas MRI is more sensitive in detecting bone marrow infiltration. 12 , 14 
In some cases, determining whether a small area of sclerosis is part of the disease or a benign bone island, fibrous dysplasia, nonossifying fibroma, or an aneurysmal bone cyst can be challenging. 6 PET/CT is useful in differentiating between benign lesions and plasmacytoma by assessing the metabolic activity. In this cohort, all plasmacytomas were found to be FDG-avid. In another study of solitary plasmacytomas, 82% of the lesions demonstrated FDG avidity, although small lesions were less FDG-avid than larger lesions. 15 It can be difficult to decide whether to treat all questionable lesions, particularly small non–FDG-avid lesions. Input from an experienced radiologist is crucial for guiding appropriate treatment options in this setting. 
A unilateral bone marrow aspiration and trephine biopsy is recommended for all patients with POEMS syndrome to look for the presence of clonal bone marrow plasma cells that necessitate a systemic therapy approach. Previous studies have shown that flow cytometry is a more sensitive method for detecting clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow. Hill et al 16 demonstrated that multiparametric flow cytometry could identify occult disease in bone marrow samples from patients with solitary plasmacytoma of the bone (SPB). In a series of 50 patients, 68% were found to have occult disease, which was associated with a higher risk of disease progression compared with that in those without (72% vs 12.5% at a follow-up of 3.7 years). Paiva et al 17 reported that 17 of 35 (49%) patients with SPB had clonal marrow plasma cells. Notably, 71% of patients with positive flow cytometry evolved to multiple myeloma, compared with only 8% of those with negative flow cytometry. This indicates that flow cytometry is a valuable and now more widely available tool in the detection of occult systemic disease. 17 Radiotherapy is likely to yield the best outcomes in patients with a solitary lesion on PET/CT and no monoclonal marrow plasma cells, as defined by multiparametric flow cytometry. 
The role of immunochemotherapy in solitary plasmacytoma remains controversial. A randomized controlled trial by Aviles et al 18 in 1996 showed that adjuvant melphalan and prednisolone improved multiple myeloma–free survival and OS. However, subsequent retrospective studies showed conflicting results. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 The IDRIS trial was designed to evaluate whether adjuvant lenalidomide could improve PFS compared with radiotherapy alone in patients with high-risk SPB. Although the trial was closed early due to low recruitment rates, there was some evidence that adjuvant lenalidomide prolonged PFS compared with the control arm despite the smaller than planned sample size (median not reached vs 13 months; hazard ratio, 0.32; 95% confidence interval, 0.09-1.07; P = .06). 24 
Currently, there is limited published evidence to support the use of combined treatment in the context of POEMS syndrome. 25 A Korean study reported that 6 of 7 patients with POEMS syndrome and plasmacytoma demonstrated a clinical response to chemoradiotherapy. In the Korean cohort, 3 patients had a single bone lesion, whereas 4 had >3 lesions. 26 Since 2018, our center has incorporated immunochemotherapy alongside radiotherapy to maximize response rates and shorten the time to response. Patients received a fixed number of cycles of immunochemotherapy, which was most commonly Rd and initiated immediately upon diagnosis, with additional cycles after completing radiotherapy. The response rates for patients undergoing the combined treatment were slightly higher (clinical, VEGF, hematological, and radiological overall response rates of 100%, 92%, 100%, and 100%, respectively) than those for the radiotherapy-only group (87%, 92%, 85%, and 79%, respectively). 
Notably, none of the patients who received the combined treatment progressed during the follow-up period. One possible explanation is that the immunochemotherapy may have suppressed occult systemic disease for this period. These findings suggest that the combined approach leads to improved longer-term outcomes. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, taking into account the relatively short follow-up period and small cohort size, which limit the robustness of these conclusions. 
Current guidelines recommend radiotherapy of 40 to 50 Gy in 1.8 to 2.0 Gy fractions (20-25 total fractions) to the involved site for solitary plasmacytoma. Treatment with 35 to 40 Gy is an acceptable alternate for solitary plasmacytomas <5 cm in size due to the high rates of local control reported for smaller tumors. 13 This typically takes 4 to 5 weeks to deliver, with a response assessment conducted at 3 months following the completion of radiotherapy. Additional time is needed for the treatment to take effect. Although the treatment of solitary plasmacytoma without POEMS syndrome focuses primarily on achieving local disease control, it is crucial to manage systemic symptoms and halt disease progression to limit organ damage, particularly peripheral neuropathy, which significantly affects daily functioning in POEMS syndrome. 1 , 25 This highlights the potential role of systemic therapy in this patient population. 
Our study confirmed that a fixed duration of Rd was well tolerated by most patients, with only 1 discontinuing the drug due to a severe rash. Lenalidomide has been widely used in patients with multiple myeloma, and common side effects include fatigue, gastrointestinal upset, rash, and hematological toxicities. 27 In addition, lenalidomide may increase the risk of thrombosis because POEMS syndrome itself is a prothrombotic condition. However, all patients received thromboprophylaxis in accordance with our local policy, 5 and no additional thrombotic events were reported. 
A pragmatic consideration is that radiation to the axial skeleton in transplant-eligible patients may hinder future peripheral blood stem cell harvest, particularly in cases of relapse in which autologous stem cell transplantation is planned as salvage therapy. For patients with primary lesions in the pelvis or sacrum, preemptive stem cell harvest and storage before initiating radiation therapy may be appropriate. 25 In our cohort, preemptive stem cell harvest was performed in 4 patients. One of them relapsed after radiotherapy and subsequently underwent consolidation with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation following induction treatment with Rd. 
Future studies could examine whether the combined approach hastens clinical response, thereby shortening the length of hospital stays and rehabilitation. However, this was not evaluated in this study due to the lack of standardized follow-up intervals, a limitation inherent to the retrospective design. Larger, prospective studies over extended periods are necessary to validate these findings and better understand the long-term benefits of integrating immunochemotherapy with radiotherapy. However, the rarity of POEMS syndrome poses a challenge in conducting these studies. 
In conclusion, this study demonstrated the high efficacy of radiotherapy in treating POEMS syndrome with solitary plasmacytoma and highlighted the potential therapeutic benefits of combining radiotherapy with immunochemotherapy. However, these promising results should be interpreted with caution due to the small cohort size and limited follow-up duration. Further research is essential to confirm the efficacy of these combined treatment strategies.
