Population PK of VWF: toward personalized surgery

In this issue of Blood Advances , Daniel et al 1 describe a population pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) model for high-purity von Willebrand factor (VWF) concentrate (Wilfactin) during surgery in von Willebrand disease (VWD), paving the way for personalized perioperative dosing. VWD is the most commonly inherited bleeding disorder that arises from quantitative or qualitative deficiencies in VWF, a crucial plasma glycoprotein that mediates platelet adhesion to damaged vascular endothelium and stabilizes factor VIII (FVIII) in circulation. Patients with VWD are prone to excessive bleeding, particularly during surgical procedures. 2 
The standard therapy for the prevention and treatment of bleeding in VWD is the infusion of VWF concentrates, which aim to increase both VWF and FVIII levels. These products vary in composition; some contain both VWF and FVIII, in which case both endogenous and exogenous FVIII contribute to the overall FVIII levels, whereas others, high-purity preparations, are nearly devoid of FVIII and rely on endogenous FVIII stabilization by the infused VWF. 3 , 4 , 5 
In surgical settings, VWD management involves precise VWF concentrate dosing to prevent bleeding while minimizing thrombosis risk. The current dosing guidelines for VWF concentrates are largely empirical and based on consensus recommendations from several organizations, namely the American Society of Hematology, International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis, National Hemophilia Foundation, and World Federation of Hemophilia. 6 For major surgeries, an initial loading dose of 40 to 80 IU/kg VWF:RCo is advised, followed by maintenance doses of 20 to 60 IU/kg every 8 to 24 hours to maintain the trough levels >50 IU/dL for 7 to 14 days. Minor procedures may require lower doses, such as 30 to 60 IU/kg initially, with monitoring adjusted to bleeding risk. These guidelines emphasize frequent laboratory monitoring of VWF and FVIII levels, often daily after infusion, to guide adjustments. 
However, challenges in dosing of VWF concentrates persist. Empirical dosing overlooks patient-specific factors, leading to resource inefficiency, increased monitoring burden, and potential complications. Moreover, the aforementioned guidelines do not consider the major differences between VWD subtypes in which the type of qualitative VWF defect affects the relationship between FVIII and VWF. 
Daniel et al present a pivotal study on high-purity VWF concentrate (Wilfactin) in perioperative patients with VWD, underscoring the substantial PK/PD variability and highlighting the limitations of one-size-fits-all approaches. Furthermore, this is the first study to integrate the pathophysiology of VWF and its qualitative and quantitative aspects into a PK/PD model. 
This retrospective analysis involved 74 patients who underwent 99 surgeries and used population PK/PD modeling to characterize VWF antigen (VWF:Ag) and FVIII activity (FVIII:C) dynamics. The model revealed high interindividual variability in key parameters, namely the baseline VWF:Ag (76.8% coefficient of variation [CV]), volume of distribution of VWF:Ag (54.5% CV), clearance of VWF:Ag (54.5% CV), baseline FVIII:C (38.8% CV), and the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC 50 ) of VWF:Ag as it pertains to FVIII clearance (31.9% CV). Overall, the PK variability ranged from 25.3% to 73.8% across parameters, emphasizing the unpredictable nature of concentrate response, even with high-purity products. 
The authors identified several covariates that significantly influenced these parameters. Body weight positively correlated with VWF:Ag volume of distribution, necessitating dose increases of 33% for patients who were underweight and reductions of 50% for patients with obesity to achieve equivalent exposure. Age influenced the baseline VWF:Ag levels with pediatric patients showing values ∼15% lower, and older patients aged >85 years showing levels ∼9% higher. Patients with blood group O had double the VWF:Ag clearance rate (157.6 mL/h vs 74.2 mL/h in non-O) with a reduction in the area under the curve of 10% for the VWF parameters. The VWD type affected the ratio of VWF activity to antigen (VWF:RCo: 0.42 in types 2A/B/M vs 0.61 in others, equating to a 31%-41% reduction) and IC 50 (0.46 IU/mL in types 2N/3 vs 1.00 IU/mL in others, indicating stronger FVIII stabilization in severe forms). VWF propeptide (VWFpp) positively correlated with the baseline VWF:Ag (exponent 0.24), reflecting endogenous production rates and varying by type (eg, 0.74 IU/dL in type 1 vs 0.08 IU/dL in type 3). 
However, this study was not the first to address the PK/PD data of VWF concentrates in surgical settings. A study by Bukkems et al introduced a population PK model that explored the VWF-FVIII interaction during perioperative treatment with a plasma-derived VWF/FVIII concentrate (Humate P/Haemate P) at a VWF/FVIII ratio of 2.4:1. 7 The study emphasized the protective role of VWF on FVIII half-life, because VWF inhibited FVIII clearance in a concentration-dependent manner. Furthermore, the model revealed that repetitive dosing, which is standard for major procedures, triggers FVIII accumulation with levels rising progressively because of the prolonged half-life. That explained the frequent supraphysiological FVIII spikes observed clinically, which increased thrombotic risk. 
In contrast, the study by Daniel et al focused on high-purity VWF concentrate to isolate the endogenous FVIII dynamics and to avoid confounding from coinfused FVIII in combined products. Simulations demonstrated that standard doses led to wide prediction intervals, particularly in type 2N (high FVIII variability) and type 3 (cumulative elevations with repeated dosing), thereby increasing the risks for thrombosis or bleeding. 
The value of population PK/PD modeling lies in its ability to integrate this variability for tailored dosing. Unlike empirical guidelines, these models use nonlinear mixed-effects approaches to estimate population parameters while accounting for individual deviations and covariates. In the study, the semimechanistic model, which incorporated VWF:Ag, VWF activity, and FVIII:C with an indirect response linking VWF to FVIII clearance, provided accurate predictions, as validated by visual predictive checks. By simulating scenarios (eg, 50 IU/kg loading followed by maintenance), it optimized regimens; for a patient with type 2A VWD, adjusted dosing led to better balanced levels than standard treatment, thereby minimizing waste and monitoring needs. 
This approach enables precision medicine in surgical VWD management. Clinicians can input the patient covariates (age, weight, VWD type, blood group, and VWFpp) to forecast responses and adjust doses preemptively. In resource-limited settings, it may reduce the reliance on frequent assays, thereby decreasing monitoring frequency while maintaining safety. Broader implications include cost savings and improved patient outcomes by averting complications such as venous thromboembolism from FVIII overaccumulation. 
The authors have appropriately acknowledged the study’s limitations. Notably, the model’s retrospective design and small subtype cohorts (eg, limited type 3 and pediatric patients) highlight the need for prospective validation. Although surgery type did not significantly influence variability, unmodeled factors, such as hematocrit fluctuations, could enhance precision in future iterations. 
In conclusion, dosing of VWF concentrates in surgical settings requires recognition of the profound PK/PD variability as demonstrated by recent modeling studies. Population PK/PD approaches, by incorporating key covariates, will help to create personalized strategies that enhance efficacy, safety, and efficiency. As VWD management continues to improve, adopting these models could transform perioperative care, thereby ensuring optimal hemostasis for diverse patient profiles.
