Pumping up immunity: exercise flexes memory NK cell attack

In this issue of Blood Advances , Batatinha et al 1 identify that aerobic exercise prior to blood natural killer (NK) cell collection enhances the function of cytokine-activated and memory-like (CIML or ML) NK cells, manifesting as improved in vitro and in vivo killing of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) (see figure ). 
Open in a new tab 
Exercise-mobilized human blood NK cells improve the function of both activated and ML NK cell function and in vivo xenograft leukemia control. 
The health benefits of exercise are well established, reducing the risks of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and boosting musculoskeletal and mental wellness, and brain health. Indeed, current recommendations for adults include 150 minutes of moderate intensity exercise plus muscle-strengthening activities on ≥2 days of each week. A lesser-known fact is that moderate intensity exercise has a multitude of positive effects on immunity. The field of exercise immunology has cataloged this impact by defining the changes in peripheral blood immune cell number and function before, during, and after exercise. 2 Consistent with this, clinical studies also demonstrate immune benefits. For example, individuals who regularly meet exercise guidelines have reduced mortality following influenza virus or pneumonia infections compared to sedentary people. 3 These findings have inspired a number of ideas to harness exercise to improve hematopoietic stem cell mobilization, T-cell expansion ex vivo, and notably, the overall functionality of NK cells. 4 
NK cells are cytotoxic innate lymphocytes that protect us from viral infection and mediate antitumor responses via immune functions similar to T helper 1 (Th1) (interferon gamma [IFN-γ], chemokines) and cytotoxic CD8 T cells (direct killing of cancer targets). As such, there is considerable rationale to leverage NK and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)–engineered NK cells as cancer therapy, in part based on their favorable safety profile in clinical trials, avoiding cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity often caused by CAR T-cell therapies. 5 However, conventional NK (cNK) cells collected from the blood of healthy donors have modest antitumor activity due to inadequate cytotoxicity, imperfect recognition of blood cancer targets, and limited in vivo survival. One strategy to address these challenges is to endow cNK cells with memory programs via interleukin-12 (IL-12), IL-15, and IL-18 signals, which upon differentiation generates CIML or ML NK cells. These ML NK cells have enhanced function, cancer target recognition, metabolic fitness, and vivo persistence compared to cNK cells. 6 Clinical trials utilizing ML NK cells for patients with high-risk relapsed or refractory AML have shown that this approach can safely induce complete remission in many patients. 7 , 8 Clinical production of ML NK cells requires collection of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients or healthy donors, yet little attention has been paid to the heterogeneity and status of the cNK cells at the time of collection, illuminating an opportunity to potentially enhance ML NK cell generation via an improved “starting cell material.” 
Building on prior work that showed that moderate aerobic exercise improves aspects of NK-cell functionality, 4 this study evaluates whether exercise-mobilization results in improved cytokine-activated (IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18 overnight stimulated) and ML NK cells (that arise 5-7 days later). First, cytokine-activated, exercised cNK cells were shown to have enhanced function against leukemia targets, suggesting that exercise increased cytokine receptor signaling in blood cNK cells. The benefits included greater in vitro cytotoxicity against several leukemia targets, as well as increased degranulation and IFN-γ production. By combining NK cells with a donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI), a strategy that is promising in clinical trials, 9 the authors found that exercised DLI and exercised ML NK cellular therapy provided the best control of leukemia xenografts. This improvement was associated with increased in vivo frequency of NK cells within the human lymphocyte compartment both 1- and 2-weeks following ML NK infusion in immunodeficient mice that underwent xenograft transplantation. Thus, this study showed that a simple preparative exercise session could enhance key functions of ML NK cells differentiated from exercised cNK cells, and this translated into improved in vivo leukemia control. 
Pondering the translational impact of this study, we found ourselves envisioning rows of healthy donors on treadmills who are dutifully exercising while attached to an apheresis instrument, undergoing hours of leukapheresis. What could go wrong? Thankfully, prior work in the field had identified β-adrenergic signals as a key mechanism responsible for exercise-mobilized immune cell changes. Such a pharmacologic simulation of exercise may be more feasible to implement as a strategy to collect “exercised” NK-cell products. This study also opens new avenues of research, for example, to elucidate mechanisms whereby exercise promotes this enhanced response by DLI and ML NK cells against AML targets, and whether distinct cNK-cell populations mobilized by exercise, are responsible for the improved NK-cell therapy results. Indeed, 2 subsets of ML NK cells have been identified, derived from cNK CD56 bright (eML1) or CD56 dim (eML2) subsets. 10 Because exercise preferentially mobilized mature CD56 dim NK cells, one hypothesis that need to be further tested is that exercised cNK cells result in selective promotion of the eML2 subset. Although NK cells are the focus of this study, prior work has also identified the benefits of exercise in several lymphocyte types, broadening the potential impact of “exercised” immune cells as starter materials for other activated cellular therapy products (eg, CAR T cells, γδ T cells). Finally, this study is an important reminder of prior findings in the exercise immunology field and to continue to creatively think outside the box for new ways to improve cellular therapies. Go hit the gym and pump up your NK cells!
