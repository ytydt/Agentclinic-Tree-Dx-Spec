Real-world data in the absence of RCTs: the case for DOACs

In this issue of Blood Advances , using data from a US-based commercial health care database, Dawwas et al 1 report that among patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) with venous thromboembolism (VTE), risks of serious bleeding events were lower with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) than with warfarin. The risk of recurrent VTE was similar for the 2 medications. 
This finding is important as it adds to a growing body of evidence suggesting that DOACs are a safe alternative to both vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) such as warfarin and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) for the prevention of recurrent VTE in patients including those with IBD. Recent clinical guidelines from the United States have favored the use of DOACs over VKAs for the treatment of VTE except in a few specific circumstances. 2 This study also found that the risk of serious bleeding events with DOACs among patients with IBD was approximately double that in a matched group of those without IBD. Overall, the incidence of recurrent VTE was 11 per 100 person-years in the DOAC group compared with 12.7 per 100 person-years in the warfarin group, with slightly more deep vein thrombosis than pulmonary embolism events in both groups. Serious bleeding rates per 100 person-years were 8.2 and 13.6 in the DOAC and warfarin groups, respectively. Among the DOAC group, gastrointestinal bleeding events were the most frequent (27/49) followed by hematuria, with a similar hazard ratio compared with warfarin for different types of bleeds. There was no statistical evidence of effect modification by IBD type or presence of an IBD flare for both outcomes. 
These results are consistent with those from a multicenter observational study of 1025 patients with cerebral venous thrombosis, which also found that patients receiving DOACs had a similar risk of recurrent VTE but lower risks of bleeding than those taking warfarin. 3 Recent relevant data are also available from a systematic review of 5 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies exploring DOACs with LMWH for extended prophylaxis following colorectal surgery. 4 However, for the outcomes of VTE and major bleeding, event numbers were low, so a direct comparison between DOACs and LMWH was not possible. Guidelines worldwide on the management of VTE must deal with heterogeneity between medications which fall within each drug class as well as in the patient group. Patients with cancer are the largest single group covered by these guidelines. In a meta-analysis of 4 well-conducted RCTs, Mulder et al 5 concluded that compared with LMWH, patients with cancer with a VTE and receiving DOACs had a nonsignificant lower rate of recurrent VTE and with no clear direction of result for major bleeding. This is somewhat discrepant with a study using US-based commercial health care databases, which found apixaban to have a lower rate of both recurrent VTE and major bleeding than LMWH among 14 000 people with active cancer. 6 The same study also found patients receiving apixaban to have lower risks of recurrent VTE and major bleeding than those receiving warfarin, although the difference for bleeding was not statistically significant. A similar finding was observed in a separate observational study using health care databases from Quebec, Canada. 7 As Dawwas et al highlight in their discussion, conducting adequately powered RCTs of VTE management in specific patient groups presents a challenge, hence we are reliant on data from well-conducted observational research to inform clinical practice. However, as the above data for cancer and VTE highlights, the potential for differences in findings between observational studies and randomized trials should not be ignored. 
Propensity scores are a method used to mimic an RCT with the intention that patients receiving an intervention are, on average, similar to a control group with respect to factors which may influence the outcomes of the study. 8 This is achieved through estimating the probability of treatment given observed characteristics and using this score to match a patient receiving an intervention, with 1 or more receiving the comparator. A strength of the Dawwas et al study was that 97 separate variables were included in the propensity score, many more variables than it would be feasible to incorporate into a conventional statistical model where confounding variables are adjusted for individually. After matching DOAC and warfarin users according to the propensity score (leaving 1087 patients in each group), the authors were able to show that groups were more comparable than the original cohorts. However, propensity scores are not a panacea which circumvent the limitations of observational research, and the potential for residual confounding remains. 8 As acknowledged in the study, indicators of disease severity and lifestyle variables were not captured by the MarketScan IBM database. As such, based on discrepant findings from RCTs and observational studies, we cannot be entirely confident that a well-designed RCT in this patient group would produce the same result or that in a hypothetical scenario where large numbers of patients switched from warfarin to DOACs, we would see fewer major bleeding events with no impact on rates of recurrent VTE which the results from this study would indicate at a surface level. 
DOACs which have been marketed since 2008 in Europe and 2010 in the United States, have become an increasingly important option for clinicians in the management of VTE, owing to potentially greater reductions in recurrent VTE and improved tolerability compared with LMWH and lower rates of major bleeding than warfarin. Consideration of optimal treatment is especially important for patients with IBD, who are at a higher risk of a major bleed as reported in this study and elsewhere. 9 Current European guidelines aimed specifically at those with IBD, advise DOACs as the first-line therapy for treating VTE. 10 In the absence of RCTs, future research should make use of large electronic health care data sets to conduct studies across different settings and among subgroups of patients to increase external validity of recommendations. Studies with longer term follow-up would also be beneficial to inform the appropriateness of DOACs where extended anticoagulation beyond 3 months is required. Overall, the Dawwas et al study deserves recognition for extending the evidence for DOACs to a group of patients known to be at high risk of VTE, in a real-world setting.
